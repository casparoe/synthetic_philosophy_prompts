A friend who builds robot controllers has an argument that functionalism is empty, and I can't locate the misstep. (1) Functionalism says a system's mental states are its internal states as individuated by their causal relations to inputs, outputs, and each other; whether a system is in pain depends only on whether it realizes the right organization, which can be written out as a finite state-transition table. (2) Now take a wall: over any hour its surface passes through a vast number of microscopic configurations. (3) For any finite table, those configurations can be grouped so that the groups stand in exactly the transition relations the table's states stand in — she says this is just combinatorics plus the freedom to group, the way you can always find a path through a dense enough graph. (4) The grouping needn't be written down by anyone to exist. (5) The wall's transitions are genuinely caused by its own earlier states, so it isn't as though causation is in short supply. (6) So the wall realizes a simple pain-and-avoidance table — indeed, by the same recipe, every table any machine has ever run. (7) But the wall feels nothing; so functionalism is either false or so liberal that it attributes minds to walls, and either way it has lost its point. When I suggested that some groupings must be gerrymandered, she demanded a criterion of a real grouping that doesn't just stipulate that walls are dumb, and that also gives the right answer for a slow, noisy robot running the very same table. I want to keep (1): I think functionalism is roughly right about what pains are, so I'd like the flaw to be in (3) or (5), but I can't make either one misbehave. Tell me which step or inference to reject and what rejecting it costs; give the criterion she demands, saying what it yields for the wall and for the robot; and don't hand me a menu of candidate diagnoses — commit to the one load-bearing flaw. Please return your answer as a single JSON object with the fields "verdict" (the numbered step or inference you reject), "diagnosis" (why it looked fine and why it fails), "criterion" (your account of when a grouping constitutes realization, with its wall and robot verdicts), "cost" (what the rejection costs, and to whom), and "residual_worry" (the strongest surviving objection to your criterion).
