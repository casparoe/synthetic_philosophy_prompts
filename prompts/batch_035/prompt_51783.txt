A friend and I argued about the agricultural revolution late into the night, and near the end he compressed his case into a summary he insisted was just the standard view, worth checking on its own terms: hunter-gatherers were, in a phrase he took from Marshall Sahlins, the original affluent society — a few hours of work a day and plenty for everyone. Farming appeared, and from then on the average person's life got worse: skeletons from early farming villages show more malnutrition and disease than forager remains, because farmers ate a narrow diet and lived in their own waste next to their animals. Farming also invented inequality — foragers held essentially no property and so had no rich and poor, while every agricultural society has elites extracting from peasants. And once a population farms, it cannot go back, because its numbers have grown past what hunting can support; so for thousands of years the average farmer was worse off than a forager many millennia earlier, and only elites gained. He closed by borrowing Jared Diamond's line that farming was the worst mistake in the history of the human race. When I objected that no one ever chose agriculture over hunting in a single decision — it accumulated in small steps, each locally sensible, so "mistake" seems like the wrong word — he dismissed that as semantics. I'm inclined to agree with his substance, but the whole thing felt too tidy. Go through his summary claim by claim: which parts would an archaeologist or anthropologist sign off on, which are contested, and which are quietly wrong — especially his use of Sahlins, whose actual argument I only half-remember. Then tell me whether his conclusion, that the average person was worse off and only elites gained, survives once the corrected parts replace the faulty ones. And please address my objection directly: was he right that calling it a "mistake" versus a bad unchosen outcome is merely verbal?
