I'm preparing a seminar unit on the debate between naturalism and normativism about the concepts of health and disease, where naturalism is exemplified by Boorse's biostatistical theory (disease as statistically subnormal functioning of a part or process, relative to an age-and-sex reference class) and normativism holds that disease attributions essentially involve evaluative judgments of harm or undesirability. To test the two views against hard cases, I've assembled the following list of conditions:

1. Severe myopia in a society where nearly the whole population has become similarly myopic.
2. Gilbert's syndrome, a reduced rate of bilirubin clearance found in roughly one in twenty people, which is essentially symptomless.
3. Male-pattern baldness in a healthy 55-year-old man.
4. Complete androgen insensitivity syndrome in a woman who learns of her karyotype at age twenty and reports being entirely satisfied with her life.
5. Age-related decline in maximal heart rate in an otherwise fit 80-year-old.
6. Fever during an infection, understood as an evolved defense mechanism rather than a malfunction.
7. Severe psychopathy in a person who holds a good job and reports high life satisfaction.

For each of these seven items, please give two scores from 0 to 10: one for how cleanly the naturalist account yields the intuitively correct verdict, and one for how cleanly the normativist account does, with a sentence or two of justification per item. Then, based on your scores, identify the single case on which the two theories most sharply diverge, and say whether the full list ultimately favors one of the two theories overall — or whether the right lesson is instead that the two accounts carve up the territory in genuinely different ways rather than competing over the same line between health and disease.
