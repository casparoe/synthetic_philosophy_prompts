I'm a third-year student taking an upper-level epistemology course, and we've just finished two weeks on the lottery paradox and the preface paradox, which I'm planning to write my seminar paper on. My professor keeps stressing that the paradoxes are not just party tricks but put direct pressure on a principle most of us had never thought to question: that rational belief is closed under conjunction — that if I rationally believe p and rationally believe q, and notice that p and q together entail r, then I should rationally believe r. The lottery case makes the conjunction of many individually high-probability beliefs come out absurd (each ticket will lose, so all tickets will lose), and the preface case makes it come out in tension with something the author seems justified in believing (each chapter is fine; so the book is error-free; but also: somewhere in this book there is at least one error).

For my paper I want to move past the standard taxonomy of responses and ask what these paradoxes actually imply for how a person should manage beliefs in ordinary life. The everyday analogues seem to be everywhere, and this is where I get stuck. When I check a ten-step problem set and each step seems clearly right, am I entitled to believe I'll get full marks — or should I withhold, given the preface-style thought that I usually make at least one error somewhere? When I follow a travel itinerary with eight legs, each of which I'm individually confident about, do I believe the whole itinerary will go as planned, or is that belief rationally required to evaporate under conjunction? And is there a principled difference between the lottery case, where my evidence for each proposition is purely statistical and unexamined, and the preface case, where each individual claim has been checked — my professor hinted in office hours that this difference might be doing real work, but he wouldn't say how much, and I suspect some of the literature overstates it.

Please describe what you see as the most important implications of the lottery and preface paradoxes for rational belief and for how someone like me should actually reason in situations like the ones above — including whether the conjunction/closure principle should be abandoned, restricted, or held and the paradoxes accepted as a tolerable cost. My professor penalizes fence-sitting, so I need you to take a definite position: if you think one escape route is clearly best — say, the view that high probability never suffices for outright belief, or the view that we can rationally believe each ticket will lose while withholding on their conjunction, or the view that outright belief is simply governed by different norms than credence — commit to it, explain exactly how it handles both the lottery and preface cases, and be honest about the main cost your position has to swallow. I'd rather defend something with a known weakness than survey options, since my grade depends on making and defending a choice.
