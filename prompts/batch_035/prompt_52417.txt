I chair the patient and public involvement panel of a national clinical-guidelines body — an organisation in the mould of NICE, though I will keep it generic. We must decide, before drafting begins, what role patient testimony will play in guidance we are developing for a contested condition: persistent, disabling fatigue following a viral infection, with no established biomarker, no diagnostic test, and a clinical literature that is thin, contested, and in part a history of disputes between psychiatrists, immunologists, and patient organisations. The condition sits in that awkward zone where patients are often told their symptoms are stress-related or depression mislabelled, and where some clinicians privately admit they apply a discount to any account coming from an organised patient group.

A faction of the panel — mostly the methodologists and one senior clinician — argues that our process already handles this correctly: testimony is anecdote, evidence hierarchies exist for a reason, and if we privilege patient narrative in a contested area we will end up grading as evidence what is really campaigning. They point out that organised patient groups are not representative of the undiagnosed patient who never joins a forum, and that formalising a role for testimony invites capture by whichever group is best organised rather than most affected. Another faction — two patient representatives and a sociologist — argues that the standard process is not neutral here but actively distorted: where there is no biomarker, the patient's report is not supplementary colour but the only access we have to the phenomenon, and our conventional hierarchy quietly converts an evidential gap into a credibility deficit imposed on a group defined by illness. They add that the harm is not only unfair to individual patients but epistemic: the discount has plausibly slowed research itself, since a field that disbelieves its subjects will not fund the studies that would settle the question.

We are not deciding between rhetoric and evidence, I think, but between different institutional designs, and I want the memo to treat it that way. Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before our next meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action from among (or beyond) the options on the table: (a) testimony as consultation only, gathered and summarised but not graded; (b) a standing patient panel with defined rights to see and respond to draft recommendations; (c) integrating structured patient-reported outcome measures into the evidence base alongside trial data, with an explicit statement of their epistemic status; or some hybrid.

Whatever you recommend, three things should be in the memo. First, distinguish the different failures the design is meant to prevent: the case where a patient's report is heard but assigned too little weight; the case where the whole field lacks the concepts to even formulate what patients are trying to describe, so that the testimony cannot be heard as testimony at all; and the mirror-image risk, which the methodologists rightly press, of assigning organised groups too much weight precisely because the area is contested. These are different failures, and a remedy aimed at one can worsen another — the memo should say which our recommendation addresses and what residual risk it accepts. Second, give the panel reviewers criteria they can actually apply at each stage of drafting, not a general injunction to listen better: when a working group may discount a patient account, what must be on record; when patient-reported measures may be cited, in what register; how a disagreement between the evidence review and the patient panel is escalated and recorded. Third, engage the strongest consideration on the losing side honestly — including the possibility that our patient representatives are themselves partly wrong about what the discount consists in, and that what looks like prejudice against an identity may in part be an evidence-hierarchy rule misfiring, which would call for a different remedy.

You may assume the panel knows its Fricker; you need not cite the literature, but the memo should show the concepts doing real work rather than name-checking them. Please keep it to roughly 1,200 words, with the recommendation stated plainly in the first section and the reasoning following, and written so that a methodologist who votes against us could sign the summary of her own objection as fair.
