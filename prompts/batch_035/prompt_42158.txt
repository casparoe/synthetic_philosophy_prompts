So this just became my problem in a very direct way. I run a small business in the UK with 12 employees, and a few years back when pension auto-enrolment came in, I dutifully enrolled everyone who qualified. Three of my employees asked to opt out, which is allowed — you fill in a form, get a refund of what you'd paid in, and you're out. Fine, their choice, I thought. But here's the thing that's been bugging me since a letter from the pensions regulator arrived last month: every three years, I have to go through "re-enrolment." I must put those same three people back into the pension scheme automatically, treat them as enrolled all over again, and then — if they still don't want in — they have to opt out again, by a deadline, or keep contributing. One of them is a guy in his late twenties who's adamant he'd rather have the cash now; he's now had to jump through the opting-out hoop twice and I'll have to make him do it a third time.

I was chatting about this with a friend who works in HR and she said, matter of factly, "well, people stick with the default, that's just how people are." Which made me wonder what the whole setup actually assumes about the people it applies to. The default seems to rest on some idea that inertia, not considered judgment, is doing most of the work in these choices — that if we left people alone, most of them wouldn't get around to saving, and that their opting out is more likely a product of present bias or procrastination-on-forms than a settled preference about their own money. And then re-enrolment adds something stronger: it treats an opt-out as something that goes stale, like a decision that has a shelf life, so the state keeps re-testing it every three years. It's a nudge that refuses to take no for an answer indefinitely.

So what I want to know is this: what view of autonomy and paternalism does this system actually presuppose? Is the thought that a genuine preference would express itself repeatedly, so the inconvenience of opting out again and again is a filter that separates real preferences from mere inertia? Or is it the opposite — that the burden is deliberately asymmetric because the designers judged that people who opt out are mostly wrong about their own interests, and a friction that only bites one way will slowly catch the "mistaken" ones? And is there a principled line here at all — like, would it matter if re-enrolment happened every year, or every ten years, or is any cycle just as defensible? I'd also be curious whether this is consistent with the state's attitude elsewhere, since we don't re-test people's other big decisions this way (nobody re-signs you up to smoke so you can quit again). Basically: reconstruct what the policy is implicitly assuming about choice, self-knowledge, and whose judgment about a person's future counts, and tell me whether the three-year re-enrolment cycle can be justified under any theory of paternalism that still takes the opters-out seriously as agents — or whether it just quietly doesn't.
