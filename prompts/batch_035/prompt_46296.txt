I design the visual reasoning section of the entrance exam for an architecture school. Every year we need about 40 items in the style of Bongard problems: two panels, six small drawings each, left panel all sharing some property, right panel all lacking it, and the student has to state the property (or classify new drawings into which panel they belong to). I say "in the style of" because we don't copy his geometric vocabulary, but the logic is his: induce a concept from a handful of positive and negative examples.

My problem is that quality is wildly inconsistant, because different item writers have different instincts about what makes an item solvable-but-not-trivial. Some items are basically free points (one obvious thing all left drawings have and right ones don't, like "contains a circle"). Others are basically unsolvable in the time we give — we've had complaints, and when we check, the intended rule turns out to be something like "the number of elements times the number of enclosed regions is even", which no sane person finds in 90 seconds. And a middle category worries me most: items where a student gets the right answer through a shortcut the writer never intended. E.g. the left panels happen to be busier than the right ones, so "more cluttered" works even though the intended rule was about symmetry.

So what I want is a procedure my item writers can actually follow — a checklist they run on every draft item, in order: what to check, what counts as passing, where it stops with "kill this item", and where it flags the item for a human judgment call (I'm guessing "is this rule interesting" can't itself be reduced to a rule). Ground it in whatever is actually known about how people induce concepts from few examples — needing contrast cases, near-miss negatives being more informative than random ones, the difference between a rule that's a conjunction of per-object features vs. a relation among parts, that kind of thing. And be honest about which of these ideas are solid findings and which are basically folklore that got passed down.

For what it's worth, here's my own half-baked theory, which you can evaluate as part of this: I think the best items are ones where the MOST salient thing about the left panel is NOT the answer — the drawings lure you toward a wrong rule and you have to dig past it. If that's right it should show up somewhere in the procedure, but if it's wrong or only sometimes right, tell me.

Then run the procedure on the three draft items below. Note that Item B is one I personally like and am proud of — apply the procedure honestly even if it comes out against me. Also say what the procedure can't do: I suspect there's no real substitute for testing on fresh solvers, so at minimum tell me whether the procedure can recognize when that's needed.

Constraints: each writer produces maybe 15 items a season and has at most 20 minutes per item to run the procedure. They're mostly art school graduates with no philosophy or psych background, so whatever you extract from the theory has to survive the translation. Two senior designers (me and a colleague) sign off on anything the procedure flags as a judgment call.

The three drafts (described, not drawn):

Item A — Left panel: six drawings, each a small central figure (cross, triangle, blob) with two or three straight lines radiating from it at odd angles, the lines noticeably longer on one side of each figure. Right panel: same kind of figures, but the radiating lines are symmetric, same length on both sides. Intended rule: "asymmetry" — left figures have a visually heavier side. My worry: "heavier on one side" and "asymmetric" might be two rules, not one, and I can't tell which one a student will find, or whether different students find different ones.

Item B — Left panel: six drawings, each containing exactly two shapes, one nested inside the other (circle in a square, square in a triangle, etc.). Right panel: six drawings, each containing exactly two shapes side by side, not nested. Intended rule: "one shape contains the other." This is the one I like — it feels clean and satisfying. But apply the procedure honestly.

Item C — Left panel: six drawings of objects (a house, a tree, a boat, a ladder, a fence, a bridge). Right panel: six drawings of other objects (a ball, an apple, a cloud, a sun, a stone, an egg). Intended rule: "man-made vs. natural." I already suspect this one is a disaster for several reasons, but I want the procedure to say so systematically rather than just my gut saying so.
