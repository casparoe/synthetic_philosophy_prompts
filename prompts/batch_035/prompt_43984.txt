My best friend and I spent most of a long weekend arguing about when a person is dead, and by the end I had given up on persuading her and started trying to work out what I myself actually believe, because my verdicts on particular cases turned out to be far more stable than anything I could say in general. Her position was the clean one: a person is dead when the conscious someone is permanently gone, so a ventilated body with no brain function is dead, and so, by her lights, is a patient in a permanent vegetative state. I found myself resisting her at nearly every case while being unable to state a principle of my own. Since then I have been cataloguing my verdicts, and I would like to know whether they add up to anything.

1. A patient whose entire brain, including the brainstem, has permanently ceased functioning, but whose heart keeps beating with ventilator support: dead. I am confident.
2. A patient in a permanent vegetative state, brainstem intact, breathing without assistance, cycling between sleep and waking, but with no evidence of consciousness: alive. My most confident verdict of all.
3. An anencephalic infant, brainstem present but no cortex ever developed, breathing on its own: alive. Yet this one wobbles the moment I set it next to (1). If losing the whole brain makes you dead, why does never having had most of it leave you alive?
4. A donation-after-circulatory-death donor: the heart stops, five minutes pass with no sign it will restart on its own, organs are retrieved: dead, and comfortably so.
5. A person in cardiac arrest being actively resuscitated: not dead yet, and in my judgment becomes dead only when the resuscitation is abandoned.
6. A severely hypothermic hiker found with no circulation and no detectable brain function, who could plausibly be revived by rewarming: I hold, with the rescue workers, that such a patient is not dead until rewarmed and still without a pulse. But this collides with (4): in both cases the heart has stopped and will not restart spontaneously, and the only difference is that in (6) someone might reverse it. If the mere existence of a possible intervention keeps the hiker alive, why not the arrest victim in (5)? If what matters is only that the heart will not resume on its own, why not declare the hiker dead on the mountainside?

So my question is this: is there a single principle, whether a whole-brain criterion, a circulatory one, a permanence-versus-irreversibility distinction, or something I have not thought of, that delivers all six verdicts? If so, state it precisely and tell me which clause does the work in each case. If not, tell me which of my verdicts has to go and what the simplest defensible principle that remains would say. And please tell me what your principle predicts about the case I keep circling back to: a pregnant woman who satisfies the whole-brain criterion for death, whose body is maintained on support for four months so that the fetus reaches viability. Is she dead while her body grows a child — and does your answer make her death depend on what her body is being used for?
