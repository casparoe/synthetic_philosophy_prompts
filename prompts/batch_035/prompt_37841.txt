I teach political philosophy, with a long-standing side interest in famine and social choice theory, and for the past three years I have served on the ethics and advocacy advisory panel of a large European humanitarian organization. The panel has been asked for something we are not trained to produce, and I would like your help producing it.

Twice a year, sometimes more often, the organization faces what its advocacy director calls the "man-made question." A food crisis is underway; the press wants to know whether to call it a natural disaster or something with authors; and the language we choose affects fundraising, government access, and, occasionally, whether our country teams are expelled. The decision is currently made ad hoc by whoever is senior in the room, under a 48-hour news deadline, by people with no training in the relevant analysis — desk officers who know the logistics of food aid but not the difference between a famine of food-availability decline and a famine of entitlement collapse. The panel was asked to give them something better. The conceptual resources are ones I know well from the literature — Amartya Sen's entitlement approach, his argument that famines are about people losing their command over food rather than food vanishing, his observation that no substantial famine has occurred in a functioning democracy with a free press, and the further body of work arguing that certain famines are better described as crimes than as disasters, turning on whether deaths were foreseen, intended, or exploited. But knowing the literature does not tell a desk officer what to do by Thursday, and that is the gap I need you to fill.

What I want is a written procedure the desk can actually run: an ordered sequence of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates with a firm classification, where it terminates with an explicit "cannot classify on available evidence," and where it must hand the decision back to a human because the remaining question is one of values or organizational strategy rather than fact. One structural requirement, which I suspect is where most attempts go wrong: the procedure must keep the classificatory verdict (is this famine a natural disaster, an institutional failure, or a political crime?) strictly separate from the tactical one (given the verdict, should we say so publicly?), and must make clear that the second is never settled by the first. It should also be robust to disputed death tolls and contested causation — it should classify under uncertainty rather than demand numbers nobody has.

Then run the procedure on three historical cases as worked examples: Bengal in 1943, the Irish famine of the 1840s, and the Great Leap Forward. These are cases where the categories have been contested — including whether what distinguishes a crime from a failure is intent, recklessness, foreseeability-ignored, or exploitation after the fact — and where the procedure's treatment of that distinction will determine the verdicts. I will tell you honestly: I expect the procedure, if it is any good, to classify at least one of these cases in a way I am uncomfortable with, probably by treating Bengal as less than a crime on an intent-based test, and I would rather see that worked through than smoothed over. Please include one worked case where the procedure defers to human judgment, and say explicitly where you think the procedure will misfire in live crises.

Finally, a request about your own epistemic posture: wherever the procedure issues a verdict on one of the three cases, say what argument or consideration, if it held up, would reverse that verdict, and identify the single step in the procedure where you think the reasoning is most likely to fail. I would rather know where the machine breaks than be told it works.
