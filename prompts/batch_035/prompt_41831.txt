I'm taking a political philosophy course this semester, and our distributive justice unit is built around a question one economist famously framed as: equality of what? Last week we each had to draft a policy memo on compensating people with mobility disabilities for the extra cost of getting around, and two people in my study group, Maya and Ben, got into it and never really resolved anything.

Maya went first: the only fair metric is resources. You figure out the extra money a wheelchair user typically spends on transit, and you give everyone with that disability the same amount, period. Anything else, she said, makes equality depend on people's subjective states, which are unmeasurable and nobody's business. Ben pushed back with the case of someone in a rural county with no accessible buses: handing her the same cash you'd give a wheelchair user in a city with paratransit everywhere is formal equality only — she ends up with the same bank balance but a fraction of the actual ability to leave her house, so in any sense that matters she's far worse off, and the extra support she'd need to recieve to be equally mobile isn't captured by Maya's flat payment. Maya answered that once you start equalizing outcomes instead of inputs, you've opened the door to the expensive-taste problem: resources flow to whoever reports the least ability or the least satisfaction, which rewards people who don't cope and punishes people who heroically adapt — the person who ingeniously organizes her life around her limitations ends up with less support, purely for being resourceful, which seems like a strange thing for egalitarians to do. Ben said that was actually the argument for his view, not against it: he was never proposing to poll anyone's happiness. The metric should be capabilities — real, effective opportunity to do basic things like get to work, see friends, see a doctor — which is objective enough to plan policy around, doesn't reward grumbling, and also handles the case Maya's metric can't see: someone who has internalized deprivation and sincerely reports being content with very little (adaptive preferences, I think he called it), who would recieve nothing under resource equality and correctly nothing under welfare equality, but who is obviously not doing well.

Maya's last word was that Ben's list of capabilities is just someone's value judgments enshrined as policy: there is no principled way to decide what counts as basic, or how much of each capability the state actually owes — one person's basic is another person's luxury — and besides, capabilities are only instrumentally good. We value being able to get around because of what getting around does for our lives, so Ben has smuggled welfare back in as the real bottom line and just won't admit it. They sort of ran out of steam after that. I mostly took notes and stayed out of it. Can you go through their exchange move by move and tell me who's right about what at each step, whether either of them misdescribes the other's position anywhere, and what, if anything, both of them are missing?
