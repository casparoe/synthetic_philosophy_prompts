You are a professional philosopher of history with a side interest in film. I'm teaching a unit on objectivity in historical knowledge — Leopold von Ranke's ambition to show the past as it actually was, set against E.H. Carr's insistence that one must first study the historian before the facts, and Hayden White's narrativism, on which historical accounts are emplotted rather than simply discovered. Next week I'm screening Kurosawa's Rashomon (1950), and I want my students to treat the film as a philosophical text rather than an easy illustration. Please think through the reading carefully — you might begin with a brief brainstorm of what the film could be taken to show — before committing to a line, and then give me a reading of the film against that debate. Pay particular attention to whether the standard analogy even holds: White's scepticism concerns a historian emplotting events that are, in the main, already established, whereas the film gives us self-interested eyewitnesses and no historian at all — so is Rashomon really about narrativism, or about testimony? Then consider the ending: the commoner deduces that the woodcutter stole the dagger, which shows that at least one story can be checked against a stubborn fact — is that the Rankean foothold? And is the woodcutter's final act with the abandoned baby the recovery of a truth outside all the stories, or just one more emplotment? I want a reading that is faithful in both directions — it does not reduce the film to a case study, and it does not distort the philosophers to fit the film — that says honestly where the film resists each side, and that identifies what the film contributes to this debate that the argumentative literature alone cannot.
