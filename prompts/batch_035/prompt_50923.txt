In my philosophy of economics seminar we just did Friedman's 1953 methodology essay, the one where he says the literal truth of a theory's assumptions doesn't matter much and that the more significant a theory, the more unrealistic its assumptions are allowed to be. Afterwards a classmate (Dan) laid out an argument he thinks gets him everything Friedman wanted and more, and I keep nodding along at each step and then hating where it ends up.

(1) Any model that explains anything must be simpler than what it models, so every assumption in every model is, taken literally, false — frictionless planes, ideal gases, firms that adjust prices instantly with zero information costs. (2) So the criticism "that assumption is unrealistic" applies to every model ever built, successful ones included. (3) A criticism that would apply no matter what the model got right or wrong cannot count against any particular model — it's like criticizing a map for not being the territory. (4) Therefore the only legitimate test of a model is whether its predictions succeed, and complaints about unrealistic assumptions are always out of order. (5) But Dan also happily concedes that we can reject in advance a traffic model that assumes drivers are perfectly attentive altruists, and that the frictionless-plane treatment of a marble dropped in honey is not on all fours with the frictionless-plane treatment of a curling stone on ice. (6) So (4) has to be false. Dan says no: what's really happening in (5) is that "that assumption matters here" is just a disguised prediction that the model will fail in a certain domain, so assumption-talk collapses into prediction-talk and (4) survives untouched.

I want to know which step to actually reject and what rejecting it costs. My instinct is that the damage is somewhere between (2) and (3) — something about a criticism applying to everything versus applying to everything to the same degree — but when I try to spell that out, Dan says I'm just redescribing predictive success with extra words. And if I take his escape hatch in (6), it feels like the thesis quietly changed shape, from "only predictions test models" to "assumption criticism is prediction criticism in a costume," and I can't tell whether those have the same consequences when you're actually fighting about a model or only sound the same. Help me price the options: which premise goes, why it looked fine, and what each side of the fight has to pay.
