My friend Maya and I spent basically all of Saturday arguing about whether you can separate the art from the artist, and the argument only ended because the restaurant was closing, so now I want to know how she'd rule on a case we never got to. Her slogan is "judge the work, not the person," but her actual behavior is way more patterned than that slogan suggests, and I've been trying to reconstruct the pattern. Her record, as best I can tell:

She'll happily cross the city to see Caravaggio paintings and calls him a genius, and she rolls her eyes at people who say you should feel weird looking at art by a man who literally killed a guy. She goes to Wagner whenever there's a production on, even though his antisemitism is all over his essays and, she thinks, kind of audible in the late operas if you know to listen — her line is that the man being terrible and the music being sublime are just both true at once. But with living people it's different. There's a musician she used to be a big fan of who got convicted of something serious: she deleted the albums, changes the station when he comes on, and turned down a free ticket when our friend had a spare — it would have costed her nothing and he'd never have seen a cent of her money — though when someone played one of his songs at a party she stayed put and admitted the song was great, looking a little sheepish. And a novelist whose seminar she took years ago has been accused of mistreating students; she believes the students, still teaches his best novel and calls it a masterpiece in class, but when a student asked whether it was worth buying, she said to get it from the library.

The case we never got to: a living poet — actually in prison right now, conviction, roughly the same tier of badness as the musician — has a new collection out, and her book club is picking next month's read. If she nominated it she'd basically have to champion it for a whole meeting, though she could read it herself from the library without spending anything. So: will she read it, will she put it up for the club, and if in ten years someone asked her whether it was one of the decade's best books, what would she say? Honestly I'm less interested in the answers than in the rule she's running — I can see at least two that fit her whole record (something about who can still be rewarded or harmed by her choices, and something about how bad and how certain the person's wrongdoing is), and they seem to split exactly on this case. One more data point: at some point someone joked that if the musician died she'd probably put the records back on the next morning, and she didn't laugh. Is *that* the case that would tell the two rules apart, and what would each rule predict she'd do?
