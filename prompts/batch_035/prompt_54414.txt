i'm running a seminar session on higher-order theories of consciousness (the rosenthal-ish kind: a mental state is conscious when there's another, higher-order thought directed at it) and i want to close with a pantoum i can hand out. the form seems almost made for this: in a pantoum each line gets picked up again in the following stanza, recontextualized, so a plain first-order state literally becomes the object of the next stanza's attention — the theory's move, enacted by the form. i want it to start with something dumb and bodily, like a stubbed toe, and let the recurrences do the work: each time a line comes back it should feel more conscious but also more mediated, more at arm's length, the pain known-about rather than just had. somewhere in the middle it should run into the objections my students keep raising — that this seems to deny consciousness to animals and infants, or else it threatens an infinite regress of thoughts about thoughts about thoughts. and i honestly don't know how it should end, so don't decide for me: let the last stanza (which, after all, repeats the first stanza's lines) wonder whether its awareness of the pain makes it more vivid or means its just lost track of the thing itself. no name-dropping and no jargon if you can help it — the poem should be the argument, so that someone who knows the debate recognizes exactly which move each repetition is making
