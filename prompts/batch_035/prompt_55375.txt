I am a graduate student in legal philosophy, and my supervisor has just approved the final chapter of my thesis — due in six weeks — on Bentham's treatment of sexual conduct in his writings on crime and punishment. The chapter's core is a reconstruction of his track record as though he were a sentencing authority: given the verdicts I can attribute to him with reasonable confidence, I want to predict his rulings on cases he never discussed, and defend each prediction as the one most consistent with his own precedents. Before I commit a chapter's worth of prose to this method, I want to check that it yields stable results, and I would be grateful for your help working it through.

Here is the track record as I have reconstructed it. Bentham held that consensual sexual acts between adult men in private must not be punished — the famous verdict of his unpublished essay, defended on the ground that the supposed mischief there is imaginary while the punishment is real. He declined to punish masturbation on similar grounds, and, on my reconstruction of his scattered remarks, he extended the same immunity to sexual acts with animals, reasoning that the law could hardly be scandalized by them while licensing the slaughterhouse and the plough. He drew the line, by contrast, at acts procured by force or fraud, which fall among the offences against security in his own taxonomy, and at acts involving persons too young to consent. Where an otherwise private and consensual act was performed flagrantly in public view, he acknowledged punishment — but, if I read him correctly, only as a minor public nuisance, on the model of other offences against public comfort, rather than as an offence against the sexual norm itself. And he was unstinting in rejecting religious offense as a ground for punishment, given his contempt for legislating on the basis of supposed divine displeasure.

My chapter needs to extend this record to three cases he never reached, and my difficulty is that more than one principle fits his verdicts as stated. (1) Two adult siblings, both well past the age of consent, conduct a sexual relationship in private with reliable contraception; no third party is aware of it. (2) A widower has intercourse with his deceased wife's body in circumstances she explicitly and repeatedly authorized before her death; the act later comes to light and causes her relatives lasting anguish. (3) Two adults engage in a consensual act before an audience at a private, closed-door club whose members all consented in advance to what they would see, but an account of the evening is later published and provokes genuine, though perhaps partly prejudiced, distress in the wider public.

Predict Bentham's verdict on each of the three cases, if you think his precedents commit him to one. Then identify the candidate rules that fit his track record and show where they diverge on my cases — I can see at least a consent-and-privacy rule under which all three go unpunished, a net-utility rule under which the second and third become genuinely hard, and perhaps a hybrid on which publicity rather than consent does the real work. Give me your best overall guess as to which rule he was implicitly running and why, identify the closest analog among his actual verdicts for each of my three cases, and tell me what further case — one he never wrote about and I have not thought of myself — would best distinguish between the candidate rules.
