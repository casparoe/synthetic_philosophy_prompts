Two people from my decision theory reading group got into it last week and asked me to referee, and honestly I couldn't give a clean verdict, so I'm outsourcing it.

We were arguing about whether you're rationally required to have transitive preferences. Jules said obviously yes, because of money pumps: if you prefer A over B, B over C, and C over A, some trader can offer to swap your C for a B, then the B for an A, then the A for a C, taking a penny each time, and loop until you're broke. Being exploitable in principle just IS the irrationality, he said.

Priya pushed back that the pump only works on someone who can't see it coming. Any real person notices after the first loop and refuses further trades — she called this 'looking ahead and just not playing'. Once you know your preferences cycle, you adopt a policy of not trading with strangers, and no pump can touch you.

Jules said that concedes his point: you're precommitting to block your own future choices, which only makes sense BECAUSE the underlying preferences are defective.

Priya: no, it shows one-shot choice was the wrong unit all along, rationality attaches to plans not individual picks, and a resolute agent with cyclic preferences does just fine.

Jules: fine, but then cyclic prefrences in one-off, non-repeatable settings were never shown to be irrational, so the pump proves less than advertised. And anyway, he said, the pump still gets you if you can't tell when the trading day ends — a blanket policy of refusing trades has costs too.

Priya's last word: pumps exploit inattention more than incoherence — someone who literally can't perceive penny-sized differences gets drained even with perfectly transitive preferences, so what was the irrationality supposed to track?
