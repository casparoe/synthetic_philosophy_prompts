I cover science and ideas for a mid-sized regional daily, and I've spent weeks reporting a dispute inside my own newsroom that I now find I cannot referee, even though writing it up fairly is supposed to be my job. Since you seem equipped for this kind of thing, I'd be grateful for a considered verdict.

Some background. Two years ago, our publisher installed a dashboard that scores every story on a composite engagement measure — reader completion rate, time on page, and share rate, with weights set centrally. At first it was framed as purely informational, a way of seeing what our audience actually reads. Last year, quarterly bonuses for the writing staff became partly tied to the score. Nobody announced a policy change about what kind of journalism we do; the change happened quietly.

The dispute, as I've reconstructed it from a long editorial meeting and follow-up interviews:

(1) Editor R said: when the dashboard first went up, completion rate genuinely measured whether people were reading our journalism. Now writers front-load cheap payoffs, cut the "slow" context paragraphs that make a story intelligible, and split one story into three so each registers as "completed." The measure paid the bills, so the measure became the product. The moment we made it a target, it stopped telling us anything.

(2) Editor M replied that this is simply what measurement is for: you learn what is rewarded and you produce more of it, and if that turns out to be shorter, punchier stories, that may just be what readers want — and a paper's duty is to readers, not to its editors' sense of what deserves attention. The alternative on the table is editorial instinct, which is to say optimizing for the loudest editor's mood.

(3) R responded that instinct, whatever its faults, is a proxy with a track record built over decades of journalism, whereas the dashboard rewards whatever is easiest to produce for the number: time on page rewards pagination, completion rewards slideshows, shares reward outrage. Writers aren't getting worse at journalism; they're getting excellent at a different, invisible assignment.

(4) M said every decision rule is a proxy — instinct included — so the real question is which proxy is legible and correctable, and the dashboard wins on both counts: it can be audited, weighted differently, argued over in a meeting. A number that misleads can be fixed; a mood cannot. If completion rate is being gamed, weight it less. You repair a bad instrument; you don't smash it.

(5) R said revising the weights doesn't escape the trap, it just restarts the cycle: whatever single number is made decisive, a hundred smart, underpaid writers will find the shortest path to it, and that path will run through whatever the number fails to see. The only fix is to stop having a decisive number at all — hold bonuses to human editorial judgment, and keep the dashboard strictly for information.

(6) M answered that "strictly for information" is a fantasy of self-discipline no human institution has ever achieved: whatever the editor sees on a screen every morning becomes the effective target whether or not a bonus is attached, because writers figure out what pleases, and editors can't be asked to see the numbers and then not see them. R's proposal doesn't remove the target; it just hides it and calls that integrity.

I confess I can find a reasonable answer for every one of the six moves, and then a reasonable answer against it. I'd like you to adjudicate the exchange move by move — for each step, say whether it's sound, roughly right but overstated, or mistaken, and why — and then tell me who is more right overall. And if both editors are missing something — some third arrangement that keeps the numbers informative without letting them become the target, or some reason to think no such arrangement is possible in principle — I'd very much like to know that too, because I have to file the piece.
