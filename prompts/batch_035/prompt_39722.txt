I've been reading LessWrong and adjacent blogs for over a decade, and I've never been moved by the standard "you can't naturalize meaning" arguments — they mostly read to me like arguing from the limits of one's own imagination. But I've noticed that when teleosemantics gets invoked in those spaces, it's really two different theories wearing one label, and I want them pulled apart.

On one hand, there's the Millikan-style picture: a state's content is fixed by the evolutionary proper function of the mechanism that produces it — what the mechanism was selected for doing, historically, in the ancestors of the organism. On the other, there's the Dretske-style picture: content is fixed by what the state carries information about, with natural selection (or learning) playing the more local role of stabilizing which indicators the rest of the system actually relies on.

Please compare these two approaches carefully. I'm especially interested in where they come apart on the hard cases: misrepresentation (say the state that normally indicates predators firing at a wind-blown branch), swampman-type cases where there's no selectional history at all, and functions that are novel rather than inherited. Then apply both to a case neither theorist had available: a large language model trained by gradient descent to minimize predictive error on text. Under each view, does anything in such a system count as representing anything, and if the answer comes out badly wrong by our intuitions, is that a problem for the theory or for the intuitions? I want your actual verdict on which approach naturalizes content more successfully, not a survey. And since I've already been wrong once about how unified this literature is: tell me explicitly what argument or consideration would most likely change your verdict, and where in your reasoning you'd bet you've made your mistake.
