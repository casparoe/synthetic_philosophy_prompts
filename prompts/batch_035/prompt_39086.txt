For over forty years the debate that Amartya Sen opened with his lecture on "equality of what?" has circled the same ground. On one side are welfare egalitarians, who say what matters for equality is how well people's lives actually go, so we should equalize welfare or opportunities for welfare. On another are resource theorists in the vein of Dworkin, who insist that equalizing outcomes is perverse, because people's outcomes reflect their own choices — cultivating expensive tastes, working less — and that justice requires equalizing resources, not what people make of them, with responsibility doing the work between the two. And cutting against both is the capability approach, which observes that resources are not ends and welfare is too plastic: the same resources convert into very different real freedoms for someone in a wheelchair, or someone with a higher metabolism, or a pregnant woman, so equality should be measured in what people are actually able to do and be.

The striking thing is that nobody converts. The same counterexamples have been volleyed back and forth for decades — expensive tastes, disabilities, preferences deformed by deprivation, people who choose demanding religious observance — each side folding them into its metric with a supplementary device (refined preferences, insurance schemes, lists of basic capabilities, responsibility-sensitive welfare), and the camps end up hardly closer than when they began. My working hypothesis is that this isn't really a dispute about a metric at all: underneath it lie (a) a deep disagreement about whether people are responsible for their preferences at all, and (b) a latent split between those who think equality is a distributive axiom applied to impersonally specifiable goods and those who think it is a person-affecting ideal about lives as lived, and each side answers the "currency" question by smuggling in its answer to those. A further possibility I'd like assessed: the question "what is the currency of equality?" may simply be ill-posed in presupposing there is one commensurable thing to equalize.

So: diagnose the disagreement for me, not by adjudicating it. Is it substantive, verbal, or a case of two questions traveling under one heading? What is each camp's position actually doing the work — and given your diagnosis, what would count as progress? A thought experiment that isolates the residual disagreement, a formal result, a reformulated question, or nothing at all? And if nothing, explain why — whether the dispute bottoms out in bedrock intuitions about responsibility and the value of life-experience that argument cannot reach, or whether the appearance of a stalled debate masks slow convergence that partisans are professionally disinclined to acknowledge.
