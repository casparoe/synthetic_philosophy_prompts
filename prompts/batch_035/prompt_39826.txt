I'm halfway through a novel set during Suez — Eden in '56, convinced Nasser is another Hitler partly because he'd watched Munich happen from inside the government — and my editor keeps needeling me with an argument that I half want to give to one of my characters because I can't tell if it's brilliant or broken. It goes like this:

(1) Munich 1938 taught the West a genuine lesson: conceding to an aggressor with a fixed appetite just invites the next demand. (2) A lesson of history, if it's real, applies wherever its conditions recur. (3) Eden in '56, the Americans in Vietnam, and the people who pushed for Iraq in 2003 all sincerely judged that the conditions recurred, and in each case the parallels were not stupid — a revisionist leader, a war-weary public, a belief that standing firm now avoids a worse war later. (4) Each of those applications turned out worse than the alternative would probably have been. (5) So every visible application of the lesson has misfired, and a lesson whose applications reliably misfire isn't a lesson, it's a liability. (6) Therefore history's most famous lesson is actually a trap: the better statesmen learned Munich, the worse they governed afterwards. (7) And if that's right, the only real lesson of history is never to reason from historical analogies at all. (He drags the Thucydides trap in here too — the idea that we're fated to fight a rising China because Athens and Sparta did something similar once.)

He loves (7) because it plays well at dinner. My problem is that (7) feels like it proves too much — wouldn't it also discredit whatever reasoning got him there? — but I can't actually name the broken step, and my narrator is going to make some version of this speech in chapter nine so I'd rather not have him sound like an idiot. Don't give me a survey of where one *might* poke at it: pick the step you'd actually reject, tell me why it looked so solid, and tell me what it costs to give up. And if you think the argument is basically sound, say that plainly and I'll let my narrator win the argument.
