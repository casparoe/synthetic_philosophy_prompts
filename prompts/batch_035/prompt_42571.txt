I cover science and ideas at a mid-size news outlet, and at our shop the framing of famine stories — is it a natural disaster or something people did — gets decided by my editor, who's been there forever and has strong veiws. His record, from what we've actually run:

- The Irish famine of the 1840s: man-made, full stop. We ran it as a policy crime by Westminster, food leaving the country on ships while people starved. He genuinely gets angry about this one.
- Bengal 1943: same call. Churchill's war cabinet, the denial policies, hoarding — we treated it as a political story, not a weather story.
- The Great Leap Forward: man-made, obviously — requisition, grain exports while millions died, officials lying about yields.
- Somalia and the Horn of Africa in 2011: natural disaster. We went with the worst-drought-in-decades framing, basically treated it like an earthquake with a slow aid response attached.

Now the story on my desk is the famine in southern Madagascar, the one that's been going since 2021 — years of drought, people eating cactus and wild leaves, and the WFP pushing the line that this one is driven by climate change rather than war. No conflict to speak of, no Churchill, no Mao, no one obvious to point a finger at. But from what I can tell there was food in the country that the affected people simply couldn't afford once their crops and livestock and livelihoods went. My editor is leaning natural disaster, climate story, and honestly part of me agrees, since nobody in particular did this to anyone. But I've read enough Amartya Sen to suspect the word natural is doing a LOT of work in that headline.

So: predict how he'll rule, but more importantly, what rule is he actually running? Because at least two fit his track record — something like the food test (if there was food around while people starved, and they starved because they lost the means to get it, that's political) and something like the named-villain test (if you can point at the government that did it, it's a crime, otherwise it's a tragedy). Spell out what each rule predicts about Madagascar, give me your best guess at his verdict with reasons, and tell me what kind of case would split the two rules apart — a famine that comes out political on one and natural on the other. And since I'll be arguing with him either way: be honest about the weakest point in my Sen-inflected view. If the drought really did wipe out the food and nobody was hoarding or exporting anything, is natural disaster actually the right call, or is that exactly the framing Sen spent his career trying to bury?
