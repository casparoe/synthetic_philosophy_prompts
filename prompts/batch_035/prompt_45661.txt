Something I keep turning over: the US patent office won't consider a perpetual motion machine application unless the inventor actually submits a working model, and before that the French Academy of Sciences announced in 1775 it would stop looking at perpetual motion proposals altogether. So here are institutions that are normally maximally open-minded — they'll entertain claims about anything — declaring in advance that a whole class of machine cannot work, no matter how clever the design. What view of the laws of nature does that policy presuppose? My hunch is it only makes sense if laws govern — if conservation of energy is a constraint that holds with necessity, something that could not have an exception, rather than just a handy summary of what happens to go on. But I've also heard Humeans say laws are just the best compression of the mosaic of facts, and I can't tell whether the patent office's stance really rules that out or whether it's compatible with it. A Humean examiner doesn't believe in violations either, so maybe the rejection survives on his view too — or maybe he's only entitled to "no violation has ever been part of the best summary so far," which isn't the same as "no working model will ever show up," and the policy is quietly doing governing-law work. Related thing that bugs me: physicists themselves happily talk about universes where the laws are different, while the patent office treats our laws as unbreakable — is that an actual inconsistency in how the two practices presuppose different things about laws, or do they just care about different kinds of modality (empirical vs. metaphysical or whatever)? Lay it out for me: what the working-model rule strictly needs as a presupposition, what it merely sits comfortably with, and where the practice is underdetermined enough that a couple of rival views of laws would both license it. Under 400 words please.
