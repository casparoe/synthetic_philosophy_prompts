I've got a draft piece on the Kavka toxin puzzle and want some feedback before I send it anywhere. I'd mainly like comments on (a) whether the central argument actually works or trades on something fishy, and (b) whether the ending undercuts itself. Accuracy nitpicks welcome too -- there's a decent chance I'm mangling someone's position somewhere.

Draft:

The setup, roughly: a billionaire offers you a million dollars if, at midnight tonight, you intend to drink a toxin tomorrow. The toxin will make you quite ill for a day -- no lasting harm, no death. The money is paid on the intention alone: you keep it whether or not you actually drink. And you know now that tomorrow morning, with the million already banked, you'll have no reason at all to drink. The upshot is supposed to be that you can't form the intention tonight. Which is wierd, because people abandon intentions all the time, and we can apparently intend things we'll later have no reason to do.

The standard reading treats this as a Newcomb cousin: the billionaire is like a predictor, and the question becomes which decision theory lets you walk away with the million. I think that's the wrong frame. The interesting thing isn't that rationality "loses" the money; it's that the case only works because of a special feature of intention. Here's the claim I want to defend: you can't intend to drink because intending p while confidently expecting not-p is Moore-paradoxical in the first person -- "I intend to drink it tomorrow, and I know I won't" isn't an intention plus a prediction, it's something closer to a self-refuting performance, because intention is partly constituted by a disposition not to reopen the question. Belief can be self-deceived into place ahead of the evidence; intention can't, since intention has to be transparent to your own expected future deliberation or it just isn't an intention, it's a wish. So the real lesson of the puzzle isn't about decision theory, it's about which attitudes are self-verifying or self-undermining with respect to the agent's own future choices.

Then the ending I'm unsure about: I gesture at the thought that Gauthier-style constrained maximizers -- people who can genuinely bind their future choices -- would recieve the million, and that this suggests the "ability to intend at a distance" is more like a skill you can train than a state you're in. But a friend of mine says this just re-describes the puzzle rather than solving it, and honestly she might be right. Should I cut the ending, or is it the point?

One more thing: if the middle section only works because I'm quietly assuming that intending to do something entails expecting to do it, flag that -- I can't tell whether that assumption is load-bearing or harmless.
