I teach graduate epistemology, and this semester's unit on the epistemology of testimony has crystallized a pattern I want examined carefully: the seminar has split into two camps, and I have come to suspect that what separates them is not primarily an argument but a pair of competing analogies, each doing covert argumentative work.

The anti-reductionist camp, taking its cue from Burge's treatment of testimony as a primitive, non-empirical entitlement, leans on the analogy that accepting what a speaker says is like perceiving: both are default-entitled rational routes through which content reaches a subject, both come with a defeasible warrant that stays on until positive reason to the contrary, and both would be destroyed by a demand for independent antecedent evidence of general reliability. Their favorite rhetorical move is to argue that the reductionist's requirement would generate a corrosive skepticism about perception too, since the perceptual entitlement is likewise not earned.

The reductionist camp counters with the analogy that testimony is like reading a dial or an instrument: an instrument's outputs tell you about the world only given antecedent background knowledge — of the mechanism, of the calibration, of the conditions of operation — and so, they say, with speakers: whatever justification a hearer has must be reconstructed out of other sources (observation, induction over track records, memory of previous exchanges), with no source-specific default and no entitlement that outruns that reconstruction. Their favorite rhetorical move is to point out that the child learns when testimony is untrustworthy only by cross-checking it against perception, never the other way around.

I would like a systematic audit of both analogies. First, give me the correspondence table for each: for the perceptual analogy, say what plays the role of the perceptual appearance, the percept, the viewing conditions, illusion, and cognitive penetration; for the instrument analogy, say what plays the role of calibration, the theory of the mechanism, and reading error. Second, identify where each mapping breaks, and — crucially — separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended use from those that do not: the anti-reductionists stress that we can, in principle, validate an instrument against theory while hearers cannot check speakers without further testimony; the reductionists stress that perception is developmentally prior to testimony in a way testimony is not to itself; I want to know whether either observation actually carries the weight assigned to it. Third, tell me what each analogy smuggles in: does the perceptual analogy import a whole externalist conception of entitlement that a reductionist could happily accept while denying the analogy? Does the instrument analogy import the assumption that all epistemic support is acquired rather than native, which is precisely what is at issue? Finally, adjudicate: once the disanalogies are on the table, can either analogy actually carry the conclusion it was invoked to support — that testimony is, or is not, a generative source rather than a merely transmissive one — or does the question of basicality have to be settled by other means entirely? And if neither analogy can carry it, tell me what a better one would be: is testimony like memory, like trusting a map, like following an API contract, like a rule-governed practice whose entitlement is practical rather than epistemic? Please answer in American English, and feel free to use tables where they clarify the mappings.
