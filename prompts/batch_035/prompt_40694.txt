I've just been made performance manager at a mid-sized regional transit authority, and our board has decided to move to "performance-informed budgeting": every department's next funding cycle will partly depend on a scorecard of metrics that I'm tasked with designing. I'm wary, because everyone I talk to has a Goodhart story — the last agency that paid drivers for on-time performance found buses quietly skipping stops or idling at terminals just long enough to reset their clocks, and fare inspectors who were told to prioritize "interactions per shift" started carding the same easy commuter stations rather than the problem routes. I've read enough to know there's actually a useful taxonomy hiding behind the slogan: the kind of Goodhart's law where you select on a noisy proxy and the noise gives out (regressional), the kind where you push into a region of the proxy that never contained real quality (extremal), and the kind where the metric itself causes the behavior that destroys it (causal / specification gaming). I want those distinctions in the procedure, not just the proverb.

Please turn all of that into a procedure I can actually run when a department proposes a new metric for the scorecard. I need an ordered set of questions an analyst with no philosophy background can apply in an afternoon: what to check about how the metric was validated, how to reason about what the metric is a proxy for and where the proxy relation is only locally valid, how to anticipate the gaming strategies available to the people being measured (including ones that are individually rational and hard to call misconduct), and what a sufficient mitigation looks like — paired metrics, sampling audits, capped incentives, or refusing to attach money at all. Mark clearly which steps produce a defensible no, which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and where the procedure has to hand the decision back to me or the board because the question is one of values. Then run it on three live proposals from our current draft scorecard: (1) bus on-time performance with a 2% budget bonus attached, (2) a "customer satisfaction" score from an SMS survey sent after each ride, and (3) track-maintenance departments scored on "number of defects closed" by quarter-end. On the last one I expect the procedure to flag something specific and unwelcome, and I'd rather hear it now than after the maintenance backlog shows up in a derailment report — so tell me what the procedure says even when it's inconvenient, and say plainly what the procedure cannot do.

For context on constraints: the board meets quarterly and will not accept "we couldn't find a good metric" as an answer more than once or twice before they design metrics themselves without me, so the procedure needs a sanctioned way to decline that doesn't read as obstruction. Analysts have two weeks per review cycle, we can't afford more than a small pilot or audit budget per metric, and the transit workers' union is represented on the committee that signs off — anything that reads as surveillance theater will die in committee even if it's methodologically sound. I want the procedure honest about that political surface too: a step is useless to me if following it guarantees the whole scorecard gets voted down.
