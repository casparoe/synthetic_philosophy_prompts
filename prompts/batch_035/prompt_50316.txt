I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, though I use probability constantly in my work — A/B testing, reliability engineering, that kind of thing — and lately I've been trying to understand what probability actually *is* rather than just how to compute with it. At lunch last week, a colleague of mine who reads a lot of philosophy of science on the side offered to sort me out, and gave me what he called the standard map of the territory. Here is my best reconstruction of what he said, from memory and my notes: frequentism is the view that probability just is long-run relative frequency in a series of repeatable trials, so strictly speaking it can't attach a number to a one-off event — a probability of 0.7 for *this particular* election result is meaningless on that view, since the election happens once. Subjectivism says probability is degree of belief, which handles one-off events fine, but at the cost that any two people's probabilities are equally valid however careless or prejudiced they are, and since priors are arbitrary, Bayesian updating mostly just compounds the arbitrariness rather than correcting it — garbage in, garbage out, iterated. The propensity view treats probabilities as single-case physical dispositions, and my colleague said Popper developed it largely to handle quantum mechanics, where single events really do have propensities; but he added that propensities are really just frequentism dressed up in metaphysical language, and that the view was more or less refuted decades ago anyway. His conclusion was that frequentism is the only genuinely scientific interpretation, and that this is why serious empirical work should stick to frequentist statistics.

I found this genuinely clarifying at first — it's the first time the three options have been laid out for me as a choice — but a few things nag at me. My colleague is confident in a way I've learned to be suspicious of, and I have a vague sense that practitioners of Bayesian statistics would not concede that their machinery "compounds arbitrariness," whatever the merits of the priors point. I'm also unsure whether the claim that frequentism is the "only scientific interpretation" follows from anything true in his summary, or is just a rhetorical flourish.
