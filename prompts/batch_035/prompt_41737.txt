Over the past few months I've been writing down my gut verdicts on whether particular lives are meaningful, on the theory that if a coherent position is in me, it's in the cases rather than in the slogans I'd endorse. Here is the current list.

A woman spends forty years setting cryptic crosswords for a small regional paper; her readers love her, there are fewer of them every year, and the paper folds shortly after her death. Meaningful — obviously, and only slightly less so than the same life with a growing readership.

A mathematician spends her career on a proof that turns out, after her death, to rest on an irreparable error, so that her work contributes nothing to mathematics. Still meaningful, barely diminished if at all.

A second mathematician proves a theorem that was quietly settled decades earlier in an obscure journal; his work is sound but redundant. Meaningful, but a shade less than the first woman's, and I can't explain why, since neither of them changed mathematics.

Nozick asks us to imagine the gods implanting in Sisyphus a burning desire to roll his boulder up the hill, so that he gets exactly what he wants, forever. Not meaningful, or meaningful in some vanishing way.

A woman spends thirty years coordinating a vaccination campaign that saves tens of thousands of children; the work suits her no better than a tax office job would, she feels nothing for it, and such fulfillment as she has comes from her evenings as an amateur pianist. Meaningful — and I'm surprised to find I would hardly dock her at all, though she herself would insist the piano mattered more.

A gifted man serves for life as the chief propagandist of a vicious regime; he believes in the cause, the work exercises every talent he has, and he dies decorated and adored. Not meaningful. This is the case I'm most certain about, and it's what makes me distrust any view on which fulfillment alone suffices.

An archivist spends her career digitizing the records of a vanished civilization — meticulous, absorbed, devoted to the standard of the work itself — knowing full well that the storage medium is projected to decay before anyone plausibly retrieves a file. Meaningful, and I can't see that the last clause makes any real difference, though it feels as if it should.

A man takes an elixir that leaves him healthy and unaging and simply keeps the life he has: he remasters his hobbies, rereads everything, watches his enthusiasms thin across the centuries, and by year four hundred has no projects that are more than ways of passing the decade. Viewed from outside, the life seems to have lost the meaning its early decades had — but I can't reconcile this with the archivist, whose work also does nothing for anyone and yet isn't drained by that fact.

So my question: is there a single principle — presumably something in the neighborhood of meaning as engaged pursuit of genuinely worthwhile ends, with the engagement rather than the output carrying most of the weight — that delivers all of these verdicts? If some verdict has to go, tell me which and why. Note that my Sisyphus verdict and my retracted-proof verdict pull in opposite directions on whether worth enters only at the level of the ends pursued or also at the level of what the pursuit actually achieves; a principle that handles both has to say something precise about that distinction, and I'd like it said precisely. Then tell me what the principle implies for two cases I haven't decided: a man who spends his life raising a child with love and education in a bunker after a plague has left them the last humans, with no future following from it; and a composer whose music is first performed two centuries after her death, to audiences who find it profound — was her life meaningful while she lived it, and does the later performance change anything retroactively? Finally, I feel the pull of the absurdist thought that from no vantage point does anything we do finally register, and that the demand for a justification standing outside all our projects is one we can neither satisfy nor abandon — yet my case verdicts show no trace of it. Does the principle you build explain why the cosmic perspective leaves my judgments untouched, or does it merely coexist with the absurdist thought? If it can't explain it, I'd rather hear that than have the tension papered over. No citations needed; refer to positions and arguments however is useful and I'll track down the sources myself.
