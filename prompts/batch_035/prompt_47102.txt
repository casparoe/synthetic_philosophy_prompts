I'm taking a seminar this semester on Buddhist ethics and its encounters with Western utilitarianism, and for our final session each of us has to bring one "artifact" that expresses a position we studied rather than merely explaining it. I've chosen to write a poem about the view sometimes called tranquilism — the claim that the only thing that is positively good is the absence of suffering and craving, and that beyond that relief there is no further positive state to be attained. On this picture, peace is not a feeling added on top of the extinguished craving; it is just the craving gone, the way health is the absence of the fever rather than a second thing the fever leaves behind as a reward. I want the poem to take this seriously as a view about value, not as a mood.

Please act as an accomplished poet who also has serious training in Buddhist philosophy, and think carefully about the form before you write. I'd like a villanelle. My idea — and I'd like you to push back on it if you see a better way — is that the form is almost embarrassingly well suited: a villanelle is built on two refrains that return compulsively, line after line, stanza after stanza, whether the speaker wants them or not. That compulsion is itself a portrait of craving, of the mind replaying its fixations. So I'd suggest one refrain should carry the voice of craving itself (the insistence that something is missing, that reaching for the next thing will complete it), while the other refrain should carry the tranquilist's counter — the claim that nothing was ever missing once the reaching stops. The two should stay recognizably distinct as positions throughout, so that someone who knows the debate could point to any refrain and say which view is speaking. But here is the difficulty I keep running into, and I'd like the poem to face it honestly rather than dodge it: a poem that longs for the end of longing is still longing, and a villanelle that dramatizes compulsion cannot simply declare itself free at the end without cheating. The closing couplet, where the refrains finally stand next to each other, should do the real work — I'll leave it to you whether that ending enacts release, an uneasy truce, or the recognition that the speaker has merely traded one fixation for another.

A few constraints: please keep the treatment philosophically careful — if you use terms like dukkha or tanha, use them the way they'd be used in a Pali-texts seminar, not as incense-and-spa shorthand — and avoid both the self-help register of serenity clichés and the opposite trap of making tranquilism sound like a nihilist's shrug. The tone can be skeptical of the view or devoted to it, but it should earn whichever stance it takes. Someone who has never heard of tranquilism should be able to read the poem simply as a good poem about a mind at war with its own wanting, and someone who knows the view should find that it gets the view right. Feel free to brainstorm briefly on the refrains before committing, since in a villanelle everything hangs on those two lines.
