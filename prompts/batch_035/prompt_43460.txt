I have read the rationalist literature on multipolar traps for years, from Ginsberg's Moloch through Scott Alexander's "Meditations on Moloch," and I thought I understood the concept: whenever agents compete, the competition selects for whatever wins it, indifferent to whether anyone wanted any of it, and the stable equilibrium can be one that every participant hates. I use "Moloch" for this phenomenon, as that community does. But when I try to say which actual situations count as Moloch and which do not, my case-by-case verdicts refuse to cohere, and I would like you to find the principle behind them, or tell me which verdicts to give up.

My verdicts. Two factories each dump untreated waste into a shared river, because installing filtration would add a cost their competitor does not bear: Moloch, the paradigm case. Nations stockpiling nuclear weapons far beyond any deterrent need, because unilaterally disarming would look weak: Moloch. The doping era in professional cycling, where riders doped because everyone else did, and clean riders were simply not competitive: Moloch. Credential inflation, where jobs that once required a bachelor's now require a master's, so everyone spends more years and more money for roughly the same relative position: Moloch, though this one wobbles for me, since workers do come out the other end knowing more things. And now the verdicts I cannot reconcile. Retail price wars, where each firm cuts prices toward cost, margins evaporate, and every firm would prefer an arrangement with restrained pricing: I do not think this is Moloch; it just seems like capitalism doing what it is for, even though structurally it resembles the doping case — costly expenditure that cancels out positionally, mutual restraint preferred by all participants, and each firm's unilateral restraint losing it market share. Top-level chess, where players spend years memorizing opening theory so deep that games between the very best are increasingly drawn before the human part of the game even begins: also not Moloch, I think, and I cannot say why, since it looks more like the arms-race case than the price war does — an escalating positional expenditure with no third party capturing the surplus.

My tentative principle was something like this: a situation is Moloch when every participant would prefer the outcome under mutual restraint, mutual restraint is available in principle, but unilateral restraint is worse for the one who restrains. But that fits the price war as well as it fits the river, and I do not want to say price wars are Moloch. Perhaps the missing clause is about where the losses go: in the price war, the consumer gets what the firms lose, so from anywhere other than the firms' point of view nothing is trapped, while in the river and stockpile cases the loss benefits no one. That clause fixes the price war, but it mispredicts chess, which also has no third-party beneficiary — unless my chess verdict is wrong and opening-theory escalation really is Moloch, which is where I keep going back and forth.

Please do four things. First, state the best principle or small set of candidate principles you can, precisely, with each clause tied to the specific case that motivates it, and say which of my verdicts each candidate saves. Second, say whether the principle requires participants who can prefer at all: on my reading of the source essay, biological evolution — no one's goals anywhere in it, selection all the way down — is supposed to be Moloch's purest form, but my preference-based principle would seem to exclude it, so either the principle needs reworking or that verdict does. Third, apply your best principle to two cases I have not mentioned: a competition among social-media platforms to add engagement-maximizing features that erode users' attention, and a partial-coordination attempt such as arms-control treaties with verification regimes — I want to know not just whether these are Moloch but what stage of it, if it comes in stages. Fourth, tell me honestly which of my verdicts your best principle cannot accommodate, and whether saving all of them is worth whatever extra machinery it would take. Please write your answer in American English.
