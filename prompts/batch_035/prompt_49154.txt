I write historical fiction, and I'm deep into planning a novel set around one of those hinge moments — think July 1914, the sort of week where you can point at four or five individual decisions and say "if this guy had caught his train" or "if that letter had arrived a day earlier." My problem is that whenever I talk about this stuff with actual historians, I fall into the same fight every time. One camp (mostly the narrative historians, honestly the ones I get along with at parties) insists the outcome was wildly contingent, that the men involved were making genuine choices in real time and could easily have gone the other way. The other camp (mostly the social-science types) says that's fantasy: the tensions were structural, the alliances were locked in, and if it hadn't been that nail it would've been another. Pascal's bit about Cleopatra's nose gets invoked, then Tolstoy's argument that great men are just labels we stick on events, and then nothing. Nobody budges. It's been going in some form for over a century.

Here's my suspicion, as someone who thinks about plot for a living: the two sides aren't actually disagreeing about the past at all. I think one side is answering "how did this actually unfold, step by step" — which of course makes it look contingent, because narrative attention is on the individual links — and the other is answering something like "how likely was this outcome given the underlying conditions" — which of course makes it look overdetermined. Two questions, one word ("caused"). And there's a second layer I can't get past: contingency claims seem like they should be testable in principle — you're basically claiming the outcome was sensitive to perturbations, and sensitivity is a thing you can measure in other fields — but nobody ever measures it, they just gesture at it.

So that's what I want from you, not a rerun of the arguments but a diagnosis: is this thing verbal, or is it a real empirical dispute wearing a verbal costume, or is it just two temperaments (the novelist's temperament and the statistician's) that can't argue with each other because they have no shared standard? And crucially for me: if your diagnosis is right, what would even count as progress here — a sharper distinction, some way of actually testing sensitivity claims, a truce where everyone agrees to relabel their questions? I also want your honest take on whether the question is just ill-posed. I have a novel to structure, and I'd like to know whether my characters are actually making history or whether I'm just aesthetically required to pretend they are.
