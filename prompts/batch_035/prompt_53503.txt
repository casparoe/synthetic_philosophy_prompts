In my philosophy of perception seminar we've spent several weeks on cognitive penetration, and my instructor keeps leaning on an analogy that I want pressure-tested before I build my term paper around it. The picture, which she takes from popular presentations of predictive processing, is that perception is a scientist: the brain begins with hypotheses about what is out there, collects data in the form of sensory input, and settles on the hypothesis that best explains the data, and the winning hypothesis then becomes what we consciously see. From this she draws two conclusions. First, that perception is cognitively penetrable, because what we believe supposedly functions as one of the priors, so a change in belief can literally change what we see — an expert ornithologist and a novice, given the same indistinct shape overhead, see different things. Second, and more ambitiously, that this undermines any hope of a theory-neutral observational vocabulary, since there is no stage of perceptual processing that beliefs leave untouched. Please audit the analogy. Lay out in prose what is supposed to map onto what — the hypothesis, the evidence, the prior, the experiment, the revision, and the scientist herself — and then say where the mapping breaks. Some breaks seem clearly irrelevant to her conclusions, for instance that the brain does not publish its methods or risk its reputation; others look potentially fatal, for instance that scientific inference is defeasible by argument, accessible to the whole person, and answerable to norms of rationality, whereas the visual system's hypotheses are not states the subject can entertain, reject, or justify. I would like you to sort the disanalogies into those that matter for the penetration claim and those that do not, using this test: a disanalogy matters only if it closes off a channel by which the content of beliefs, as opposed to mere causal traffic, could rationally influence perceptual content. Then tell me, given the disanalogies that survive the test, which of her two conclusions survives with them, and whether a weakened version of the scientist picture might still carry the penetration claim even if the ambitious one cannot. Throughout, keep cognitive penetration distinct from the neighboring phenomena we have covered — the effects of attention, perceptual learning, sensory adaptation, and shifts in response criterion rather than in appearance — since the analogy is only doing real work if it secures the semantic, content-mediated kind of influence rather than any of those.
