I'm drafting a position statement on the badness of death for a graduate seminar on well-being and want a consistency audit before I circulate it. Here's the package: (1) death is bad for the person who dies, not merely for those left behind; (2) its badness is wholly a matter of deprivation — death is bad for you when, and only when, the life you would otherwise have led contains more good than the one you actually led; (3) Lucretius's symmetry argument fails: there is a genuine asymmetry between the nonexistence before birth, which is not bad for us, and the nonexistence after death, which is; (4) people can be harmed by events occurring after they die — broken deathbed promises, posthumous slander — so a subject can be wronged by events at times when she no longer exists; (5) nothing can be bad for a subject unless that subject exists at some point, though the bearer need not exist at the time the bad thing happens; and (6) whenever death is bad for someone, it is bad for them at some particular time. I've been told (5) is where an Epicurean would press the attack, but I think I've built it to resist that, and in any case I can't see which flank is actually exposed. Which of these commitments are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and what is the least costly revision? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than flagging every pair as equally suspect, and for each genuine tension say what would have to give and what giving it up costs.
