I have drafted notes for a short essay on material constitution and would appreciate comments, both on whether the central argument works and on whether the position it defends is genuinely interesting or merely a relabeling of the problem.

The thesis: the statue and the piece of clay constituting it are two objects, but the difference between them is purely modal and sortal. They share every categorical, historical, and locational property at every time they both exist; they differ only in their persistence conditions (the clay can survive squashing, the statue cannot) and in which sortals apply to them. Since sortal-relative persistence conditions are fixed by the meanings of our terms rather than by how the world is, the dispute over whether the clay is the statue is verbal, and the question of how many objects sit on the pedestal has no sortal-independent answer.

The argument I sketch: (1) at every time, all of the non-modal properties of the clay are also properties of the statue, and vice versa; (2) if two candidate objects differ only modally, then there is no substantive fact about whether they are one thing or two; counting objects requires a sortal, and under the sortal "piece of clay" the answer is one, under "statue" also one, and under their fusion, two; (3) therefore the ontological question dissolves.

The payoff section applies this to the too-many-candidates problem: there is also a statue-minus-one-molecule, a clay-constituting-that-statue-minus-a-different-molecule, and so on. I argue this is a feature rather than a bug: once counting is sortal-relative, the proliferation of candidates is exactly what we should expect, and the arbitrariness objection to abundant coincident objects loses its bite, since the extra candidates are never counted under any ordinary sortal.

My questions: Does premise (2) survive the objection that modal differences can be ontologically weighty — for instance, the clay's being able to survive squashing looks like a dispositional difference, and dispositions seem to be features of how the world actually is, not merely of our language? Does the sortal-relativity move simply push the problem back to which sortals are legitimate, so that the arbitrariness reappears at the level of sortal choice? And is the payoff section too quick in claiming that never being counted under an ordinary sortal is enough to defuse the arbitrariness objection?
