You're a professional epistemologist who's comfortable putting actual numbers on contested claims, so I want your help with something that comes up every year in my graduate seminar on skepticism. Every year the same fight breaks out about transcendental arguments, and every year it dissolves into people talking past each other, so this time I want to walk in knowing where the real crux is. Here's the question I want you to answer: what is your credence that at least one transcendental argument, properly reconstructed, genuinely refutes a substantive version of external-world skepticism? By "substantive" I mean a skeptical hypothesis that could in principle have been true — I'm not interested in refuting positions defined so loosely that no argument could ever touch them — and by "refutes" I mean establishes that the skeptical hypothesis is false, not merely that we can't coherently doubt its negation while doing science. Those two qualifiers are where all the trouble hides, so hold onto them.

I want the number decomposed, not just asserted. Here's how I'd carve it up, though feel free to carve it differently if you think my terms are wrong: (a) the probability that there's a genuine presupposition relation of the right strength running from something undeniably available to us — that we have experience, that we can make judgements, something the skeptic can't mock away without incoherence — over to claims about mind-independent objects, rather than only over to claims about our conceptual scheme or vocabulary; (b) the probability that such an arguement can cross what Barry Stroud, in the famous objection from the sixties, pointed out is a gap between what we must believe or presuppose and what is actually true — I take this to be the single biggest question mark in the genre, so push back if you'd weight things differently; (c) the probability that the skeptic's position, once stated carefully, is a determinate thesis capable of being false at all, rather than a stance that retreats every time the argument advances; and (d) the probability that, if the argument only gets us to "we are in fact warranted in believing in an external world," that should count as a genuine refutation of the skeptic rather than a concession to some quietism. Give me a rough probability for each with a line or two of justification, combine them however you honestly think they combine, and then tell me which single term the final number is most sensitive to.

For context on why I care: my two senior colleagues split cleanly on this and I've never been able to pin down where. One, who I'd guess sits around 0.6, thinks the Stroud gap is bridgeable because the skeptic can't even state her thesis without helping herself to the very concepts the argument targets, so her position is somehow self-undermining. The other, maybe 0.15, thinks transcendental arguments are at bottom dressed-up inference to the best explanation, and that once you strip the dressing off they lose to plain abduction and we should just say so. So besides your own number, tell me which of those two people you think the disagreement actually lives in: is it (b), is it (c), or is it the reduction claim, which would be a fifth term I haven't even listed? Please write all of this as continuous prose, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way you'd write a long email to a colleague. And think hard before committing to any numbers: do a quick informal brainstorm first about what the strongest candidate argument even is, because if you struggle to name one, that fact alone ought to drag your overall credence down, and I'd like you to say so if it does.
