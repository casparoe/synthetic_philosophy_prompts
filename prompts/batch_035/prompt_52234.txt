I'm a novelist, several books into a career spent mostly on quiet domestic realism, and my current manuscript has drifted somewhere I'm not equipped to follow on my own. It concerns twin sisters: one remains in the small coastal town where they grew up, the other leaves, and the novel's spine is a series of preternatural resonances between them — one falls ill and the other, a continent away, takes to bed; one lies to her husband and the other feels the lie like weather. My editor adores this and has been dressing it in borrowed physics. Her marginal notes now lean heavily on a phrase everyone attributes to Einstein — spooky action at a distance — and she reports that a physicist friend of hers assured her that "Bell proved the universe is nonlocal," so my device is, in her words, scientifically licensed. She wants the sisters explicitly framed as an entangled pair in the jacket copy.

I have no wish to dismiss this out of pride or laziness, but my physics education ended two decades ago, and I can't tell whether her gloss is a genuine warrant, a harmless flourish, or a claim about the world that happens to be false. So I'd like you to act as a philosopher of physics with a patient eye for loose metaphor, and to think carefully before answering — I'd rather you brainstorm the possibilities first and then organize them, since my real need is a menu, not a verdict on one reading. Please lay out, as a numbered list I can work through with my editor, the distinct precise claims that could be hiding behind invoking entanglement and Bell's theorem to bless my twins. For each reading, tell me whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or straightforwardly false, and — most importantly — whether it actually supports anything my novel does (mirrored states, distant responsiveness, one sister's circumstances constraining the other's).

Some of what I half-understand, to give you my lay of the land, for whatever it's worth:

1. Something about correlations that outrun any local explanation — where I gather "local" is doing serious and contested work, and that the stronger senses of it can be split apart into separate conditions that theorists have given names. If one of those conditions can fail while others hold, I want to know what that means for what Bell actually established versus what it's popularly taken to establish.

2. The claim that measuring one member of a pair does something to the other — which is where my fiction would most like the physics to help, and which is exactly where I suspect the help is hollow.

3. The claim that something — influence, information, a signal, a perturbation — actually travels from one to the other faster than light. My instinct is that this is where the physics stops licensing anything at all, but I'd like it said carefully rather than just insisted upon.

4. Whatever merely definitional or metaphorical reading is left over — correlation without mechanism, or without mediation — and whether, if that's all there is, the physics gloss adds anything beyond a flattering atmosphere that the jacket copy has no right to.

And finally: "Bell proved the universe is nonlocal" is starting to sound to me like one of those sentences that compresses several theses into one word. If you could disentangle the things "nonlocal" could mean there — and say which of them follow from what Bell actually showed, in the form his result is usually stated — I would count that as the centerpiece of the menu. Please be as thorough as you like; I would rather be corrected at length than flattered briefly.
