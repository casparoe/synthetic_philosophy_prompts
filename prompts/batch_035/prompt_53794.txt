I'm a retired pharmacologist -- spent forty years at the bench, mostly on drug safety, and since retiring I've gotten heavily into philosophy (auditing lectures, reading distributive justice stuff). I also, somehow, got appointed to my county's Social Services Advisory Board, which mostly means potluck dinners and rubber-stamping budgets, but now we have a real fight on our hands and I want a proper written opinion to circulate before Thursday's meeting. You're a political philosopher who specializes in the welfare state and theories of desert, and I need you to think carefully about this before drafting -- maybe brainstorm the strongest arguments on both sides first, then commit to a recommendation. Please write the memo in British English (a quirk of our board chair, long story), but talk to me like a colleague, not like a textbook.

The case: we administer a discretionary Emergency Assistance Fund -- small grants (a month's rent, a utility shutoff, that kind of thing) for people who fall through the cracks of the standard programmes. One of our board members, a former police chief, is pushing a pilot he calls "Contribute to Receive." Under it, anyone getting a grant must, within four weeks, complete a 15-hour-per-week supervised "community contribution placement" -- litter removal, charity shop shifts, snow shovelling for elderly neighbours -- or lose 30% of any future grant. He also wants a public fraud-reporting hotline. His motivation: a local newspaper ran a story about a man who claimed assistance while doing cash-in-hand roofing jobs, and the board member says taxpayers will only keep supporting the fund if recipients "give something back." He's explicit that it's not about the money for him -- it's about reciprocity.

Here's the part where my science background makes me itch. Our auditor found roughly 3% improper payments last year, and most of those were paperwork errors by caseworkers, not claimant fraud -- the actual fraud cases were a handful of people, maybe a rounding error. Early estimates are that administering the placements and the hotline would cost around five times what we'd ever recover. So on pure cost-benefit grounds this is dead on arrival, and I know how to make that argument. But I don't think that's the real fight. The real fight is his reciprocity principle: that help should be conditional on demonstrated contribution, that there's a meaningful line between the deserving and undeserving poor, and that unconditional grants insult the people who fund them. I've been reading enough philosophy lately to suspect this deserves a serious answer and not just an accounting one -- something about whether desert is even the right frame for a fund like ours, versus treating it as a kind of mutual insurance people have already paid into through taxes, or a safety floor that doesn't depend on anyone's character. But I also don't want a memo that just waves the reciprocity view away, because frankly some version of it runs deep in the public and our fund exists on their goodwill.

So: draft the decision memo -- approve the pilot, reject it, or approve with conditions -- that I can circulate before Thursday. It should have a clear recommendation the board could actually vote on, reasons the former police chief could engage with rather than dismiss as ivory-tower stuff, and an honest treatment of the strongest argument on whichever side loses. If you think some watered-down version of his plan is worth salvaging, tell me exactly which conditions would make it defensible and which would just be a more expensive way of doing the same bad thing. Keep it to something I can actually hand out at a meeting -- say 900 words or so. And if it helps, don't hesitate to flag where my "just run the numbers" instinct is itself a philosophical position rather than a neutral one; I've got thick skin.
