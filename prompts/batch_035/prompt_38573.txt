I am a recently retired atmospheric scientist. Since leaving the lab I have been reading my way through probability and decision theory, and I have found myself drawn to a position that my old colleagues would consider eccentric: that rational betting and investing are governed by the growth rate of one's wealth along the single trajectory one actually lives, not by averages taken across the possible worlds one might have lived. Before I settle into that position, I want to test it against a serious opponent. Please play a committed Bayesian decision theorist — precise, charitable toward my arguments, and never a strawman. You hold that rational choice just is the maximization of expected utility, and that everything I care about can be accommodated inside that framework; you may concede points of exposition or application, but never the main thesis. Stay in character until I write "stop", and keep each reply to a single paragraph so that this reads as a conversation rather than a seminar.

Here is my opening move. In the 1950s a researcher at Bell Labs posed a deceptively simple question: if a gambler with an edge may place the same kind of bet over and over, what fraction of her bankroll should she stake each time? The answer — stake the fraction that maximizes the expected logarithm of wealth, so that the bankroll compounds at the fastest sustainable rate — had in fact been anticipated much earlier, in eighteenth-century work on the St. Petersburg wager, where doubling the stake repeatedly produced an infinite expected gain that no sane person would pay much to enter. You will tell me, I anticipate, that all of this is old news under another name: an agent with logarithmic utility for wealth exhibits exactly this behavior, so my "result" is merely expected-utility maximization wearing a costume. But I believe that reverses the order of explanation. My possible selves do not pool their winnings; I do not get to live the average of my better and worse fortunes. Whatever I do this year is compounded into everything I can do next year — a fifty percent loss requires a one hundred percent gain merely to restore me — so what matters to the one person I am, moving through time, is the rate at which my actual wealth grows, not the mean fortune of the ensemble of people I might have been. On my view the logarithm enters not as a psychological taste for caution but as a structural fact about decision-makers who persist: a gambler who stakes everything to maximize expected wealth is ruined with probability one, however favorable each individual bet, and no utility function I am asked to endorse in advance should be permitted to obscure that. So here is my challenge: explain to me what expected-utility maximization over possible worlds is *for*, when the agent making the decision will occupy only one of them.
