As a science journalist, I have spent the last few years writing about what one might call the reputation economy: LEED-certified headquarters with their solar arrays placed where commuters can see them, luxury electric sports cars, sponsored endurance runs for charity, companies that voluntarily publish their own emissions audits rather than waiting to be compelled. In nearly every interview, someone reaches for the same reference point: the peacock's tail. The argument runs that the tail is expensive, does nothing for survival, and is honest precisely for that reason — no peacock in poor condition can afford one — so costly displays in human life should be trusted too: the conspicuous price tag proves the owner really can afford to care about the climate, the punishing marathon proves real commitment to the cause, the voluntarily disclosed audit proves the numbers weren't cooked. My editor, however, has recently pushed back with the same bird pointed the other way: if all this is signaling, then the environmental content of the displays is beside the point — they are status feathers, and covering them as environmental news is a category error. One analogy, two opposite conclusions.

Before I write the follow-up essay, I want the analogy itself audited rather than either conclusion endorsed. Give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the tail, of the peacock's underlying condition, of the peahen's choice, and — this seems to matter most — of the predators and parasites whose existence is what makes the tail genuinely costly rather than merely decorative. Then show me where the mapping breaks. I gather the underlying biology is less tidy than the popularizers imply: that a costly trait can be attractive without being honest, that costliness on its own guarantees nothing, that there are honest signals that are nearly free and deceptive ones that are ruinously expensive, and that whether signals stay honest depends on how much the sender's and receiver's interests actually conflict. Some of these disanalogies will matter for the debate between me and my editor, and some will be merely ornithological — I need to know which are which. There is also, I suspect, a problem about who the peahen is supposed to be when a corporation signals: an audience of consumers with no incentive to police the signal, and investors who may actively want to be convinced, is not a peahen under selection to choose well.

So, finally: does either conclusion survive the audit — that expense proves sincerity, or that the displays are "merely" status and therefore environmentally irrelevant — or was the peacock carrying both, with the real work being done by assumptions about verification and common interest that the analogy never made visible? If a better analogy exists for the cases I cover, name it and say what it gets right about the points where the peacock fails.
