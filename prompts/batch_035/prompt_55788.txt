I am a third-year philosophy undergraduate currently enrolled in an upper-level metaphysics seminar on causation, and our unit on causation by omission has now run for three meetings without anyone visibly changing position. I would like a diagnosis of the standoff rather than another round of argument for one side.

The case that keeps anchoring the discussion: a gardener fails to water the plants while away for a fortnight; the plants die. Did the gardener's not watering cause the death? The seminar has settled into three camps that no longer engage one another.

The first camp are realists about omissions. They say the omission is a genuine relatum of the causal relation, and their best point is that in law and ordinary life we confidently make and act on claims of exactly this form, with no principled difference in kind from cases where a positive event produces a delayed effect. One member of this camp adds that the non-watering should be understood as the non-occurrence of a specific positive event — the gardener's watering of those plants on those days — so that absences are, in a sense, already on the map of events, and the causal claim comes out strictly true.

The second camp are deflationists. They hold that strictly speaking nothing negative is in the causal chain at all: what caused the death were positives — the soil dried out, the roots desiccated, the cells collapsed — and talk of the omission causing anything is a device for locating which positive facts matter for a given purpose. On their view the realists have mistaken a useful shorthand for an ontological report.

The third camp grant both sides something and locate the real issue elsewhere. For any omission, indefinitely many others are equally available — the Queen of England also failed to water the plants, and so did every other gardener in the world. Since the Queen's not watering did not cause the death while the gardener's did, the difference between them cannot be a difference in the causal relation itself; it must be a difference in background expectations about what was supposed to happen and who was on the hook. So, they say, the sentence about the gardener is not, or not only, a causal claim: it is a claim about deviations from a norm, and the dispute is being driven by a word that quietly does two jobs.

My hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis: this is in large part a verbal dispute under a single heading — the camps are answering different questions, one about which relations hold among events and one about how responsibility is assigned against a default background, while using the same sentence forms. But I can already hear the third camp replying that the norm-dependence is itself a substantive discovery about causation, not a dissolution of the dispute, and I do not know how to tell those two things apart.
