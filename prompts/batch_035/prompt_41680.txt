At dinner the other night a friend of mine who did a philosophy PhD tried to settle the whole art-forgery question for us. Context: we'd just watched a documentary about Han van Meegeren, the Dutch guy who painted fake "Vermeers" in the 30s and 40s, sold one of them to Hermann Göring, and got caught after the war. She explained the issue using Nelson Goodman, from Languages of Art I think, the 60s? Anyway, here's roughly what she said, as best as I can reconstruct it: "Goodman splits the arts into two kinds. Painting and sculpture he calls autographic — arts where the distinction between an original and a forgery is meaningful. Music, literature, poetry are allographic — arts where that distinction just doesn't apply; in music you can't forge a work, the worst you can do is plagiarize it, put someone else's name on it, which is a different offense. And for the autographic arts he proved why the original always beats the forgery aesthetically: a forgery is by definition a copy, and a copy is always a lesser creative achievement than the thing it copies. So the original is necessarily the better work. That's also what provenance is really about — when the art market pays millions for an original, what it's actually tracking is Goodman's point that the original is always the better art."

Some of this sounded right to me, but two things felt off. The van Meegeren fakes weren't copies of any real Vermeer — they were new paintings in a Vermeer-ish style — so I dont see how "a forgery is by definition a copy" is supposed to work. And it seemed weird to say the original is *necessarily* the better work, as if that was proven rather than just asserted; I vaguely remember reading that the debate about whether forgeries are aesthetically worse is a whole separate fight, but I can't place it.
