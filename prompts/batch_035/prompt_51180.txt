I am assembling a seminar on higher-order evidence — evidence about what one's own evidence supports — and before we evaluate any particular theory, I want the specification any adequate theory of how rational belief should respond to higher-order evidence must meet. Write that list of desiderata.

Some parameters. The theory's domain is the ordinary run of cases plus the hard ones; the list should be motivated by cases, and should include at least these among them: (a) evidence of bias — you learn that people in your position, reasoning as you do, tend to get a certain question wrong, though you cannot locate any flaw in your own reasoning; (b) peer disagreement — you learn that an epistemic peer with the same first-order evidence has reached the opposite verdict; (c) debunking — you learn that the process that produced one of your beliefs was shaped by interests that make its reliability suspect, even though nothing else about the belief's basis changes; (d) a drunken-vision case — the intrinsic character of your experience is unchanged, but you learn it was produced by an unreliable mechanism; and (e) an examination case — you have checked your work and it seems to you that you earned an A, but the instructor announces that she assigns grades below B to ninety percent of students who feel exactly as confident as you do.

Among the requirements I would expect the list to weigh, argue about what genuinely belongs there: whether rationality must be transparent from the inside (can it ever be rational to believe p while believing one's total evidence doesn't support p?); whether higher-order evidence can genuinely defeat first-order evidence, and how, given that it never bears on the world directly; whether the theory must terminate — no infinite regress of evidence about evidence about evidence; whether it must explain the apparent irrationality of akratic pairs without mandating blanket deference; whether it must leave room for reasonable persistence in one's first-order judgment when the higher-order evidence is itself contested; and whether it must be action-guiding, telling an agent what to believe now rather than only what not to have believed.

For each desideratum, state the case or consideration behind it, and mark whether it is non-negotiable or instead a prejudice of one camp — the camp that treats first-order judgment as basic and higher-order evidence as noise, or the camp that treats any evidence of possible error as immediate defeat. Then identify which subset, if any, of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable: I suspect transparency, genuine defeat without revising the first-order basis, and non-arbitrariness cannot all be had at once, and I want to know whether that is a structural result of the area or merely a contingent failure of ingenuity. That joint-unsatisfiability question is the part I most want to see. Finally, in a brief closing paragraph and without a full scorecard, indicate how the two crude default views — stick with your first-order reasoning and dismiss the higher-order evidence entirely, versus defer wholesale to your evidence about your evidence — each fail the list, so that I can tell the list has teeth.
