And here is the student's essay:

"Reserve Police Battalion 101 consisted of about 500 middle-aged working-class reservists from Hamburg, judged too old for the army, so on paper they are exactly the 'ordinary men' of Browning's title — not SS fanatics. At Józefów in July 1942 their commander, Major Trapp, visibly distressed, told the men what was coming and made an offer: any of the older men who did not feel up to the task could step out. Only around a dozen did. Browning's explanation for this is essentially Milgram's: the men kept shooting because they were following orders under pressure from authority — the same mechanism as in Arendt's banality of evil, where Eichmann was a mere cog carrying out orders. Conformity to comrades plays a role for Browning too, but only a secondary one. Goldhagen, writing before Browning, disagreed: he argued that the interrogations show not reluctant order-followers but volunteers — during the later 'Jew hunts' the men tracked down hidden Jews eagerly, even treating it as a kind of sport, which no order required. So for Goldhagen what explains the killing is a German culture of eliminationist antisemitism, built up over centuries, not the situation the men happened to be in. And since Goldhagen establishes that the men were never coerced — Trapp's offer proves opting out was possible, and nobody who stepped out was ever punished — the situational explanations are refuted. The real question was never 'why did they obey' but 'why did they want to', and only a deeply antisemitic culture can answer that. Still, Browning's observation that the postwar interrogations are self-serving sources cuts against Goldhagen too, since Goldhagen relies on the very same testimony. And Asch-type experiments can settle nothing here, since no experiment can recreate shooting women and children at point-blank range week after week. Overall, then, Goldhagen wins the exchange: voluntarism plus a deep antisemitic culture explains more than Milgram-style obedience ever could."
