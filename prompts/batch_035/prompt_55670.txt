I co-teach an undergraduate seminar on value theory, and next week we're doing a unit on incommensurability and hard choices — mostly Raz on constitutive incommensurability and Chang on items being on a par and what it means to put yourself behind an option rather than choose it for reasons. My co-instructor, a committed consequentialist, keeps insisting that the whole framework is a consolation prize for people who can't figure out which option is better, and I'd like to complicate that with a novel rather than another article. I'm assigning Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day, which I know well, and I want a serious philosophical reading of it that I can circulate beforehand — the students will have finished the book.

Here is the reading I want: Stevens's decades of choices between his professional ideal (the dignity of the great butler, serving a great man whose cause he does not judge) and his almost-relationship with Miss Kenton — the missed opportunities, the scene where he reads a romance novel and is caught at it, his refusal to leave his father's deathbed during the crucial conference downstairs, and the late-life meeting with the now-divorced Mrs. Benn at the bus stop in the rain. The central question: is this a hard choice in Chang's sense — dignity and intimate love as genuinely on a par, neither better nor worse, so that his choosing duty constitutes rather than reveals his identity — or is it simply a case where one thing was better all along and Stevens errs, so that incommensurability-talk just launders a mistake into a tragedy? Consider the Razian angle too: if dignity-as-Stevens-conceives-it partly consists in refusing to weigh it against private happiness, then comparing the two may be not merely difficult but conceptually ruled out — the value is partially constituted by its exclusiveness — and ask whether the novel endorses that structure or exposes it as a rationalization constructed by a man (and a class, and a country) that needed it.

Two constraints. First, the novel may resist the reading: Stevens is an unreliable narrator, and his appeals to dignity and professional restraint coexist with cowardice, repression, and what looks like willful self-deception about Lord Darlington's politics. A reading that treats his choice as a paradigm of par-hood risks taking a deluded man's self-description at face value, and I want you to address where the text supports that worry and where it undercuts it — including whether Miss Kenton's own account of her unhappiness, which Stevens reports only secondhand, should change the analysis. Second, my co-instructor needs answering: tell me what the novel dramatizes about hard choices that the philosophical literature itself doesn't — something about how a choiceless life can be retrospectively narrated as chosen, or about the temporal asymmetry between the person who puts himself behind an option and the person who must later live as that option — and whether that addition is an insight the theories should absorb or an artifact of fiction. Please write the reading as continuous prose rather than a bulleted list of claims, roughly essay length, and assume the reader knows the novel's plot but not the philosophy.
