My brother works in factual drama, and over Christmas we got into a running argument about what we owe the real dead when we dramatise them. I kept issuing verdicts on particular films without any principled basis, so I have tried to write down what I actually think, case by case, and I would like to know whether a principle captures it. Compressing ten years of patient work into one triumphant afternoon in a laboratory: fine. Giving a historical figure invented dialogue that expresses views she demonstrably held and wrote down elsewhere: fine. Creating a composite character — a rival stitched together from three real contemporaries so that no single person carries the blame: fine, and possibly more honest than the alternative. Having a named real person commit a wickedness for which there is no evidence — implying a colleague sabotaged the experiments when the record shows nothing of the sort: not fine, and not fine even with a disclaimer in the end credits. Inventing an affair between the subject and a real subordinate: not fine. Wholesale invention of private interiority — deathbed scenes, invented weeping, a diary no one ever found — for a long-dead public figure who put herself before the world: fine. The same wholesale invention for someone obscure who never sought attention and left little record: here I balk, though I can't say what changed.

I can see two problems with my own list. First, the last two verdicts seem to run on publicness rather than fidelity, and I don't know whether that is a principled distinction or a prejudice about whose lives are fair game. Second, the affair verdict and the deathbed verdict point in opposite directions: both are inventions about a real person's private life, yet one imputes vice and the other imputes virtue, and only the former strikes me as a violation. That suggests a tentative principle of mine — something like: we owe the dead fidelity in their vices and licence in their virtues, so you may make the dead braver or kinder than they were, but not baser — and I would like this principle tested and repaired rather than accepted. It gets uncomfortable fast, though, because by its lights it should be fine to give a fully documented war criminal an invented moment of private decency, humanising him for the audience, and that is the case I keep going back and forth on: part of me says the principle licenses it and part of me says this is exactly where the licence should stop.

So, please: state the principle or principles that best capture my verdicts, making each clause precise and tying it to the case that motivates it. Then tell me honestly which of my verdicts the best simple principle cannot accommodate, and whether I should pay that price or buy the complexity needed to save them all. Finally, apply the result to cases I haven't considered — the humanised war criminal, but also a drama that gives a real, long-dead disaster victim, someone who left barely a trace, an invented act of courage, and a biopic that rearranges the chronology so that events the subject caused appear to have merely happened to her. I want your predictions for those cases together with an argument for which verdicts, if any, I ought to give up.
