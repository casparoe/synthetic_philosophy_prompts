In the mind-uploading debates I follow, the patternist side leans above all on one analogy: the self as a wave (sometimes a dance, sometimes a song) rather than as the water that carries it. The version I met last week, in an online thread on destructive scanning, ran roughly as follows: a wave crossing the ocean keeps its identity even though no water molecule travels with it; the water is exchanged continuously while the wave's form persists; so what persists through time is a pattern, not a parcel of matter. My interlocutor then used the analogy for a further step that I found the most interesting part: he denied that there is any philosophically significant difference between gradual replacement (swapping neurons for silicon one at a time, with function preserved at each step) and destructive scan-and-upload (reading the brain out, destroying it, running the readout elsewhere), on the ground that the wave doesn't care whether the medium turns over a little at a time or all at once. The conclusion being pushed: both procedures are survival, and neither is death.

I want an audit of this analogy rather than a verdict on uploading.

First, the correspondence table. What plays the role of the wave, the water, the form, and the medium exchange — and, hardest to fill in, what plays the role of the wave's persistence conditions? It matters here that wave identity is ordinarily fixed by the spatiotemporal continuity of a process, not by similarity of shape, and that a wave reproduced in a tank from recordings would be a new wave. State plainly whether these facts about waves help the patternist or quietly sabotage him.

Second, the disanalogies, sorted into those that matter for the use the analogy was put to and those that don't. The candidates I can see: a wave has no inside — no point of view that could continue or fail to continue; wave individuation is somewhat observer-relative and gerrymandered; and there is no standing metaphysical dispute about wave identity, whereas personal identity is precisely the thing in dispute. Which of these, if any, break the inference from the wave's surviving medium exchange to my surviving destructive uploading?

Third, and most important: the gradual-versus-destructive collapse. The standard continuity argument for uploading — the one built on thought experiments about gradually replacing neural components while function is held fixed — establishes at most something about the gradual procedure. The wave analogy was invoked precisely to stretch that conclusion to the destructive procedure. Does it succeed there, or does it equivocate between two different claims: that the underlying matter is irrelevant (which gradual replacement concedes too) and that the manner and tempo of the transition are irrelevant (which gradual replacement most emphatically does not concede)? As a test, construct, on the model of the wave, a case in which the medium is exchanged all at once and we have firm pre-theoretic intuitions about persistence or non-persistence, and check whether the analogy's verdict matches those intuitions. If the analogy can be described so as to match either intuition, say so — that would suggest it is not doing independent work at this point but merely re-asserting the conclusion it was brought in to support.

Finally: if the analogy fails at any of these joints, say whether a better one is available to the patternist, or whether the lesson is instead that this stretch of the debate cannot be carried by analogy at all and has to be argued on its own terms.
