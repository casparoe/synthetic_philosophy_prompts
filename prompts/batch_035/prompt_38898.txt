I referee a lot of manuscripts for a mid-sized interdisciplinary journal, and the stuff I get is heavily "evolutionary explanation for human behavior" flavored -- why we like music, why morning sickness exists, why people get depressed, why men prefer certain waist-to-hip ratios. My problem: I'm sympathetic to evolutionary thinking but I've also read enough Gould and Lewontin to be suspicious of just-so stories, and I never know where to draw the line in a review without either rubber-stamping a panglossian fairytale or being that referee who just says "not an adaptation, byproduct" to everything without argument.

So what I want is a decision procedure, built out of the actual substance of the adaptationism debate (spandrels/byproducts, exaptation, drift and constraints, the whole Gould-Lewontin vs Dawkins/Dennett slugfest, plus the more sober versions of the argument from design), that I can run in about half an hour per paper with no data analysis and no ability to demand new experiments. It should be an ordered list of questions with, for each one, what counts as a pass, what counts as a fail, and what just gets flagged in the review as unresolved. It needs to actually terminate in one of a few verdicts -- reject the adaptationist claim, accept it provisionally, or "the paper is fine as long as it's reframed as a hypothesis, not a finding" -- since those are the three moves available to me in a referee report. And I want the procedure to be honest about which steps are my judgment dressed up as a checkbox, because I know myself well enough to know I'll rationalize.

Some specific things it has to handle, since they come up constantly:

1. How much weight to give "the trait is complex and well-engineered for X, and you don't get that kind of fit by accident" when the paper never demonstrates the fit, just asserts it.
2. Whether cross-cultural universality helps an adaptationist claim, given that plenty of byproducts are universal too.
3. The case where a trait plausibly started as a byproduct but the paper claims it was later shaped by selection for its current use (exaptation). When is that a real explanation and when is it a free pass?
4. The "depression is adaptive because it signals yielding in social conflict" style paper, where the proposed function explains why the trait isn't lethal but can't obviously explain why it has the specific form it has.

Then run it on three manuscripts I'm making up but which are representative:

- Morning sickness as an adaptation for avoiding teratogens in the first trimester (this one has actual comparative and timing evidence behind it, for whatever my memory of the literature is worth)
- Our love of landscape paintings featuring water and open trees as an adaptation for savanna habitat selection
- Sad music enjoyment as an adaptation for social bonding, in a paper whose only evidence is that people report feeling more connected after listening together

Also: one of these I actually want to accept (the music one, it's charming and I'd love it to be true), so you'll have to show me what the procedure says even when it embarrasses me. And include at least one failure mode where the procedure itself misfires -- e.g. a true adaptation it would wrongly kill -- so I know when to overrule my own checklist. Keep it usable by a tired person on a Sunday night, not a philosophy seminar.
