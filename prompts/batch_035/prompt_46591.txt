I'm a software engineer who builds forecasting and calibration systems, no philosophy background, and a colleague has walked me into an argument about the reference class problem that I can't find my way out of. (1) Any probability I can defend for a one-off event has to come from a base rate: the frequency of the target outcome in some reference class of cases I treat as interchangeable. (2) Every concrete event belongs to many overlapping reference classes, and these classes disagree. My cousin, a 43-year-old running her first marathon after a year of serious training, is in "first-time marathoners over 40" (mostly slow), in "runners who trained seriously for a year" (mostly fast), and in "residents of a flat city"; each class hands me a different number for the chance she breaks four hours. (3) Nothing about the event itself selects one class as the right one: whether she breaks four hours does not depend on how I file her. (4) So there is no fact of the matter about the correct probability; the number I report is a convention I adopt. (5) Every real forecasting task — insurance pricing, medical prognosis, weather — has the same many-classes structure, so all probabilities for one-off events are conventions, not estimates. (6) Then a 70% rain forecast is no more correct than a 30% one, and my calibration scores measure not accuracy but the internal consistency of my own conventions. That conclusion seems to prove too much, yet each step looked fine when my colleague stated it. Premises (1) and (3) strike me as obviously true, and I would rather keep both. Please reply as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: the step to reject, stated precisely; why that step looked fine to us; what rejecting it costs, i.e. where else that kind of premise was doing work I care about; the most tempting alternative rejection and its price instead; and one concrete forecasting case whose outcome would confirm or disconfirm your diagnosis.
