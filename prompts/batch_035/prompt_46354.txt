I sit on the defense committee of the parliament of a small, prosperous, militarily neutral state of roughly six million. Our force follows the militia pattern: universal male conscription with a lottery, relatively short service, and a large trained reserve, and the system's legitimacy has been reaffirmed by referendum. The committee must choose among three options before next spring's budget vote: (1) retain universal conscription as it is; (2) replace the lottery with a selective draft in which service remains a formal obligation but only motivated applicants are actually called — the model Sweden moved to when it reinstated a gender-neutral draft in 2017 after its all-volunteer experiment left the ranks unfilled; or (3) abolish conscription and field a professional volunteer force, accepting a far smaller reserve. The ministry's own figures concede that our budget cannot buy a professional force of comparable mobilization depth, so the real disagreement in the committee is philosophical. One faction holds that universal service is exactly what makes neutrality credible and roots the military in society: let soldiers become employees, they argue, and deterrence becomes a staffing problem while civil-military ties decay. The other faction holds that conscripting a random minority of each cohort for a year is an unjustified burden on liberty that the current threat picture no longer warrants, and that a smaller professional force would be better at the missions we actually ask of it. Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before the vote: recommend one option, spell out implementation conditions and a realistic time frame, and give the strongest argument on the losing side a fair hearing rather than a caricature. Throughout the memo, keep two registers apart: mark which supporting claims are relatively settled — the arithmetic of manpower and cost at a given budget, and the historical record of comparable neutral or conscript-based systems in respects we can verify — and which remain genuinely contested among informed observers, for instance whether conscription builds civic cohesion or how a professional force would change the military's political standing. Do not present a contested judgment as though it were established fact or committee consensus.
