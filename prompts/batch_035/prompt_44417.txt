I teach in the first-year "Doctoring" course at a medical school, and I have been given a single fifty-minute small-group section to open our unit on clinical reasoning. The topic: what makes a condition a disease — whether "disease" picks out an objective fact about bodies, as the naturalist maintains, or whether it partly tracks harms, values, and expectations, as the normativist maintains. My students are bright, science-confident, and fresh off a lecture on ICD-10 coding, so several will arrive believing the question is settled by the codebook, and that anything called a disease must be a lesion, a pathogen, or a measurable physiological failure. I don't want a lecture on the topic from you; I want a plan for my fifty minutes.

Here is the material I would like the session built around, unless you can convince me something else would do the job better: two patients on one handout. Patient A is 58, feels entirely well, and came in for an insurance physical; her labs show an eGFR of 55, confirmed on repeat, and her chart now reads "CKD stage 3a." She has a disease label and no complaint. Patient B is 41, has had near-daily headaches for two years, is visibly distressed and asking for help, but his imaging and labs are unremarkable and his chart says "tension-type headaches, medically unexplained." He has a complaint and, arguably, no disease. My hope is that the pair does the work of pulling apart the two ingredients — biological dysfunction and harm — that the word "disease" fuses together, and that students leave able to say which patient raises which half of the question and why a naturalist and a normativist would give different answers for each.

So tell me: what has to come first and what has to wait — can the distinction even be stated before the pair is presented, or must the pair do the stating, and what does that imply about the order of the whole section? How should I handle the examples I expect to be flung at me from the floor (homosexuality's removal from the diagnostic manuals, the Deaf community's rejection of deafness-as-pathology, and the line that a disease just is whatever doctors treat), and where in the sequence should each be absorbed into the argument rather than swatted down as a special case? I anticipate two misconceptions I would like you to confirm, complicate, or replace: first, that normativism entails diseases are subjective or invented, so nothing is really wrong with Patient B; second, that naturalism simply is science, so anyone who doubts a disease classification is doubting biology. Give me as well the one question I can put to the room in the last five minutes whose answer would show the distinction has actually landed — something better than "define naturalism and normativism." And tell me what I should explicitly decline to cover at this level, and how to mark that deferral as a deferral rather than a dismissal.
