I've been arguing for weeks with a friend of mine who writes software for a living and is a strict materialist, and I'd like someone to untangle it for me. It started when we watched a documentary that said young sunflowers turn to follow the sun across the sky in order to catch more light. My friend laughed and said that sentence is strictly false. In his view, a reason is something a reasoner has: someone must first conceive of an outcome and then act because of that conception. The sunflower conceives nothing, nobody designed it, and blind natural selection cannot be a reasoner either — "blind" is the whole point, he says. So, according to him, all there really is inside the flower is a chain of chemical causes, and calling the tracking "for" light capture is a human metaphor that only becomes literally true at the moment some human observer forms the thought. Before any mind existed, the universe contained no reasons at all — just events.

I think he's wrong, and I can almost feel where, but I can't argue it. It seems to me that the tracking was for light capture even if no one had ever existed to notice it, and that this would have been just as true a hundred million years before anyone could think it. I keep reaching for something like: the reason is sitting there in the pattern of what the organism is doing, the way the shape of a key holds the reason for the lock, without anyone needing to have the key in mind. But when I say this out loud it sounds like mysticism, which is the last thing I mean, and he accuses me of smuggling a ghost into the sunflower.

There is one more case we got stuck on, which might be the real test. He will grant that a thermostat has a reason in a derivative sense, because a designer put the purpose there. And he will even half-concede the sunflower once I point out that natural selection kept the trackers around and weeded out the non-trackers — he says the reason then traces back to a kind of mindless keeping, which he finds suspicious but can live with. But neither story covers what happened in our neighborhood this summer: a bird that, as far as anyone can tell, has newly taken to spreading its wings to shade its chicks on hot afternoons. Nobody designed it, and if it is too recent for selection to have rewarded it yet, nothing has selected it either. The bird plainly isn't working it out like an engineer; the behavior just spills out of it. So here are my questions. Is there any principled way of deciding when it is legitimate to say that something happens for a reason even though nobody — no person, no animal, no designer, and no history of selection — ever had that reason? And if we decide the shading is reasonless, hasn't my friend drawn the line in a strange place, since the sunflower and the bird look like the same kind of thing from the inside? I'd be grateful for a careful answer: which of us, if either, is right, and if the reasons I want to keep really exist, what are they made of?
