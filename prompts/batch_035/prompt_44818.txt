I am one of three citizen members of my state's Commission on Higher Education Affordability, appointed after a career running a small heating-and-plumbing business; I never finished my own degree, and I hold no academic credentials beyond a strong interest in the question. The commission must issue a written recommendation to the legislature this autumn, and the live options are: (a) making public college tuition-free and funding it from general taxation, roughly along the lines of Germany or the Nordic countries; (b) keeping tuition where it is but strengthening the loan system — more generous limits, income-based repayment, and a one-time forgiveness program; or (c) means-tested free tuition for families below an income threshold. Please draft the written recommendation I will circulate to my fellow commissioners before the vote. It should take a clear position on which of the three paths to recommend, give the reasoning in terms the legislature and the public could actually follow, and treat the strongest argument on the losing side honestly enough that its proponents on the commission could recognize their view in it.

To show you where I've gotten to on my own, I'll tell you how I've come to frame the question, and I'd like the memo to engage this framing rather than talk past it. It seems to me that tuition financed by loans is not really a price in the ordinary sense; it is a tax that is levied only on people who attended college, assessed not on what they had at the time but on what they'd earn later, with interest, and collected only from those who had to borrow. If a state openly proposed a special income tax applying only to graduates between the ages of twenty-two and forty-five who came from families without savings, we would call that perverse, yet that is what our aid-by-debt system amounts to. On the other hand, I can see that making tuition free from general taxation has its own awkward feature: general revenue includes taxes paid by people who never went to college and whose children may not, while the average college graduate earns well more than the average taxpayer. So one of my homemade convictions is that we are not choosing between a tax and no tax, but between two different taxes, and the real question is which one is fairer and which does less collateral damage. A second thought I keep circling: when something is made free but places are limited, the price does not vanish — it reappears as something non-monetary. In the German and Nordic systems, I understand that access is rationed by academic selection at an earlier age, by the structure of degree programs, and by things like housing costs in university cities, which to my eye means the cost is pushed partly onto families who must be able to support a young adult for years, and partly onto a sorting process that happens before anyone asks who can pay. Whether that is better than our system of rationing by sticker price plus debt is exactly what I cannot settle by myself.

A third matter I want the memo to face, because our chairman will raise it: the plain empirical claim that universal free tuition in those countries coexists with governments spending public money on families who could have paid, and the claim sometimes made about our own system that easily available loans are precisely what has allowed tuition to climb — that the debt system feeds the price it is meant to soften. I don't know how much weight these deserve, and the memo should be candid about the uncertainty rather than pretend the comparison to Germany and the Nordics is a clean one; those countries differ from ours in more ways than tuition policy. What I want, in the end, is a document I can stand behind at the public hearing: a recommendation, the case for it, an honest account of the best case against it, and some acknowledgment of what we would be gambling on if we are wrong. If your own reasoning leads you to think my tax-framing misdescribes the situation, say so and show why — I would rather have my view corrected in a memo nobody sees yet than in a hearing everybody does.
