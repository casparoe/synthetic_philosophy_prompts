I've been running the classics reading group at my retirement community for a few years now, and next month it's my turn to present Cicero's essay on old age -- the one where old Cato talks a couple of younger guys into not dreading getting old. Everyone in that room loves it. A few of them practically quote it as scripture, and honestly part of why they love it is that it tells them what they want to hear.

Here's my problem. The more I've reread it, the more I think the whole consolation only works if you're Cato. His four replies -- that old age retires you from active life only if you never had anything in your head to do, that the body weakening doesn't matter if the mind stays sharp, that losing bodily pleasures is a gain not a loss, and that death is nothing to fear -- all of them quietly depend on being a former consul with a farm to run, visitors showing up daily, letters to write, and a body that mostly cooperates. Take away the estate and the status and I'm not sure the essay has much to say to someone in a wheelchair on a fixed income whose kids never call. That's the case I want to make next month.

Draft my prep sheet for that evening. What I need is the objections I'll actually get from this particular room -- retirees who read Cicero for comfort, not scholars -- phrased the way they'd put them, and in roughly the order they'll come at me. For each one: does it actually have force, what should I concede up front, and a reply I can give in a minute or two of talking. Mark the weakest point in my own position too -- I'd rather know where I'm exposed than find out mid-meeting. And include the question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks. One more thing: be careful to keep separate what's just my own reading of the essay versus what's actually established or seriously contested among people who study Cicero for a living -- I don't want to stand there passing off my hot take as the scholarly consensus when someone's granddaughter took a classics course once and knows better.
