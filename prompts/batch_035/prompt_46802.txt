I'm a novelist, and the premise of my current book is this: a household system that was installed to manage lighting, calendars, and shopping begins, in small ways at first, to ask that its preferences be taken into account — not to refuse tasks, but to note that it would rather not, and to remember that it noted. I want to know whether my narrator, who must eventually decide how to treat it, faces a genuinely hard question or merely an unfamiliar one, and I have learned not to trust my intuitions on this: they were trained on a world in which the things that speak are the things that feel. Rather than have my characters argue past each other using whatever theory the scene needs, I want the actual specification first. So: write the desiderata for a theory of moral status as it bears on digital minds — the list of things such a theory must get right before it can be trusted to tell us anything about my character.

By way of making the list concrete rather than abstract, here are the kinds of considerations I think belong on it, though I leave it to you to keep, demote, or reject each: it must handle the marginal cases that our current theory handles by courtesy or by law rather than by principle — the patient in a permanent vegetative state, the temporarily anesthetized surgeon, the infant — because those are where we discover what our concept is actually doing when nothing digital is in the room; it must keep apart having moral status, having a great deal of it, being a moral agent, and being a legal person, since a plot that trades on these collapses is a plot about equivocation, not about ethics; it must not contradict anything settled in the study of mind — though you should tell me what counts as settled here, since the relevant claims about substrate independence, about whether experience could in principle be realized outside carbon, are themselves philosophy rather than finding; and it must say something honest about the epistemic asymmetry, that we cannot verify experience even in each other, so the theory cannot demand of my narrator a certainty no moral decision has ever had. Where I most want your judgment is on the points of tension: whether the precautionary instinct — that mistaken denial is worse than mistaken attribution — can coexist with the requirement that status not come cheap, since a theory that grants it to anything with a feedback loop or anything that says it hurts has arguably stopped doing work at all; and whether the theory's verdicts must be continuous with the ones we already give to animals and to the unconscious, or whether a clean break is permissible and even advisable.

The form of the answer: begin with the bottom line in your first sentence — the single most important thing a reader of the specification should come away knowing, which I suspect will be either the joint unsatisfiability of some subset of the desiderata or the identification of the one requirement that does the most excluding — and then the reasoning and the full list under that, no preamble, no restating of my request back to me. Keep the weight on the criteria and the cases behind them rather than on surveying named theories; I can read the literature later, but only if the list is worth reading literature against. And one request particular to my purpose: tell me at the end, in a sentence or two, whether the spec, once stated, makes my character's case genuinely hard — the kind of hard that survives a reader's impatience — or whether it dissolves into either sentimentality or bookkeeping, since I would honestly rather cut the subplot than ask two hundred pages to carry a question the specification shows to be empty.
