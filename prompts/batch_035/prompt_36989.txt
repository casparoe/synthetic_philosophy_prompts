My housemate just finished a seminar on philosophy of language and gave me this account of Austin on performative utterances: Austin noticed that some utterances do not report on the world but act on it — saying "I promise to have it back by Friday" does not describe a promise but makes one. He called these performatives and said that instead of being true or false they are happy or unhappy, depending on whether the felicity conditions hold: the right person must speak in the right circumstances, the procedure must be executed properly and completely, and the speaker must have the requisite thoughts, feelings, and intentions. According to my housemate, if any of these conditions fails, the act is null and void — so an insincere promise is not a promise at all, which, he says, neatly explains why we don't hold people to promises they never meant to keep. He added that there is a clean grammatical criterion for spotting performatives: you can insert the word "hereby" (as in "I hereby promise") and still make sense, unlike with ordinary statements. He concluded that the lasting upshot of Austin's theory is the dichotomy between performative and constative utterances, and that insincerity voids speech acts across the board.

Two things bother me. First, when my housemate once muttered a sarcastic "sorry" after denting my car, my sense was that he had apologized badly rather than not apologized — the act was still there, just defective. Second, I have a vague memory that Austin ended up changing his mind about something central here rather than leaving the dichotomy standing. How much of my housemate's account would a philosopher of language accept, where exactly does it go subtly wrong, and does his explanation of insincere promises survive the corrections?
