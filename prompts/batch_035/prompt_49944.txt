I'm teaching an upper-level seminar on decision theory that spends its last third on imperfect recall and time-inconsistency, and I'm writing the final exam. I want one essay question on the absent-minded driver — the problem Piccione and Rubinstein introduced in their 1997 paper on decision problems with imperfect recall — but I'm tired of answers that just say "the optimal probability is 5/8" and stop, as if the number were the lesson. The question should force students to confront the dissociation between the planning-stage analysis and the action-stage analysis: at planning time the driver maximizes p·1 + (1−p)·p·4 over exit probability p, gets p = 5/8 and expected payoff 25/16; but at an intersection, unaware which one he's at, he can compute that given plan p the probability he's at the first intersection is 1/(2−p), i.e. 8/11 when p = 5/8, and I want the question to make them discover and interpret the fact that under this computation exiting for sure and continuing both yield exactly 20/11 — an indifference that looks like magic until you understand what kind of reasoning generates it. It should also expose the seductive bad attractor: the plan "never exit" feels self-consistent to a driver at an intersection who reasons naively about which intersection he's at, yet it delivers the worst payoff in the problem.

Please write the exam question itself, a model answer of roughly 600 words, and a marking scheme worth 20 points built around five must-hit moves. Among the moves I'd want recognized: (a) correctly deriving the planning optimum and explaining why "5/8" is a plan and not an action; (b) carrying out the action-stage conditional computation and explaining why it does or does not constitute a genuine deviation opportunity, including what the action-stage reasoner is entitled to condition on; (c) diagnosing the p = 0 trap and saying precisely which interpretation of the information set makes it look stable; (d) explaining the two standard repairs — treating the driver at an intersection as one of several selves in a game, versus treating his current choice as an input to a policy evaluated across all his selves — and what each says about whether the ex ante optimum is self-enforcing; and (e) connecting the structure to something the students already know, such as commitment problems or the Newcomb family, without letting the analogy do the work. For each must-hit move, give the marks it's worth and a one-line note on the way students typically fumble it (for instance confusing the probability of being at the first intersection with the probability of having reached it, or treating indifference at 20/11 as an accident of the numbers rather than a fixed-point property). The question should be answerable in 75 minutes by students who have the payoff structure in front of them, and a student who has merely memorized the 5/8 answer should be unable to fake their way past part (b).
