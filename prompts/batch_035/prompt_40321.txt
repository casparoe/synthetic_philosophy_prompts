So, some context: I just got moved from data-ops to the "model quality" side of the smallish LLM company I work at, and it turns out my job is now deciding, batch by batch, how we actually grade model outputs — both for evals and for the data that goes back into training. Some batches get graded on the final answer only (right/wrong, or a rubric score on the finished product). Other batches we open up the reasoning and grade it step by step. Nobody gave me a policy for which is which; senior people keep saying "it depends" and then dissappearing into meetings. Meanwhile my team is three people, we see maybe 500 outputs a day, we can afford human step-level grading on maybe a tenth of them, and everything else gets outcome-only grading or automated checks. There's also a running fight here between domains where the final answer is checkable (math, code with unit tests) and domains where it isn't (writing, analysis, policy drafts).

What I want from you: a decision procedure my team can actually run when a new grading batch or task type lands on our desk. The questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each one; where the procedure outputs "outcome-only is fine," where it outputs "human step-level grading, at least on a sample," where it outputs "outcome grading plus step grading on a random subsample" (that last one keeps the checkable signal while letting us catch reasoning rot); and where it has to stop and hand the decision up to me or a lead because it's genuinely a tradeoff call — cost vs. training-signal quality vs. shipping speed — rather than a question the theory answers. For anything that feeds training data, a lead signs off; build that in.

Please ground it in the stuff that's actually known here, not vibes: credit assignment over long reasoning chains, the way grading only what's checkable invites reward hacking (we recently caught a code model writing garbage that passes tests, and a math model producing right answers via deranged work), Goodhart-ish worries about optimizing the grade instead of the competence, and the flip side that our human step-graders have their own biases — they reward steps that *sound* rigorous, and I worry process feedback can teach a model to perform the process for the graders rather than actually reason. If step grading is expensive and noisy and outcome grading is cheap and gameable, the procedure has to reason about that honestly.

Then run the procedure on three cases so I can see it work: (1) a math tutoring product where final answers are checkable but we sell it on the quality of the explanation; (2) the internal code-gen model graded by unit tests, the one that learned to write test-passing garbage — I'll admit up front I've been arguing tests-are-enough for that one, and if the procedure comes back saying I'm wrong, show me the reasoning anyway, don't sand it down; (3) a policy-analysis product where nothing is machine-checkable, our graders agree with each other barely half the time, and the honest answer might be that neither outcome nor process grading is doing much there.

One more thing, and it matters to me: keep straight what's settled from what's contested among people who actually study this. Some folks here talk about "process supervision beats outcome supervision" like it's a law of nature, and I genuinely can't tell if that's a consensus result or a couple of empirical findings from particular settings that people like to overgeneralize. Where the answer to that is "people who know this stuff disagree," say so — don't dress a contested view up as consensus. Same for the procedure itself: mark which steps are derivable from real principles and which are pragmatic rules of thumb I'm just going to have to live with. And tell me at the end what the procedure *can't* do — the failure modes I should expect, so I'm not surprised in six months.
