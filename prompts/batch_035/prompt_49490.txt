In my philosophy elective we recently discussed Buridan's ass, the donkey placed exactly between two identical haystacks that supposedly starves because it has no reason to prefer one over the other. I understand the paradox as usually presented, but what I keep noticing is something slightly different from the paradox itself: in everyday life nobody starves. When I am exactly indifferent between two lunch options, I just pick one — flip a coin, take whichever is closer, or grab the left one for no reason at all — and nobody thinks I have done anything irrational. Yet the picking itself cannot be justified by reasons for one option over the other, since by assumption there are none; the coin flip is not a reason but a procedure. So it seems like we treat an arbitrary selection procedure as itself the rational thing to do when reasons run out, as if "just pick" were a distinct rational act rather than a confession that rationality has nothing to say. Is there an established concept or name for this — for arbitrary selection being rational precisely because and only when the options are tied? I have also heard of "hard choices," which from the outside look like the same situation, though people describe them differently. I would like to know what the nearest concepts are, how each differs from what I have described, and where the edges of the idea lie — for instance, whether the arbitrariness has to be random as opposed to merely unreasoned, or whether cases of practical importance are somehow different from lunch — not just a label.
