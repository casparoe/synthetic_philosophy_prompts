In my political philosophy seminar we have just finished Michael Walzer's 1973 essay "Political Action: The Problem of Dirty Hands" (in Philosophy & Public Affairs), together with some of the follow-up literature on moral residue. During our discussion section, a classmate defended the dirty-hands idea with what she called the "occupational grime" analogy: doing politics is like being a mechanic. A mechanic comes home with grease under his fingernails every day; nobody thinks the worse of him for it, because the grime is a foreseeable byproduct of necessary work, it belongs to the trade rather than to him, and it comes off with soap. Likewise, she said, a political leader who lies, threatens, or makes compromising deals gets a certain moral "grease" on his hands; that is just what the job involves, and the guilt should be regarded as a professional stain that we wash away at the end of the day — no deeper than the mechanic's. She then drew a strong conclusion: dirty-hands cases involve no genuine wrongdoing, only messiness, so the agonizing that Walzer describes (his example of the official who must torture a captured rebel to find where the bombs are planted) is misplaced sentimentality.

I am not convinced the analogy carries her conclusion, but I also do not want to dismiss it too quickly, because parts of it seem to map well onto the phenomenon Walzer is pointing at: the sense that the dirt is structural rather than a matter of personal vice, the pride some practitioners take in having done the necessary but unpleasant thing, and the thought that we should judge leaders by different standards than we judge private citizens. So I would like a careful audit of the analogy, along the following lines. First, give me the explicit correspondence table: what in the political case plays the role of the grease, the engine that must be repaired, the mechanic's employer, and the soap? Second, identify where the mapping breaks down — in particular, I want to know whether the disanalogy between grime that sits on the agent's own hands and harm that lands on third parties (the tortured rebel is not "grease" on anyone) matters, and whether the analogy can accommodate the idea, central to Walzer and to the moral-remainder literature, that appropriate guilt can survive an act being all-things-considered justified. Third, tell me which of the disanalogies are fatal for my classmate's use of the analogy and which are merely incidental: does the conclusion "no real wrongdoing, so no real guilt" survive, or was the analogy doing the work of quietly redefining guilt as external mess rather than internal burden? Finally, if the mechanic picture fails, is there a better everyday analogy available — the surgeon who must cut, the triage nurse, the parent who lies to a child — and if so, what does it get right that both Walzer's talk of "hands" and my classmate's talk of "grime" get wrong? I would appreciate a rigorous answer rather than a survey: my term paper is supposed to take a position on whether the dirty-hands metaphor itself, and not just the cases it is applied to, is philosophically respectable.
