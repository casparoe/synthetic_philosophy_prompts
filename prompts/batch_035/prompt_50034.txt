So I moved to Montreal last year, and for the first time I'm actually living inside a debate I used to only read about. The one I mean is Quebec's Bill 21, the secularism law from 2019 — it bans public employees in positions of authority (teachers, cops, judges, prosecutors) from wearing religious symbols at work, and the government invoked the notwithstanding clause up front so it couldn't just get struck down as a Charter violation. Everyone here has an opinion about it, and the split doesn't map onto anything I'd expected from the outside.

Back home I'd have called this an easy case: liberal state, individual rights, freedom of religion, done. But the people defending it here aren't being cynical, at least not mostly. They talk about laïcité, about the neutrality of the state, about Quebec as a small francophone community that survived centuries of pressure from English Canada and from the Church, and that now gets to decide collectively what its public institutions look like. And I know enough to know this isn't pure rationalization either — Quebec comes up in the actual literature, like Kymlicka on group-differentiated rights, and Taylor (who is literally from here) arguing that liberalism has to make room for communities like this one.

So help me analyze Bill 21 through the communitarianism vs liberalism lens, and run the mapping in both directions like a proper case study:

- Who is the "community" in the communitarian story — Quebec as a whole, or francophone Quebec? And what happens to members of it who wear hijabs or turbans, who are also Québécois? Is this the community reproducing itself, or the majority culture policing it's own internal minorities? I've started to feel like that distinction is the whole ballgame and nobody here makes it.
- What does the liberal side say — the Rawlsian neutral-state version, and the Kymlicka version, which is more sympathetic to group rights? Does Kymlicka's framework actually cover a case like this, or was it built for language rights and never anticipated religious symbols?
- The notwithstanding clause move deserves its own treatment, I think. Quebec used the constitution's escape hatch to say, in effect, "this is who we are, regardless of individual rights." Is that the communitarian position in its purest form? Or does communitarianism not actually commit you to overriding individual rights at all, and this is just majoritarianism wearing its clothes?
- Where does the mapping break? What can neither framework explain — like the way the government spent weeks tied in knots over whether the big crucifix in the national assembly counted as a religious symbol or as "heritage"?
- And the big one: taken as a test of the theory, does this episode confirm communitarianism, falsify it, or reveal it never anticipated a case where the community asserting itself is illiberal toward part of itself?

Be disciplined about where each theory is genuinely silent instead of forcing it to speak. One more fact that might matter: polling pretty consistently showed a majority of Quebecers supporting the law while most of the rest of Canada opposed it — I mention it because it seems to cut right to the question of whose "community" we're even talking about.
