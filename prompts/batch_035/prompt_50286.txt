I'm a PhD student in philosophy finishing a term paper on Hume's "Of the Standard of Taste," and I've hit a wall with the circularity objection — the worry that Hume identifies the true judges by their verdicts and vindicates the verdicts by appeal to the judges. My advisor suggested an odd corrective before I write the criticism proper: spend a week trying to inhabit the view so sympathetically that I could satirize it fairly. Her rule is that if the satire has to cheat — if it has to make Hume say things he doesn't say, or rely on the caricature of taste as mere snobbery — then I don't yet understand the view well enough to attack it. I've discovered I'm bad at satire, so I'd like your help with a draft.

Please write an agony-aunt advice column, in the voice of a certified Humean true judge, of roughly 700-900 words. Call it something like "Ask a True Judge." It should consist of four or five letters from imagined readers with replies from the judge. The letters should raise situations Hume's framework actually has to handle: a reader whose friends prefer the crude pleasures of the tavern to refined conversation and who wants to know whether they can be saved; an aspiring connoisseur asking what certification is involved — how much practice, how much comparison across ages and cultures, what the examination of delicacy looks like; a poet distressed that his work is adored in his own village but ignored by the critics in the capital; and a hostess wondering whether she should serve the wine her guests enjoy or the wine that is, as it were, the wine. I'd like at least one reply to retell, in the judge's own words, the old story of the two of Sancho's kinsmen who tasted the wine and detected the leather thong and the iron key at the bottom of the hogshead — Hume's own example of delicacy vindicated — and I'd like the judge to be sincerely blind to how badly that story cuts against him, given that the kinsmen needed the physical evidence to be believed.

The humor has to come from getting Hume right rather than caricaturing him: the judge's verdicts should follow from delicacy, practice, comparison, and freedom from prejudice taken entirely seriously, and his snobberies should be entailed by the theory rather than added for color. The piece should land the standard objections — the circularity, the problem of how few judges are ever truly qualified, the conservatism of the test of time and the canonization of Milton over Ogilby, the awkward question of what the standard does for ordinary people's ordinary enjoyments — without ever stating any of them outright. A Hume scholar should laugh in recognition and grudgingly concede the piece is fair; a reader who has never opened the essay should still find the judge funny and be able to tell what's being ironized. Keep the judge deadpan and internally consistent throughout — he must never admit that the standard is awkward, because he genuinely can't see it. Please write in American English, and don't rely on verbatim quotation from Hume's essay; allusions and paraphrase are fine.
