I have become quite taken with Dennett's picture, most fully worked out in "Real Patterns" and the essays of The Intentional Stance: a system has beliefs and desires insofar as adopting the intentional stance towards it buys compression and predictive power that the physical stance could only match at absurd cost, and the pattern one thereby detects is objectively real — as real as a centre of gravity — rather than a projection of our explanatory habits. Rather than argue about it in the abstract, I have been testing my gut reactions against concrete cases, and I would like to know whether a single principle captures my verdicts, or whether something has to give.

Here are the verdicts:

1. A glider in Conway's Game of Life is a real pattern. You can count gliders, predict collisions, and reason about them far more cheaply than by tracking individual cells — but it has no beliefs, and nothing whatever is gained from the intentional stance.
2. A thermostat does not believe the room is cold. Saying so is a manner of speaking, handy for a moment, and I would resist calling it a real intentional pattern even though the intentional-stance description is trivially accurate.
3. A chess engine does believe its knight is pinned. The intentional stance yields genuine, cheap predictions about its play that I could not otherwise obtain.
4. A stray dog crossing an unfamiliar city believes the alley offers a way out and wants to avoid the traffic. Obviously a real intentional pattern.
5. "The market is nervous about inflation" describes a real intentional pattern: it is predictive, and robust under intervention, even though no individual trader is the market.
6. "The average taxpayer earns £46,000 and resents the property levy," as deployed by a finance ministry, is not a real pattern at all — a summary statistic that purchases no novel prediction and supports no counterfactuals.
7. A lookup table containing, for every conversational situation, the exact reply my friend Sarah would give: the intentional stance applied to the table predicts Sarah perfectly, and yet I insist the table has no beliefs. This one is uncomfortable, since predictive purchase was supposed to be precisely what made beliefs real.

My tentative hunch is that the difference lies in counterfactual robustness: the market and the chess engine support good predictions in genuinely novel situations, whereas the average taxpayer and the lookup table merely re-encode what has already been observed. But I cannot get the hunch to deliver all seven verdicts — the thermostat, after all, responds correctly to unbounded novelty in the temperature, and the glider is a real pattern whose reality seems to consist entirely in reliable regularity rather than anything novel.

So here is what I would like from you. First, say whether there is one notion of real pattern that covers these cases, or whether I am conflating two notions — real patterns as such versus real intentional patterns — or none at all. Second, test my counterfactual-robustness hunch against the seven verdicts and tell me precisely where it breaks and whether it can be repaired, perhaps by distinguishing the novelty of inputs from the novelty of the patterns themselves. Third, state the resulting principle, or the best competing candidate, precisely enough to generate verdicts on cases I have not listed, and give those verdicts for: a bacterium swimming up a sugar gradient; a bureaucracy that continues to demand a form nobody can any longer justify; a large language model, treated as believing things about its interlocutor; a person acting in perfect sincerity on a hallucination; and a weather system whose moods folk forecasters track with uncanny success. Finally, be honest about which of my verdicts the best principle cannot accommodate. My own suspicion is that it is either verdict 2 or verdict 7 that has to go — they seem to pull in opposite directions — and I would like to know which one I should surrender, and what it costs to give it up.
