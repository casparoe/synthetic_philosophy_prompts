You are a philosopher specializing in formal epistemology, comfortable with the technical literature on credence, scoring rules, and epistemic utility theory. I recently heard a podcast whose show notes gave the following summary of "accuracy-first epistemology," and I'd like a careful audit of it. Before you begin, take a moment to brainstorm the field's actual central claims and the standard theorems, so your evaluation rests on the real results rather than on the podcast's framing. Then go through the summary claim by claim.

Here is what the notes said:

"The new school of accuracy-first epistemology holds that the whole point of having degrees of belief is to match the truth, and that everything else epistemologists talk about — evidence, justification, coherence — is merely instrumental to accuracy. Its founding theorem, due to Richard Pettigrew and others, proves that any set of credences that violates the probability calculus is dominated: there exists an alternative set of credences that is guaranteed to be more accurate no matter which propositions turn out true, a kind of epistemic Dutch book, except without the money. The proof rests on two assumptions: that rationality is expected-utility maximization, and that you should never assign credence zero or one to a proposition that could turn out either way, since a credence of exactly zero can never be raised again by conditionalizing on new evidence. It follows that the perfectly rational agent hovers just below certainty on every contingent matter forever, and that the only things that really matter epistemically are the numbers themselves."

For each claim in that paragraph, please give one of three verdicts — accurate, a defensible simplification, or a misrepresentation — together with a short explanation of what the careful version of the claim would say. In particular, I'd like you to address the following worries of mine:

1. Is the theorem's attribution plausible, and is describing the dominance result as "a kind of epistemic Dutch book" fair, or does it conflate two genuinely different styles of argument whose differences the accuracy-first people themselves take to matter?
2. Are the two stated assumptions really the ones the dominance argument needs? If not, what assumptions about how inaccuracy is measured (for example, about propriety or continuity of scoring rules) actually do the work, and would the podcast's reader come away with a distorted picture of where the argument is vulnerable?
3. Where does the "never assign zero or one" idea actually come from, and is it a premise of the theorem, a separate rule of caution with its own rationale, or a misremembering?
4. Does the closing claim — about the rational agent hovering just below certainty forever, and only the numbers mattering — follow from the accurate parts of the summary, or does it overreach? If it overreaches, say precisely where the inference goes wrong.

You need not assume the errors are evenly distributed: some of the paragraph may be entirely correct, and if so, say so plainly rather than manufacturing doubts. Conclude with an overall judgment: whether the summary would give a curious layperson a serviceable first picture of the view, and the single most important correction you would make before letting them repeat any of it at a dinner party.
