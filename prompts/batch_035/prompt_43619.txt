I'm drafting a short position statement on consciousness for an interdisciplinary workshop (mostly cognitive scientists, a few philosophers) where each participant submits their commitments in advance so others can challenge them. Before I send mine out, I want a consistency audit. Here is the set:

(1) The distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness marks a real difference, and any theory of consciousness should be evaluated separately on the two: a theory might be right about one and silent on the other.

(2) Global workspace theory is essentially correct about access consciousness: a state is available for report, reasoning, and the control of action precisely because it has won competition for global broadcast, and there is nothing more to availability than the workspace architecture that implements it.

(3) Overflow is real. Sperling-style partial report experiments show that subjects are briefly phenomenally presented with all or nearly all of a display's items — they say so, and they can report whichever row they're cued on — even though they can only access a few items. So phenomenal consciousness outruns access, and any theory that identifies the two gets the phenomena wrong.

(4) Dennett was right that there is no Cartesian theater and no privileged moment at which content becomes determinately conscious: content is revised and fixed by probing, and postdictive effects like the cutaneous rabbit show that even the apparent order and timing of experience are settled after the fact. There is no "thin red line" separating conscious content from preconscious processing.

(5) I'm a physicalist, and the explanatory gap is epistemic rather than ontological: phenomenal states just are physical states, and a completed neuroscience together with the right conceptual repertoire would close the gap in principle.

(6) Lately I've been drawn to a deflationary gloss on the phenomenology of richness: the sense that experience contains vivid detail beyond what we can report is itself a product of the workspace — the brain's model of its own access limitations posits rich-but-inaccessible detail, the way it posits a stable visual field despite saccades.

Go through the pairs and tell me which are in genuine tension and how badly; for each, say what the cheapest fix would be and what it costs elsewhere in the set; tell me which of the tensions are merely apparent and dissolve on inspection; and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I genuinely don't know where the problem is, so don't soften the audit to spare me.
