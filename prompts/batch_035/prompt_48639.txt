I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and the last couple years a lot of my beat has been fertility tech, falling birthrates, IVF policy — population stuff. Which is how I ended up, embarrassingly late, actually engaging with population ethics. And I've come out the other side convinced of what they call the procreation asymmetry: it's wrong to bring into existence a child whose life will predictably be full of suffering, but it's not obligatory (maybe not even good, in the same way) to bring into existence a child whose life will be happy. Making people happy is good; making happy people is not a duty. I've put versions of this in copy and editors find it natural, but whenever I talk to philosophers they tell me it's a minority taste, and honestly I want to know if I'm about to build a whole feature on a view that can't take a punch.

So: what would count against it? I want the full map of where this view is exposed. Which concrete cases does it have to swallow — and I know some of them have names, like the one where you compare a couple that has a child who'll be miserable with an identical couple that just doesn't, and the asymmetry says only the first did something wrong, which people find weird once you press on it. Which of those would the view's own defenders concede as counting against it, versus wave off? Is Benatar's whole thing a natural extension of the asymmetry or an unstable branch off it — and if the asymmetry pulls you even an inch toward him, is that a defeater or just a bullet some people are fine biting?

Here's the part I actually can't get straight, and it's what the piece would hinge on. There seem to be two different asymmetries mushed together. One is about creation: there's a moral difference between making a person exist and helping a person who already exists. The other is about timing: bad things that happen to someone are worse when you set them up before the person is around to be the patient of anything — like the wrong is done at the moment of creation, not at the moment the suffering lands. My half-formed suspicion is that the second one does all the real work, and that if you pull them apart, the version that survives is weaker and less intuitive than the one I keep putting in my ledes. Is that right? And if it is, which rival view — totalism, the ones with averages, whatever — steps into the gap, and does it do any better on the cases the asymmetry fumbles?

Rank the defeaters for me from fatal to merely awkward, tell me whether the view has been quietly insulated by its fans refusing to say how bad a created life has to be before creating it becomes wrong, and be honest: if I build the feature around this, is it load-bearing or is it a nice line? Also — one typo I keep making in drafts is writing "concieved" for "conceived," please don't inherit that from me.
