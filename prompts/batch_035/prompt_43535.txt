"Malcolm argued that dreams cannot be experiences, largely on the grounds that the criterion for having dreamt is the dream report given on waking, and that 'having had experiences' during sleep is not independently checkable. Dennett replied with a two-part alternative: on the 'cassette' model, dream content is composed and inserted into memory at awakening, so the report is still accurate, just about something that happens at wake-up rather than during sleep; and he also claimed that the composition of dream content could happen during sleep, before waking, in distributed fashion. Explain Malcolm's argument, explain Dennett's reply, and evaluate whether the reply actually refutes Malcolm or only relocates his worry."

The student's answer (pasted below) got a lot right, but I suspect there's at least one subtle problem in it that I'd feel bad missing. Here it is:

"Malcolm's argument rests on Wittgenstein's view that the meaning of psychological language is fixed by its use, and the use of dream talk is anchored in waking reports. Since the only criterion for saying someone dreamt is the report they give when they wake up, there is no fact of the matter about whether experiences occurred during sleep itself. The concept of experience, Malcolm says, requires the possibility of verifying its occurrence, so unverifiable sleep-experiences are not just unknown but incoherent to attribute. This is a verificationist argument, and Malcolm is happy to accept that consequence.

Dennett agrees that the introspective report is the evidence but denies it can only be evidence of something happening during sleep. On the cassette model, the brain composes the dream content right at the moment of waking and writes it backwards into memory, so that the subject sincerely remembers having dreamt during the night. If this model is possible, then the waking report could be perfectly accurate even though no experiences occurred while asleep, which shows that Malcolm was wrong to assume the report must be about an experience. Dennett also notes that lucid dreams, where subjects report awareness of dreaming while it happens, refute Malcolm outright, since the dreamer can in principle confirm the dream while asleep.

In my view Dennett wins on the first point: once the cassette model is coherent, the connection between the report and an experience during sleep is contingent rather than conceptual, which is all Dennett needs. However, the second point about lucid dreams is weaker, because lucid dream reports are also only public once the dreamer wakes up. So Dennett's reply doesn't refute Malcolm, it only relocates the dispute from the content of dream reports to the timing of the events they report."
