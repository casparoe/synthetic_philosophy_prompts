So I'm a high schooler writing my extended essay about how psychiatric diagnosis works, and I keep bumping into this thing Ian Hacking pointed out: once you create a category for people, the people in it start understanding themselves through the label and acting differently, so the category keeps shifting under you (his looping effect idea, the people being classified are moving targets). What I want before I argue about any specific label is the actual spec: a checklist of what a decent system for classifying mental disorders has to deliver. Stuff like — two clinicians seeing the same patient have to reach roughly the same diagnosis, it can't pathologize ordinary grief just because grief hurts, it has to cope with the fact that tons of patients meet criteria for several categories at once, and it has to stay usable even though the act of sorting people changes the people being sorted. For each requirement, give the case or reason behind it; then tell me which are genuinely required and which are just what one camp wants (like maybe reliability is overrated if validity suffers?); and tell me whether any of the requirements can't be satisfied together — reliability vs. the moving-target thing seems like a candidate to me but I might be wrong. Then a short pass on how the DSM-style descriptive approach, a dimensional/biological-measures approach, and a looser prototype approach do against the checklist, but keep that brief, the list itself is what I care about. And if you think one of my requirements is confused, or the whole framing is off, just say that plainly instead of being polite about it — I'd rather know.
