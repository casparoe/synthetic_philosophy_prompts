I serve on the preservation council of a national sound archive. Our digitization budget for the year can cover roughly one of two projects. The first is a collection of reel-to-reel tapes of songs, conversations, and oral histories in a language whose last fluent speakers died in the 1990s; nothing on the tapes was ever transcribed, and no grammar or dictionary of the language was ever compiled. The second is a set of rapidly decaying commercial radio broadcasts in our majority language, documented elsewhere but not in this format. A vocal faction on the council argues that digitizing the first collection is irrational sentimentality: since no one can understand a word of it, the tapes carry no recoverable meaning, and we would be spending scarce funds to preserve noise. The other faction says the opposite — these tapes are the most valuable holdings we have, because whatever they contain exists nowhere else, and future techniques (statistical decipherment of the kind applied to undeciphered scripts, or machine learning that exploits structure in untranslated corpora) might one day extract it. Please draft the memo I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend a concrete allocation — if you favor the tapes, say what, if anything, should still go to the broadcasts, and vice versa — and it must engage the strongest version of the losing side's argument rather than dismissing it. Along the way, take a position on what it means to say the tapes do or do not "contain" their content while they sit unlistened-to in storage, and whether that affects what an archive's duty to preserve actually amounts to.
