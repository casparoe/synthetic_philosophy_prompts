Prompt: In a secular-student discussion group I belong to, a member recently cited the Victorian mathematician and philosopher W. K. Clifford to argue that a friend of ours is doing something wrong by remaining religious despite harboring serious doubts. He referred to Clifford's 1877 essay "The Ethics of Belief," which I have since read. Clifford tells the story of a shipowner who talks himself into believing his old, patched-up vessel is seaworthy, sends it out full of emigrants, collects the insurance when it sinks, and concludes that the man is guilty of the passengers' deaths — the question of right or wrong having to do, as the essay puts it, with the origin of his belief, "not whether it turned out to be true or false, but whether he had a right to believe on such evidence as was before him." The essay then closes on a famously sweeping note: something like the claim that it is wrong — always, everywhere, and for everyone — to believe anything on insufficient evidence. My group member wields that maxim as if it settled our friend's case, and when I pushed back — Can you really be morally culpable for a belief you can't simply choose to drop? He's not captaining any ships! — he kept sliding between different formulations, so I could never pin down what exactly he was asserting versus what the maxim actually commits one to. What I'd like is a careful disambiguation of Clifford's principle: the distinct precise claims it could be expressing, laid out as a numbered menu I could take back to the group. For instance, is the wrongness at issue moral blame, epistemic irrationality, or culpability for the belief-forming *process* rather than the resulting state? Does the principle target synchronic belief-states or diachronic acquisition, and does it presuppose some form of doxastic voluntarism to even get off the ground? What could make evidence "insufficient" — insufficient for truth, insufficient to warrant acting on the belief, or merely less than the inquiry a person's situation demanded (which is what the shipowner story seems to involve)? And is the universal quantifier — always, everywhere, for everyone — defensible against William James's later objection that some genuine options cannot be decided on evidence of the kind Clifford demands, so that withholding belief is itself a choice with stakes? For each reading, tell me whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or false, and — crucially — whether it actually supports my group member's use of the maxim against our doubting-but-believing friend, as opposed to merely sounding like it does.
