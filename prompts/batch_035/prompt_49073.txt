The exam question (25 points total):

(a) In the one-shot prisoner's dilemma, defection strictly dominates cooperation. Explain why this makes mutual defection the unique rational outcome, and why repeating the game can change the situation — including the role of the discount factor and the "shadow of the future." Be explicit about the difference between a finitely repeated game with a known endpoint and one with an indefinite or unknown horizon. (10 points)

(b) Axelrod's computer tournaments were won by Tit-for-Tat. Does winning a round-robin tournament show that a strategy is the best strategy for the iterated prisoner's dilemma, or that it is evolutionarily stable? Explain what would have to be shown for either conclusion, and assess whether the tournament results establish it. (15 points)

The rubric for (b) the graders agreed on: a full-credit answer distinguishes a strategy's total score in a fixed field from its pairwise performance and from its invadability as a resident; notes that the tournament winner need not beat any opponent head-to-head; and says what evolutionary stability would require of TFT, including any ways TFT falls short.

The student's answer, verbatim:

"Part (a). In the one-shot PD, defection strictly dominates cooperation: whatever the other player does, you do better by defecting. So mutual defection is the unique Nash equilibrium, and since mutual cooperation Pareto-dominates it, both players end up worse off than they could have been. Repetition alone does not change this: if the number of rounds is fixed and commonly known, backward induction ensures universal defection, because on the last round there is no future to protect and the game is effectively one-shot; knowing this, both players defect on the second-to-last round, and so on back to the first. This is not an empirical claim about what people do but a theorem about dominance, so no rational player in a finitely repeated PD with a known endpoint will ever cooperate. What repetition does change is the case where the horizon is indefinite or unknown. Then there is a shadow of the future: a defection today can be met with retaliation tomorrow, and this threat can make cooperation self-enforcing. Whether the threat is credible enough depends on the discount factor w: Axelrod showed that cooperation can be sustained only if w is high enough that future payoffs weigh enough against the present temptation. TFT embodies this: it is nice, retaliatory, forgiving, and clear, and these properties explain its tournament wins.

Part (b). A strategy is evolutionarily stable if a population playing it cannot be invaded by a mutant strategy, which on the definition means that no mutant can do better against the residents than the residents do against themselves. TFT satisfies this. It is true that TFT's tournament score depended to some extent on the field of entries — a tournament is a round-robin, and the winner is the strategy with the highest total score, not necessarily one that beats every opponent pairwise. But TFT did beat every other strategy in the tournament, so no strategy from the pool could invade a TFT population, and no more exotic mutant could do better either, since any mutant's advantage would have to come from exploiting TFT and TFT cannot be exploited: after the first round it matches the opponent's moves, so no opponent can hold an advantage over it for long. Against ALL-D, for example, TFT takes the sucker's payoff once, and thereafter both players receive the same payoff each round, so in a long game TFT's average score against ALL-D is as high as its average score against another TFT player. Finally, TFT's forgiveness protects it in noisy environments: since TFT returns to cooperation as soon as the opponent does, a single mis-executed move does not lock two TFT players into a feud; each retaliation is answered by cooperation, and cooperation is quickly restored. So TFT's tournament success, together with these properties, shows that it is evolutionarily stable."
