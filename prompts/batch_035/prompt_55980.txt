so I'm writing up a position statement for a phil of science reading group, and before the group picks it apart I want a stress test of whether my views actually cohere. here's what I've landed on:

(1) I'm a scientific realist. Mature, predictively successful theories are at least approximately true, and their central theoretical terms genuinely refer — I believe in electrons roughly the way I beleive in tables.

(2) My main reason is the no-miracles argument: the empirical success of our best theories would be an unexplained miracle unless they were at least getting something approximately right about a mind-independent world.

(3) I think the pessimistic meta-induction is a genuinely serious argument, not a cheap rhetorical move. Caloric theory and phlogiston made real successful predictions and are flat false. And I don't think "well those theories weren't REALLY mature/successful, ours are different" is an honest response — picking which past theories count as genuinely successful only after seeing which were later vindicated feels like retrospective cherry picking.

(4) So I've retreated to structural realism: what past successful theories got approximately right was their structure — the equations, the relations — not the nature of the entities. Fresnel's equations basically survive in Maxwell even though the luminiferous ether doesn't. So I'm a realist about structure and only cautiously about anything beyond it.

(5) I also hold that novelty of prediction matters: accommodation is weaker evidence than prediction, and it's predictive success specifically that cries out for explanation.

(6) And I'm a naturalist: epistemology of science shouldn't appeal to a priori standards that science itself couldn't meet, and where metaphysics and current science conflict, I side with science about what exists.

Do these hang together? My half-articulated worry is that (4) quietly guts (2) — if the "approximate truth" that no-miracles posits is only structural, maybe the explanation is too thin to block the miracle charge, and the argument loses its teeth. I also can't tell whether (5) helps me or hurts me: in the classic theory-change cases where structure survived, was the surviving structure established by prediction or by accommodation, and does my novelty emphasis cut against trusting it? And there's something uneasy between (1) and (4) — I want to say "electron" refers but "caloric" didn't, but articulating the difference between them without a criterion I've already disavowed in (3) is beyond me. Plus someone at the group will probably claim (5) and (6) are in tension, that making novel prediction an evidential gold standard is exactly the kind of a priori epistemology naturalism forbids — I suspect that one dissolves but I can't say why.

So: go through the set, tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly, which ones only look like it, rank the tensions by severity, and for the worst ones say what the cheapest fix is and what it costs me. I'd rather know which single revision cleans up the most than defend every piece. Don't be gentle — if the whole package is just realism held by inertia with a series of ad hoc retreats bolted on, say so.
