Next month I'm teaching a philosophy of religion unit on divine hiddenness, and I want to screen Bergman's Winter Light (1963) rather than assign another paper. Help me build the discussion around it.

Here's the reading I'm tempted to argue for, so you can pressure-test it: Jonas Persson, the fisherman terrified by the news that China is developing the atomic bomb, comes to the pastor seeking some word from God and instead gets Tomas's confession that he has no faith — and Jonas goes home and shoots himself. Jonas looks like a textbook case of nonresistant nonbelief: a man who wants to believe, who does what a believer in his world would do to reach God, and who receives nothing but silence, at the cost of his life. If Schellenberg is right that a perfectly loving God would ensure no one ends up in that position, the film appears to dramatize the argument from hiddenness at its most brutal: the seeker's silence-problem is inherited by the very minister supposed to mediate God, so the hiddenness is structural, not a matter of Jonas's pride or inattention. And Algot the sexton, near the end, seems to offer the theological counterweight when he suggests that the worst of the Passion was not the physical suffering but God's own silence on the cross — as though hiddenness were not evidence against God's love but a divine attribute, something God has always practiced.

I want you to tell me where that reading works and where it cheats. Tomas's own nonbelief looks resistant rather than nonresistant — anger at God is not longing for God, or is it? And what do I do with Märta, the warm, untroubled atheist whose loving family managed fine without religion: does her case strengthen the hiddenness argument, or does her final turn toward prayer undercut it? Does the ending — Tomas holding the service anyway, starting the Sanctus with a congregation of two — answer the problem or merely evade it? And is there something the film captures that the analytic literature misses — say, that hiddenness can be transmitted pastorally, that the seeker is destroyed by a believer's doubt — or am I romanticizing what Bergman, who at that point was working out God's silence in his own terms, actually put on screen?

Also flag where my framing is confused: if you think the Schellenberg apparatus distorts the film — if this is a film about faith's collapse rather than about whether God exists — say so directly rather than making my reading fit. I'd rather rework the session than teach a misreading. Point me to the specific scenes I should build the discussion around, and note where your interpretation leans on a contested reading of either the film or the argument.
