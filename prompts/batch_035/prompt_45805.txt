Writing group got heated last night and I was asked to referee and honestly couldn't. I'm a novelist, and my current draft has a six-year-old character, Bea, whose family never talks about death but performs all the rituals around it — black clothes, casseroles, lowered voices. After the grandmother dies, Bea uses the word "dead" correctly in every sentence, but when her mum tries to actually explain what it means, Bea just repeats the word back at her.

James (1) said Bea understands death as well as any six-yearogen does: understanding a word is being able to go on with it, take part in the practices around it, and Bea does that, so that's what her understanding consists in. Priya (2) said that can't be right — she's learned the moves, like a parrot that says "who's a pretty boy" at the right moments; genuine understanding is grasping what the word picks out, the fact itself, and Bea shows no sign of that. James (3) said if you demand an inner grasp you can never check it — we credit understanding in each other from outward behaviour, there's no other access, so his standard is the only workable one. Priya (4) said that proves too much: by that argument a chatbot understands death whenever it outputs the right sentences, and she isn't prepared to say that. James said the difference is Bea can go on in new situations and a chatbot just recombines — that's the real line. Priya said then the line is flexibility and novelty-handling, not participation in the rituals, so he's quietly swapped his story midway.

They left it there and looked at me. Can you go through it step by step and say who's right about what? And one thing I'd like your view on, since I invented Bea and her "inner life" is only whatever I write it to be — does that make the whole question about her somehow different from the question about a real child? Wittgenstein got name-dropped a few times and none of us had read him properly, so if he genuinely bears on this, tell me where.
