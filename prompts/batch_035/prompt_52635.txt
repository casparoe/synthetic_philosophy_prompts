I have spent some years teaching an undergraduate course on causation, and each year I find that my own settled opinions on causation by omission are less settled than I pretend in the seminar room. I have written out my verdicts on a set of cases below, and I should like to know whether they trace to a single principled account, or whether they merely reflect a patchwork of duty, counterfactual dependence, and salience that will not knit together. I would ask you to reconstruct the principle, if there is one, tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and say what it predicts on a case I have deliberately left unresolved.

The cases, with my verdicts:

1. A gardener is engaged and paid to water the plants while the owner travels; he never comes; the plants die. His omission caused their death.
2. Several thousand other residents of the town, each physically able to walk over and water them, also never did. None of them caused the plants' death.
3. A nurse forgets to administer a prescribed evening dose; the patient dies that night. The nurse's omission caused the death.
4. A passing physician, fully competent to administer the same dose, notices the situation but walks on. He did not cause the death; he merely failed to prevent it.
5. Two river-keepers are each responsible for opening a sluice gate after heavy rain; either gate alone would have drained the floodwater; both stay at home; the village floods. Both of their omissions caused the flood.
6. An uncle promised to drive his eight-year-old nephew home from school, forgot, and the boy walked and was struck by a car. The uncle's forgetting caused the injuries.
7. A neighbour had, without any promise or arrangement, driven the boy home every day for a term; one day she did not, for no reason at all; the boy walked and was struck. Here I find myself resisting the word 'cause', even though the counterfactual is exactly as in case 6, and I confess I cannot say why.

I take cases 1 and 2 to be the sort of thing every theory must explain, and I take the pair 6 and 7 to be the pair I find hardest to reconcile: if the neighbour's omission did not cause the injuries, it is hard to see what beyond the promise does the work in case 6, and if the promise does that much work, I should like to know why duty attaches so much more causal weight than the settled, reasonable expectation the boy had in case 7 — a difference, I note, that would not exist in cases of positive action, where a promised push and a habitual push knock me over equally.

So: is there one account — whether of counterfactuals restricted by norms, of default-deviation, of responsibility, or something else — that yields all seven verdicts? If not, which verdicts must be surrendered, and in what order? And please tell me what your best account says about the case I have been circling: a bystander sees a swimmer in difficulty, but another bystander is already calmly dialling the emergency services, and our bystander walks on; the call turns out to be a prank. Did the walker's omission cause the drowning? I genuinely do not know, and I should like your principled answer, not my unprincipled hesitation.
