Longtime lurker in a group chat that argues about education policy, and we've gotten into a loop I can't get out of. One guy (did econ, now does hiring) insists that a college degree is basically a peacock's tail: it's honest precisely because it's expensive and, on his telling, mostly useless — anyone can claim they're smart and hardworking, but only the smart and hardworking can afford to sit through four years of it, so the cost does the work that the credentials can't. He leans on the peacock picture hard: the tail is a handicap that only a genuinely fit peacock can carry, the interviewer is the peahen choosing among tails, and the whole thing is an equilibrium that nobody designed. From this he concludes that if signaling explains most of the college wage premium, college is mostly social waste — burning resources to sort people who could have been sorted more cheaply — and that anything that lowers the cost of college (subsidies, online degrees) just weakens the signal without producing anything real.

My roommate is an evo bio grad student and says the analogy is doing way more work than anyone in the chat realizes, and that if you actually check it piece by piece the anti-college conclusion doesn't obviously fall out. She mentioned a few things: that in the biological version the signal has to be differentially costly depending on the quality being advertised, not just expensive; that the peahen's interests and the employer's interests line up in some ways and not others (the peahen shares genes with the offspring, the employer never shares anything with the hire); that in the economics version the "equilibrium" can be pooling or separating and only one of those sorts anyone; and something about how the cost of the tail in biology is a fitness cost paid in the same currency as the benefit, which tuition isn't. I only half followed her, honestly.

So: can you give me the full correspondence table — what plays the role of the tail, the peahen, the cost, the fitness, the heritability/inheritance of quality, the evolutionary dynamics — and then go through where the mapping breaks down? For each disanalogy, say whether it actually matters for his conclusion or whether it's just pedantry. And then the part I care about most: does the "college is wasteful" conclusion survive once you take the disanalogies seriously, or was the peacock tail carrying it the whole way? If there's a better analogy for credentialing than the handicap principle one, I'd like to hear it — my roommate kept muttering something about whether diplomas are more like a marathon time than a tail, but wouldn't explain herself.
