take the july crisis of 1914 and use it as a stress test for the covering-law vs narrative debate in philosophy of history. i know the basic outline -- sarajevo, the blank check, the austrian ultimatum, mobilization timetables, everyone in by august -- so no need to re-narrate the whole thing for me. what i want is this: first, construct the strongest candidate for a "law" that the july crisis could be an instance of. something like: multipolar alliance systems + rigid mobilization schedules + a great power committed to a local war = general european war. spell out what the antecedent conditions would actually have to be for it to count as a proper covering law (hempel-style, deductive or at least inductive support), not just a description with the word "law" stapled on.

then run the coverage test, because this is where i think the whole thing wobbles: the 1908 bosnian annexation crisis and the 1911 agadir crisis look like they satisfy most of the same antecedent conditions -- alliances, timetables, brinkmanship, a local grievance -- yet nobody mobilized and nobody got a general war. so either your candidate law needs extra conditions smuggled in to rule those cases out (at which point you're retro-fitting, basically fitting the law to the one case it was designed to explain), or it predicts war in cases where war didn't happen. tell me honestly which problem you think is worse.

then do the flip side: say what narrative explanation actually buys you here that the law can't. i have a suspicion it's something about the counterfactual structure -- the july crisis feels like it turned on things like grey's hesitations, the serbian partial acceptance of the ultimatum, russia's earlier mobilization decision, stuff that seems like it could have gone the other way, and narrative can track that sensitivity while a law kind of has to treat the outcome as what the conditions produce. but i also worry narrative explanation overfits in its own way: if any story can be told about how the crisis "could have" de-escalated, isn't that just as unfalsifiable as a bad law? end by telling me whether this episode is an argument that covering-law explanation just fails for history, or that we've been strawmanning it, or something in between like "laws work for some questions about 1914 and not others." be specific about which questions.
