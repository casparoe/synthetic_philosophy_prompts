I'm a science and ideas journalist drafting a feature about a question I've become preoccupied with while reporting on machine olfaction — labs that build electronic noses by, in effect, breeding them. Rather than programming rules for "smoke" or "spoiled milk," engineers run populations of sensor arrays against training samples, keep the variants whose alarm outputs correlate with the target odour, and let the selection process iterate until the device reliably chirps at the right things. The result is an artifact whose internal structure has a selection history even though nothing in it evolved in anything like a biological sense.

My draft essay, in outline, runs as follows. Philosophers who want to naturalize mental content — to explain how a physical state can be *about* something without smuggling in unexplained mentality — have often placed selection at the heart of the story. A state carries content because it is the sort of state that was selected for indicating a particular condition: the honeybee's waggle dance indicates nectar because indicating nectar is what dances of that type were for. So far, so familiar. My provocation is that machine-olfaction-style devices look like a clean test case for this picture, and they expose a problem that biological cases obscure.

The problem, as I develop it, is what I've been calling in my notes the "two selectors" worry. In a device bred by engineers, there are two selection processes operating on the same internal states: the external one, in which the engineers cull variants that fail to alarm on target odours, and an internal one, in which the device's own reinforcement circuitry strengthens the connections that have recently produced rewarded alarm behaviour. When the internal selector diverges from the external one — say the device is deployed in a pepper-processing plant, its internal circuitry re-weights toward capsaicin, and it begins chirping at chilli — it is genuinely indeterminate whether the alarm state has changed its content (it now means "chilli," per the internal selector) or retained its content and gone systematically false (it still means "smoke," per the external selector). I argue that both answers are equally sanctioned by selection-based accounts, and that the intuitions we lean on to break the tie — the designers' intentions, what the consumers downstream of the alarm do with it — are exactly the resources those accounts were meant to replace with something purely naturalistic.

I then consider a rejoinder, which I take seriously rather than as a foil: that the divergence case is an artifact of reading "selection" too thinly, and that once we attend to what a state is *consumed for* by the larger system, the indeterminacy dissolves. If the alarm state's only consumer is a mechanism that shuts down the conveyor belt, and that mechanism was itself selected for by the external selector, then the external reading wins; content follows the consumer, not the producer. My worry about this rejoinder — and here I feel on least steady ground — is that it seems to push the indeterminacy one level up rather than dissolving it, because the device's re-weighted circuitry is also a consumer of its own sensory inputs, and the engineers, who are the ultimate consumers, have intentions about both the device and the plant it operates in.

I'd be grateful for your comments on the draft, on three fronts in particular. First, accuracy: does the "two selectors" problem, as I've framed it, genuinely cut against selection-based approaches to content, or am I redescribing a challenge those views already handle — for instance, by appeal to proper functions of whole cooperating systems? I want to be fair to that literature, but I don't want to concede so much that my hook evaporates. Second, depth: is the engineered-artifact case doing real philosophical work here, or is it just a colourful illustration of an issue that arises anyway for acquired biological functions — a predator-tracking circuit that drifts onto tracking rustling grass? If the drift case suffices, my editor will ask why the piece needs the lab-breeding angle at all, and I'd want an answer. Third, and most importantly: does my treatment of the consumer-based rejoinder hold up? I have the uneasy sense that I've gestured at a regress without showing that the regress is vicious, and I'd welcome either a diagnosis of where the rejoinder fails or a frank verdict that it succeeds.
