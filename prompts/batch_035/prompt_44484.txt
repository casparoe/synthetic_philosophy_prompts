I write a column on science and ideas, and I keep running into Hayek's old line that prices are information. It surfaces most often in pieces about the socialist calculation debate — the twentieth-century argument between Mises and Hayek on one side and Oskar Lange and his followers on the other about whether a planned economy could allocate resources rationally without market prices. But lately I've noticed it being redeployed in a newer argument: the claim that whatever was true of Soviet planners with their punch cards, the era of big data, logistics-superstar firms that internally coordinate millions of products, and possibly AI-driven planning changes the picture. Twice in the past month I've watched someone end that discussion by intoning that prices are information, as though the sentence itself settles it. The underlying source, as I understand it, is Hayek's 1945 essay "The Use of Knowledge in Society," where he writes that "the knowledge of the circumstances of which we must make use never exists in concentrated or integrated form but solely as the dispersed bits of incomplete and frequently contradictory knowledge which all the separate individuals possess," and describes the economic problem as "a problem of the utilization of knowledge which is not given to anyone in its totality." But the one-liner people actually repeat has come loose from all of that machinery.

Before I write about this, I want to be clear on what the slogan could actually be claiming, because I suspect it trades on an ambiguity. Lay the readings out as a menu — from the near-trivial to the genuinely radical — with a verdict on each: which are platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, which are probably false. For instance, one reading might be the banal observation that prices carry news about scarcity and demand; another, that the price system is the uniquely efficient aggregator of dispersed, tacit, and time-sensitive knowledge; a third, something about the knowledge itself being impossible to centralize in principle, not merely hard to collect; and there may be others you can identify. For each reading, say whether the person deploying it against the "big data changes everything" position is actually entitled to it.

In particular, tell me which readings would genuinely survive the objection that modern information technology can pool and process staggering amounts of fine-grained data, and which would quietly depend on contestable assumptions — e.g., that the relevant knowledge is tacit or embodied in skills and local circumstances rather than codifiable, that it is generated on the spot and expires too quickly to be transmitted upward, or that aggregation loses something no amount of processing power can recover. I'd like the whole thing worked out carefully enough that I can cite the distinctions, without attribution or quotation, in a column of my own. Please do not give me a reading list; the analysis itself is what I need.
