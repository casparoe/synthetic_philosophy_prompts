here's the set:

1. What's good for a person is what they would want for themselves if they were fully informed and reasoning well — the informed-desire idea of well-being.
2. I'm strongly anti-paternalism. A doctor should respect a patient's actual refusal of treatment even when the patient's fully-informed self would clearly have accepted it. What matters for consent is the actual person's say-so, not the idealized self's.
3. Idealization should only ever correct beliefs, never values. If you let the procedure improve someone's tastes, then the extrapolated self is basically a different person, and the whole thing looses contact with whose good we were supposed to be tracking.
4. But I also think some sets of values are just worse than others in a way that isn't only missing information — a committed racist's preferences aren't a badly-informed version of decent ones, they're bad at a deeper level than that.
5. On AI alignment I'm drawn to coherent extrapolated volition: build the AI so it satisfies what humanity would want under idealized conditions, which keeps the programmers' own values out of it and seems like the neutral option.

Order them by severity instead of just listing everything, and for each real tension tell me what would have to give and roughly what it costs to give it up.
