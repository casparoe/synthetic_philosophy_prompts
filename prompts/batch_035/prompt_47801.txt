The midterm question was:

"Mencius and Xunzi agree that anyone can become a sage and that the ritual tradition (li) is central to a flourishing life, yet they famously disagree about human nature (xing). Explain how each thinker's view of xing shapes his account of how moral cultivation works. Then explain how each would account for the same two observed facts: that people frequently behave badly, and that moral self-improvement is hard even for those who sincerely attempt it. Conclude by arguing, with reasons, which of the two offers the better explanation of the difficulty of cultivation."

The student's answer, reproduced in full:

"Mencius held that human nature is good (xing shan); Xunzi held that it is bad (xing e). This single disagreement drives their entire philosophy of cultivation.

For Mencius, we are born with the four sprouts (siduan): incipient tendencies toward compassion, shame, deference, and discernment of right and wrong. These are proto-virtues — the sprout of compassion grows into ren, the sprout of shame into yi, and so on. His evidence is the child at the open well: anyone who suddenly sees a child about to fall in feels alarm and distress, immediately and without calculation. Since we possess innate knowledge of good and evil from birth, Mencius's picture of cultivation is one of protection rather than construction. The sprouts develop into full virtues unless something interferes, and what interferes is the environment: hence the parable of Ox Mountain, a hill once forested, stripped bare by axes and grazing until passersby conclude it was never wooded — just as people conclude that ordinary folk were never good. Bad behavior is therefore always a kind of damage, and the difficulty of self-improvement lies in damaged surroundings, not in any resistance within us.

Xunzi, by contrast, holds that human nature is bad in essentially Hobbes's sense: we are by nature egoistic, and absent political order life would be a war of all against all. Since nature provides nothing to work with, cultivation is artifice (wei). Raw material must be reshaped from outside, as a carpenter steams and bends crooked wood straight, or as a potter forms a vessel from clay. Ritual (li), invented by the sage kings, is the tool of that reshaping: it channels desires so they can be satisfied in an orderly fashion. On this view bad behavior needs no special explanation — it is simply untutored nature — while moral improvement is difficult because it runs against the grain.

The two also differ on ritual itself. For Mencius, ritual is at best scaffolding: if the sprouts are genuine, li is a set of external conventions a cultivated person could keep or drop at will. For Xunzi, ritual is the very substance of morality, its indispensable instrument.

Finally, Xunzi better explains why cultivation is difficult. Mencius owes us an account of failure: if the tendency toward good is innate, its widespread defeat is mysterious, and the Ox Mountain story explains environmental damage but not why people so rarely repair themselves. Xunzi faces no comparable puzzle, since difficulty is his default condition and only improvement requires explanation."
