I've been a fixture of the rationalist corner of the internet for well over a decade — I worked through the Sequences back when the decision-theory threads were the liveliest thing on the forum, and possible-worlds talk has seeped into my thinking to the point that I phrase ordinary regrets in terms of how nearby worlds go. A friend from my local meetup goes further than the rest of us: he's a genuine modal realist in the mold of David Lewis, and he holds that I exist in exactly one world. When I say I could have been a sailor, he tells me, what's true is that in some other concrete world a person relevantly similar to me — my counterpart — is a sailor. He insists this is a substantive doctrine, worlds being concrete and spatiotemporally disjoint, and emphatically not a notational variant of the classical transworld-identity view on which the very same person, me, exists in many worlds.

To needle him, I constructed an argument that concludes his theory secretly collapses into the classical one. The trouble is that the conclusion now seems to prove too much: if it's right, then the long war between the Lewis camp and the transworld-identity camp was a squabble about grammar, which I find incredible. The argument:

(1) Every truth is made true by how things are, and how things are consists in particular things having properties and standing in relations.
(2) The claim that I could have been a sailor is a truth about me specifically — it attributes a possibility to me, the person, not to a cast of substitutes.
(3) On counterpart theory, that claim is true just in case some other world contains a relevantly similar person, my counterpart, who is a sailor; that is the whole analysis, and on modal realism the contents of all the worlds are all the contents there are.
(4) A truth about a particular thing must be made true by facts involving that very thing. Facts about stand-ins won't do: that my doppelgänger is in Vienna does not by itself make it true that I am in Vienna, however similar he is.
(5) By (3), the only person on the scene when the modal truth gets made is the counterpart; by (2) and (4), the person on the scene must be me. So the counterpart is me, and the counterpart relation turns out to be identity stretched across worlds.
(6) Therefore counterpart theory, followed to its conclusion, delivers transworld identity: I do exist in many worlds, and the similarity relation was a disguise for the very doctrine Lewis built his system to avoid.

I can't accept (6), but I also can't locate the failure between (3) and (5). I'd rather keep (4), since the truthmaker intuition feels load-bearing in the rest of my thinking, but I want to know what that preference costs me. Which step do you reject, why did it look fine going in, and what is the price of rejecting it? Please keep your answer under 400 words, and open by naming the single step you'd give up.
