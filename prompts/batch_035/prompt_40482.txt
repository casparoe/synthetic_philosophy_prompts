My apartment has a recurring thermostat dispute, and at a dinner party last weekend two of my guests, Maya and Jonah, spent the better part of an hour arguing about whether it can even be resolved on the merits. For context: I keep the place at 62 degrees and my roommate wants 70, and no compromise temperature has stuck. Maya's opening position was that there is no fact of the matter about whose situation is worse. Each of us has a private scale of comfort and discomfort, she said, and while "today is worse than yesterday, for me" makes perfectly good sense, "the cold bothers me more than the heat bothers him" is strictly meaningless — two intrapersonal rulers with no shared unit. Jonah answered that we make exactly these comparisons all the time, and not arbitrarily: triage nurses rank patients' pain, and everyone judges that a broken arm is worse than a stubbed toe for whoever has it, which only makes sense if intensity rankings transfer across persons.

Maya conceded that coarse cross-person comparisons work in extreme cases, grounded in shared physiology, but argued that they don't add up to anything useful. Setting a thermostat, or deciding whether to tax many people a little to relieve a few people's severe suffering, requires cardinal comparisons — how much worse, not just which is worse — and for that the two scales would have to be calibrated against each other, which nobody has ever done. Jonah then offered what he called a choice-based way out: maybe comparisons can be elicited through decisions rather than introspection. If I had to undergo one of two afflictions tonight, knowing everything about how both sufferers react to them, my choice between the afflictions would reveal which one is objectively worse, and nothing about that choice seems meaningless. Maya replied that this only reveals what I anticipate on my own scale: my guess about how bad someone else's migraine feels is calibrated against my own past headaches, not hers, so the thought experiment manufactures an answer rather than discovering one. Jonah's last word was that practical life cannot wait for calibration — the thermostat gets set either way, medicines get funded or refused — so "no fact of the matter" functions as nothing more than cover for whoever happens to have the power to decide.

They eventually turned to me to referee, and I honestly couldn't. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move rather than just declaring an overall winner. In particular: is Maya right that ordinal cross-person comparisons, even if real, can't support the kind of addition that policy choices seem to invite? Does Jonah's choice-based device do the work he claims for it, or does Maya's calibration objection sink it? And is Jonah's closing pragmatic point a genuine consideration against Maya's view, or a confusion between there being no fact of the matter and our being unable to act without pretending there is one? I'd also like to hear what, if anything, both of them are missing.
