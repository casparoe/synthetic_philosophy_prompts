Two classmates of mine got into it at the pub after our human rights seminar, over a hypothetical the professor sketched: a state rounds up the children of an indigenous minority into boarding schools where their language is banned, forcibly adopts a bunch of those kids out to majority families, tears down some of their places of worship, and disappears a few hundred community leaders and activists -- but there's no campaign of mass killing, and the group's overall population stays about the same.

Maya said this is genocide, full stop -- the Convention's own definition lists forcibly transferring children of the group to another group as a genocidal act, so you don't even need the killings, you need the intent plus one act from the list. Dan said that's way too fast: the Convention requires intent to *destroy* the group in whole or in part, and everything in the scenario aims at assimilation, not destruction -- the state wants the group gone *as a group* but not the people dead, and that's the line the drafters drew. Maya came back that Lemkin himself, when he coined the term, was talking about destroying a group's institutions, culture, language, and religion -- the physical stuff is just one prong -- and that the child-transfer clause only makes sense if destruction isn't purely biological. Dan answered that the word does its political work precisely because people hear "extermination," and if you stretch it to every forced assimilation you flatten the difference between a boarding school and a death camp, which is exactly what deniers want. Maya said no, the deniers are the ones who benefit from a hierarchy where only mass murder counts.

They asked me to referee and I genuinely couldn't. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right at each step -- not just who wins overall -- and also what both of them might be missing? I suspect they're each assuming "genocide" is one thing when it might be several.
