For years I've followed the rationalist blogosphere — Overcoming Bias in its heyday, LessWrong through its iterations, and the recurring debates there about whether Aumann's agreement theorem tells us anything about real groups of real people. That's why Sidney Lumet's Twelve Angry Men has always fascinated me: it's the canonical fictional case study of disagreement persisting under (mostly) shared evidence and then dissolving through discussion, and I want to read it through a specific formal lens rather than the usual vague celebration of deliberation. The lens is judgment aggregation: the literature that grew out of the doctrinal paradox in law — where a panel votes separately on premises and a conclusion, and majority voting on the premises can yield the opposite verdict from majority voting on the conclusion — and that Philip Pettit reframed as the discursive dilemma, the demonstration that a group of fully rational individuals can collectively hold an irrational set of judgments.

The film starts with a secret ballot on the conclusion alone: eleven guilty, one not guilty. From there, Juror 8 effectively forces the group to decompose the verdict into premise-level questions — the uniqueness of the knife, whether the old man could have reached the door in fifteen seconds, whether the woman across the street could see clearly without her glasses — and verdicts flip as premises fall. So here is what I want from a reading: does the film dramatize the discursive dilemma, dissolve it, or sidestep it? Some things to work through: with a unanimity rule and a beyond-reasonable-doubt standard, can the doctrinal paradox even arise, or does the film's voting structure change the problem into something else — perhaps a question of agenda control, since Juror 8 singlehandedly decides which premise gets examined next, and judgment aggregation theory itself says whoever sets the agenda steers the outcome? And what about the jurors whose votes are clearly not premise-driven at all — the one whose anger is really about his son, the one motivated by prejudice? The formal models assume each member votes on the basis of reasons; the film's richest material is arguably about members who don't. Does the lens illuminate them or break on them?

I'd like the reading to be faithful in both directions: don't force the film into an illustration of the theorem, and don't quietly distort the aggregation results to fit the drama. Say honestly where the film resists the reading — for instance, whether the ending, in which the group converges on unanimity rather than on any aggregated verdict, should be read as deliberation triumphing over aggregation or as the problem being deferred rather than solved. And close by telling me whether the film shows something the papers on judgment aggregation don't — whether watching eleven men reason badly, and then less badly, adds anything to the formal results about collective rationality, or whether my rationalist friends are right that a theorem already said it all. Context: I'm presenting this to a reading group on collective rationality, some of whom know the formal literature cold and others of whom have never heard of the discursive dilemma, so the reading should work for both.
