For thirty-one years I was a seismologist with the federal geological survey, and since retiring I have been working my way through the philosophy curriculum at the university near my home — first as an auditor, and now, to my mild astonishment, as the department's unpaid assistant for an undergraduate seminar called Rational Choice and Social Epistemology. The instructor is away at a conference and has left me to grade the take-home midterm, including a question on common knowledge that falls squarely in the part of the course I came to last. I refereed enough manuscripts in my day to trust my nose for a fudged step in an argument, but epistemic game theory is new territory for me, and I would rather be careful than confident. What I would like from you is a grade and a point-by-point evaluation of one student's answer — specific enough that I can lift your comments into the margins, and even-handed about what is right as well as what is wrong. The course has not asked students for citations, so the answer contains none, and your comments should not need them either.

The question read as follows:

"Question 4. In the muddy children puzzle, a group of children return from playing outside, some with mud on their foreheads; each child can see every forehead but their own, all are perfect reasoners, and all of this is common knowledge among them. Their father announces, in their presence: at least one of you has mud on your forehead. He then asks, each round, whether any child knows whether their own forehead is muddy, and the children answer simultaneously and truthfully. (a) When two or more children are muddy, every child already knows, before the father speaks, that at least one child is muddy. What, then, does the public announcement contribute — at the first order of knowledge, and at higher orders? (b) A classmate of yours claims that common knowledge is nothing over and above mutual knowledge of every finite order: everyone knows E, everyone knows that everyone knows E, and so on without end. Does the puzzle support that gloss, or does it show that something more is going on — and if so, what work is done by the announcement's being public that iterated knowing alone does not do? (c) Two commanders can take a fortified town only by attacking simultaneously; either one attacking alone is doomed. Whether conditions are favorable is seen only by the first commander, whose messenger then sets out for the second; the delivery of any message automatically triggers a confirming message back the other way, and each delivery independently fails with a small probability ε. Explain what happens to the commanders' prospects of coordinating as ε becomes very small but remains positive, and what this shows about whether mutual knowledge of high but finite order is as good as common knowledge."

And here, transcribed exactly as the student wrote it, is the answer I have to grade:

"(a) With two or more muddy children the father's announcement is a formality at the level of facts: every child already sees a muddy forehead, so it tells no child anything he did not already know. Its indispensable role is anchoring the induction in the degenerate case: if exactly one child is muddy, that child sees no mud at all, and the announcement tells him he is the muddy one, so he can step forward on the first round. Thereafter the children's silence does all the work. A muddy child sees k − 1 muddy foreheads, reasons that the induction would have resolved matters by round k − 1 were he clean, and so steps forward at round k. The clean children already know exactly which children are muddy, so the rounds are informative only to the muddy children; the timing of the resolution does not matter to them.

(b) The classmate's gloss is right, and the puzzle shows it working. Common knowledge is nothing more than the infinite hierarchy of mutual knowledge — everyone knows E, everyone knows that everyone knows E, and so on — and in the standard possible-worlds treatment these two are provably the same, so nothing is being papered over. The father's announcement delivers the first level, and each round of public silence, observed by all, delivers the next: the puzzle is the hierarchy being climbed level by level, round by round.

(c) In the email game, conditional on the chain of confirmations having reached depth n, the commanders have mutual knowledge of the favorable state to roughly order n. With ε small, the expected depth of the chain is enormous, so with overwhelming probability they achieve hundreds of levels of mutual knowledge — and by (b), that is common knowledge for all practical purposes, minus a vanishing tail. Each additional confirmation matters only with probability ε, so a commander who declines to attack on such grounds is abandoning a near-certain gain to avoid a negligible risk; as ε approaches zero the commanders' situation converges to the complete-information case, and they coordinate. The discontinuity that is sometimes claimed for this game seems to me an artifact of insisting that the chain terminates at some definite finite depth; in expectation it runs arbitrarily deep. Aumann's electronic mail game therefore illustrates the same moral as his agreement theorem: two agents with common priors cannot disagree once their information has been exchanged, and the back-and-forth of the confirmations is precisely such an exchange."

The answer reads fluently, and I would ask you to grade the reasoning rather than the prose: in my old profession we learned to treat elegant chains of inference no differently from clumsy ones.
