I'm a novelist, four chapters into a book about a Montana ranching family, and the research has unsettled my own habits along with my characters'. As best I can state it, here is where I've landed: (1) I've replaced chicken with beef in my own kitchen, on the ground that one steer's death is divided among hundreds of meals while a single chicken dinner costs nearly a whole bird; (2) I don't think death is a serious harm to a creature with no projects and no sense of its own future — what matters is how much suffering a life contains; (3) I believe most wild animals' lives contain more suffering than happiness, and that wild animal suffering is the largest single mass of disvalue in the world; (4) I give annually to a land trust that keeps open grassland from being developed; and (5) I buy only pasture-raised beef and avoid soy and corn on the theory that plowing and pesticide kill wild animals, whereas well-managed rangeland lets them live alongside the herd. Before I let these convictions harden into the moral geography of the novel, I'd like an audit. Which of these commitments genuinely pull against each other, which only appear to, and what is the cheapest way to restore consistency? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and for each genuine one, say which commitment would have to give and what giving it up would cost the rest of the package. Please respond in American English.
