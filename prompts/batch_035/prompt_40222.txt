My roommate has an argument that expected value reasoning eats itself, and I can't find the hole in it.

(1) Rational choice under uncertainty is EV maximization: pick the act with the highest sum of probability-times-value over outcomes. (2) For any probability p > 0, however small, and any act you might do, there is a possible outcome of that act with enough value that p times that value beats the EV of any ordinary alternative. (3) Every outcome that isn't strictly logically impossible deserves some nonzero credence -- assigning exactly zero to something not ruled out by logic is dogmatism. (4) So for any act A, you can name a possible payoff of A -- say A somehow triggers the creation of a simulation with 10^40 flourishing lives -- such that, given any nonzero credence in that payoff, A has higher EV than anything sensible you could do instead. (5) Therefore EV maximization, taken seriously, ranks the fanatic gamble above studying for your exam, donating to bednets, whatever -- not occasionally but always, since for every option there's a more extreme possibility that dominates it. (6) And it's not even stable: for any gamble you finally pick, there's an even more fanatical one that outranks it, so EV maximization never actually issues in a choice. (7) So (1) is false.

He concludes we should scrap EV and go with something like satisficing, or just discount tiny probabilities to zero. My problem is I want to keep (1) -- it seems to follow from pretty basic axioms about preferences over gambles -- but I honestly can't tell whether the rot is in (3), or in the slide from "you can name a possible payoff" in (4) to "that payoff is in your expectation" in (5), or somewhere I haven't looked. Which step do I give up, why did it look fine, and what do I pay? And if he's right that the theory never issues in a choice, is that actually an objection, or does any decision theory have some tie-breaking problem like this at the edges?
