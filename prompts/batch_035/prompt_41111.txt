Every time I mention how depressing the news is someone hits me with "we live in the most peaceful time in human history," like that just ends the conversation. This weekend my brother-in-law deployed it to argue that worrying about violence is basically irrational — that it's a story my brain tells me because bad news gets clicks — and that we shouldn't be pouring more effort into violence reduction than we already do. I want to push back but I realize I don't actually know what the sentence even claims. Can you break down the different precise things it could be saying? Like: is it about deaths per capita or total deaths (if the population triples and the death rate halves, absolute deaths still go up — is that progress or not, and who decides the denominator)? Does "violence" mean war deaths, or homicide, or state violence, or domestic abuse (which was basically invisible to the record until recently), or what people call structural violence — and could someone who counts factory farming be making the opposite claim come out true? What's even the comparison class: medieval Europe, the 20th century with its world wars, prehistoric foragers? And for each reading, say whether it's the kind of thing there could be a fact of the matter about, or whether it bottoms out in a value judgment about how you weigh a death against a humiliation against a life spent in fear. Then tell me which combinations would actually deliver my brother-in-law's conclusion (that my worry is irrational and we're doing enough) versus which ones only get him "cheer up" — I suspect he's smuggling from a statistical claim to a normative one. A menu I can bring to Thanksgiving, please, not a lecture.
