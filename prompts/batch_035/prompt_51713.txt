Someone in a decision theory reading group told me there's a theorem (I.J. Good, I think?) that a Bayesian should never turn down free information: the expected value of knowing is always at least zero, since in the worst case you ignore it and do what you were going to do anyway. I want to believe it, but my gut verdicts on actual cases refuse to cooperate. Here they are — I'd like the principle behind them, or to be told which verdicts have to go.

— Facing a choice between two surgical options, I'd pay alot for a second opinion from a specialist. Obviously right.
— My friend is at risk for an incurable late-onset disease. There's a test, but there's nothing she could do differently with the result — no treatment exists, and she's already made her choices about kids and career. She's declined the test, and I think she's right: not knowing buys her decades without dread hanging over every dropped spoon.
— Same friend, different disease: this one you can slow down a lot if you catch it early. Here I'd nag her until she takes the test — refusing seems almost irrational.
— I avoid reviews and trailers for movies I've already decided to see. Spoilers would ruin it. Feels obviously right, even though structurally it's the same as the first disease case: nothing I'd do differently.
— A friend offers to tell me, before I drink them, which of the two glasses in front of me came from the $90 bottle and which from the $12 one (same wine, or close enough). Right now I'd enjoy both. After being told, the cheap one will taste worse to me. I'd pay him not to tell me — but I also suspect I'm being a baby about it, since the wine doesn't change.
— A lawyer says she never asks clients whether they actually did it, so she can defend them wholeheartedly in good conscious. I used to think this was a dodge; now I think the not-knowing is doing real moral work for her.

And the one I keep flipping on: a chess player who won't study his opponent's past games before a match, on the grounds that it clutters his head with patterns. Sometimes this sounds like discipline (playing the board, not the man), sometimes like an aesthetic handicap dressed up as strategy.

My first stab at a principle was "information is worth having exactly when it could change what you do" — but that gets the two disease cases exactly backwards, and the wine one wrong in the other direction. So don't give me a menu of options: commit to the best principle you can construct, even if it needs two or three clauses, say plainly which of my verdicts it overturns, and use it to predict a new case — specifically, whether I should want to know, in advance, the exact date of my own death, assuming the knowing itself is the only thing that would change.
