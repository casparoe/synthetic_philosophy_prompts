Something I've been chewing on: genetic counseling has this whole edifice around the "right not to know." If you're at risk for Huntington's — incurable, no prevention, nothing the result could change — counselors will walk you through everything and then fully support never taking the test, and if your status shows up incidentally in some other labwork, the norm is not to disclose it unless you ask. Same with APOE4 and Alzheimer's risk: for a long time doctors mostly just didn't offer it. But here's my puzzle: Good's theorem says an expected-utility maximizer should never pay to avoid free information — its value is always nonnegative, because in the bad case you just do whatever you were going to do anyway. And medicine elsewhere is obsessed with the opposite: informed consent basically forces information on people. So what's the implicit theory the right-not-to-know practice is running on? Does it presuppose that anticipatory states (dread, hope) are themselves bearers of welfare, so a belief-state can be bad for you even when it never feeds a decision? Or that patients are irrational and won't be able to ignore what they learn? Or something about autonomy over your own epistemic condition that has nothing to do with decisions? Reconstruct the view of information and autonomy the practice actually needs, say whether it's in real tension with informed consent or whether one consistent picture licenses both, and point out where the practice is underdetermined — i.e. which parts of it several rival theories could equally well explain.
