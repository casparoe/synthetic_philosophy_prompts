I was recently appointed as a lay member of the clinical ethics committee at our local trust, not long after sitting at my mother's bedside while her clinicians withdrew ventilation with her consent, and I would like to sort out what I actually believe before real cases reach me. My moral philosophy is rusty — one undergraduate module, many years ago — so I shall simply report my gut verdicts and ask what principle, if any, captures them.

The cases:

1. A ventilator-dependent patient, fully competent, asks that the ventilator be withdrawn; the team does so and she dies within the hour. Permissible, clearly.
2. A patient with end-stage disease develops pneumonia; the team, with the family's agreement, does not start antibiotics, and she dies. Permissible.
3. Much like (2), except that the family asks for antibiotics and the team declines, judging that they would merely prolong a dying process. I still think the team may decline, but this feels considerably less comfortable than (1), even though switching off a ventilator is surely a more active thing to do than not reaching for a chart.
4. A patient with an inoperable tumour is given escalating doses of morphine which the consultants agree will probably shorten her life, in order to control her pain. Fine: intended relief, foreseen death.
5. A consultant deliberately gives a massive overdose of morphine to a patient whose pain could have been controlled without it, with the express purpose of ending his life. Not permissible.
6. A patient with motor neurone disease, fully competent, decides to stop eating and drinking; the palliative team support her with mouth care and analgesia until she dies twelve days later. Permissible — and the nurses moistening her lips are not, it seems to me, killing her.
7. A patient in a minimally conscious state, having left no advance decision, is tube-fed through a gastrostomy placed before her condition deteriorated. The family, supported by clinicians who judge the treatment futile and burdensome, ask for the tube to be removed; the team withdraws it and she dies over ten days or so. I believe this is permissible, but it strikes me as much harder than (1), and I cannot say why, given that the clinicians judge both treatments futile.

My working hypothesis is that the doing/allowing distinction is doing less work in my verdicts than either consent or intention — (5) is the only case in which I object to a positive act, and intention rather than activity seems to be what marks it out. But the pieces will not quite fit. Cases (6) and (7) both end in death from dehydration over days; if consent explains why (6) is easy and (7) is hard, I cannot see why (7) feels so much harder than (2), where nobody consented to anything either. And if it is the manner of death that troubles me about (7), then (6) should trouble me equally, and it does not.

So: is there one principle, or a small set of principles, that captures these verdicts, or close enough? Please state it precisely, motivate each clause by appeal to a specific case, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which one I ought to give up. Then tell me what it predicts about a case I have not yet decided: a man whose partner, dependent on a home ventilator after a progressive diagnosis, retains capacity and asks him to disconnect the machine himself, which he does, without any clinician present. I want to know whether my discomfort in that case is a residue of the old act/omission distinction that I should learn to ignore, or the part of the truth that my consent-plus-intention hypothesis has quietly thrown away.
