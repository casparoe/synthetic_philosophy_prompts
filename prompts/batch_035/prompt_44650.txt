I'm a physics grad student whose department has a philosophy reading group that's been arguing about whether spacetime is a thing or just a bookkeeping device for relations among bodies, and it's basically been going since Newton and Leibniz and we're not doing better. The bucket argument comes up every session: the water climbs the sides, so there's centrifugal effects, and Newton said rotation must be absolute since the rotation is not relative to the bucket walls. Our resident relationist always counters with Mach's line that the bucket only rotates relative to the distant masses, and that in an otherwise empty universe maybe the water wouldn't climb at all. Then someone brings up that GR has vacuum solutions where spacetime structure exists with no matter at all, and the relationist says fine, treat the metric field as just another relational player, not as a container. And then somebody mentions the hole argument and we all go home. My working hypothesis is that after 300 years this isn't really a physics dispute at all -- no observation could decide it -- but a dispute about what you're allowed to say exists, and each side quietly answers a different question (one about what the equations need, one about what there is). Is that right? Diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is using what the sides concretely say, tell me whether anything (an observation, a formulation of the theory, a conceptual distinction) could genuinely settle it or move it, and be honest if the answer is that we should just stop having this reading group.
