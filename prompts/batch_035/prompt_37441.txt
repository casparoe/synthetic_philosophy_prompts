I'm a second-year PhD student in political theory, gearing up for comprehensive exams, and a popular social-science book on surveillance that several people in my cohort swear by summarizes Foucault like this: his central idea is that knowledge is power, meaning that claims to truth are really claims to authority, and what we call objective facts are just the beliefs of whoever happens to be in charge; in his history of the prison he showed that modern societies replaced spectacular punishment with surveillance, and the panopticon, a prison design in which an unseen guard watches everyone, is his model for society as a whole, so that we all now police one another constantly, discipline having entirely replaced law; since power produces truth rather than merely repressing it, no belief is more or less true than any other; and because power is everywhere and held by everyone in equal measure, there is nothing outside power from which to resist it. My committee includes a Foucauldian, so I can't afford to absorb a distorted version. Please go through the summary claim by claim: which parts would a careful Foucault scholar accept, which are defensible simplifications of his local historical claims, and which does Foucault himself explicitly deny or argue against? In particular, I'd like to know whether the reading of power/knowledge as a reduction of truth to authority, and the reading of power being everywhere as making resistance impossible, are positions he held, positions he rejected, or conflations of two things he kept distinct -- and whether the book's further conclusion, that Foucault's framework leaves no room for normative critique of surveillance, follows from any accurate part of the summary.
