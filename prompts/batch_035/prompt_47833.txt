I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. A colleague who is deep into the free will literature sent me a summary of Galen Strawson's Basic Argument, taken from a popular philosophy book he's been reading, and I want to know how much of it I should trust. He's usually careful, but the book's framing struck me as stronger than what I understood the argument to be claiming. Here is the passage, quoted from his notes:

"Strawson proved that no one is ever morally responsible for anything. Since every choice you make flows from character traits you didn't choose, and those traits in turn trace back to factors outside your control, the buck never stops with you. Even if determinism turned out to be false, random quantum noise wouldn't make you responsible either. So the only way to be genuinely responsible would be to have literally created yourself — to have authored your own character from scratch — which is impossible. Strawson therefore concludes that blame and punishment have no rational basis and that a just society would stop holding people responsible altogether."

What I'd like is an audit of this summary. Go claim by claim and tell me: which parts would Strawson himself sign off on, which are compressions a defender would accept with a shrug, and which are misrepresentations of what the argument actually asserts or concludes? I'll flag the two things that most make me suspicious, so you can confirm or correct them.

First, my sense from other things I've read is that Strawson distinguishes between two different things one might mean by "responsibility" — something like ordinary accountability versus a much stronger, ultimacy-flavored sense — and that his conclusion is only about the strong sense, not the everyday one. If that's right, then the summary's opening line ("no one is ever morally responsible for anything") quietly drops a qualifier that does a lot of work. Second, I understand the argument is supposed to be neutral on whether determinism is true — it's supposed to show that ultimate responsibility is impossible whether or not our choices are determined — whereas the summary reads as if the non-existence of free choice is doing real argumentative work in the first step.

There's also a part of the argument the summary skips that I'd like you to fill in, because it's the part I find most interesting as an engineer: the regress. As I understand it, the core move is that to be truly responsible for what you do, you'd have to be truly responsible for the way you are (mentally speaking) in such a way that led you to act as you did; but to be responsible for the way you are, you'd have had to have chosen to become that way through earlier choices; and to be responsible for those choices, you'd have had to be responsible for the way you were at that earlier time; and so on, with no base case. To me this looks exactly like a proof by infinite regress — an argument that some recursively defined function is never actually grounded — and I'd like to know whether that analogy is apt or whether it flattens something important about how the regress is supposed to terminate in impossibility rather than in mere groundlessness.

Finally, the book apparently goes on to say that if Strawson is right, then retributive punishment is unjustified and criminal justice systems should be purely rehabilitative. Setting aside whether that's a good policy view on its own terms, does it follow from the parts of the summary that survive your corrections? Please distinguish what follows from the argument itself, what follows only with additional premises Strawson may or may not endorse, and what he has explicitly disclaimed — I've read that he doesn't think we could abandon blame and responsibility even if his argument is sound, and I want to know whether that's really his position or whether I'm misremembering a different philosopher, since I know his father also wrote a famous paper in this area and I may be crossing the streams.
