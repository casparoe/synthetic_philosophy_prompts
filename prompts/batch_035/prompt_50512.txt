You are grading a short-answer response for an upper-level undergraduate metaphysics exam. The exam question read as follows:

"According to Lewis's counterpart theory, possible worlds are isolated: nothing that exists in one world exists in any other. Explain how counterpart theory nonetheless manages to say something true and de re with a claim such as 'Dukakis could have won the 1988 election,' and contrast this treatment with the transworld identity alternative. Then present one objection to counterpart theory and assess whether the counterpart theorist has a good reply."

The student's answer reads:

"Counterpart theory, developed by David Lewis, holds that an individual exists in exactly one possible world and has no presence whatsoever in any other. So there is no man who is Dukakis in another world. When we say that Dukakis could have won the election, counterpart theory interprets this as follows: in some possible world other than ours, there is a counterpart of Dukakis — an individual who resembles Dukakis closely in the relevant respects — and that counterpart wins the election there. De re modality generally is handled the same way: Dukakis is essentially a human being iff every one of his counterparts across all worlds is human, and he is possibly a winner iff at least one counterpart wins.

The counterpart relation itself is a similarity relation between individuals in different worlds, and it works much like identity: since whoever most resembles Dukakis in a world is his counterpart there, each person has at most one counterpart per world, and the relation turns out to be an equivalence relation — reflexive, symmetric, and transitive — even though it relates individuals across worlds rather than within one.

On the transworld identity approach, favored by Kripke and Plantinga, modal claims are not about stand-ins at all. Dukakis himself, the very same man, exists in other possible worlds and wins in some of them. On this view, we do not stipulate what goes on in other worlds; rather, we discover it by qualitative resemblance, so that the worlds where Dukakis wins are the worlds most similar to ours except for the election's outcome.

One famous objection, due to Kripke, is that counterpart theory mislocates the modal fact. When we say that Dukakis could have won, we are not thinking of somebody else, however similar, winning in his place; we are thinking of Dukakis himself. Counterpart theory seems to convert a fact about Dukakis into a fact about strangers who happen to resemble him. Lewis's reply is that the possibility is genuinely Dukakis's own, because the counterpart relation is itself a relation of relevant similarity, so that what his counterparts do is the right measure of what he could have done.

It is also worth noting that the two approaches are notational variants of each other: any de re claim comes out true on one just in case it comes out true on the other. For example, both approaches agree that identity is necessary, so that if the Evening Star is the Morning Star, then necessarily the Evening Star is the Morning Star, and no version of counterpart theory can allow that Dukakis is distinct from himself in some world."
