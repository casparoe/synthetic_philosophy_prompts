I'm designing a seminar unit on how historians establish facts about the distant past, running from Herodotus, who routinely marks off what he saw with his own eyes from what he was merely told, and Thucydides, who opens his history by complaining that oral tradition inflates events into legend within a generation, through the nineteenth-century codification of the discipline into external criticism (is the artifact itself what it claims to be: provenance, chain of custody, material, script) and internal criticism (does the content cohere with everything else we know: anachronisms, consistency with independent testimony). To keep the seminar from feeling like a museum of antiquities, I want to test the apparatus against a modern case: the Hitler diaries affair of 1983, where the West German magazine Stern paid roughly nine million marks for sixty volumes it believed to be Hitler's private diaries, kept its source and the documents secret to protect the scoop, sold serialization rights abroad (the Sunday Times among the buyers), and had the historian Hugh Trevor-Roper pronounce the diaries genuine — a judgment he publicly reversed within days. The forger turned out to be Konrad Kujau, a Stuttgart memorabilia dealer working with, and partly around, the reporter who had brought the diaries in; and the decisive forensic testing of ink and paper was carried out only after publication had already been announced. As far as I can tell, almost nothing in the content was checked against what was already known until after the collapse.

Map the episode onto the apparatus, and be disciplined about the mapping: who counted as the source, what the authenticators were actually doing under each heading, and which branch of criticism was engaged at which point. I take it that internal criticism was largely bypassed — the secrecy that protected the scoop also meant few independent eyes were on the content — but I want to know whether that is an accident of this particular scandal or something the apparatus itself predicts, and what it predicts about cases where a document's evidentiary value depends on its being kept exclusive. Then run the analysis in the other direction, and here is where I most want your judgment: the classical apparatus seems built around an adversary who targets the document, with Valla catching the Donation of Constantine in an anachronism as the paradigm. But Kujau appears to have targeted the authenticators rather than the artifact — supplying whatever volume of material the buyer's enthusiasm demanded, producing work crude enough that even elementary external checks, once finally performed, unraveled it immediately, and letting Stern's own sunk costs, monopoly on the source, and fear of being scooped do the authenticating for him. Does the apparatus from Herodotus and Thucydides through Ranke have a place for a forger who aims at the authenticator's prior and incentives rather than at the document, or does the episode expose a blind spot the framework never anticipated? Where is the theory silent, please say so rather than forcing it to speak.

End your analysis with a single final line of the form \answer{...} containing exactly one of the following labels, reflecting your considered judgment of what the affair primarily was on your own analysis: external-criticism failure, internal-criticism failure, incentive failure, outside the framework.
