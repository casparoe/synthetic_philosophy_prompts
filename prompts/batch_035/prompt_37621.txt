I am a novelist, and for most of my working life I have accepted, without much examination, a piece of received wisdom that circulates through every workshop and craft essay: write interiority with confidence, because nobody can check it. The picture that usually accompanies this advice — my own teachers used it almost ritually — is that a person's experience of the world is like a painting hung in a private gallery to which the subject alone holds the key. The gallery is closed to all visitors; the subject, however, has lived with the painting for years, and whatever the subject reports about it counts as testimony from the only qualified witness. On this picture, a first-person narrator's account of how things taste, ache, or shine carries an authority that no description of outward behavior could ever match, and this is often offered as the reason first-person fiction can go where reportage cannot.

Lately my confidence in this picture has been shaken. I've been reading Daniel Dennett's 1988 essay "Quining Qualia," with its intuition pump about the two professional coffee tasters, Mr. Chase and Mr. Sanborn, whose job is keeping a brand's taste constant year after year. One of them has drifted from loving the coffee to finding it unbearable, and Dennett argues that no introspective act can settle whether his experience of the coffee changed while his judgments stayed fixed, or his judgments changed while his experience stayed fixed, or his memory of the earlier experience was quietly rewritten by the later ones. If that is right even for the subject's own current and remembered experience, the gallery analogy looks shaky in an uncomfortable way: not merely because the gallery is closed to visitors, but because the sole witness may not be qualified — his reports may be downstream effects of the very causes they purport to report. I am currently revising a novel that follows one woman's sensory life across sixty years, and I would like to know whether the special authority I have been claiming for her interior narration was ever really there.

Audit the private-gallery analogy as it does its work in my craft. Give me the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the gallery, the painting, the key, the witness, and (I suspect this is where it gets interesting) the memory of the painting — then lay out where the mapping breaks, separating the disanalogies that damage the workshop conclusion from those that don't, and say plainly whether the conclusion that first-person interiority has an unmatched authority survives once the breaks are on the table. Take seriously one complication my editor raised: in fiction, unlike life, the gallery arguably exists, because I write the painting into being — I can stipulate that my character's experience changed while her judgments did not, a stipulation the real coffee-taster cannot make. I honestly cannot tell whether that saves the analogy for my purposes or merely changes the subject, so tell me which, and if the analogy cannot be saved, give me the better analogy a novelist should be working with instead.
