I'm a novelist, and at a drinks thing last weekend two friends of mine got into a proper argument about a throwaway comment I made, and I've been thinking about it ever since because I honestly can't referee it myself. The comment was that my agent reckons there's maybe a 30% chance my current manuscript gets picked up by a publisher who'll actually push it, rather than letting it quietly die. My friend Maya, who's doing a stats PhD, pounced on that number, and my friend Jonas, who's a philosophy postdoc, defended me, and it went like this:

(1) Maya said the 30% is basically meaningless. Probability is about long-run frequency — the chance of heads is what you'd get over many tosses — and my manuscript is a one-shot event. It either finds a home or it doesn't. Saying it has a 30% chance is like asking what the probability is that a particular historical event happened.

(2) Jonas said that's far too narrow: the 30% is my agent's credence, her degree of belief, and it's perfectly meaningful because it pins down what bets she'd take — she'd sell a ticket that pays out if the book sells for anything less than fair odds on 30%. Subjective probabilities are as real as prices.

(3) Maya said betting odds tell you about the agent's psychology, not about the manuscript. You've just measured how confident she feels. The book itself contains no probability the way a die sort of does.

(4) Jonas said if that's right then enormous chunks of practical life are meaningless too — a surgeon telling a patient there's a 20% chance of a complication, a weather app saying 70% rain tomorrow. Nobody thinks the surgeon's number is just vibes.

(5) Maya said the surgeon and the weather forecaster are different, because those get their meaning from being members of a repeatable class — many similar surgeries, many similar weather days — and over many forecasts you can check whether it rained 70% of the times they said 70%. My manuscript gets one trial, so there's no calibration to be had, no class to average over.

(6) Jonas said okay, but which class, exactly? All debut novels? Debut novels in my genre? In my genre at my word count, by writers my age, submitted the same month? Every choice gives a different frequency and frequentism has no principled way to pick, so Maya's account doesn't actually deliver a meaning for the surgeon's number either — it just relocates the arbitrariness. Meanwhile the credence reading at least says something definite: here is a person, here is her evidence, here is what she'd stake on it.

(7) Maya said the reference class thing is a fair headache but not fatal — some classes are just more relevant than others, statisticians make these judgment calls all the time — and anyway her deeper point stands: credences are irrevocably personal. Two perfectly rational people with the same evidence could hold different numbers about my book, which shows they're tracking attitudes, not facts.

(8) Jonas said two thermometers can disagree without temperature being make-believe, and that if you share evidence and keep arguing you'd expect the numbers to converge — the divergence signals missing information between them, not that there's no fact being estimated.

They sort of ran out of steam around there and everyone refilled their glasses. Can you go through the exchange round by round and tell me who's right about what at each step — not just who wins overall? And if there's something both of them are missing that a third view (I've heard people talk about probabilities as tendencies built into the situation itself?) would say, I'd genuinely like to know, because as a novelist I spend my whole life making up numbers like that 30%.
