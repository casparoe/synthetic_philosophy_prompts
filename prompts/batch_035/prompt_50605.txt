I am a master's student in systematic theology, and in three weeks I defend a thesis arguing that Molinism — the account of providence descending from the Jesuit Luis de Molina — is the most satisfactory reconciliation of divine sovereignty with libertarian freedom. At last week's colloquium, the external examiner, a resolute Thomist, walked me into the following argument, and I have not found my way out since.

For context, stated as I understand the view: Molinism holds that God possesses, logically prior to the decree to create, middle knowledge — knowledge of all true counterfactuals of creaturely freedom, propositions of the form "were this agent placed in these circumstances, she would freely do this." These truths are contingent, are not willed or determined by God, and are not settled by the creatures themselves, who do not yet exist. God surveys them and then decrees a world. That, the Molinist claims, gives God meticulous providence without making him the author of sin or collapsing freedom into determination.

The examiner's argument:

(1) Prior to any decree, the complete set of true counterfactuals of creaturely freedom is fixed: for every possible agent and circumstance, it is already, timelessly, true how that agent would freely act.
(2) God, knowing these truths, decrees to create me in certain circumstances.
(3) Given the decree and the counterfactuals, it is not possible that I do otherwise than I in fact do: any world in which I act differently is either a world God could not have chosen given the counterfactuals he found true, or one he chose not to actualize.
(4) An act is free only if, at the moment of choosing, it is genuinely open to me to do otherwise.
(5) Therefore no act of mine is free. Molinism was constructed precisely to secure libertarian freedom against deterministic readings of providence, and it delivers fatalism instead: everything I will ever do is settled before I exist, by truths I had no hand in making and by a decree I did not vote on.

The step I do not trust is (3), but I cannot say exactly what is wrong with it. My advisor's first reply was that the counterfactual about me is true because of what I would freely do, not the other way around — the direction of explanation runs from my choice up to the truth, not from God's selection down to my choice. The examiner answered that this makes the truth "settled before you exist, settled by nothing you have done, and yet somehow yours." His positive claim is that a truth which is prior to my existence, not willed by God (since if God willed it, step (5) becomes even worse for me), and not grounded in my choice (since I do not yet exist to ground anything) is a truth with no ground at all, and that it would be more honest to deny such truths exist.

Here is what troubles me about that retreat: it seems to prove too much. Counterfactuals about creaturely freedom are not the only counterfactuals theism needs. If his grounding complaint shows that no counterfactual of creaturely freedom can be true, I do not see why it halts there — it appears to take out counterfactuals of divine freedom as well (had God created differently, had he not elected Abraham), and perhaps ungrounded counterfactuals generally, and I doubt he wants that conclusion.

So I have three questions, and I want the argument taken apart rather than a defense of my thesis:

First, which step do I reject? Is (3) an equivocation between what the counterfactuals settle as truth and what they determine as cause? Is (4) smuggling in a standard of freedom — the ability to do otherwise holding the entire prior order fixed — that the Molinist never accepted and perhaps no libertarian should? Or is the flaw in the examiner's truthmaker demand itself? Name the step, and explain why it looked fine.

Second, what does rejecting it cost? If I deny (4), have I genuinely preserved libertarian freedom, or traded it for something the Thomist could accept just as happily, in which case my thesis loses its reason for existing? If I deny the truthmaker principle, what else in his metaphysics goes with it? If I deny (1) outright and concede there are no true counterfactuals of freedom, what remains of providence that simple foreknowledge or timeless knowledge could not supply equally well?

Third, is my "proves too much" complaint about his grounding reply actually sound, or does the Thomist have a principled way to let unactualized counterfactuals about God's own freedom survive while the creaturely ones fall?

For what it is worth, I would most like to keep both my grip on (4) and Molinism itself; if I must lose one, tell me honestly which is cheaper, since I have three weeks to decide what my defense concedes.
