I'm a high school senior who became interested in philosophy of physics through popular science books, and for the past few months my brother — a second-year physics undergraduate — and I have been arguing about string theory. He says it isn't really science because it has never been tested and may never be; I've read that some physicists and philosophers defend its credibility using what they call non-empirical confirmation, the idea that a theory's standing can legitimately be raised by considerations other than successful predictions, such as the absence of any alternative research programme that comes close to unifying gravity with the rest of physics, the theory's unexpected explanatory coherence — the way pieces of mathematics developed for other purposes keep turning out to fit together in string theory when nothing required it — and the track record of earlier theories that were similarly untested for decades before being vindicated. My own credence that string theory, or something recognizably descended from it, will turn out to be on the right track is about 0.35; my brother puts his at 0.1 or lower, and nearly all of his weight sits on the single step "no empirical evidence, therefore no rational credence above bare possibility." I would like your own number, but what I really want is the decomposition behind it. Roughly, I think the terms should be something like: the probability that non-empirical considerations of this kind can legitimately raise a theory's credence at all, as opposed to merely rationalizing our fondness for beautiful mathematics; the probability that they apply to string theory in particular, given that its closest testable consequence — low-energy supersymmetry — has come to look less likely as colliders have found no superpartners; the probability that the string landscape, with its enormous number of possible vacua, makes the theory more testable in principle (because it at least predicts generic features) or less so (because it can accommodate almost anything); and the probability that "on the right track" is even a well-defined target, since string theory might end up true in its core claims about the world while being useless, or false as physics while permanently enriching mathematics. Assign a rough probability to each of these with one line of justification, show how they combine into your final credence, and identify which single term your answer is most sensitive to. Finally, please tell us what evidence would actually move the number — suppose the next thirty years of collider results and cosmological observations come back as quiet as the last thirty: does that drive the credence toward zero, or does the non-empirical case survive empty data? — and say whether you think the real disagreement between my brother and me is about the evidence, about what counts as evidence, or about how much we each care about unification for its own sake.
