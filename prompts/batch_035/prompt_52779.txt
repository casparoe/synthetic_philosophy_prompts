I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm hoping for help thinking through something at my own workplace before I decide whether to write about it. Our publisher has just rolled out a new benefits enrollment system that replaces the old uniform default with a personalized one: a model trained on last year's enrollment data predicts which of the fifteen or so health plans each of us would most likely pick, and that prediction arrives pre-selected, with the full menu one click away. Opting out takes no more effort than accepting. Nobody would have known the defaults were personalized except that two colleagues compared screens and found different plans pre-selected for each of them.

A friend of mine on the benefits committee defends the system: the defaults are accurate, colleagues who would have chosen the same plan anyway lose no time and make no error, and anyone who would choose differently pays a single extra click. Our union rep is nonetheless pushing back, saying the system is manipulative in a way an ordinary default isn't, though when I pressed her she couldn't articulate why beyond a feeling that the company is, as she put it, reading us. I find myself half agreeing with each of them.

So, before I take a side: is there a principled difference between a personalized default of this kind and a plain uniform one, given that the personalized default only ever reproduces what the model predicts a person would choose anyway? Can steering someone toward their own predicted preference wrong them at all, or does the objection only kick in when the prediction is wrong? And does the fact that the personalization was undisclosed change the ethics of the arrangement, or is that merely a transparency problem that a memo could fix?
