I teach moral and political philosophy, and every spring I co-teach a seminar on mass atrocity with a historian colleague — let's call her Dana. Dana has strong opinions about which events deserve the word genocide and hands out verdicts freely in seminar, and over the years I've pieced together her track record. The Holocaust and the Armenian genocide, obviously yes. The Holodomor, yes — for her a deliberately engineered famine aimed at a national group counts as biological destruction, full stop. The Stolen Generations in Australia, yes — forcibly transferring the children into the majority population counts even though relatively few people died. The campaign against the Uyghurs, yes, on the strength of the birth-prevention measures, though she's noticeably less breezy about that one. On the other side: the death tolls of the Congo Wars, whatever their scale, weren't genocide in her book — no intent to destroy any group as such. And purely cultural destruction — a language banned, heritage dynamited, forced assimilation with no killing — gets a flat no: a grave crime against a group, but not that one. The thing is, she never cites the Convention or any court; her rulings are all from the gut, which is exactly why it's fun to try to reverse-engineer them.

Next month we have a guest speaker presenting a case, kind of a composite stitched together from several real campaigns, and I want to be ready before Dana reacts in front of forty students. The setup: a Central Asian republic's campaign against a mountain-dwelling minority. The leadership and elders were killed, the villages razed, the population driven across the border, the land resettled by members of the majority group. The children of the small number of remaining survivors were put into boarding schools where their language is banned, and the group's archives were destroyed. On the other hand, the government's own documents only ever speak of removal and restoring order, the state never hunted the exiles abroad, and perhaps a fifth of the group was killed while most survived in refugee camps across the border. The speaker wants to call this genocide. Dana will have a verdict within about ninety seconds.

So here's what I want from you. Predict her verdict. But more than that: lay out the candidate rules that could be generating her track record — as best I can tell there's a strict Convention-style reading (specific intent to destroy a protected group in whole or substantial part, via any of the enumerated acts including famine and birth prevention) and something more Lemkin-shaped (intent to destroy the group as a viable social unit, inferable from the totality of measures even when the documents only say "removal"). Check both against every verdict I listed, tell me whether they genuinely diverge on this new case and what each predicts, give me your best guess with reasons, and then — since I suspect her track record underdetermines this — tell me what further case, presented at some future seminar, would split the two rules apart given everything she's already ruled. I'd like to have my anwser rehearsed before I'm sitting next to her at the front of the room.
