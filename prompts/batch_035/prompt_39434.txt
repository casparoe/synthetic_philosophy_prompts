I am roughly two-thirds through a novel whose central character is a retired circuit-court judge, a woman who spent thirty years sentencing people in a small county and who, when I interviewed her real-life counterparts while researching the book, kept describing her craft in terms that unsettled me: she trusted her sense of a case more than any rule she was supposed to apply, insisted that the same fact could tilt a sentencing decision in opposite directions depending on what surrounded it, and dismissed the state's sentencing guidelines as a machine for making novices feel wise. Writing her decisions scene by scene, I have come to suspect that the views I keep putting in her mouth are ones I actually hold, and since the last third of the novel turns on her philosophy colliding with her clerk's very different one, I would like to know whether the position I have given both of us is even coherent before I commit it to several hundred more pages.

Here is the set of commitments as I'd state them. (1) There are no true general moral principles: whether a feature of a situation counts in favor of an action, and how much it counts, depends on the other features present, so that even lying can count in favor in the right surroundings; rightness is determined case by case rather than derived from anything general — I understand this position has a name, moral particularism, associated with Jonathan Dancy, and that is roughly what I mean. (2) Moral judgments are truth-apt and sometimes true independently of anyone's attitudes; I am a moral realist. (3) Moral progress is genuine — we know things about the wrongness of hereditary subordination that our ancestors did not, and people a century from now may know things we do not. (4) Moral knowledge is a matter of trained perception, closer to connoisseurship than calculation: the seasoned judge's verdict deserves more weight than a poll of first-year law students, and this is not elitism but epistemology. (5) Yet the moment the scene shifts from individual acts to policy — how to structure welfare programs, whether sentencing should be guided at all — I become an unembarrassed consequentialist: rank the available rules or institutions by their aggregate outcomes and choose the best, and I find the usual objections to this squeamish. (6) I also hold that there are at least a few genuine exceptions to (1): a small number of truisms, such as that gratuitous cruelty to a child is wrong, hold in every context without exception, and I take myself to know this in advance of any case, not by surveying them.

What I would like from you is an audit rather than a lecture. Go through these six commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly, which only appear to be, and, for each real tension, what the cheapest repair would cost me elsewhere in the set. Then tell me which single revision, if I made it, would clean up the most. And please do not manufacture tensions to give me something to fix: if you think some of these commitments are not really in the market for consistency with each other, or that the whole exercise rests on a confused picture of what a moral position is, say so plainly — I would rather rework the character than preserve a muddle out of politeness.
