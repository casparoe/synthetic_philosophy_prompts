Question: "What is at issue in the debate over the units of selection? In your answer: (a) explain what Wynne-Edwards argued and why his form of group selection was largely rejected after 1966; (b) explain the gene's-eye view and how it purports to account for altruism; (c) say whether kin selection and group selection should be regarded as rival explanations; and (d) distinguish the unit of selection from the level of selection, if indeed they come apart."

Student's answer:

"Wynne-Edwards (1962) claimed that many behaviour patterns, such as the epideictic displays by which birds apparently census their own numbers, function to regulate population density: groups that restrain their breeding avoid overexploiting their food supply and so avoid extinction, whereas groups of unrestrained breeders die out. Traits like reproductive restraint would then evolve because they benefit the group. This view was largely rejected following Williams's Adaptation and Natural Selection (1966). Williams argued that group selection could hardly ever override individual selection, and the reason is a matter of timing: groups reproduce much faster than individual organisms do, so selection at the individual level strips out any altruistic tendency long before group-level benefits can accumulate.

The gene's-eye view relocates the beneficiary of selection. Since genes are the only things passed on across generations more or less intact, the gene is the real unit of selection and organisms are merely temporary vehicles. Altruism looked like a problem for this picture, but Hamilton's kin selection showed how it could be accommodated without gene-level selection: a gene for altruism can spread even at a cost to its bearer, provided the cost to the actor, weighted by the relatedness between actor and recipient, exceeds the benefit to the recipient. Helping a sibling is really a gene helping copies of itself in another body. Kin selection and group selection are thus rivals — two competing explanations of the same behaviours — and the subsequent history of the debate is simply that kin selection won and group selection was abandoned.

More recently, multilevel selection theorists such as David Sloan Wilson have revived the idea using the Price equation, which partitions selection into within-group and between-group components. Whether this rehabilitates group selection or merely redescribes inclusive-fitness bookkeeping is to some extent verbal, since in many cases the two frameworks make the same predictions. Either way, the unit of selection is the gene, and so is the level, since that is where replication occurs."
