I run a philosophy of mind seminar, and every year around week six someone drops "consciousness is a user illusion" — the line the Dennett/illusionist crowd has been repeating for decades — as if it settles whatever we were arguing about. This time it was during a discussion of the grain of experience: a student asked whether the striped surface she was looking at had a determinate number of stripes *in her experience* before she counted them, and a second student waved it off — the stripes are "just icons on the desktop," they dont exist the way they seem to, so the question is confused, not hard. Everyone nodded and we moved on, and I sat there realizing I couldn't say, on the spot, which parts of that gesture were earned and which were bluff. The metaphor gets used interchangeably for claims I suspect are cheap and claims I suspect are false, and I'd like that sorted out before I teach this again next fall.

What I'd like is a menu of the distinct precise theses that could be hiding in that one sentence, ordered from weakest to strongest. Off the top of my head there's at least: the boring computational reading (introspection is mediated and lossy, so dont expect reports to match the machinery — true, but nobody in the room disputes it); a middling reading on which the *objects* of experience, the phenomenal properties themselves, are like the folder icon, i.e. they exist as representations but misrepresent; and the full eliminativist reading on which there is nothing it is like at all, and the "illusion" claim is that qualitative-experience talk goes in the bin with witch-talk. For each, tell me whether it's a platitude, substantive but contested, or as far as we can tell false — and, this is the part I actually need, whether it supports what my student did with it: treating a question about the structure of experience as *confused* rather than merely difficult. My hunch is only the strongest reading does that, and that one is the least believed, but I want to check it before I build a class session on it.

Format-wise, could you organize it as: (1) "The menu" — one entry per reading, roughly four to six sentences each, with a verdict tag at the end of the entry (platitude / contested / false); (2) "Who believes what" — about 200 words on which reading the people who repeat the line mostly intend; (3) "Back to the stripes" — a paragraph on whether any reading licenses calling my student's question confused, and what I should say to the student who waved it off; (4) "What I'd tell the seminar" — three or four sentences max on the honest takeaway. Please dont pick a favorite reading and run with it — I want the whole menu, laid out seperately.
