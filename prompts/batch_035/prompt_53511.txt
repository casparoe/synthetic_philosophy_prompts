The transit agency where I live puts little stickers on every escalator: a standing figure on one side, a walking figure on the other. But the same agency's safety guidance says hold the handrail and don't walk on escalators at all. And I gather some systems have tried scrapping the one-side rule entirely and just asked everyone to stand on both steps, and commuters drifted back to the old custom within weeks. Whats going on philosophically here? What view of how norms work does the sticker practice presuppose — that the agency is creating the rule by announcing it, or that it's just making an already-existing rule salient? The custom seems to have shown up on its own, nobody legislated it, and it even flips sides between countries (walk on the left in London, on the right in Chicago), which makes me think it's not a regulation at all but something people sustain by expectation: everyone stands on the right because everyone expects everyone else to. Funny detail too — even when an escalator is completely empty, most people still stand on the right. Why would anyone bother if the rule were just about not blocking other people? So if the rule really lives in mutual expectations rather than in anything the agency owns, what is the sticker even doing? Why can't the agency abolish the custom by decree the way it can change a fare? And is it being consistent when it props the convention up with signage while its own safety rules tell people to break it — or should I say the two halves of the agency are quietly working against each other? I'd also like to know what the agency would have to do differently if it understood the rule as a convention sustained by everyone's expectations, rather than a regulation it can publish and enforce like any other.
