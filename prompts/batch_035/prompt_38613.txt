After a career in experimental condensed-matter physics, I have spent my retirement working through philosophy of physics, and I have become quietly obsessed with the arrow of time. The standard story — that the asymmetry between past and future is fixed not by the laws but by a special low-entropy boundary condition in the distant past — strikes me as both beautiful and oddly fragile, and I want a case that stresses it in a controlled way.

Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. The world you build must (i) obey microdynamics that are exactly time-reversal invariant, so no appeal to the weak interaction's tiny T-violation — you may simply stipulate a universe in which T-violating processes are absent; (ii) contain a single low-entropy boundary condition placed at what we, from the outside, would call the *future* end of the relevant epoch, with entropy rising smoothly and monotonically as one moves away from it in either temporal direction — no wild fluctuations, so the Boltzmann-brain shortcut is unavailable; (iii) admit observers somewhere in the middle of the epoch, with macroscopic regularities, thermodynamic behaviour, and something functionally like records and memories, on both sides of their location in time; and (iv) involve no demons, time machines, retrocausal signalling, or laws beyond ours — the only departures from our universe are the placement of the boundary condition and the removal of T-violation.

The scenario itself should be a single paragraph, and it must not smuggle in the verdict: describe only the physics and the boundary conditions, and do not stipulate which way the inhabitants' memories point or which direction they call the past. Then run the check. State the laws, the boundary condition, the entropic profile, and what each of the following accounts predicts about the direction of the inhabitants' mental and record-keeping asymmetry: a Past-Hypothesis-style view on which the arrow of time is grounded in a past low-entropy condition; and a perspectival or anthropic view on which the direction we call past is fixed by the standpoint of creatures like us rather than by the boundary condition itself. Show whether the two accounts in fact diverge on this world, or whether they both end up saying the inhabitants call the low-entropy direction the past, and, if the latter, say plainly what the case then fails to discriminate between.

Finish with three honest notes. First, identify which single feature of the construction is doing the work, and what happens to the case if that feature is removed — specifically, if you replace the single boundary condition with either no low-entropy boundary at all or with one at both ends. Second, explain how a defender of the Past Hypothesis would attack the case: I anticipate the objection that calling the boundary condition a *future* one already presupposes a temporal direction, and I want to know whether that objection can be made to stick without the defender resting on a flat definitional fiat. Third, tell me whether any variant of this construction could discriminate between entropic accounts of the arrow and causal or interventionist accounts, or whether all such cases are condemned to be neutral between them — I would rather be told the case cannot do the work I want than be flattered.

Please answer in British English, in polished prose, and treat the scenario as a piece of physics-first world-building rather than as fantasy: where you must idealise, say so explicitly and say why the idealisation is benign.
