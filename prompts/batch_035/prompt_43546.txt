I'm taking a graduate seminar on moral responsibility, and as a closing exercise each of us has to submit a short position statement that the rest of the group will press on. We spent the first half of the term on compatibilism and the second on Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" and the literature it spawned, and my statement has ended up as a fairly predictable mixture of the two halves. Before I hand it over, I'd like an outside audit of whether my commitments actually hang together, and if they don't, which one has to give.

Here is the set, roughly as I've written it: (1) I'm a compatibilist — determinism by itself doesn't undermine responsibility, and what matters is an agent's capacities to grasp and respond to moral reasons, roughly the reading of Strawson that Wallace later developed. (2) I don't require "could have done otherwise" for blame: in Frankfurt-style cases, where someone would have been coerced into the same act had they shown any sign of pulling back but never needed to be, the agent is fully blameworthy. (3) I think an agent whose values and desires were covertly engineered — the Pereboom-style manipulation cases, where a team quietly produces the psychology that later issues in a killing — is not responsible, even though that agent has every ordinary capacity of moral understanding and the act expresses the same quality of will an ordinary murderer's would. (4) I follow Strawson on exemptions: the psychopath and the severely mentally ill are not proper targets of the reactive attitudes, since the participant stance presupposes capacities they lack; and I also accept Strawson's deeper quietist point that the reactive attitudes don't stand in need of a rational vindication from somewhere outside the practice. (5) In hard-luck cases — someone shaped into callousness by sustained childhood abuse, say, but who now has fully intact capacities — I think blame is mitigated rather than full-strength.

Something about the list feels unstable to me, but I can't locate exactly where, and I'd rather not find out from my classmates. So please audit it: tell me which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and rank the tensions by severity instead of treating every friction as equally serious; for each real one, say what would have to give and what that costs. And please don't do the seminar-room thing of laying out the live revisions and leaving the choice to me — take a definite position on which single commitment should be revised (or which apparent tension should instead be dissolved), and give your reasons for that call.
