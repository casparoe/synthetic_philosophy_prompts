My father is in the late stages of frontotemporal dementia, and my family will soon have to decide whether to decline aggressive interventions in favor of comfort-focused palliative care. A close friend who has spent fifteen years in palliative medicine has effectively told me that we would not be wronging him by making that choice, and that, in her experience, families who do so rarely come to regret it. I find myself leaning heavily on her judgment, and this has made me want to understand the philosophical problem of moral testimony properly before I act on it.

Please explain the core puzzle of moral deference: why it seems perfectly reasonable to take a physicist's word about black holes, yet somehow defective — epistemically, or perhaps in terms of my own agency — to take a morally wise friend's word about what morality requires. In working through this, I would be grateful if you could address the following interlocking questions:

1. Where exactly does my friend's testimony fall? It seems to have (at least) three components: (a) a fairly direct moral verdict ("you would not be wronging him"), (b) testimony to morally relevant empirical facts ("families rarely regret it"), and (c) something like testimony about what dying and its aftermath are actually like, drawn from having witnessed hundreds of deaths. Is component (c) a genuine third category — something like phenomenal moral testimony — or does it collapse into (b)?

2. How does the distinction, associated with philosophers such as Alison Hills, between merely having moral knowledge and genuinely understanding a moral matter bear on my situation? If I act on my friend's verdict without myself understanding why comfort-focused care is permissible, have I fallen short in a way that matters practically — for instance, by being unable to respond to my family's objections or to new complications in my father's condition?

3. Is the asymmetry between deference about physics and deference about morality best explained epistemically (e.g., by the thought that moral knowledge must be earned through first-personal reflection to be knowledge at all), by concerns about alienation of agency (my decision no longer being my own), by the thought that moral judgment is partly constitutive of my relationships and character, or by something else? And do any of these explanations actually apply to testimony that is partly empirical and experiential, as my friend's is?

I should stress that I am not asking for practical guidance on the decision itself, nor for a reading list — only for a clear conceptual map of the territory, so that I can better understand what, if anything, is philosophically suspect about the epistemic position I now find myself in.
