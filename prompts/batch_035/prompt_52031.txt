I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've spent the past month on a long feature — roughly sixty discrete factual claims, each of which went through our research desk individually. Before the piece went to press, two colleagues of mine had a disagreement about whether we could honestly publish it, and since they know I write about exactly this kind of thing, they asked me to referee. I couldn't, cleanly, so I'd like your read on it.

Their exchange, as best I can reconstruct it: (1) My editor, Dana, argued that every claim had cleared checking on its own — each one supported by documents, experts, or data — so the piece was sound and could run. (2) Our head of research, Marcus, replied that each individual check still leaves some small chance of error — call it one or two percent — and with sixty claims, the probability that the piece contains no error at all is quite low. He likened our situation to the author of a scholarly book who believes each sentence of his own book, yet writes in the preface that errors surely remain: it seems rational to accept each claim and rational to expect the whole to be flawed, and publishing the feature as a body of verified fact is effectively asserting the conjunction. (3) Dana answered that this proves too much: no newspaper, textbook, or encyclopedia could ever be published on Marcus's standard, and it's absurd to hold publication hostage to the conjunction of every sentence. She added that readers don't receive a feature as one giant conjunction anyway — they take each claim on its own evidential merits. (4) Marcus conceded the point about readers, but pressed that readers do come away with an overall impression of solidity, which is the whole reason features carry authority; and he drew a comparison to a large lottery, where each ticket is overwhelmingly likely to lose, it's tempting to say you know each one will lose, and yet you obviously can't know that no ticket will win. His proposal: since something must give, either acceptance just isn't closed under conjunction — in which case Dana should stop talking as if per-claim clearance settles the matter — or the standard we demand per claim must rise with the number of claims in the piece, and long features should be held to a stricter per-claim bar than briefs. (5) Dana called that backwards: how well a given claim is supported doesn't depend on how many other claims sit near it, so adjusting the per-claim threshold by article length confuses the evidence for a claim with the costs of being wrong in aggregate.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: for each of the five numbered moves, say whether it is sound, confused, or somewhere in between, and why. Then tell me who is more right overall, and identify anything substantive that both of them missed — including whether Marcus's two escapes in (4) really are the only options.
