I am a PhD student in cognitive science working on judgment and decision making; my advisor's group consults for the research office of a large public university, and I have been handed a task that is proving harder than it looks. The office convenes committees that choose everything from survey software to data-management platforms for funded projects — fifteen to twenty decisions a year, ranging from a few thousand dollars to several hundred thousand, each one documented because the outcomes get audited when a grant is questioned. The committees' standing complaint is not that they choose badly but that they cannot stop choosing: vendors keep submitting proposals past the deadline, a search for what else is out there never terminates, and the same committee can burn three meetings on a $6,000 decision. My advisor, who is fond of Herbert Simon, has asked me to translate bounded rationality into something the committees can actually run, and I would like your help drafting it.

What we need is a decision procedure for when to stop generating options and commit to one — a stopping rule, in effect — that a committee of non-specialists can execute inside a single ninety-minute meeting with no math beyond counting and no software. It should be grounded in the bounded rationality tradition: search is costly, the relevant standard is an aspiration level rather than the optimum, and a satisfactory option is the target rather than the best one. But the translation is where I keep getting stuck, and I want you to take the following complications seriously rather than smoothing them over.

First, aspiration levels in the literature are allowed to adjust as the search proceeds — Simon made the adjustment of aspirations central to the account — but committees adjust them in a specific way I have not seen discussed: after a surprisingly strong proposal arrives, the committee's standards ratchet upward, so that options that would have counted as satisfactory last month no longer do. Whether that upward drift is legitimate (they have learned the market is better than they thought) or illegitimate (it is regret and social comparison dressed up as evidence) seems to depend on facts the procedure cannot see. The procedure has to say something about when adjustment of the standard is allowed and in which directions.

Second, option arrival is asynchronous. There is no natural point at which the option pool is complete; new proposals arrive while the committee deliberates. So the procedure actually needs two separate stopping decisions — when to close the pool to new options, and when to stop evaluating the ones already in hand — and I suspect these come apart in ways that matter, because closing the pool early forecloses and closing it late just delays the same choice.

Third, sunk costs. Committees that have spent four meetings on a search refuse to commit to the satisfactory option on the table because they feel they have come too far to settle. Any honest procedure has to handle the case where its own rule says to stop and the committee's psychology says to continue, and it cannot do so by fiat, since the committee members outrank the procedure.

Please write the procedure: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops with a commitment, where it stops with a continue-searching verdict, and where it hands the decision to the committee because the question is one of values rather than facts. It must produce an auditable record — the office gets asked, a year later, why a platform was chosen — so each step should leave a trace a non-member could read after the fact. Then run it on three cases from our files: (a) a survey platform for a three-year longitudinal study, where one platform meets every stated requirement but a committee member keeps forwarding one more to look at; (b) a data-manager hire where a clearly satisfactory candidate has an offer expiring Friday while a stronger applicant might still materialize; and (c) a $400,000 platform decision where the procedure, as far as I can tell, will commit the committee to the second-best option, which I expect they will hate. Please say plainly what the procedure cannot do — where it will systematically misfire and which judgment calls I have tried to disguise as facts — rather than pretending the procedure eliminates judgment.
