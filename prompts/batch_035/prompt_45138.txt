So I've been pretty sold for a while on Dennett's idea that the self isn't a thing inside you but more like a center of narrative gravity — an abstract posit we use to organize a person's behavior and biography, roughly the way physicists use a center of mass. No inner audience in the theater, no little presenter; the self is something the brain's narrative-spinning processes posit, and it's no more mysterious that a "self" exists than that a center of gravity exists.

Lately I've been pushing it a bit further with an extension of my own, and I want to stress-test the whole package. The extension goes: if selves really are like centers of gravity, they should inherit the weird properties abstracta have. A center of gravity can bifurcate (imagine an object slowly separating into two lobes — where did the original center go?), it can sit outside the physical object entirely (a donut's or boomerang's center of gravity is in the hole), and two objects can share one. So I'd expect selves to have the same profile: a self can be smeared across two people in tightly couples relationships, one person can host two, and asking "which one is the REAL self" is sometimes like asking which point is the real center — i.e., a badly posed question that the theory itself diagnoses as badly posed.

What would count against this? I'm not fishing for a defense. I want the defeasers ranked from fatal to merely awkward: which cases, results, or arguments would genuinely hurt — split-brain stuff, thought insertion in schizophrenia, depersonalization, whatever else belongs on the list — and which of those a committed Dennettian could shrug of with a straight face. Along the way, tell me whether my extension actually does any work the original view doesn't, or whether I've just dressed up "selves are kind of fuzzy" in physics clothing and should drop it. And be honest about whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability by all the "badly posed question" moves, and whether that even matters for a view of this kind — maybe a theory of the self is allowed to be mostly interpretive, maybe it isn't.
