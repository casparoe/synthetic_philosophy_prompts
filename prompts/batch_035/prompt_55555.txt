In Freedom and Resentment Strawson addresses the stand-off between the pessimist, who holds that if determinism is true no one is genuinely responsible, and the optimist, who holds that our responsibility practices are justified because they are natural and useful for social life. Strawson's own position is the optimist's, and he supplies the argument the optimist lacked.

The argument begins with the reactive attitudes: resentment, gratitude, forgiveness, hurt feelings, and their vicarious analogues such as indignation and disapprobation. These are natural human responses to the quality of another person's will towards us, and they can only be felt from within the participant stance, from inside a relationship with someone one regards as a responsible agent. The contrast is the objective attitude, in which one views the person not as a responsible agent but as an object of social policy, something to be managed, treated, or cured. Strawson argues that this is the attitude we ought generally to take towards offenders, since it is more humane than resentment.

When a wrongdoer answers for what they did, Strawson distinguishes two kinds of plea. Some pleas, such as he was pushed or he didn't mean to, exempt the agent from responsibility altogether: they show the deed did not express a bad quality of will, and so there was never anything to hold the person responsible for. Other pleas, such as insanity or immaturity, merely mitigate, reducing the charge while leaving responsibility intact.

Since the reactive attitudes are natural and unavoidable — Strawson says a wholly objective attitude to human beings cannot be sustained as a general policy — determinism turns out to be irrelevant to moral responsibility. The pessimist's premise may be true or false, but either way our practices stand; so compatibilism is established and the free will problem is dissolved.
