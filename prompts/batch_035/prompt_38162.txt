Quick setup before my question: I'd like you to take the role of a professional philosopher of biology who also knows conservation law, and I'd rather you start with a bit of brainstorming — lay out the live options before committing to a view. Don't just dump the first framework that comes to mind.

Some background on me: I'm a retired museum taxonomist. Thirty-five years of describing and revising water beetles, which means I spent my whole career doing the thing philosophers now tell me is philosophically impossible — deciding, specimen by specimen, where one species ends and the next begins. Late in my career I got dragged into a listing decision because one of "my" beetle groups had a population that a conservation NGO wanted protected, and whether it counted as its own species or just a subspecies depended entirely on which species concept you used. Interbreeding criteria said one species, the phylogenetic ones said two, and the morphology (my own specialty, embarrassingly) pointed every which way. The lawyers didn't care about the conceptual mess; they just wanted a yes or no.

That experience is why I finally started reading philosophy of biology seriously after retiring, and the thing I can't get untangled is this. The whole legal and institutional apparatus of conservation — endangered species acts, CITES appendices, IUCN listings — is written in terms of species. You list a species, you delist a species, you ban trade in a species. And this has real consequences: a taxonomic revision can move an organism from "endangered" to "not even a candidate" overnight, without a single individual dying or a single acre of habitat being lost — or the reverse, suddenly making a common local population the last survivors of a "species." Meanwhile those of us who actually make these calls have never agreed on what a species is. The main concepts (biological, phylogenetic, morphological, ecological) come apart constantly, and competent people split or lump the same populations depending on which one they favor.

So here's my question: what does this practice presuppose about what species are? A few sub-questions I keep chewing on:

1. Does conservation law need a species concept at all, or does it work in practice precisely because it stays vague and outsources the question to us taxonomists? (In which case the "practice" is maybe doing something clever by not committing.)

2. Is there a kind of implicit essentialism in the way the statutes are written — naming and protecting a species as if it were a definite thing with boundaries, like a plot of land — even though the very existence of "subspecies" and "distinct population segment" language in the same statutes seems to quietly concede that the species rank isn't doing the important work?

3. If you took the law seriously as presupposing one of the rival concepts, which one would it have to be, and which ones would it quietly rule out? And are the different parts of the apparatus even consistent with each other?

4. And the practical end: suppose we swapped in a properly phylogenetic picture, species as lineages or branches with no ranks at all. What would a conservation law built on that have to protect instead — clades, population lineages, evolutionarily significant units? What would actually change, and would the changes be at the edges or at the core?

One thing I want your take on specifically: I can imagine someone saying the law is philosophically safe because it's pluralist — it just needs *some* grouping, and any of the main concepts would license roughly the same protected list. But that seems wrong to me. The lists differ exactly at the edges, and the edges are where the lawsuits and the listing battles happen. Does that mean the practice isn't actually underdetermined, that it *does* need a concept, it just borrows ours without acknowledging it?

Take the question seriously, reconstruct whatever implicit theory (or incoherent mix of theories) you think is actually there, and please distinguish between what the practice strictly presupposes versus what it merely tolerates. If the honest answer is that it's underdetermined and could be rebuilt on several foundations, say so and tell me what would change under each.
