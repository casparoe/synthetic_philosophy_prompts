I work on programming-language tooling for a living — mostly static analyzers and type checkers, and a couple of years ago I wrote the kernel for a small hobby proof assistant, so I have a decent technical grip on what it means for a machine to check a proof line by line. I have no formal philosophy background: my exposure is the encyclopedia entry on Benacerraf's dilemma, a couple of popular books, and a lot of arguments at lunch.

For about three years now, two of my colleagues have been arguing about the status of mathematics, and they argue past each other in a way I find frustrating because each of them seems to me obviously right about something. One of them, call him the Platonist, holds that mathematical statements are about mind-independent abstract objects, that theorems are discovered rather than invented, and that the truths of arithmetic would hold even if there had never been any humans, symbol systems, or computers. The other, call him the Nominalist, holds that mathematics is a human symbolic practice: there are no abstract objects, and what we call mathematical truth is, at bottom, facts about what follows from axioms by permitted manipulations — what a proof checker could in principle verify, whether or not anyone runs it. Each thinks the other's position cannot explain something that plainly needs explaining, and each keeps restating his own view more forcefully, which has convinced nobody.

I have read about the technique of adversarial collaboration, and I want to commission the document I believe the two of them should be able to produce after a good-faith, moderated effort. I would like you to draft that joint statement. The two of them are both familiar with Benacerraf's dilemma — roughly, that if we want mathematical statements to be true in the same robust sense as ordinary factual statements, it looks like they must be about objects outside space and time, which then makes it mysterious how creatures like us, with only causal contact with symbols on pages and screens, could know anything about such objects — and with Benacerraf's older observation that the natural numbers can be modeled as sets in multiple incompatible ways: on one standard encoding, 3 is the set whose members are 0, 1, and 2, each number being the set of its predecessors; on another, 3 is a singleton of a singleton of a singleton of the empty set. Both encodings satisfy the Peano axioms and support identical arithmetic, and neither candidate set seems to have any claim to being the number 3.

Please structure the document as follows.

First, a numbered section of claims that both parties can sign. These should be substantive, not vacuous — things like: both encodings of the numbers support the same theorems, and the choice between them is arbitrary as far as mathematical practice goes; my proof-assistant kernel certifies theorems while inspecting only syntax, and it has no access to anything beyond its inference rules, yet its verdicts agree with those of human mathematicians who believe themselves to be tracking truths about an independent subject matter; independent lines of argument converge on the same results; and the plain fact, which both should be able to state, of what the kernel is doing at the mechanical level. Draft each agreed claim so that it commits both parties to something real without smuggling in either side's metaphysics.

Second, a disagreements table. Each row should contain the precise statement of one remaining disagreement, phrased twice: once in wording the Platonist would accept as a fair statement of his view, and once in wording the Nominalist would accept as a fair statement of his. Candidate rows, which you may adapt or supplement: whether mathematical statements have truth values in the same sense as empirical statements, or only in a derived, practice-internal sense; what exactly a mathematician knows when she knows Fermat's Last Theorem — a fact about abstract objects, or a fact about derivability from the axioms; whether the sentence stating that the number 3 exists reports a fact; and what my kernel is tracking when it accepts a proof — the rules of a game, or truths that hold independently of all rules and games. The phrasings must be ones the respective signatories would genuinely endorse, not slanted caricatures of their opponents.

Third, a cruxes section. For each crux — a sub-question such that settling it would move at least one party — specify an argument, case, or phenomenon that both parties agree bears on it, and record each party's prediction in advance. I would particularly like the following to be treated as a test case both must accommodate, since it is my own contribution and neither has addressed it head-on: my kernel is a small, fully audited, deterministic program that checks derivations against inference rules and nothing else; it works, reliably, across all of the mathematics that has been encoded for it, without any contact with a semantic realm. The Nominalist says this shows that mathematical knowledge needs no contact with abstract objects, since the kernel has none. The Platonist replies that the kernel's reliability is exactly what his own view predicts, but that the Nominalist owes an account of why these particular symbol manipulations are the ones that converge, and why the practice is answerable to anything at all. Force both of them to state, in the document, what would count as evidence against their own view on this point. A second crux I want included: what to make of statements like the continuum hypothesis, where the accepted axioms provably do not settle the question — whether there is a fact of the matter that current practice fails to reach, or whether the question is in some sense not fully posed.

Fourth, a short section in which the document flags which of the residual disagreements are substantive and which, if any, are verbal. I suspect that the multiple-encodings observation pushes both of them toward saying that numbers are positions in a structure rather than any particular object, and I want the document to address explicitly whether this drift dissolves any part of their dispute or merely relocates it, and whether each party would accept that assessment.

Please keep the whole thing written in the register of a jointly authored document — first person plural where appropriate — rather than an essay about the two parties. I do not want a bibliography, citations, or references to specific papers; the two of them know the literature and I do not, and I want the document to stand on its own formulations. Do not have either side concede more than a good-faith collaborator plausibly would, and do not resolve the disagreement in the drafting; the deliverable is a text both could sign, with what remains between them stated exactly.
