I'm doing a philosophy of language course this term and we've just finished a block on the semantics and pragmatics of slurs — expressivist accounts, prohibitionism, the whole fight about whether the derogatory content is truth-conditional or some non-semantic baggage that rides along. For the final essay we're allowed to bring in any case study we want, and I'd like to do Blazing Saddles, because honestly I can't think of another mainstream film that floods the audience with slurs for two hours while clearly wanting us to laugh *at* the racist speakers, not their targets. So here's what I want: a reading of the film through the philosophy of slurs. Mel Brooks's whole gambit (the film is from 1974, co-written by Richard Pryor, Cleavon Little plays Bart the Black sheriff sent to the all-white town of Rock Ridge) is that the joke is always on the bigot — the townspeople's mouths open and slurs fall out and they're the idiots. The question is whether that actually works, and what theory of slurs would explain *how* it works if it does.

Some things I want the reading to get its teeth into. First, the speakers inside the film: if slurs have derogatory content as part of their literal meaning (as, say, a combinatorial expressivist would have it), then how are we meant to process two hours of them without the film itself becoming the thing it mocks? If instead the damage is largely pragmatic — speaker intentions, who's talking to whom — then the film looks like a giant natural experiment in keeping the pragmatic conditions fixed while varying the speakers, including scenes where the slur gets thrown back at white characters by Black ones, or gets used affectionately after the town has (very grudgingly) accepted Bart. Second, and this is the bit I keep going back and forth on: the utterances aren't real utterances at all. They're actors performing lines written for fictional characters. Does a prohibitionist account of slurs — the view that the words are just off-limits regardless of intent or context — even get a grip on fictional use, or does it have to say something awkward about the film? And does the expressivist do better, since whatever attitude is expressed is the *character's*, not the actor's? Third, the recurring claim you hear that the film "couldn't be made today" — I don't want the essay to just take a side in that culture-war squabble, but I do want to know whether any of these theories actually predicts or illuminates it, or whether it's an anomaly they're all silent on.

A couple of ground rules. Please don't just give me a list of scenes with a theory label slapped on each one — I want an actual argument that runs in both directions: what the film dramatises that the philosophical papers miss, and where the film resists the reading (there are bits, I think, where the laughter is not entirely at the racists, and an honest essay has to say so rather than pretend Brooks nailed it). Don't worry about quoting the film verbatim or citing specific papers — I've got the film and my course pack, so paraphrase scenes and positions rather than worrying about exact lines or page numbers. And please write it in British English. Length-wise I'm aiming for roughly essay-sized, so treat this as a full reading, not a sketch.
