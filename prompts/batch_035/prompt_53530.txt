The examination question was:

Rawls contends that utilitarianism, by aggregating welfare across persons, fails to take seriously the separateness of persons. (a) Reconstruct the objection. (b) Distinguish it from the complaint that utilitarianism is indifferent to how utility is distributed, and explain why the distinction matters to Rawls's argument against utilitarianism. (c) Explain how Parfit's reductionism about personal identity bears on the objection, and assess whether the objection survives.

The student's answer, transcribed in full:

Utilitarianism holds that the right action is the one that maximizes total welfare over everyone affected. Within a single life, this is unproblematic: I can rationally accept a loss now for a larger benefit later, since the same person who bears the cost also reaps the compensation. Rawls's insight is that utilitarianism extends this intra-personal logic to society as a whole. It treats the community as one large agent with a single pool of welfare, so losses to some can be justified by gains to others. But different people cannot compensate each other in this way: the person who suffers is not the person who benefits, so a burden imposed on one person is never really made up for. That is the failure to take persons seriously as separate.

Ultimately, though, the objection is about inequality. Since utilitarianism cares only about the total, it is indifferent to distribution: an arrangement in which a few do extremely well and many do somewhat worse is acceptable provided the sum comes out higher. Nozick's utility monster is the classic illustration of this — one creature gets such enormous pleasure from others' losses that everyone should be sacrificed for it, which shows that utilitarianism ignores the separateness of persons. Nozick pressed the separateness of persons objection most influentially in Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974), and Rawls followed him in A Theory of Justice. Rawls's remedy is the veil of ignorance: because the parties do not know their place in society, they would not gamble on principles that permit sacrificing some for others, and they therefore choose the difference principle, which permits inequalities only when they benefit the least advantaged. Scanlon's contractualism embodies the same respect for separateness through the test of principles that no one could reasonably reject.

Finally, Parfit dissolves the objection. His fission and fusion thought experiments show that personal identity is an illusion. Since persons are not really separate, there is no fact of the matter about one person's loss being compensated by another's gain; the boundaries between lives are conventional. Williams's integrity objection makes much the same point. So the separateness of persons objection, while historically important, rests on a metaphysics of persons that we now know to be false, and it does not survive Parfit's work.
