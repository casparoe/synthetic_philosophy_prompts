I am a novelist, and I am writing the afterword to the paperback edition of my most recent book — a historical novel that plays deliberately and rather freely with the documented record. Part of the afterword concerns what, if anything, entitles a work of fiction to be called "true": I have a strong intuition that some novels are truthful and others are not, and that this has little to do with the accuracy of any individual sentence in them. At a dinner party last week, a philosopher friend cut this line of thought off at the knees. Truth, he said, is not some deep property awaiting analysis: to say that a claim is true is just to assert the claim, or to endorse it, or to remove the quotation marks around it — the word "true" contributes nothing of its own, and there is nothing substantive left for a theory of truth to be a theory of. He invoked, I believe, the tradition of redundancy and disquotational accounts of truth, though he may have been gesturing at something stronger still.

I do not know whether I was dismissed by a platitude or by a substantive metaphysical thesis, and the difference matters to my afterword. Please lay out, as a structured menu I can actually think with, the distinct precise claims that might be hiding in what he said: the readings on which it is a harmless grammatical observation about particular sentences; the readings on which it makes a real claim about the logical work the truth predicate does — generalizing over claims we cannot enumerate, for instance, or endorsing a corpus sight unseen; the readings on which it denies that truth is a genuine property at all rather than a shallow one; and any readings on which it would be flatly false or self-undermining. For each, tell me whether it is defensible and, more importantly, whether it actually supports my friend's dismissal. My suspicion is that only the most radical reading touches my question about novels, and that the modest readings — on which "true" is a useful device for endorsing things wholesale — leave plenty of room for the distinction between a truthful novel and an accurate one. But I would rather you check that suspicion than humor it, and I would like you to flag any reading under which my question collapses into something narrower, such as whether sentences of fiction are even in the market for truth.

A few constraints: write for a reader with no technical training beyond a good introductory philosophy course; where a reading has a standard name in the literature, give it (I have gathered there are redundancy, disquotational, and minimalist variants, and that they do not all say the same thing), but do not let the names substitute for the claims. The deliverable I need is a disambiguation with verdicts — which readings are trivial, which are substantive and plausible, which are false — together with, under each defensible reading, a considered answer to whether my afterword's project survives my friend's objection.
