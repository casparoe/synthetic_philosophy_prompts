I am a recently retired physicist — condensed matter theory, mostly transport problems, for the better part of four decades — and I have spent the past two years reading philosophy of science for pleasure. Most of it I can latch onto, but I have run into a position in economic methodology that I cannot fit anywhere in my own conceptual toolkit, and I would like to know whether it has a name and a literature of its own.

In my line of work, we idealize constantly: point masses, frictionless planes, ideal gases, infinite lattices. But we treat these as controlled approximations. There is supposed to be a regime in which the neglected effects are demonstrably small, part of the discipline lies in estimating the error you introduce, and the idealization is in principle removable — you can add the friction term back and watch the solution deform continuously toward the real thing. What I keep meeting in economics is something that looks superficially similar but, on inspection, seems to be a different animal: the claim that it is positively a virtue for a theory's assumptions to be wildly false, provided the theory's predictions hold up. I am told a famous mid-century essay by a Chicago-school economist argues this in so many words, illustrating it with an expert billiards player who sinks shots as if he had solved the relevant equations, with leaves arranged on a tree as if the tree knew where the sunlight would fall, and with firms that price as if they were solving a marginal-revenue-equals-marginal-cost problem even though the managers themselves, if you asked them, would report doing no such calculation. The striking move, to my ear, is not that the assumptions are false — ours often are — but that their falsity is advertised as a strength, and that the essay apparently counsels against ever testing a theory by the realism of its premises. No physicist I have ever worked with would say the falsity of the ideal-gas assumptions is a point in favor of the ideal-gas law; we say they are nearly true in a specified regime, and we can tell you how near. The economic stance does not seem to be that, and yet its defenders sound as though they thought it was.

So, my questions: Does this specific doctrine — the claim that unrealistic assumptions are not merely tolerable but a mark of a significant theory, with predictive success as the only test — have an established name, and who coined it? How exactly do philosophers of science carve up the space here, given that it cannot be the same thing as the garden-variety idealization I practiced? I would be particularly grateful for the carving rather than the label: whether anyone has usefully distinguished kinds of false premises (for instance, ones that hold approximately in some limiting regime versus ones that are simply nowhere true, or ones whose failure is harmless for the question at hand versus ones that quietly do explanatory work); what the standard objections are; and what the best defense of the position amounts to — whether, that is, one can coherently hold that a model's postulates are nowhere near true and still take them to explain anything, rather than merely to predict. If there are several nearby concepts that a person in my position is likely to be conflating, please separate them for me and say where the edges lie. Please answer in American English.
