This semester I am taking a course that treats religion as a natural phenomenon, to be studied with the same tools we would use for any other persistent feature of human behaviour. In the opening lectures the lecturer mapped the theoretical landscape, and one position was introduced through an analogy I also keep encountering in popular atheist writing and in seminar discussions: religion is a virus (or parasite) of the mind. On this picture, religious ideas spread the way pathogens do — they get themselves transmitted because they are good at transmission, not because they do us any good; they exploit pre-existing features of our cognition the way a virus exploits a cell's machinery; and since they serve only their own propagation, we would lose nothing of value, and perhaps gain some health, by shedding them. The rival picture my course takes equally seriously is that religion is more like an organ (or an organ system): something that persists across populations because it does something for the organisms or groups that carry it — coordinating commitment, binding communities into larger wholes, making suffering bearable — even if, like any organ, it can malfunction or become vestigial in changed conditions.

I would like you to audit both analogies, since each is being used to support a research programme and, at least in the virus case, a practical attitude. Please give me the correspondence table for each. For the virus: what plays the role of the pathogen, the host, the host cell's machinery that gets hijacked, the mode of transmission, and the fitness interest that is being served — and served by whom, given that an idea has no interests of its own? For the organ: what plays the role of the organ, its function, its malfunction, and the vestigial case, and what exactly is supposed to be the organism whose good the organ serves — an individual believer, a group, a lineage of ideas, or all three at once?

Then identify where each mapping breaks down. Some worries I already have, which you should feel free to correct or extend: religious ideas seem to have no replication machinery of their own — their transmission depends entirely on human minds finding them in some way compelling or useful, which may already tilt the virus picture toward something more mutualistic, like gut flora rather than influenza; and the claim that the fitness of the idea and the welfare of its carriers can come apart — the strict parasitism the analogy requires — may be much harder to establish than it sounds, since an idea that visibly harmed most of its carriers might struggle to be transmitted at all, while one that visibly benefited them might be maintained for exactly that reason. On the organ side, I suspect the analogy quietly imports a function-bearing whole: organs have functions only relative to an integrated organism with a life of its own, and it is unclear that a society supplies anything with that structure, or that whatever selected for religion did so because it performed a function rather than as a byproduct of capacities selected for other purposes.

Finally, tell me which conclusions, if any, each analogy can actually carry. In particular: can the virus picture support the practical conclusion it is usually deployed for — that religion is, on net, something we would be better off without — or does that conclusion have to be argued for independently, with the analogy merely decorating it? And can the organ picture support the conclusion it is sometimes used for — that religion is likely indispensable or healthy and should be left in place — or does it equally fail to carry that weight? If neither analogy can bear the load placed on it, I would like to know what a better analogy would be, if there is one.
