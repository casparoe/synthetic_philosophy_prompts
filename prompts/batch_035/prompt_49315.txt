I teach a graduate seminar on theories of equality, and for the unit on the equality of what? debate (Sen, Dworkin, Cohen), I want an essay question that pushes beyond the usual expensive-tastes back-and-forth. Here is what I would like you to produce: an argument for the thesis that Dworkin's equality of resources, as operationalized by the envy test, is unstable — that when pushed on the right pair of cases, it turns out to be covertly performing welfare judgments it officially disavows.

The pair of cases I have in mind:

First, a connoisseur of fine wine whose expensive tastes are, in the ordinary sense, an achievement. She did not merely wake up with a taste for first-growth Bordeaux; as a young adult she preferred sweet fortified plonk, and she deliberately, at some cost in effort, money, and years of attention, retrained her palate. Her refined preferences are something she built. Under the envy test, she does not envy anyone else's bundle of resources — she regards her own material holdings as adequate, and merely wishes her money went further. So she receives no compensation, and Dworkin's standard verdict is that this is correct: her tastes are her responsibility, and resourcism is deliberately insensitive to whether a person's welfare is cheap or expensive to produce.

Second, someone who has spent her life in grinding scarcity and has, as Sen puts the point in his discussion of adaptive preferences, adjusted her expectations downward until she is content with very little. She, too, does not envy anyone else's bundle; her horizon has contracted to fit her circumstances. So the envy test certifies her as equal as well.

The essay should argue that the resourcist cannot rest with these twin verdicts. The natural thought — that the second woman is not equal but diminished, and that her contentment is precisely the problem — is not available from within the envy test, which only registers what people prefer given the preferences they actually have. To distinguish the two cases, the resourcist seems forced to reach for something like a counterfactual or hypothetical-preference judgment: what she would have wanted under conditions of decent upbringing, or what her welfare profile reveals about her real position. And that, the argument should conclude, is a welfare- or capabilities-style judgment wearing a resource-theoretic costume. The envy test's apparent neutrality between cheap and expensive tastes holds only so long as we never meet anyone whose cheapness of taste is itself the injury.

A few constraints on the argument: (1) it should engage with Dworkin's actual apparatus — the envy test, the distinction between personal and impersonal resources, the ambition-sensitivity/endowment-insensitivity slogan — rather than refuting a generic resourcism; (2) it should consider the strongest resourcist reply available, namely that the second woman's deformations of preference can be traced to deficits in her impersonal resources (upbringing, education, social background), so that compensation is owed on grounds of resource inequality after all, and it should say why that reply either fails or concedes the point by letting a welfare diagnosis (that she is diminished) do the work of identifying which resource deficits matter; and (3) it should not claim more than the cases show — the target is the adequacy of the envy test as a test of equality, not the wholesale truth of resourcism.

Aim for something a strong graduate student could be asked to write in 2,000 words, and write the argument itself at that level of precision: no strawmen, no name-dropping in place of reasoning.
