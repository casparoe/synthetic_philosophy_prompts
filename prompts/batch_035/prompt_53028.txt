I teach a 300-level seminar titled "Determinism, Prediction, and Their Limits," with fourteen students (philosophy of physics majors plus a few from STS) and a single 80-minute session. Design an in-class game, built around the logistic map at r = 4, that makes the students feel rather than be told the gap between determinism and predictability — the fact that a system can be completely fixed in advance, by a rule they all know, and still be unforeseeable in practice.

The game should have students wagering tokens on the future of a system whose governing rule is posted on the projector the whole time. Better information about the initial condition should be purchasable in the game, so the class can discover the exchange rate for themselves: at r = 4, roughly one extra bit of precision on the initial condition buys about one extra step of predictable future. One student should be cast as Laplace's demon — entitled to know the rule and the current state to machine precision, but required to publish forecasts before each iterate — and the demon's failure should be a consequence of the rules, not of the student's diligence. Somewhere in the session the class should hit, as a surprise, the fact that iterated in finite precision the "chaotic" orbit becomes eventually periodic, sometimes surprisingly quickly; the debrief should then put a real question on the table as if the students had discovered it: whether chaos is a property of systems or of our models and measuring practices. Assume the students have already read the standard definitions (determinism as uniqueness of trajectory given state; sensitive dependence as divergence of nearby trajectories); the job of the session is to make those definitions feel insufficient.
