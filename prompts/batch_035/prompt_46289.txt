You are serving as a philosophical consultant: a philosopher of science trained in epistemology and decision theory, who has spent enough time around regulatory agencies to know how the rules actually operate on the ground, not just how they read on paper. Please think carefully and work in stages. First, brainstorm internally: generate a list of candidate philosophical commitments that the regulatory pattern below might be resting on, and test each candidate against the details I give you — a commitment counts as presupposed only if rejecting it would force some change in the practice; discard the ones the practice could survive without. Then write a structured analysis that (a) reconstructs the implicit view, (b) assesses whether its parts are consistent with one another, and (c) says concretely what would change if that view were replaced.

Here is the practice that puzzles me. In the United States, a company that wants to market a new food additive must convince the FDA beforehand that the substance is safe — the burden of proof sits on the proponent, and absence of evidence of harm is treated as a reason to hold off rather than a reason to proceed. A provision from the 1958 food additives amendments goes further: if a substance causes cancer when fed to humans or animals in any test dose, it may not be approved as a food additive at any level of exposure, however small — no dose is low enough, and exposure in the real diet is irrelevant. New prescription drugs have the same shape: nothing reaches patients until the manufacturer has affirmatively demonstrated safety and efficacy. Yet the same legal system, in the same domain of ingestible products, treats dietary supplements under nearly the opposite default — a supplement can go to market with no premarket safety review at all, and the burden falls on the agency to prove harm before it can act. Across the Atlantic, European food law explicitly names precaution among its principles, and the chemicals regime requires manufacturers to generate safety data before market access, while substances already in use when the rules arrived are generally presumed acceptable.

So my questions. First, what epistemology of evidence does this pattern presuppose? The regime seems to treat long familiarity as if it were data — as though a century of consumption without visible catastrophe functions as evidence of safety. Under what account of induction is that legitimate, and does the practice presuppose that novelty, or being synthetic, resets the evidential clock? Second, what decision rule does the zero-tolerance cancer clause presuppose — a no-threshold dose–response model, a maximin rule under uncertainty, a refusal to trade small probabilities against benefits — and is it consistent with the rest of the regime, which elsewhere appears to accept that hazard is not harm and that the question is always which exposure? Third, can the additive/supplement asymmetry be squared with any single theory of the burden of proof, or does the system hold incompatible commitments depending on which aisle of the store you are standing in? Where several different underlying views would license exactly the same pattern, tell me the practice is philosophically underdetermined there, and say what additional premise would be needed to pin it down. Finally, if regulators swapped the implicit view for a straightforward expected-value, risk-based one — safety judged by actual exposure rather than hazard alone, and burdens of proof assigned by expected impact rather than by category — describe concretely what would change: which defaults flip, which products on the shelf would disappear or newly appear, and which parts of the practice would carry on untouched because they never needed the philosophy in the first place. At the end, briefly report which of your brainstormed candidates survived as genuine presuppositions and which you discarded as mere affinities.
