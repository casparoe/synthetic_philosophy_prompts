The question they were answering was:

"Grice distinguishes what a sentence conventionally means from what a speaker may implicate by uttering it. (a) Explain the difference between conventional and conversational implicature, give an example of each, and say what role the cancellability test is supposed to play. (b) Explain how, on Grice's account, the maxim of Quantity generates the standard implicature that someone who says 'Some of my friends are vegetarian' believes not all of them are. Say whether this implicature is generalized or particularized and why. (c) State Grice's analysis of speaker meaning in terms of intentions, and explain why the analysis needs the hearer's *recognition* of the speaker's intention, and not just the intention itself. And here's the student's answer (transcribed as written):

"(a) An implicature is something the speaker communicates over and above what the sentence literally says. The two kinds differ in their source. Conventional implicature is carried by particular words, like 'but' in 'She is poor but honest' — the truth conditions are the same as with 'and', and the word just signals a contrast between the clauses. Conversational implicature instead arises from the Cooperative Principle, the presumption that the speaker is trying to make a helpful contribution, which Grice took over from David Lewis's work on convention. The Cooperative Principle breaks down into maxims of Quantity, Quality, Relation, and Manner. Conversational implicatures pass the cancellability test: you can cancel 'Some of my friends are vegetarian' by adding '— in fact, all of them are', so what was implicated is not entailed. Since conventional implicature is also non-truth-conditional, it passes the cancellability test too: 'She is poor but honest, though I don't mean to suggest any contrast between being poor and being honest.' So cancellation separates implicature from entailment, but doesn't separate the two kinds of implicature from each other, which is fine since they work the same way anyway.

(b) The maxim of Quantity says to make your contribution as informative as required. When the speaker flouts Quantity by saying only 'Some of my friends are vegetarian' instead of the stronger statement, the hearer, assuming the speaker is being cooperative, reasons that if the speaker believed all of them were, she would have said so; so she must believe not all are. This is a particularized implicature, because it depends on the assumption of cooperativity holding in that context rather than on the meanings of the words, which is what makes something generalized.

(c) Grice's analysis: a speaker means something by an utterance when the speaker intends the utterance to produce a belief in the hearer, and the hearer's belief is produced by the utterance. No mention of truth conditions is needed — meaning is a matter of belief production. Consider the case from the question: she intends him to infer she's open to a date, and he does come to believe it because of her leaving the address, so on Grice's analysis she has meant that she's open to a date. But intuitively she only means it if he's meant to recognize what she's doing — which Grice's analysis as I stated it does capture, since his recognition is part of how her intention was carried out. However this seems to show the analysis is circular: it explains communication by means of the very recognition it is trying to analyze, so Grice's project of reducing speaker meaning to intentions fails."
