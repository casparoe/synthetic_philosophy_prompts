I should say upfront that I have no academic background in philosophy. A couple of years ago I picked up a cheap edition of Pascal's scattered notes, the ones published after his death, mostly out of curiosity, and I got stuck on the fragments about justice and force. I have been chewing on them on my own since, and I think I finally understand why they disturb me, but I want to test my understanding by having someone satirize it. If the satire comes out right, that will tell me I have the ideas straight.

Here is the picture I have formed from those fragments, stated in my own words. Pascal observes that justice without force is helpless, because the righteous get crushed; force without justice is tyranny, so the strong have an interest in dressing themselves up as just. He notices that we call laws just, but when he asks why we obey them, the honest answers come back circular: we obey because they are the law, because they are custom, because they have been around long enough that they no longer look arbitrary. He openly admits that we cannot take what is truly just and make it strong; the only thing in our power is to take what is strong and get it called just. He presents all this as a lament — a diagnosis of something broken in the human condition, with the proper cure being the joining of justice and force, which he thought we could not accomplish on our own. What disturbs me is how easily the same observations read as a manual instead of a lament, and I want a piece of satire built on exactly that reversal.

The form I would like is a marketing page for a management consultancy, around 600 words, written in the breathless tone of business-to-business software advertising, selling a service you might call Legitimacy-as-a-Service. The consultancy's pitch should be Pascal's diagnosis repackaged as a product: the firm does not waste clients' money trying to make their rules just, it makes their rules obeyed, and then lets custom do the rest. The page should include tiered pricing plans, three or four invented client testimonials, and a short FAQ. At least one testimonial should come from a skeptical client, perhaps an ethics officer, whose objection is genuinely Pascal's — for instance that calling something just merely because it can be enforced empties the word of meaning — and the firm's confident reply should contain the very trap Pascal identified, stated with a straight face. Somewhere the page should advertise a Customization Timeline, since Pascal noted that custom is what makes arbitrary arrangements feel right, and a rule looks unjust only while it still looks new; the firm should quote lead times for making a policy seem like it has always been there. A legal disclaimer at the bottom should quietly concede that the firm cannot make anything just, only believed, and should present this as a standard limitation of liability.

Two constraints on the humor. First, it should come from getting Pascal right: someone who knows these fragments well should recognize every joke as an exaggerated version of something he actually says, and should feel the satire is fair to him. Second, the piece should never announce that Pascal meant this as a warning, but a careful reader should come away uneasy, sensing that the cheerful consultancy is profiting from a gap Pascal spent those fragments grieving. The satire's target is the readers who mistake his diagnosis for a business opportunity, not the man himself.

Please use only the ideas as I have described them above; do not pad the piece with other parts of Pascal's thought, and do not put any words in Pascal's mouth beyond paraphrase. Beyond the length and the required elements, the details — the firm's name, the pricing, the testimonials, the FAQ questions — are yours to invent.
