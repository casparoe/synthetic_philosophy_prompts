There's a guy in my philosophy of mind reading group who keeps reaching for "competence without comprehension" and it's starting to do a LOT of work in our discussions. He says Dennett coined it. It gets deployed basically any time someone asks whether a system "really" understands something: does the language model we all argue about understand French, or does it just produce fluent French? Competence without comprehension, next question. Does a thermostat want the room to be 20 degrees? Does a bee know where the hive is? Do individual neurons grasp anything? Every single time, the phrase gets trotted out like it ends the discussion, and honestly half the room treats it as a deep insight and the other half (me included lately) suspects it's just a description, not an explanation.

The thing is, I suspect the phrase is actually several different claims wearing one trenchcoat, and they vary a lot in how strong they are. At the trivial end it seems to just say "some systems do smart-seeming stuff without anything inside understanding it," which nobody disputes. At the strong end it sounds like it's saying comprehension is literally nothing over and above sufficiently complicated competence — that once competence gets elaborate enough, calling it understanding adds no further fact, so asking "but does it REALLY understand?" is like asking whether the machine "really" computes. And maybe there are readings in between: that understanding requires some extra architecture (recurrent feedback, world models, grounding in action), or that it requires a certain kind of history, or that it's a matter of degree rather than kind. Our guy also says Darwin and Turing made the same "inversion of reasoning" — that Darwin showed you get design without a designer, Turing showed you get comprehension (or at least its effects) without comprehension, same move twice — but I don't fully see how those two pair up, one is about the origin of a system and the other is about what a system is doing when it runs.

Can you help me untangle this? Please lay out the distinct precise claims "competence without comprehension" could be making — a menu of readings, from the boring-but-true to the genuinely radical — and for each one say whether it's plausible, whether it's actually doing any explanatory work, and, crucially, whether it would even support the move my group keeps making with it, i.e. treating the question "does it understand?" as already answered (or as meaningless) rather than open. If some readings support that move and some don't, I'd like to know which is which, because I want to bring it up at our next meeting without being that guy who quibbles for an hour. And if you can clarify whether the Darwin analogy and the Turing analogy are really the same inversion or only superficially similar, that would help too.
