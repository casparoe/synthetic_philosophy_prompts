I've basically bought the competitiveness constraint as the governing fact of AI safety: any alignment technique that costs real capability -- makes the model less useful, refuses things customers want, needs big extra compute, delays launch -- just won't get deployed at the frontier, because the first lab that eats that tax looses to the lab that doesn't. So on my view the whole game is finding safety measures with zero (or ideally negative) alignment tax, and everything else is a hobby for regulators. What would count against this? Not the standard "regulation could change the incentives" hand-wave -- I want it ranked honestly. Observations from how labs have actually behaved that should move me (labs voluntarily holding back a model, spending frontier-scale compute on safety work, dropping a feature customers wanted for safety reasons); arguments that the tax might not really be privately borne even without regulation (liability, insurance, reputational stuff, employee revolts); and the more annoying question of whether "capability" and "safety" are even on independent axes such that a tax is a coherent thing to measure -- maybe some safety properties come free with scale or are downstream of the same training that produces capability, in which case my framing of the problem is confused in a way no evidence would fix. Also give me an honest read on whether my version has been quietly qualified into unfalsifiability: if I respond to every case of a lab eating a tax with "well, they must've expected it to pay off eventually," is that legitimate model-building or self-sealing?
