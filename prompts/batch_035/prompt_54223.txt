I'm a retired geochemist who spent thirty-seven years at a national lab, and since retiring I've gotten deep into philosophy of science, mostly by joining a reading group at the university near my house. Our last meeting turned into a two-hour argument between two of the members, and I was so busy trying to follow it that I never managed to sort out who was actually winning. I'm hoping you can referee.

The argument was about whether we should believe our current scientific theories are true, given the history of science. Marcus (retired high-energy physicist) was the pessimist, and Priya (a philosophy grad student) was defending some kind of realism.

Here's roughly how it went, in rounds:

(1) Marcus said it's basically an induction: scientists in 1800 had theories they were confident about, theories that did real work, and basically all of them are now considered false -- phlogiston, caloric, the ether. If you asked a well-informed scientist in 1880 whether the luminiferous ether existed, he'd have said obviously yes. So the base rate for "currently successful theory that later turns out false" is terrible, and by our own standards we should expect our theories to go the same way, which means believing them now is irrational.

(2) Priya answered that the induction only works if you count those old theories as genuinely successful, and they weren't -- not in the sense that matters. Phlogiston never made novel predictions, it mostly explained what it was built to explain after the fact. Real success is predicting things you didn't already know, the way relativity did with Mercury's perihelion. So the pessimist's list is full of theories that were never successful in the first place, and the induction from them to our theories collapses.

(3) Marcus said this is retrospective gerrymandering -- you only get to say phlogiston "wasn't really successful" because you know how the story ended. Contemporary chemists thought it was doing fine; it organized a whole experimental program, and Priestley was isolating oxygen while believing in it as dephlogisticated air. If the standard for success is only visible in the rearview mirror, the realist has just defined "success" as "whatever survives," which is circular and predicts nothing going forward.

(4) Priya then made what seemed like a different move: maybe we shouldn't believe the full content of theories anyway, just their structural claims. Fresnel's equations for light carried over essentially intact into Maxwell's theory, even though the ether they were equations *about* got thrown out. So the pessimist is right about the posits and wrong about everything else: what survives theory change is the mathematical structure, and that's the part realism should be about.

(5) Marcus asked how, in 1850, you were supposed to know that Fresnel's equations were the structural part and the ether was the disposable content, rather than the other way around. If you can only draw the structure/content line after the revolution, structural realism is just a description of what happened, not a guide to what to believe now. And he added that anyway, when he was running experiments, nobody in the lab believed a model the way you believe your spouse is faithful -- you used it, you trusted it in its domain, you expected it to break somewhere. He said realism was a philosopher's fantasy about how scientists relate to their theories.

(6) Priya's comeback was that this instrumentalist picture is exactly what the realist can't accept, because it leaves the success of science a miracle -- if our theories are just useful fictions, why on earth do they make accurate predictions about things nobody has measured yet? Something has to be right about them to explain that. And she said Marcus's lab attitude was itself a kind of realism about the near term, so he was refuting himself out of his own biography.

Then they both turned to me, because at some point I'd muttered that in my whole career I never once worked with a model I'd have called "true," and they each told me my experience proved their point. That's where I got off the bus.

So, please adjudicate this for me, round by round: for each of the six moves, say whether it's right, partly right, or missing the point, and why. I'd especially like to know (a) whether Priya's move in round 2 is circular in the way Marcus says, or whether there's a non-circular version of it; (b) whether the structural realism move in round 4 actually answers the pessimist or just changes the subject; and (c) whether my own observation -- that working scientists don't go around "believing" their models -- bears on this dispute at all, or whether it's beside the point, since maybe the question is about what scientists are *committed* to rather than what they'd say about themselves. And if both of them are missing something obvious, please say what.

One housekeeping thing: please answer in American English. I've lived in New Mexico for forty years and I just cannot take another spelling of "sceptical."
