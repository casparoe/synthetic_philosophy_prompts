I teach a mid-level seminar on action theory (18 students, one 75-minute session) and I want them to feel, rather than merely parse, the puzzle in Kavka's toxin case: the reward is fixed before the drinking is due, and the state that earns it — intending, now, to drink later — seems both required and unformable precisely because of what earns it. Design an in-class game with real but small stakes (I can spend around $25 on prizes, or you can design a payoff scheme that needs no money at all) built around drinking a harmless but genuinely unpleasant tea, or an equivalent mild chore, later that evening. The game should have two arms, and this is the part I care most about: in one arm, students are rewarded for a prediction about their own future behavior — they bet on whether they will in fact drink tonight; in the other, the reward is attached to their present commitment to drink. I want the two arms to come apart, so that students discover experientially that confidence about what you'll do can be bought with evidence and incentives, while intending to do it apparently cannot be turned on for those reasons. Include rules, materials, a timeline, the payoff scheme, and a debrief that maps what actually happened onto the formal distinction between belief that one will and intention to. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about where the classroom version necessarily diverges from the ideal case — the delay is hours rather than a day, the toxin is bad tea rather than illness, and crucially I can only pay on a self-report of intention rather than on the intention itself. If you think the design can't work as specified — that the two arms won't come apart, or that paying on self-reports changes the problem in a way that dissolves rather than reproduces the puzzle — say so plainly and tell me what you would change.
