Last night, after a long seminar on twentieth-century jurisprudence, a friend and I got into an argument that ran well past midnight, and I would like a considered answer rather than the stalemate we reached. The subject was the Hart–Fuller exchange of the late 1950s: the grudge-informer problem, Hart's insistence that a wicked or opaque system can still be law, merely bad law, and Fuller's reply that a system must satisfy certain procedural desiderata — publicity, generality, constancy, the possibility of compliance, and congruence between declared rules and official action — if it is to deserve the name of law at all. My friend is a committed positivist and thinks Fuller's position is a moral fantasy dressed as a conceptual claim; I found myself unable to say where, exactly, a legal system becomes so degenerate that the concept of law has been stretched past breaking. At that point my friend, who is writing a thesis on Kafka, announced that the entire debate had been dramatised a decade or more before it was held, and that if I wanted to see a system in which every one of Fuller's desiderata is violated while the apparatus of legality remains, I should reread The Trial.

So here is what I would like. Read The Trial through the lens of the Hart–Fuller debate, taking both the novel and the debate seriously. Joseph K. is arrested one morning by officers who cannot name a charge; the court sits in attics of tenement blocks; advocates speak of doctrine and precedent as though these were settled bodies of learning, yet no rule is ever produced; K. is told at once that the laws probably exist but that they are known only to the court, and in the cathedral a priest tells him the parable of the doorkeeper stationed before the law, admitting the door was meant only for him. Map this onto the debate: on Hart's account, is the court a legal system with a defective rule of recognition, or not a legal system at all? On Fuller's, is the novel an illustration of his thesis that legality is an achievement — that law which fails the inner morality of law loses the very quality that makes it law? And where does the novel resist both readings — in K.'s own stubborn insistence on treating the court as law, demanding acquittal from a tribunal whose authority he might simply have declined to acknowledge? My friend's parting shot was that Kafka vindicates Fuller: K.'s destruction is precisely what happens to legal order when the desiderata are systematically violated. My suspicion is that the novel is darker and sidestakes neither: it shows a man destroyed not by the court but by his own refusal to concede that it is not law. Say which of us, if either, the text supports, which scenes carry the most argumentative weight, and what the novel adds to the debate that the Harvard exchange itself does not. Please write your answer in British English.
