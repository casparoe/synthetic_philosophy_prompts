I write novels for a living, which means I spend my working hours simulating the moral emotions of people who do not exist, and this has given me some prejudices I would like either confirmed or cured by an essay.

Here is the question, in two parts: which is the better moral emotion, guilt or shame — and can a culture deliberately choose which of the two to run on?

By way of orientation, the essay should take seriously the standard contrast, but should not stop there. As I understand the textbook distinction: guilt attaches to the deed, is discharged by confession, reparation, or punishment, and leaves the self intact once the account is settled; shame attaches to the whole self, wants above all to be hidden, and is not obviously discharged by anything at all. So far, so familiar. What I want the essay to engage are the complications, of which I will name four.

First, my own suspicion, offered from the workshop: guilt is narratively tractable in a way shame is not. Guilt generates scenes — the confession, the restitution, the long arc of atonement — whereas shame terminates scenes; it makes the character lock the door and lie on the floor. It is possible that fiction, including the moral fictions a culture tells about itself, flatters guilt for reasons internal to storytelling rather than to morality, and that guilt's reputation as the more mature emotion is partly an artifact of the fact that guilt is easier to dramatize. The essay should assess whether this suspicion survives scrutiny, and what follows if it does.

Second, the vicariousness problem. Guilt seems to require that the agent actually did the deed; shame does not. One can be ashamed of one's father, one's country, a stranger who shares one's accent; there is no analogous guilt-by-association that is not either a metaphor or a symptom. Is this a defect of shame — an irrational emotion that punishes the self for what the self did not do — or is it the only moral emotion equipped to do genuinely collective work, since guilt can never travel across persons except by strained analogy? I would like a position on this, not a survey.

Third, the internalized-spectator dispute. The classic objection to shame is that it is heteronomous: it answers to an audience, so a shame-governed agent is moral only when watched. The reply, which I find suggestive but underargued, is that shame's spectator is normally internalized — one dreads the gaze of an internalized other, a figure one has come to admire or to want to be — and that this figure can be one's own ideal, so that shame is no more hostage to actual onlookers than guilt is. But note the counter-riposte: guilt's content is equally socially installed; one only feels guilty about what one's culture has coded as a violation, so the autonomy contrast may be an illusion on both sides. The essay should adjudicate this exchange.

Fourth, the question of cultural choice. There is an old anthropological chestnut to the effect that some cultures are shame cultures and others guilt cultures, and whatever its empirical defects as taxonomy, it at least raises the political question: can a society deliberately shift its dominant register? Some things to weigh: legal and bureaucratic institutions — courts, audits, compliance regimes — seem to presuppose guilt, since they want violations that can be itemized, adjudicated, and discharged; mass social media seems to resurrect the pillory and make shame cheap, instant, and unanswerable by reparation; and there have been conscious attempts, in various places and eras, to replace honor- and shame-based norms with inner-directed conscience. Does the evidence suggest a culture can choose, or merely that the register drifts with the technology and the paperwork, choice or no choice? And if it could choose, which should it choose?

Constraints on the essay: it should weigh the arguments on both sides fairly, but it must not end in a plague-on-both-houses. Defend a verdict. A conditional or qualified verdict is acceptable — for instance, that shame is the better emotion for some moral work and guilt for other moral work, with the two works named precisely, and a clear account of why the division of labor falls where you say it does — but whatever conclusion you reach, you must earn it against the strongest opposing considerations, not merely assert it. And on the second question, please be definite about the sense in which a culture can or cannot choose: if the answer is that it can only adjust the exchange rate between the two emotions rather than suppress one, say so explicitly and explain what such adjustment would actually consist in.
