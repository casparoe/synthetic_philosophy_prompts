Two members of my decision-theory reading group, Priya and Daniel, spent an entire meeting arguing about Newcomb's problem and a variant I brought up, and they ended by asking me to referee. I could not, so I am passing the exchange along to you.

The setup we had agreed on: a predictor with a 99% track record has already placed either $1,000,000 or nothing in an opaque box B, depending on its prediction of whether you will take B alone or also a transparent box A containing $1,000. The boxes are filled before you choose, and nothing you do afterward can alter their contents.

The exchange went roughly as follows. (1) Daniel opened with dominance: whatever is in box B, taking both boxes gets you $1,000 more; a decision theory that instructs you to knowingly choose the poorer outcome is defective on its face; one-boxers walk away rich through the luck of their dispositions, much as people who win by superstition walk away rich. (2) Priya answered that an agent disposed to take both boxes is disposed to walk away with $1,000, that the predictor's 99% accuracy guarantees this, and that Daniel's advice is tuned to the rare predictor error rather than the overwhelming probability; she said she would happily accept the label 'irrational' and keep the million. (3) Daniel conceded that she would very likely get the million, but said that this changes nothing about what she should do standing at the boxes: the contents are already fixed, and her dispositional point conflates what she should have wanted installed in herself beforehand with what she should do now; he illustrated with the version where the predictor has died since filling the boxes, so that no future fact about her can retroactively affect anything. (4) Priya replied with the simulation thought: if the predictor predicts by running a faithful simulation of you, then while you deliberate you cannot tell whether you are the agent standing at the boxes or the simulation whose choice is being recorded, and the symmetry favors taking just B; two-boxing in whichever one you are tends to leave the actual you poor. (5) Daniel said he has no probability to assign to 'I am currently the simulation rather than the agent,' and he refuses to let a decision turn on an indexical possibility for which no odds exist; he added that even if he were the simulation, his choosing two boxes there would not cause anything bad for the agent, only correlate with it.

Then I raised the variant that derailed everything. Suppose the predictor's 99% overall accuracy decomposes unevenly: it is essentially perfect about people who choose quickly and unreflectively, but nearly a coin-flip about people who engage in explicit deliberation about whether to one-box at the moment of choice. We all agreed that once you are standing at the boxes and have already deliberated, you should take both — conditional on your own deliberation, the opaque box is a near coin-flip, and dominance does the rest. But they split on what the variant shows. Priya said it vindicates a two-level picture: the act you perform is assessed against the situation you are actually in, but the more fundamental assessment is of the agent and her policies over time, and the rational policy, formed before learning about any predictor, is to cultivate an unreflective one-boxing disposition and to avoid deliberating in such situations; Daniel's act-level verdicts, she said, are a special case of this and cannot be the whole story. Daniel said that this reduces one-boxing to precommitment — something it might be prudent to arrange while it is still 'irrationally' unavailable to you, like hiding the car keys from your future self — and that calling policy selection the more fundamental level of rationality is question-begging, since the whole dispute is over which level the word 'rational' properly describes; he added that advising people not to deliberate about the case they are in is self-defeating in the way con-artists' advice to trust them is.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of (1) through (5), say whether the speaker is right, partially right, or clearly mistaken, and why; then referee the disagreement over what the deliberation-sensitive variant shows. In particular: does the variant favor either side, is it merely a disguised version of the familiar gene-linked-behavior cases, and does Daniel's 'self-defeating advice' charge against Priya's anti-deliberation policy land? Finally, tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing about the underlying dispute between evidential and causal approaches.
