so here's my situation: novelist, four books in, currently writing this multiverse thing where the same woman exists in three overlapping timelines — same name, same scar on the eyebrow, increasingly different histories. my editor keeps flagging whole chapters with "wait, is this literally her or just a copy of her?" and ive realized that's not an editorial note, its the philosophical question the entire book turns on. is the woman in timeline B the same person (so what happens to her happens to *her*, and her death is a death) or is she only a counterpart — someone else entirely, similar in the ways that matter but with no more claim to being her than a photo does? the whole emotional register of the book flips depending on the answer, since either my narrator is grieving a real loss or just mourning a near-identical stranger.

write me a pantoum about this, and please let the form do the philosophical work instead of just naming it. a pantoum repeats its lines in new stanzas, surrounded by different lines, and each recurrence shifts the sense — which is exactly the structure of my problem, because a line that comes back is recognizably "the same line" and yet arguably only a counterpart of itself once its surroundings have changed. I want the reader to finish genuinely unsure which reading is right, and the final stanza should land the returning lines somewhere their first appearance didn't prepare you for — one of them meaning close to the opposite of what it meant at the top. someone who knows the counterpart-theory literature should smile in recognition; someone who doesnt should still get a real poem about repetition, revision, and loss.

I want to slot it between the first two timelines as a section break, so keep it tight — however many stanzas the form needs, no more. and dont make it cute about it, this is a book about grief wearing a multiverse costume, not the other way around.
