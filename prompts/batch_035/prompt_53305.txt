I'm putting together a seminar on the ethics of war and we're screening Nolan's Oppenheimer at the end of it. I want a reading of the film against Elizabeth Anscombe's protest when Oxford gave Truman an honorary degree in 1956 -- her claim that the bombing of Hiroshima was the deliberate killing of the innocent, i.e. murder, and that honouring the man who ordered it displayed a corruption of moral judgement that no sophistication excuses. My hunch is that the film is basically a study of Oppenheimer's conscience: the guilt, the haunting, the security hearing framed as a trial of his soul, and the fact that we never see Hiroshima itself, only its refracted image through his imagination at the Los Alamos rally. Anscombe would presumably say that's exactly the wrong lens -- on her view the question was never whether the man suffers or feels bad about it, but whether the act was murder, and the film risks doing what the Oxford ceremony did: getting us absorbed in the agent's inner drama instead of looking at the deed. But I want you to push back where the reading strains: is there something the film dramatises that her pamphlet's categories don't quite reach, e.g. the sense that by 1954 the question of responsibility had become genuinely diffuse, or the closing shift from guilt to a future of mutual annihilation? And does the cheering crowd scene suggest Nolan knows exactly what he's withholding? Don't quote the film or her pamphlet verbatim -- paraphrase. Please write in British English, essay length, and don't be afraid to conclude the film partially vindicates her -- or partially doesn't.
