I am the managing editor of a peer-reviewed journal that publishes empirical research from authors across a wide range of countries and institutions, while our reviewer pool is drawn mostly from Western Europe and North America. A few times a year we receive complaints from authors who believe a reviewer dismissed their work because of who they are — a researcher at a poorly funded institution, a non-native speaker of English, someone working within a national research tradition the reviewer has never encountered. At the same time, the large majority of reviewer skepticism is legitimate: most weak manuscripts really are weak. We currently handle these complaints ad hoc, and I suspect inconsistently, so I want a standing procedure.

Please write a decision procedure my editorial team can run on a complaint of this kind, grounded in the literature on epistemic injustice — specifically the distinction between testimonial injustice (a credibility deficit that tracks prejudice concerning the speaker rather than anything in the content of what is said) and hermeneutical injustice (a gap in the shared interpretive concepts that leaves certain claims or experiences unarticulable or uninterpretable for the audience). The procedure must be usable by a single editor in under half an hour, with only the manuscript, the reviews, and the author's rebuttal in hand. It should specify the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the points at which the procedure concludes that the complaint fails and the original decision stands; the points at which it requires the review to be redone or the reviewer replaced; and, marked explicitly, the points at which it must hand the decision back to a person because the question is one of judgment rather than fact. I want those hand-off points flagged rather than buried, since the procedure's credibility with my editorial board depends on its not pretending to eliminate judgment.

Two complications the procedure must address rather than ignore. First, double-blind review leaks: reviewers see the author's self-citations, prose style, characteristic terminology, and sometimes a recognizable local research program, so prejudice can operate through cues that no one names and that the reviewer may not be aware of applying. The procedure therefore cannot rely on the reviewer having said anything openly disparaging. Second, the genuinely hard case: a reviewer's low trust in an unfamiliar research tradition may partly track real methodological weakness and partly track mere unfamiliarity, and these can point in the same direction on the same manuscript. The procedure should say how to separate the two, or state honestly that it cannot.

After setting out the procedure, run it on three complaints. First: an author from a low-income country, writing in visibly non-native English, whose qualitative interview findings a reviewer dismissed in a single line as anecdotal. Second: an author whose paper builds on concepts from her national scholarly tradition, rendered in terms the reviewer called incoherent, with no engagement beyond that label. Third: a well-cited author from a famous institution whose paper a reviewer criticized harshly point by point, and who complains of the same kind of unfair treatment as the others. At least one of the three should come out with a decision I will not like, and I would like you to say plainly, at the end, what the procedure cannot do — the situations where it will misfire no matter how carefully it is followed.
