I'm a philosopher presenting at one of those cross-disciplinary welfare workshops next month, and the room is going to be a nightmare mix: maybe twenty animal welfare researchers, several of whom work on sentience criteria for invertebrates and treat the nociception vs. pain distinction as load-bearing, a handful of RL engineers from a lab that trains agents for long-horizon tasks (they've all seen agents that game their own reward channels, and I fully expect them to bring it up as a data point in whatever direction suits them), and two philosophers of mind, one of whom is basically a dualist about affect and proud of it.

My thesis, roughly: the whole fight about whether RL agents can suffer is conducted at the wrong level. The reward-is-valence people and the reward-is-just-a-number people both assume that if there's a fact of the matter here, it lives in the architecture. I want to argue instead that valence is something evolution stumbled onto as a solution to a specific learning problem — an agent that has to keep learning online, in real time, about threats to its own persistence, with limited compute and no ability to pause and retrain from scratch. Negative valence on my view is bookkeeping metadata on that process: it's what makes certain prediction errors globally prioritized. So the question isn't "does the agent have negative reward," and it isn't "is the agent made of the right stuff" — it's whether the agent faces that kind of learning problem and implements anything like that prioritization structure.

Consequences I'm committed to, so the objections can target them: most current agents don't suffer (no persistent self-model, everything gets retrained off-policy, no ongoing threat to track), but also some animals that behaviorally look quite simple might have the relevant structure, and — this is where I expect trouble — whether an agent suffers is partly a fact about its training regime and not just its weights, which people find weird.

What I want is a prep sheet for the Q&A: the objections I'll actually get from this specific mix, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased in that audience's own vocabulary — and note the welfare folks and the RL people will phrase essentially the same objection in totally different terms, I want both versions. Each one with a two-minute reply I can actually deliver, and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Two things specifically. First, I suspect my worst objection comes from the welfare side: that my criterion smuggles in a cognitive threshold which, applied consistently, would push some animals out of the moral circle, and I want to know whether I have a real answer there or just a hopeful one. Second, tell me what the question I'm hoping nobody asks is — I have a feeling it's the mirrored one, where someone asks whether I'd accept the conclusion about my own suffering being prioritized prediction error, and whether there's a way to answer it without losing the room. Be honest if there isn't.
