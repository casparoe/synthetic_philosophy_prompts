I'm a statistics PhD student working on Bayesian model selection, so my day-to-day vocabulary is things like marginal likelihoods, prior predictive distributions, Occam factors, minimum description length, cross-validation, regularization, and generalization. I have to admit that when I first read about inference to the best explanation, it sounded almost trivially familiar: the idea that we accept the hypothesis that would, if true, best explain the evidence — with "best explain" cashed out via virtues like unification, mechanism, precision, and the like. My instinct is that this is just model selection under a different name: the "explanatory virtues" are the Occam factor and the fit term decomposing the marginal likelihood, "inference to the best explanation" is posterior maximization, and the philosophers' debate is a pre-formal groping toward what we now write down explicitly.

I'd like you to test that instinct properly, because I suspect it's partly wrong, and I'd like to know in exactly which parts. Please do the following:

1. Translate the core positions of the IBE debate into my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. At minimum, I want translations of: (a) the basic schema itself — accepting the hypothesis that provides the best explanation; (b) Peter Lipton's distinction between the "loveliness" of an explanation (its structural appeal: how much it unifies, how specific its mechanism is) and its "likeliness" (how probable it is on the evidence), and his claim that inference is guided by the former but tracked by the latter; and (c) the worry, pressed by Bas van Fraassen and others, that IBE either collapses into ordinary probabilistic inference (in which case it says nothing new) or adds something illicit on top of it (in which case it double-counts the evidence).

2. For each translation, give me an honest residue report. Which parts map cleanly? Which parts map only with distortion — and say what the distortion is, since a translation that quietly imports my framework's assumptions (e.g., that all epistemic value is encoded in a probability distribution, or that model comparison must come down to a scalar score) is not a translation but a verdict. Which parts have no expression in my vocabulary at all?

3. Then take a position on the residue. My working hypothesis is that the untranslatable remainder falls into two very different kinds, and I want you to tell me whether that's right. The first kind would be a defect of my vocabulary: things the philosophers can see that are real but that the model-selection formalism has no way to say — for instance, the felt difference between a hypothesis that merely fits the data and one that also makes the data non-coincidental, or the role of asking why this evidence arose rather than other nearby evidence. The second kind would be a defect of the original: parts of the IBE talk that survive nowhere because they were less clear than they looked — e.g., whether "loveliness" is anything over and above a bundle of priors and structural constraints that we already write down. If you think the division goes differently, or that the residue is all of one kind, say so and argue for it.

One last request, and it's the one I care about most. Where the translation fails, I want to know whether the failure is principled or merely notational — that is, whether someone with my vocabulary could in principle come to see what the philosophers are pointing at, or whether seeing it requires standing outside the probabilistic framework altogether. I'm not asking you to referee between the frameworks; I'm asking whether the residual disagreements are the kind that a better formalism could settle, or the kind that show up precisely as the cost of having a formalism at all.
