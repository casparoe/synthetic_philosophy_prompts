I work as a career counselor at a university alumni office, seeing clients in fifty-minute sessions, usually four or five a week, most of them facing decisions they regard as irreversible: leaving a profession after a decade of training, emigrating for a partner, declining a partnership track to care for a parent, retiring early. My training is in counseling, not philosophy, and my clients have none at all. Lately I have been drawn to Nietzsche's thought experiment of eternal recurrence — the one presented in The Gay Science as a demon's announcement that you must live this exact life, with every pain and every joy, over and over again, in the same sequence, for eternity — as a possible test I could use with clients. My worry is that I would either water it down into a generic do-what-makes-you-happy exercise or misuse it on people it would harm.

Please turn the thought experiment into a decision procedure I could actually run with a client in session. Specifically:

1. Lay out the steps in order: the questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, and how one answer moves the client to the next step rather than merely generating more conversation.
2. Keep the procedure faithful to what the thought experiment is doing in Nietzsche — an evaluative test of whether one affirms a life, closer to a diagnostic of one's orientation toward existence than a prudential rule for choosing between options — while making it usable by someone with no philosophical background. Say explicitly which distinctions from the original idea must survive the translation and which can be dropped.
3. Mark the points where the procedure must stop and hand the decision back to the client because the test does not apply or because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts, and mark where a judgment call cannot be eliminated no matter how procedural I try to be.
4. Be honest about the characteristic misfires: that the test measures how a client feels about a life rather than the wisdom of a choice; that it may select for intensity or grandiosity over prudence; that it can be cruel to clients whose current lives feel unbearable; and that it risks becoming circular, since a client may have to already love a future for the test to endorse it.

Then run the procedure on three cases:

(a) A twenty-nine-year-old lawyer who reports that she can pass the test for two completely different futures — partnership in a firm versus a lower-paying public-interest role — and expects the test to break the tie. Does the procedure have anything to say, or is the tie exactly where it goes silent?

(b) A client with persistent depressive symptoms for whom no candidate future passes the test, not even marginally. What should the procedure conclude here, and how do I distinguish a verdict about his options from a symptom of his state?

(c) A client who passes the test with total, joyous enthusiasm for a decision I privately consider reckless — liquidating his retirement savings to fund a friend's startup. Include this case honestly: if the procedure ratifies his choice, say so, and tell me what, if anything, in the procedure still licenses me to push back.

Finally, note whether it matters for my procedure that Nietzsche may have taken recurrence literally, as a cosmological thesis about time and finite force, rather than as a mere heuristic — and whether my procedure has to take a stance on that question in order to work. I do not want reading lists or scholarly apparatus; I want something I can print on two pages and use on Tuesday.
