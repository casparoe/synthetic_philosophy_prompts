I am grading take-home finals for a graduate seminar in formal epistemology and would like a second opinion on one answer before I finalize the marks. The exam question read:

State Aumann's agreement theorem as precisely as you can, making explicit exactly what is assumed to be common knowledge. Then explain what work the common prior assumption does in the proof. Finally, does the theorem show that two rational agents who candidly exchange opinions with each other must end up agreeing? If not, is there a related result that comes closer?

The student's answer, reproduced in full:

Aumann (1976) showed that if two agents have common priors and their posteriors for an event are common knowledge, then those posteriors must be equal. The proof runs by induction on the number of states in the common knowledge event: within that event, the agents' posteriors are forced to coincide, so neither can rationally agree to disagree.

What is assumed to be common knowledge is the posteriors themselves: each agent knows the other's posterior, knows that the other knows it, and so on for a couple of further levels of the hierarchy. A few levels of this suffice, since once both posteriors have been announced, further iterations add nothing.

The common prior does the real work. If the agents could start from different priors, they could disagree even given identical information. With a common prior, differences in belief trace back to differences in information, and common knowledge of the posteriors screens off the informational differences that could matter.

Aumann also proved a dynamic version: if the agents take turns truthfully announcing their current posteriors, the announcements converge to a common posterior in finitely many rounds. So rational agents who talk long enough cannot remain in disagreement, even without any common knowledge being established in advance.

Hence, wherever two rational agents with common priors stay in disagreement, something has gone wrong: either the priors differed, or one of them is not rational.
