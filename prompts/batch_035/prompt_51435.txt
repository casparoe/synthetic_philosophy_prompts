I am writing a detective novel in which the sleuth's companion, a logician, insists that every piece of non-black, non-raven clutter at the crime scene — a white shoe, a green bicycle — is, strictly speaking, evidence for the hypothesis that all ravens are black, and the plot turns on whether the other characters ought to find this profound or absurd. Before I decide what my logician can honestly say, I need to understand the issue myself, beyond the textbook framing. Please explain the conceptual core of Hempel's raven paradox and then the parts that actually divide people: why the popular reply that a white shoe confirms the raven hypothesis only negligibly (because non-black things vastly outnumber ravens) is itself considered unsatisfying, and how the more careful Bayesian treatment, on which confirmation is relative to background knowledge, is supposed to dissolve the paradox — including the awkward result, associated with I. J. Good, that under some background assumptions observing a non-black non-raven can even count slightly against the hypothesis. I would like enough precision to know exactly which of my character's claims are defensible and which I must soften for dramatic honesty.
