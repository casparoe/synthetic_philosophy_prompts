I joined the patient and family advisory council of our regional organ procurement organization after my mother spent four years on dialysis waiting for a kidney; she was transplanted last spring, and I stayed on because the council is now consulted on a steady stream of donor-compensation proposals from the state legislature and from our own transplant centers. The proposals arriving on our agenda this year include: reimbursing the funeral expenses of deceased donors' families; a refundable state tax credit for living donors; a voucher scheme in which a living donor secures a priority claim on a future transplant for a designated loved one; and, raised by one of our transplant surgeons, the suggestion that we at least study the kind of open, fixed-price government purchasing of kidneys that Iran has run for decades. Meanwhile a council member whose town was flooded last summer wants to know whether the same reasoning would condemn or permit surge pricing on bottled water and generators during the emergency, or whether that is a different problem entirely.

I am not a philosopher, and neither are the other eleven council members. What I need is a decision procedure we can actually run, in about twenty minutes per proposal at a monthly meeting, that translates the philosophical debate about commodification — the repugnant-markets literature, though I don't need citations, just the considerations — into questions a nurse, an accountant, and a dialysis patient can answer. Specifically:

First, an ordered set of questions: for each one, say what would count as a sufficient answer, where the procedure should terminate in a recommendation against, and where it must explicitly hand off to a judgment call rather than pretending to settle the matter by rule.

Second, I want two distinctions to survive the simplification even if everything else is dropped. One is the difference between objecting to a market because of what it does to the people in it — desperation, unequal bargaining position, the danger of dealing with someone who has no real alternative — and objecting to it because of what buying and selling that very thing says or does, however fair the terms. The other is a genuine middle position: remedies short of prohibition, such as regulating the terms, paying in kind, or first building up the non-market alternative, in cases where the objection is to the market's conditions rather than its existence.

Third, run the procedure on three of the actual proposals: the funeral-expense reimbursement, the living-donor tax credit, and the open fixed-price market. For at least one of these I want the procedure to return an answer I will dislike, and I would rather see that happen plainly than be softened — I will admit that I want more kidneys available, and that any framework which condemned a scheme that might have shortened my mother's wait is going to be hard for me to argue for at a public meeting. Show me how the procedure forces that honesty rather than letting my stake quietly bend the result.

Fourth, say whether the procedure transfers to the price-gouging case or whether it is honestly inapplicable there, and if it transfers, what changes.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do. Several council members believe that no checklist can determine whether paying for organs changes what giving one means, and that this question will come down to values no matter how we structure the process. If they are right, mark where the wall is — and tell me whether it falls before or after the questions the procedure can answer.
