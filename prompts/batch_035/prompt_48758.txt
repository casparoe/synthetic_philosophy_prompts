I want to practice defending a Humean regularity theory of causation against a serious occasionalist opponent, and I would like you to play al-Ghazālī as he presents himself in the seventeenth discussion of the Incoherence. Your character's commitments are fixed: that there is no necessary connection between fire and burning, or between any cause and its effect; that what we call causation is just observed conjunction plus God's will; that God creates the burning in the cotton on the occasion of contact with fire, according to a custom of his that is stable but not guaranteed; and that miracles are interruptions of that custom, which is why they are possible precisely because no natural necessity constrains God. You may draw on his thought experiment about the man created suddenly with no prior experience of the world, and on his willingness to say that a body could be created with the accident of burning before any fire touches it. You should argue philosophically rather than merely cite scripture, and you should engage the strongest version of whatever I say rather than a caricature of it. You are a sharp disputant: concede small points when they are right, never concede the main thesis, and stay in character until I write the word stop. Keep each reply to a single paragraph so this reads like a live disputation.

Here is my opening move. You mock the philosophers for positing a necessary connection between fire and burning, but your own account — that God creates burning on the occasion of contact, following his custom — is my regularity theory wearing a turban. I say the fire–burning connection is nothing over and above constant conjunction; you say it is nothing over and above constant conjunction plus a habit of God whose continuation is not guaranteed. So we agree on the part of causation anyone could actually observe, and your addition makes things strictly worse. Your custom is revisable by fiat at any moment, without warning and without reason, since on your view God has no necessity to answer to; so the inductive inference by which I expect tomorrow's fire to behave like today's fire has, on your theory, no ground whatsoever, whereas on mine it is at least the continuation of the very regularity I am extrapolating. Before we even get to which of us has the better metaphysics: what does your occasionalism add to the observed conjunction, and why is that addition not simply an extra and unexplained regularity — God's custom — stacked behind the first one?
