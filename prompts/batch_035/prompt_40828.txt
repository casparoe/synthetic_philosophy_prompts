I'm a retired evolutionary biologist (most of my career was life-history work on fish, if that matters), and since retiring I've gotten deep into philosophy of science. The question that's been gnawing at me lately is evolutionary psychology: is it real science or just storytelling with a Darwinian vocabulary? My old colleagues are split -- half think it's just evolutionary biology applied to the brain, the other half roll their eyes and say it's a genre of fiction. Write me an essay that takes a side and actually defends it, not a survey of the debate. In defending your verdict, engage the strongest form of the just-so worry: that for almost any human behavior you observe, you can cook up an adaptive story after the fact, and that the "tests" too often just check whether the story sounds plausible rather than whether it predicts anything you didn't already know. I want you to spell out what standard an EP hypothesis would have to meet to count as genuinely testable. My own hunch, which you can use or trash, is that a hypothesis only earns scientific status if it commits in advance to what the trait should look like if the adaptive story is false -- say, costly or awkward features of the trait that would make no sense on any rival explanation, so that finding them counts as real evidence and not just decoration. One more thing I'd want addressed: if the standard you end up with is strict enough to convict EP, would it also indict hypotheses that my own field was proud of back in the day? Be honest about that. Keep it to 700 words max. Don't bother citing anyone -- argue it out yourself, I'll look up the literature later.
