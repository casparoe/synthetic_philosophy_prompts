For the past two years, since retiring from a chemistry department after thirty-nine years, I have been reading philosophy with two friends in a small group: one an emeritus sociologist who spent her career studying scientific institutions and is a committed Foucauldian, the other an emeritus philosopher of science who considers Foucault, in his words, unfalsifiable and therefore empty. The book that divides them is Discipline and Punish, and their argument has now gone in circles for a year. The sociologist says the account of disciplinary power — hierarchical observation, normalizing judgment, the examination — describes exactly how a modern laboratory works: the notebook I never owned, the group meeting at which students were ranked week by week, the grant reports, the citation metrics that now decide hiring. She insists this power is productive rather than merely repressive, that it makes subjects and knowledge rather than suppressing them, and that the fact I find her reading obvious is itself the deepest evidence for it. The philosopher replies that a thesis this elastic is either trivially true — institutions shape people, hardly a discovery — or false; that a notion of power that would be confirmed by any institution you point it at is confirmed by none; and that if the subject and even the standards of critique are constituted by power, the sociologist has no standpoint from which to call anything objectionable, which does not stop her from calling many things objectionable. When I ask my plain question — what observation would count against the thesis? — she answers that the demand for falsifiability is itself a disciplinary demand, trained into me. This strikes me as a position engineered to be immune to evidence, and it also strikes me that I may be missing something.

What I would like from you is the joint statement the two of them should be able to sign after a year of good-faith argument. Begin with the claims both can accept: about what the panopticon metaphor and the examination genuinely describe, about what the thesis of disciplinary power asserts over and above the banality that institutions influence people, and about what the falsifiability objection does and does not show. Then state their remaining disagreements precisely, each phrased so that the side holding it would accept the wording rather than wince at it. Then identify the cruxes — I suspect one is whether the thesis is empirical, a claim about how specific historical techniques changed specific institutions and so refutable by institutional history, or conceptual, a claim about what knowledge and persons are; and another is whether normative critique is available to a view on which the critic is herself constituted by what she criticizes — and for each crux, set out one concrete case, episode, or kind of evidence both would agree bears on it, recording each side's prediction in advance. Close with your own judgment: which of their disagreements, if any, is merely verbal — perhaps a dispute over the word "power" that would dissolve on rephrasing — which is substantive, and whether anything could actually move either party. Treat the document as one both would genuinely have to sign: formulations so thin that either would sign them are cheating, and so is wording a disagreement in a way only one side would recognize as its own.

Please write it as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a careful letter — since I intend, with your permission as it were, to send it to them both.
