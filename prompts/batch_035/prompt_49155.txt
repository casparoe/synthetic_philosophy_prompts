I write about science and ideas for a mid-size outlet, and for the last few months a lot of my reporting has been on policy decisions with ugly tradeoffs: who gets the last ICU bed, whether a city should take on a flood risk in one neighborhood to spare two others, whether to accept a small number of serious vaccine injuries to prevent a large number of deaths. In interview after interview I keep running into the same shape of argument from the public-health people and the economists: line up the outcomes, count them up, and go with whichever option has the bigger total. The math is never really the issue. What I keep hearing back from the people on the losing end — and honestly from my own gut sometimes — is something like: okay, the total is bigger, but you're adding up things that happen to different people, and nobody gets to live the total. The woman whose house floods doesn't experience the two dry neighborhoods. The patient who doesn't get the bed isn't somehow consoled by the strangers who do. And it's not a majorities thing either — Ive seen the same pushback in one-on-one cases, where one identifiable person is asked to absorb a loss so that two identifiable people get a slightly bigger combined gain.

Let me try to state the shape of the complaint as precisely as I can, because I think there are a few things it is NOT. It's not "your numbers are wrong." It's not the ordinary rights objection, because the objectors will often say they'd accept the exact same loss if it were part of some scheme where the burdens rotate or get spread around fairly over time — what they reject is the idea that a loss to one person can just be cancelled out by gains landing on someone else, as if they went into a single pot. And it's not a complaint about inequality as such — the totals could come out equal and I think the objection would still be doing some work, though I'm honestly not sure. There's also a version of this I've wondered about for a single person: if I can suffer a lot at 30 and gain a bit at 60, that seems like a different kind of arithmetic than the cross-person case, and I don't know whether the complaint I'm describing is supposed to cover it or not.

So: does this exact complaint have an established name in philosophy? I've heard "tyranny of the majority," but that's about voting and factions, not about the adding-up itself, so it doesn't feel right. If there is a settled term I want to use it correctly in my piece, and I want to understand the carving, not just the label — what exactly the concept picks out, what its nearest neighbours are and how it differs from each (rights-talk, inequality aversion, plain anti-consequentialism), and whether it extends to the within-one-life case or only across people. If the honest answer is that there's no single settled term for the specific thing I've described, then say so and coin one, with a definition and the boundary cases where it would and wouldn't apply. Please don't just hand me a name — I need to know I'm not lumping three different complaints together. And end your reply with a final line in the form "Verdict: <the term, or 'no established term'> — <one sentence saying exactly what it covers>".
