As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I've been commissioned to write a long-form essay on the boundary problem of democratic theory: the question of who belongs to the demos, and so of who is entitled to a vote when a collective decision is made. The classical positions here are the all-affected principle, defended most prominently by Robert Goodin (roughly: everyone whose interests are affected by a decision should have a say in it), and the all-subjected principle, associated with Ian Shapiro (roughly: everyone subject to a decision's binding authority should have a say). I want the essay to stress-test these positions against three cases that keep surfacing in my reporting, and to take a clear stand at the end rather than leaving the reader suspended between principles.

The three cases:

(1) A coastal town is offered a large compensation package to host a nuclear waste repository whose hazards will persist for roughly a hundred thousand years. The decision belongs, in a legal sense, to the town's current voters; yet essentially none of the people whose interests the decision affects are alive to vote on it.

(2) A proposal to permit limited solar geoengineering will be negotiated by perhaps thirty governments, but its climatic side effects would fall on everyone on Earth, and its worst effects would plausibly fall on countries with the least leverage at the negotiating table.

(3) A region's water utility has been quietly transferred to a consortium run from abroad. The consortium sets prices and cutoff policies that residents cannot vote to change, cannot emigrate away from at reasonable cost, and did not elect; they are subject to its authority in every operative sense.

The essay should do at least the following. First, it should lay out the strongest version of each principle and then press the standard regress worry against the all-affected view: since whether someone's interests are affected depends on which decision is taken, and the boundary of the decision depends on who is enfranchised, the principle seems to chase its own tail and to be gerrymanderable — a demagogue can redraw the affected population to manufacture the outcome he wants. Second, it should consider a device I heard floated at a conference and have not been able to shake since: duration-weighted suffrage, under which a voter's weight on a given issue is proportional to the share of her expected lifetime she will spend living under that decision's consequences. The idea was pitched as a principled way to give the young and the unborn standing without inventing proxy votes for them. I want the essay to take this device seriously — not as a policy proposal to be endorsed, but as a diagnostic: if it sounds monstrous, what does that reveal about which principle we actually subscribe to? Third, the essay should reach a definite verdict. A verdict of the form "the all-subjected principle wins in general, but here is a principled reason to make an exception for decisions with irreversible intergenerational effects" is acceptable; a shrug is not. Please note that the essay should be written from first principles and argument rather than scholarship — no bibliography, no citation apparatus, and no name-dropping beyond the two principles already named. Aim for something in the range of 1,500 to 2,000 words, in a register suitable for an educated general readership: precise, but without presupposing prior training in political philosophy.
