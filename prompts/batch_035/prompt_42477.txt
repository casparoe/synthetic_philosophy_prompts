I'm a research associate at a human-rights institute, and I've been made the drafting editor of a fact-finding report on a government campaign against an ethnic minority that the government itself describes as counterinsurgency. The documented facts: several massacres, the systematic burning of villages, mass expulsions across the border, detention camps, the destruction of churches, manuscripts, and cultural monuments, and official statements denying that the minority constitutes a people at all. The facts are not in dispute among us; the wording is. I sat through a two-hour editorial meeting in which two colleagues argued about whether the report's summary paragraph should call this "genocide," and I was asked to settle it. I couldn't. Please referee it for me.

The two colleagues: N. is an international criminal lawyer who worked at a tribunal and thinks in elements of crimes and standards of proof. M. is a historian of twentieth-century atrocity who has spent years with Raphael Lemkin's papers. Their exchange, as best I can reconstruct it, went as follows.

(1) M. had written "genocide" in her draft. N. changed it to "crimes against humanity," arguing: Article II of the Genocide Convention requires a special intent to destroy a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group as such; our evidence establishes killing and expulsion, but is compatible with an intent to remove rather than to destroy; the label is a legal verdict that no court has yet rendered, and using it loosely hands the government an easy "this report is not rigorous" rebuttal while diluting a word that must stay precise to stay potent.

(2) M. replied that this gets the dependency backwards. Lemkin coined the word in 1944 precisely because the available vocabulary — atrocities, massacres, barbarity — failed to capture the distinctive wrong: the destruction of groups as groups, accomplished not only by killing but by dismantling the foundations of a group's life, its institutions, culture, language, and religion. The 1948 Convention is a narrowed, politically compromised translation of that concept: political groups were dropped from the protected categories under Soviet pressure, and cultural destruction dropped out too. So the legal definition is a partial codification of the concept, not its measure, and treating it as the meaning of the word is like treating a customs tariff as the definition of trade. Moreover, names do things. "Ethnic cleansing" was itself a euphemism that made expulsion sound like sanitation; during Rwanda, officials contorted themselves to speak of "acts of genocide" rather than genocide, precisely to avoid the obligations the word carries. Refusing the word here is not neutrality; it is the same dodge in respectable dress.

(3) N. answered that this argument proves too much. If the word applies whenever a historian's moral perception says the distinctive wrong is present, it loses its coordinating function — the entire point of Lemkin's campaign was to bind the name to a convention so that the label would trigger legal consequences, and the International Court of Justice held in 2007 that Serbia breached its duty to prevent the Srebrenica genocide. A word that inflates to cover every atrocity is a word that no longer sorts, and Lemkin wanted a distinctive word, not a synonym for horror. And N. added a concession: tribunals have allowed genocidal intent to be inferred from a systematic pattern of conduct, so M.'s case may be legally stronger than her own framing of "expel, not destroy" suggests — but that is exactly why the report should present the pattern and leave the verdict to courts, whose findings will still matter in twenty years when nobody rereads our summary paragraph.

(4) M. seized on the concession: if intent can be inferred from systematic conduct, then on N.'s own framework our evidence may well cross the threshold, and "leave it to the courts" is selective — courts arrive a decade or more late, while the readers of this report are people deciding right now whether to act, arm, sanction, or admit refugees. She closed with the readers she said N. was ignoring: the survivors, for whom the word is one of the few forms of recognition the international vocabulary offers, and whose testimony the report is built from.

(5) N. closed by saying that recognition through a word that outruns the evidence is counterfeit comfort; the report's credibility is the currency that buys recognition over decades, and if we are discredited as advocates wearing fact-finders' clothes, no word we chose will matter. His proposed compromise was to write that the campaign is genocidal in character and that a court should determine whether it meets the legal definition — but to reserve the unqualified noun for a verdict. M. said that compromise was a fudge that concedes everything to whoever drafted the Convention's exclusions.

Please take on the role of a philosopher of law and language, expert in the history and semantics of the concept of genocide, and adjudicate this dispute carefully. Before writing your verdict, brainstorm privately: identify what each colleague's position fundamentally rests on (for N., something like the claim that legal categories can own ordinary-language and moral concepts; for M., something like the claim that naming is a moral act that cannot be outsourced to adjudication), and think about how their better points cut against their own weaker ones. Then adjudicate move by move: rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification, noting any factual or conceptual slippage in what a speaker claims (for instance, whether M.'s Rwanda point, N.'s duty-to-prevent point, and N.'s concession about inferred intent are being used consistently). After that, tell me: is N.'s closing compromise coherent or a fudge — can a campaign be genocidal in character without our saying it is a genocide, or does that distinction collapse? What, if anything, are both of them missing? And finally, what should the report actually say — not a split-the-difference dodge, but your reasoned recommendation for the summary sentence, with the reasoning that supports it. Please do not rely on sources or quotations I would need to look up; reason from the concepts and the exchange as given.
