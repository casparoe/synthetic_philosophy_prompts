I hold a fairly strong version of the modernist view of nationalism: nations are not old realities that were finally discovered in the modern era, but constructions of the last two or three centuries, produced by print capitalism, mass schooling, conscription, censuses, and the administrative state — the view I associate with Hobsbawm's invented traditions and Anderson's imagined communities. I want you to tell me what would count against it.

Not the standard recap of the debate with ethno-symbolists — an honest map of the view's exposure. Specifically:

1. What would we need to find in the pre-modern record — in archives, collective behavior, mass mobilization, or the pattern of revolts and loyalties — for "nation" to be a genuine pre-modern category rather than a retrospective projection? Distinguish findings that would merely be awkward from ones that should actually move a committed modernist.
2. The hard cases defenders tend to wave at: polities like Japan, Armenia, or England, where something nation-like seems to predate print and mass politics. Is treating these as exceptions a principled qualification or a leak? At what point does the list of exceptions become the rule?
3. The view makes something like a testable prediction: where modern elites build national identity roughly from scratch, they should succeed about as well as where they inherit dense ethnic material to work with. Does the record of state-orchestrated nation-building — including its conspicuous failures — fit that prediction, and how much does a mismatch cost the view?

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. For each, say whether a thoughtful modernist would concede it as counting against the view or would dismiss it — and whether the dismissal is principled or merely protective. I'm also worried the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability: one can stretch the definition of "nation" so that every awkward pre-modern case counts as something else (an ethnie, a dynastic loyalty, a religious community) by definition. If it has been insulated that way, tell me whether that is a fatal flaw or an acceptable feature for a view of this kind, and what restatement of the view would put something real at risk.
