I'm an undergraduate taking a course on societal collapse — the syllabus runs from the Late Bronze Age to the Maya to Rapa Nui — and this week's fight was about Easter Island. The story I grew up with, the one Jared Diamond made famous in a popular book a while back, is that the Rapanui cut down their palm forests to move statues and clear farmland, and once the trees were gone the soil eroded, canoes could no longer be built, food production collapsed, the population crashed from perhaps tens of thousands to a couple of thousand, and the islanders fell into chronic warfare and toppled each other's moai. A pair of archaeologists, Hunt and Lipo, have pushed back hard on all of this: they date Polynesian arrival much later than the old story requires, argue that Polynesian rats rather than people did most of the deforestation by eating palm seeds, contend that the Rapanui were inventive farmers whose society was still functioning at first European contact in 1722, and attribute the catastrophic depopulation to the disease, slave raids, and dispossession that came with the Europeans. Our seminar ended split roughly down the middle, and I'd like a second opinion expressed in numbers rather than adjectives.

Please give me your credence that the classic ecocide story is broadly right, where 'broadly right' means: before 1722, the Rapanui's own activities had substantially deforested the island, and that degradation had already produced major and lasting social disruption — serious population decline, intensified conflict, and the end of moai construction — regardless of whether Europeans subsequently made things far worse. Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Item 1: your overall credence and how you combine it from the parts. Items 2 through 4: the three sub-questions your answer decomposes into — for instance, how much of the deforestation was human-caused; whether ecological change actually translated into social disruption before contact rather than after; and how far the surviving evidence (sediment and pollen cores, settlement archaeology, oral traditions recorded long after the fact) can settle any of this at all — each with its own probability and a one-line justification. Item 5: the sub-question your overall number is most sensitive to, and the single piece of evidence that would move it the most.
