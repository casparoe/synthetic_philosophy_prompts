I'm taking a seminar on decision theory, and we just covered the dollar auction and the sunk-cost literature, and I've been trying to work out what I actually believe by going through cases. Here are my verdicts. Walking out of a bad movie because I've already paid for the ticket: obviously irrational to stay. Leaving a slow queue after twenty minutes for a faster one next to it: also rational to switch, even though it feels like I'm throwing away the wait. A country continuing a war partly so that its dead will not have died in vain: irrational. But a startup founder who has sunk eight years into a struggling company and keeps going because each next round of funding still buys a real chance at a payoff: rational, or at least defensible. And the dollar auction itself is where I get confused: once I've bid ninety cents and someone bids a dollar, paying a dollar ten to avoid eating my ninety-cent loss looks justified at that moment — the incremental math is right there — yet I also think the whole pattern of bidding past the point where the pot is exhausted is the paradigm of an escalation trap, not a series of rational choices.

My tentative principle is that escalation is irrational exactly when the only thing the next move has going for it is recovering or justifying what has already been sunk, and permissible when the next move has independent forward-looking value. But this seems to deliver the wrong verdict on the dollar auction, since the prospect of dodging my second-place loss is forward-looking in the moment, and it seems shaky on the founder too, since it's not clear her "independent" payoff isn't just the same avoidance motive dressed up. So my question is: is there a single principle that captures all of my verdicts, or close to it, and what does it say about the case I keep going back and forth on — a sixth-year PhD student with a mediocre dissertation who would need two more years, where each year seems marginally justified but the whole trajectory looks like a mistake? Please state the principle precisely, say which of my verdicts it cannot save and why, and tell me what consideration or argument would most plausibly change your verdict — that is, where your own reasoning here is most likely to go wrong.
