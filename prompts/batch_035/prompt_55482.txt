The physicist's instinct, that symmetry-related configurations are one physical situation described twice, keeps colliding with my gut verdicts about how many objects are in these worlds. I've now written down six cases with my verdicts on each, and I cannot find a single principle behind my own answers. The cases:

A. Black's universe: nothing but two qualitatively identical iron spheres, one mile apart, exchanged by a reflection. My verdict: two spheres. The swap changes nothing qualitative, yet there are two of them.

B. An infinite universe periodic in space with period L: every L-sized cell contains exactly what every other contains. My verdict: there are infinitely many distinct planets, not one cell of content "repeated"; the translational symmetry does not collapse the count. This feels like A with more symmetry and more stuff.

C. The helium ground state: two electrons in the same spatial orbital with the spins in the singlet, so that the total state is fixed by exchange up to an overall sign, which fixes the ray. My verdict: two electrons. The permutation is as close to a gauge symmetry of the formalism as physics offers, and yet I count two particles. I'm aware this verdict is hostage to a particle ontology; in the field picture, where electrons are excitations, my confidence wobbles.

D. Two assignments of metric and matter fields to the points of a manifold, related by a diffeomorphism that is the identity outside some bounded region and nontrivial inside it. My verdict: one physical situation, not two — the standard line in the foundations of general relativity, and I hold it. Gauge-related multiplicity is not real multiplicity.

E. A classical world containing two fluids, each filling all of space, with identical density and velocity at every point — every property of the one, including location, is a property of the other. My verdict: torn, but I lean toward there being one thing there, or at least toward positing two doing no work. This sits very badly next to C.

F. A single perfectly featureless homogeneous sphere alone in space. I can carve it into two hemispheres related by a symmetry, but I don't think the hemispheres are objects on a par with Black's spheres — they are arbitrary carve-outs, artifacts of my description. Yet I have met the argument that Black's two spheres are exactly like this: the pair is one symmetric object, and the two spheres are its arbitrary maximal symmetric parts. The argument leaves me unmoved, and I cannot say why.
