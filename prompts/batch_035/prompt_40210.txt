My housemates and I do a small giving circle, and last night a two-hour fight broke out about the importance/tractability/neglectedness thing (the ITN framework, where you score a cause by how big it is, how solvable, and how many people are already on it). Two of them went at it and I took notes because honestly I couldn't tell who was winning. Roughly in order:

(1) Dev said the framework is broken on its face because tractability and neglectedness aren't independent -- a cause can be crowded *because* it's tractable, funding flows to problems that look winnable, so multiplying the two double-counts and the framework will systematically steer you away from winnable-but-popular causes. (2) Maya said neglectedness isn't about headcount, it's about marginal returns -- what an extra dollar buys -- and if that correlates with tractability, that's just a fact about the world, not the same factor counted twice. (3) Dev pushed back that treating "uncrowded = good value" only makes sense if you assume existing funding is roughly well-placed, like an efficient market, but the entire pitch of strategic giving is that donors are biased toward salient, photogenic, media-friendly causes -- so if funders are systematically irrational, crowded causes could be the underrated ones. (4) Maya said that's backwards: donor bias toward the salient is exactly *why* the neglected stuff is cheap to help, and unlike a real market there's no force that closes the gap, so the "arbitrage" persists and neglectedness is picking up a real signal. (5) Dev then pivoted to the first factor: how you count importance decides everything, because if you include all future people, longtermist causes win on scale by such a margin that tractability and neglectedness are rounding errors -- so the multiplication is theater, one contestable input drives the output. (6) Maya said that's an input problem, not a framework problem -- feed any method astronomically large numbers and it'll rank that first -- and that ITN was always meant as triage, a way to not forget to ask three questions, not a precision instrument; complaining that it produces spurious precision when you plug in guesses is fair but the alternative is going with vibes.

Please adjudicate move by move -- who's right about what in each of the six, no strawmanning, since both of them were making points I half-agreed with. Say who comes out more right overall, and what (if anything) both of them are missing. Also, since I don't know this literature: please clearly flag which parts of your verdict are pretty much settled among people who actually use these frameworks, versus genuinely contested -- don't hand me a contested reading as the consensus one.
