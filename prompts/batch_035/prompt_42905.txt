I've been staring at something that feels philosophically load-bearing but nobody says out loud, and I want you to dig into it for me. Basically: everywhere you look, the size or complexity of a nervous system is doing gatekeeping work for moral status, and I want to know what that practice actually presupposes.

Here's what I mean concretely. The UK's Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022 covers all vertebrates, and then -- after a review of the evidence -- it was extended to two invertebrate groups, decapod crustaceans and cephalopod molluscs. Notably it covers octopuses, whose nervous systems contain something on the order of half a billion neurons, with most of those sitting in the arms rather than a central brain. Meanwhile bees, with far fewer neurons but genuinely impressive learning and communication repertoires, got no legal recognition. Separately, in the world of animal-welfare prioritisation research (the cost-effectiveness spreadsheet crowd), people sometimes assign "moral weights" to different species that are literally scaled by neuron count or brain size relative to humans -- so a chicken gets some fraction of a human's weight partly because of how big its brain is, and a shrimp gets a tiny sliver. And going back further, the old encephalization quotient -- brain size relative to body size -- was used for decades to rank species by intelligence, and that ranking quietly seeped into assumptions about which animals matter.

So my question. Take this whole cluster of practices -- the legal line-drawing, the moral-weight spreadsheets, the "big brain, big deal" folk taxonomy. What theory of moral status does it presuppose, if any? I can see at least three different implicit views and I honestly can't tell which one the practice is committed to: (a) brain size/complexity is a mere epistemic proxy for sentience, i.e. we can't check directly so we use the best correlate we have; (b) neural capacity is a determinant of the *intensity* or richness of experience, so animals with more of the right neural machinery literally have morally weightier experiences, and the weights should scale; or (c) it's all basically phylogeny and familiarity dressed up in neural language, and the neuron talk is post-hoc.

Some things I'd like you to actually work through rather than dodge:

- Is the practice internally consistent? The law used neural evidence to pull octopuses in but left bees out, and octopus neurons are mostly distributed in the arms, which raises the question of whether the gatekeeping is tracking neurons per se or something more specific like centralised integration. If it's centralisation that matters, should that matter morally, or is that just a vertebrate-chauvinist way of carving things up?
- If moral weight genuinely scales with neuron count, you get consequences like: one whale being worth enormous numbers of mice, or, more awkwardly, a human with more brain tissue counting for more than one with less. If the practice presupposes view (b), is it committed to those conclusions or can it dodge them? If it can only dodge them by becoming view (a), is that an admission that size was never doing the moral work?
- What would have to change in the practice if the presupposed view were wrong? E.g. if insects turned out to be sentient in the morally relevant sense despite tiny brains, what happens to the legal taxonomy? Conversely if neuroscience showed total neuron count is nearly uninformative about sentience?
- Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, such that several theories of moral status would all license roughly what we're already doing? I suspect there's a lot of that, and I want you to be honest about it rather than reverse-engineering a neat implicit theory.
