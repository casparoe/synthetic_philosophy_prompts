I've been reading up on proposals for keeping powerful AI systems in check by having two of them argue in front of a human judge, and nearly everyone explains the idea through the courtroom: adversarial lawyers, a judge who rules. The comparison is meant to show that a judge can settle questions they couldn't answer on their own, because each side has an incentive to expose the other's mistakes, so a mistake only survives if neither side can find it. But it seems to me the courtroom imports a lot that the AI case lacks. In a trial, the lawyers represent different clients with genuinely opposed interests and separate careers, while the two AI debaters come from the same training process and might in principle do better by quietly coordinating than by winning. In a trial, the judge is a trained professional backed by rules of evidence and procedure, while in the AI case the human judge is exactly the weakest link the scheme is supposed to compensate for. And a trial settles one case at a time, whereas oversight is supposed to cover everything the system does from then on. Could you lay out what maps onto what, flag where the analogy breaks, and tell me which of the breaks actually matter for the conclusion people draw from it — that adversarial checking lets a weaker judge supervise claims beyond the judge's own ability to verify? If that conclusion doesn't survive the disanalogies, I'd like to hear what modification would rescue it, or whether a different analogy captures the situation better.
