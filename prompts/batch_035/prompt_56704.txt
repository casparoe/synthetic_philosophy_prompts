I sit on the publications and ethics committee of a mid-sized professional society for cognitive science, and we've been sitting on an awkward manuscript case for two months now. A submitted paper lists an LLM as a co-author, and the human authors aren't just being provocative: they fine-tuned the system exclusively on the corresponding author's twenty years of papers, lab notebooks, and referee reports, and they report that it proposed a novel hypothesis in the paper's central section that they tested, confirmed, and kept. Their cover letter leans hard on philosophy: they invoke Dennett's two-bitser example — the vending machine whose coin detector is "about" two-bit coins in a perfectly respectable sense — and argue that human intentionality is itself derived from evolution, culture, and parents in just the way the machine's is derived from its engineers, so demanding "original" intentionality for authorship credit draws a line that cannot survive scrutiny, since by parity of reasoning no human author has original thoughts either. A minority of our committee buys this. The majority appeals to something Searlean — that whatever the system did, it wasn't authorship in a sense we can hold anyone to — though when pressed at our last meeting they couldn't say why not without sliding into "it's just tokens all the way down."

Our authorship policy requires that co-authors (1) make a substantive intellectual contribution, (2) approve the final manuscript, and (3) accept responsibility for its claims. Clauses 2 and 3 are obviously doing the real work, but nobody agrees on what work: one faction reads them as a proxy for intentionality — responsibility requires a mind that can mean the claims it's answerable for — while another reads them as purely administrative — we just need a human to blame if the paper turns out to be wrong or fraudulent — and the split tracks the original/derived intentionality debate almost embarrassingly well. There's also a wrinkle of our own making: last year we approved a paper co-authored by a severely aphasic researcher who approved the manuscript through a yes/no communication board operated with her usual interpreter, and everyone felt that was authorship. The current authors have already pointed out that our reasoning in that case is hard to reconcile with refusing them, and none of us has a comeback that doesn't feel ad hoc.

Please draft the written opinion I'll circulate before our next meeting: a concrete recommendation — accept with conditions, refuse the co-authorship line while accepting the paper, or something we haven't yet considered — with the reasoning spelled out in a form the losing faction could actually respond to. Act as a philosopher of mind who has served on editorial boards and knows the derived/original intentionality terrain; think carefully first, and in particular about whether credit and accountability actually stand or fall together, or whether our policy conflates two things that could come apart (the aphasia case suggests they can). Don't let the memo resolve into either "it's derived intentionality all the way down, so anything goes" or "a machine can't mean anything, full stop" without showing why. And state plainly where the reasoning is most fragile and what would change the recommended verdict — for instance, whether a demonstrated capacity of the system itself to approve and correct its own claims would flip it, or whether the aphasia precedent was a mistake we should quietly stop leaning on. I've been reading the rationalist blogs and forums for over a decade, so I can follow the object-level arguments; what I need is a document the whole committee can act on, with a bottom line and an honest account of its own weak points.
