In my applied ethics seminar we are working through the inefficacy objection: the claim that an individual's contribution to a large collective harm makes no perceptible difference, so one person's refraining is pointless. I would like a genuine credence from you — a number, not a hedge — that my abstaining from chicken for one year would prevent at least one bird from being raised in intensive conditions, decomposed as follows: (1) the probability that retail meat supply responds to individual demand through discrete thresholds such as case sizes and reorder points, rather than through smooth adjustment; (2) the probability that a single person's one-year change in demand crosses such a threshold, given the granularity of the supply chain; (3) if you think it belongs in the decomposition, the probability that the correct account of causation here should count only threshold-crossings as genuine effects, rather than also counting marginal contributions to aggregate demand; and (4) any further term you judge relevant. Provide one line of justification for each term, then combine them into the final number. After that, identify which term the result is most sensitive to, and say what evidence or argument would move that term the most. Then, separately, give your credence on the normative question: that if the causal credence came out below one-half, my refraining would nonetheless be obligatory — decomposed into whether obligations here track expected benefit, whether participating in an unjust pattern can be wrong independently of what each participant causes, and whatever else you take to bear, again with a number and one line of reasoning per term. Finally, two of my colleagues put their credences on the first question at roughly 0.05 and 0.5; tell me where their disagreement most plausibly resides — in the empirical terms, in the causal term, or in something else entirely.
