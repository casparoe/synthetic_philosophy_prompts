I'm putting together my position on the species problem for a philosophy of biology reading group, where each of us circulates a written profile for the others to challenge. Before I send mine around, I want to check that it actually coheres. Here is the package:

(1) Species have no essences. There is no property shared by all and only the members of a species in virtue of which they are members, and no membership condition stated in terms of intrinsic traits could fix species boundaries.

(2) Species are real, mind-independent things. That there are eight bear species rather than seven or twelve is a fact about the world, not a summary of our classificatory interests, and where one species ends and another begins is likewise out in the world.

(3) I follow the Ghiselin–Hull line that species are individuals: species are big genealogical entities, so that an organism's belonging to a species is like a part's belonging to a whole rather than an item's falling under a concept. I take it to follow that species membership and species identity are transitive: if the populations at A and B belong to one species, and the populations at B and C belong to one species, then A and C belong to one species.

(4) I am a pluralist about species concepts. The biological species concept, phylogenetic species concepts, and ecological ones each carve something real, and each is legitimate in the contexts where it does its work; there is no single correct concept of species, and botanists and paleontologists are within their rights to count differently from ornithologists.

(5) In a ring-species arrangement — a chain of populations wrapped around a barrier, each adjacent pair exchanging genes, while the two ends meet on the far side, do not interbreed at all, and behave in every respect as separate species — the ends really are two species, each adjacent pair really is conspecific, and this is a fact about the world rather than a bookkeeping decision.

(6) Speciation is gradual. It takes many generations, there is no moment at which there was one species and a moment after which there were two, and in most cases I doubt there is any determinate fact about the exact point in the chain of descent at which the split occurred.

What I would like from you: go through these pairwise and tell me which pairs are genuinely inconsistent, which only look inconsistent and why the appearance dissolves, and, for each genuine inconsistency, how bad it is and what would have to give to repair it. Rank the problems by severity rather than listing them as equally serious, and close by telling me which single revision would clean up the most. Please don't reorganize my list for me or propose an altogether new position on the species problem; I want an audit of this one.
