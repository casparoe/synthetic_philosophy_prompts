I teach a philosophy-of-medicine seminar on the naturalism versus normativism debate about disease, and I want a thought experiment that goes beyond the usual stock examples. Construct a single, realistic primary-care encounter — one patient, one consultation — in which the patient presents two findings, and the theories swap verdicts: the biostatistical theory counts one finding as a disease and the other as not, while a normativist theory such as the harmful-dysfunction analysis reverses both verdicts. The case is meant to probe which theory better tracks what clinicians actually treat and insurers actually cover, so a verdict is only allowed to count as generated, not stipulated: it must fall out of each theory's own machinery once the findings are described, and if you cannot get the swap without resorting to an invented syndrome or an unrealistic clinical detail, say so plainly rather than fudging. The two findings should be independent of each other, so that all four verdicts stay clean. After the scenario itself, which should be one paragraph, walk through each of the four verdicts with the reasoning behind it; then identify which single design feature of the case is doing most of the work, and tell me what happens to the swap if that feature is removed; finish with the strongest line of attack available to each theory's defender against the half of the case that goes badly for it. Write everything in continuous prose, the way one would write a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
