I'm a physicist — statistical mechanics and soft condensed matter, mostly — and I just came out of a four-hour argument with an old friend who did her PhD in philosophy of mind. The topic was Dennett's claim, from Consciousness Explained, that the self is a "center of narrative gravity": an abstract object we posit, like a center of mass, that organizes our predictive and explanatory grip on a person without itself being a part or component of the person. I went in thinking I understood the metaphor immediately — it's my home vocabulary, after all — and left convinced that I understood it either much better or much worse than everyone else at the table. I'd like your help settling which.

Please do the following. Restate Dennett's position on the self, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually work in: mass distributions and their centers, coarse-graining and effective degrees of freedom, effective theories versus micro-dynamical ones, and abstractions like temperature and pressure that earn their keep by figuring in predictive laws while making no appearance in the microscopic description. Where it helps, be precise about which physical notion is doing the work — a center of mass is a function of a distribution fixed by an integral, whereas an effective variable like temperature is defined relative to a coarse-graining and an equilibrium assumption, and I want to know which of these Dennett's "narrative gravity" is supposed to be, if either. Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about:

First, which parts of the position translate cleanly? Second, which parts translate only with distortion — where the analogy has to be bent to fit, and what exactly the bending costs? Third, which parts have no expression in my vocabulary at all?

On that third point I have two suspicions I want you to evaluate rather than merely confirm. One is that in physics a center of mass is strictly derived from the underlying distribution — the distribution fully determines it, and it does no causal work the distribution doesn't already do — whereas my friend kept insisting that on Dennett's view the narrative abstraction somehow feeds back on and partly constitutes the person it's an abstraction of: the story people tell about themselves changes what they do, which changes the story. If that's right, I can think of physical setups with feedback between coarse-grained and fine-grained descriptions, but I can't think of one where the abstraction is doing anything like what she seemed to mean, and I'd like to know whether I'm missing a good mapping or whether this is exactly where the analogy dies. The other suspicion is about the observer: a center of mass doesn't care who computes it, but she said something about the self being a posit of the "intentional stance," which sounded to me like it makes the abstraction stance-relative in a way no physical quantity is. Is that a real disanalogy, or am I importing an assumption of my own vocabulary — that descriptions shouldn't depend on the describer — and smuggling it in as if it were a constraint on the analogy?

Finally, the overall verdict I want: where the residue lies, is it a defect of my vocabulary — something the physics framework simply cannot say, so I should enlarge my toolkit — or is it a sign that the philosophical claim is less clear than it looks, with the "center of gravity" framing doing more rhetorical work than explanatory work? And in your judgment, did either of us understand Dennett better? I suspect her, but I'd like a straight answer I can bring to the rematch.
