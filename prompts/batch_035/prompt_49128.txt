I work on training chatbots (not at a big lab or anything, small applied team) and something keeps bugging me about how we use the word "honesty" internally. Two pratices in particular.

First: when we collect preference data, raters systematically prefer answers that sound confident over ones that hedge. A model that says "I'm not sure, but I think it was 1971" gets rated worse than one that just says "It was 1971," even when both are right at the same rate. Everyone knows this, everyone complains about sycophancy, but the preference signal keeps rewarding the confident version anyway because raters, well, prefer it.

Second: when we eval for honesty, we score whether the model's asserted claims match ground truth in a fact-checking setup. Did it say true things about the world. Its own internal confidence, or how the confidence level it displayed matched its actual uncertainty, basically doesn't enter the score.

Each of those seems fine on its own. Together they add up to a concept of honesty as "asserting truths" rather than "representing your own epistemic state accurately." A human who says "It was 1971" while privately thinking it was probably 1969 but not wanting to seem unsure -- we'd call that misleading even if 1971 turns out to be right. But under our two practices that person, or model, is doing everything correct as long as the coin lands right side up. Meanwhile the model that says "probably 1969, maybe 1971" gets hit on both axes: worse preference scores, and if the answer key says 1971, marked less honest too. So the practice punishes exactly the behavior any ordinary view of honesty would demand.

So heres what I want: what theory of honesty does this setup actually presuppose? Is it really just "honesty = saying true things," and is that even coherent as a notion of honesty rather than plain accuracy? Or does the preference-data part smuggle in a *different* implicit view -- honesty as loyalty or helpfulness to the user -- that quietly conflicts with the eval part when the user is confidently wrong about something? And where is the practice genuinely underdetermined, meaning several different theories of honesty would license the same two policies and we're not actually committed to the "asserting truths" reading at all?

Structure it like this please:

**What the practice is** (~1 paragraph) -- restate the two practices cleanly so we're sure we're arguing about the same thing, including the case where they conflict.

**The implicit theory** (~2-3 paragraphs) -- reconstruct the view of honesty that makes both practices make sense together, and say what it treats a hedged-but-accurate answer as being *instead of* honest.

**Rival views and what they'd change** (~2-3 paragraphs) -- what a "faithfulness to your own uncertainty" view or a "non-misleadingness" view would predict for each practice, and where the current setup is compatible with those views anyway (i.e., where we don't actually have to be committed to what it looks like we're committed to).

**The fix, if any** (~1 paragraph) -- if we took a rival view seriously, what concretely changes in how raters are instructed, how preference pairs are built, or how the honesty eval is scored. Not vibes, actual changes.
