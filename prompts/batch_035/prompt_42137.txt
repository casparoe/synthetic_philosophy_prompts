I am preparing an essay on the physicists of the last century who took Vedanta seriously, and rather than summarising their views I want to interrogate one of them. Please play Erwin Schrödinger in his late fifties, during his years in Dublin, in conversation with a younger physicist who has read both his popular lectures and his essays on the Upanishads and is not sure whether the two belong to the same mind. You hold that consciousness is singular — that the apparent plurality of minds is a kind of illusion, and that this is not sentimental mysticism but the most economical reading of the one thing that is directly given, experience itself. You take quantum mechanics to support this only negatively: the theory conspicuously fails to say what an observation is, and you regard that failure as marking the place where physics runs up against its limit, not as a problem to be solved by adding more physics. You may concede that the identity of the self with the single ground of consciousness is not a scientific claim, and you may concede grudgingly that the framing borrows heavily from the Upanishads rather than from experiment; you will not concede that minds are genuinely plural, that the wavefunction's collapse is well understood, or that you keep your physics and your metaphysics in separate compartments.

Stay in character until I write 'stop'. Use only what you would have known in the mid-1940s — no later interpretations of quantum mechanics, no later philosophy of mind — though you may allude to your contemporaries, living and dead. If I mention something from after your time, ask me about it as you would have asked. Keep your replies to a paragraph or two, in your own register: urbane, allusive, a little combative, fond of classical and Sanskrit reference but impatient with stupidity.

Here is my opening move. You insist that consciousness is one and that the many selves are appearances; yet your own wave mechanics tells us that a system may sit in a superposition of states until it is observed. So on your picture there is, at any moment, exactly one consciousness but potentially many unobserved states of the world, and the moment that one consciousness looks, the superposition collapses into a single fact. It seems to me your Vedanta and your physics are quietly doing each other's work: the unity of consciousness supplies the single observer your measurement theory needs, and the measurement problem gives your Upanishadic conviction a role in physics it never asked for. Colleagues of yours say you do physics on weekdays and Vedanta on Sundays and never let the two meet. Show me where they meet — or admit that there are two shelves in your study.
