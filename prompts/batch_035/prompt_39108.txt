Two of my teammates got into a proper argument after standup the other day about whether one of them "broke a promise," and honestly I couldn't follow who was winning. Context: we're mid-migration, and three weeks ago Dev posted in our team channel "I promise the search migration will be done before the Friday freeze." It wasn't. When our PM brought it up at retro, Dev said he never actually promised anything, and that kicked off a whole thing with Mara, our tech lead. I'll try to reproduce the rounds roughly as they happened.

(1) Dev: typing "I promise" in a Slack message isn't a real promise. Words that actually DO things — like saying "I do" at a wedding, or "I resign" in a letter — need the right setting for them, and a casual work chat isn't that setting. So his message was just a statement of intent, i.e. him saying he'd try.

(2) Mara: that's nonsense — people resign by email all the time and it clearly counts, so written performatives are still performatives. Plus the whole channel saw the message, so if anything it's more binding than a quiet verbal promise made to one person in a hallway, which nobody would deny counts.

(3) Dev: ok but a promise isn't a contract, there's nothing to enforce, so the words don't magically create an obligation. All he did was make a prediction about his own future behaviour that turned out wrong — and nobody thinks you owe the world an apology for a bad weather forecast.

(4) Mara: by that reasoning nothing anyone ever says commits them to anything unless it's legally binding, which obviously can't be right — "I'll pick you up from the airport" creates a genuine obligation without any contract. And she said the fact that Dev is now litigating the technical definition of "promise" instead of just owning the miss is kind of the whole point: he's using the form of words as a technicality to hide behind, like a guy who says "I never SAID the car had no accidents, I just didn't mention it."

Dev's last word was that Mara's airport example cuts against her, because the reason you feel obliged to pick someone up is that they'll be stranded if you don't — it's the consequence that obliges you, not the utterance — and if the migration had been done by Friday nobody would care what words he used.

I'd especially like to know if theres a principled way to distinguish a promise from a plain prediction about your own future behaviour (Dev kept acting like they're the same thing and I couldn't articulate why that felt off).
