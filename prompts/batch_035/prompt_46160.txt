I've been reading a popular book on how historians work, aimed at general readers, and its middle chapter compresses a century and a half of debate about objectivity in history into one paragraph. The gist, as I understood it: the nineteenth-century German historian Leopold von Ranke taught that the historian's only task is to establish what actually happened, in exactly the way it happened, keeping every contemporary concern, moral judgment, and political purpose out of the account; and Ranke believed that because documents are objective traces of the past, history could eventually become a science comparable to physics, with historians converging on a single agreed picture once the sources were fully worked through. The book then says this program collapsed in the twentieth century: E. H. Carr demonstrated that the historian decides in advance which facts matter, since facts never speak for themselves but are chosen and arranged by a mind situated in its own time; and Hayden White went further by showing that historical writing works essentially like literature, with the historian selecting a plot structure — tragedy, comedy, romance, satire — where the choice is aesthetic rather than evidential, so there is no fact of the matter about which narrative of the past is true. The chapter closes by claiming that most working historians have accepted this lesson: because every account is shaped by the historian's standpoint and narrative choices, competing histories are best understood as competing perspectives, none more correct than any other, and the old dream of objective history is dead.

I found this pretty persuasive on first read, but a friend who studies history says the paragraph is a caricature and that the author has merged several distinct positions into one slide into relativism. I don't have the background to tell. Please go through the summary part by part and audit it: which claims would a historian of historiography accept as accurate, which are standard simplifications that professionals tolerate, and which would they wince at or flatly reject? I'd especially like to know whether Ranke's actual position was the naive copy-the-past doctrine attributed to him, or whether his motto is usually read more subtly; whether Carr's point about the historian selecting facts is being stretched here — I've heard he still defended a form of objectivity; and whether White really held that there is no fact of the matter about which historical account is true, or whether that goes beyond what he claimed. Finally, tell me whether the chapter's closing conclusion — that no history is more correct than any other, and that most working historians believe this — follows from the parts of the summary that survive scrutiny, or whether it is an inference the author has added.
