My housemate sits on the ethics consult service at a large teaching hospital, and after a long argument last night about a case she's dreading, I realize I still can't pin down what her actual criterion of death is. Her track record, as best I can reconstruct it: she holds that a patient with irreversible loss of all brain function is dead even while the ventilator keeps the heart beating and the body warm and metabolizing; she holds that a donation-after-circulatory-death donor is dead after two minutes of absent circulation, even though everyone in the room agrees the heart could in principle be restarted if anyone were willing to; she holds that a patient in a permanent vegetative state — brainstem intact, cortex destroyed — is not dead, merely beyond harm; and last spring she ruled that an anencephalic newborn was not dead, to the visible frustration of the transplant team. When I pressed her, she gestured at something like "the irreversible loss of integrated functioning of the organism as a whole," with the brain as the integrator and the two-minute rule marking the point at which absent circulation becomes permanent rather than merely stoppable — but I'm not sure that single formula is really doing all the work in her rulings.

The case now heading toward her committee: their transplant program wants to adopt ex situ perfusion for DCD donors. After the donor is declared dead on the standard two-minute circulatory rule, the team would reconnect the body to a machine that actively pumps oxygenated blood through the abdominal organs — including, effectively, restoring a form of circulation — to keep the organs in better condition during procurement. A nephrologist on the service is arguing that if you restart circulation, even artificially and only in part of the body, either the patient wasn't dead at declaration or has somehow died twice, or the declaration procedure itself needs rethinking. My housemate has to give a verdict, and I'd like to know what she'll say before our next argument.

Can you walk me through what a prediction of her verdict would look like? I want the candidate rules that fit her existing track record — not just the whole-organism integration story she'd give if asked, but the alternatives that would equally accommodate her rulings, like a purely permanence-based circulatory view, a strict irreversibility view on which her two-minute ruling was already doing something sneaky, or a higher-brain view that would actually have gone the other way on the vegetative-state case and so probably isn't hers. Say what each of those rules predicts about the perfusion case, give me your best guess at her verdict with the reasons you'd bet on it, and tell me what further case, ideally one a hospital could actually face, would cleanly distinguish between the two most plausible readings of her track record. I'm less interested in the bioethics-by-the-book answer than in what her own precedent commits her to — and in what principle she'd be silently adopting with each way she could go.
