I'm in a bioethics course and we just finished a unit on advance directives, which got me thinking about a way of framing the whole debate that I haven't seen anyone use, and I wrote up a short essay draft around it. I'd love comments on whether the central argument actually holds up, and especially on whether the objection I raise at the end is as fatal as I fear it might be. To be clear, I'm not asking about the medical/legal logistics of living wills, I'm asking about the underlying normative question: when a person is no longer competent, why should their earlier, competent self get to decide what happens to their body?

Here's my draft. "The standard defenses of advance directives appeal to the autonomy of the past self: the person I was when competent made a considered choice, and that choice binds the person I later become, much like an author's earlier chapters constrain the ending of a novel. Critics object that the demented patient is in some sense a different person, one who never made any such choice, and it's wildly paternalistic to impose the dead hand of a predecessor on them. I want to propose a third framing: the fiduciary model. Think of the earlier self not as a ruler issuing commands to a subordinate future self, but as the settlor of a trust. The directive is the trust instrument, the later incompetent self is the beneficiary, and family members and clinicians are the trustees. What's distinctive about the trust framing is what it does with the beneficiary's welfare. Trust law generally directs trustees to act in the beneficiary's interest, but a trust instrument can be written precisely so that the beneficiary cannot frustrate the settlor's intentions — think of spendthrift clauses that stop a wastrel heir from dissipating the corpus. The settlor's purposes, not the beneficiary's momentary preferences, control. Similarly, when a contented, demented patient seems happy in ways that contradict her earlier directive (say she refused spoon-feeding, but now eagerly eats and seems to enjoy her days), honoring the directive is not a betrayal of the beneficiary but fidelity to the instrument. The point of the arrangement was the settlor's autonomy, not the beneficiary's experiential welfare."

So my worry, which I gesture at but don't resolve: on the trust model, if the directive is ignored, who exactly is harmed? The settlor no longer exists in any harmed state, and the beneficiary's experiential interests are arguably set aside by the model from the start. With ordinary trusts there's an independent reason to honor the instrument — property rights persist through time — but there's no analogous fact about a self that persists independently of anyone's interest in it. So maybe the trust analogy shows that honoring directives is coherent but not that we have any reason to do it, unless we're willing to say that past selves can be harmed by events after they cease to exist in the relevant sense, which feels like a bigger commitment than most defenders of directives want to sign up for.

My questions for you: (1) does the fiduciary framing do any real argumentative work, or is it just a decorative analogy that smuggles in the conclusion by picking a legal category where settlor intent already wins? (2) is the harm-to-the-past-self worry at the end genuinely fatal, or is there a way for the trust model to answer it — e.g., by arguing the trust persists precisely because the beneficiary and settlor are partially the same continuing entity, even if that undercuts the 'different person' move against the critics? And (3) more generally, am I right that this is a live gap in how people defend directives, or is it just a restatement of a debate that's already been had under different labels? Any comments on accuracy or on where I should develop things further would be great — my professor likes pushing on under-motivated analogies, so be blunt.
