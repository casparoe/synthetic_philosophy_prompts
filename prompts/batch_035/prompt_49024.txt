hey, so I'm a backend engineer, never took a philosophy class in my life, but I keep getting into this argument with my coworker Dave about whether tech is political or not, and I want to actually win one for once. basically he's a "tools are neutral" guy — hammer can build a house or break a window, politics is in the person holding it, etc. I keep wanting to push back with that famous story about the low bridges (Robert Moses and the Long Island parkways, buses can't fit under them so poor people can't get to the beach, something like that), but Dave just says "ok so a guy with an agenda designed it that way on purpose, that proves MY point — the politics was in the designer, not the artifact."

and the annoying thing is he's kind of right that that story is about intentional design, so it doesn't actually touch his claim. so here's what I want you to do:

build me a thought experiment where BOTH views fail — the "politics baked in at design time by the designers intentions" view AND the "neutral tool, politics is all in how you use it" view. the political valence of the artifact should come from somewhere neither view is looking: I want it to come from the artifact's failure modes and its maintenance/repair side — who has to patch it, work around it, bear the cost when it breaks, and whose workarounds become invisible. not design intent, not use.

constraints:

- realistic and honestly boring: enterprise software, dev tooling, everyday office infrastructure, stuff like that. no sci-fi, no AI gone rogue, no evil designers, no dystopian governments
- the scenario itself should be one paragraph
- the neutral-tool position has to be genuinely tempting in the scenario, not a strawman — a reasonable person should be able to look at it and say "nobody designed this to hurt anyone, and literally everyone uses it the same way, so where's the politics?" it has to take actual work to see what's going on
- the case has to generate the verdicts from the two views, not stipulate them — you can't just declare the artifact political, it has to fall out of the situation

after the scenario, walk through what each of the two positions would say about it, show exactly where each one breaks down, and then tell me (a) which single feature of the case is doing all the work, (b) what happens to the case if I remove that feature — does it collapse back into just another boring neutral tool, and (c) Dave's best counterattack: the strongest move a neutral-tool defender could make to stop the case from counting, so I can at least see it coming.

this is for a blog post I'm writing called "the politics is in the pager duty" (title still wip lol), so it needs to actually be convincing to someone like Dave, not just to people who already agree with me.
