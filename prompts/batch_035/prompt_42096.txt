I'm a PhD candidate in international criminal law, and a dissertation chapter on the special-intent doctrine in genocide law has drifted, to my supervisor's quiet despair, into the philosophy of action. The object I keep staring at is this: under the 1948 Genocide Convention, killing, causing serious harm, inflicting destructive conditions of life, preventing births, and forcibly transferring children count as genocide only when done with the intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial, or religious group, as such. The courts have read that as requiring a specific, aim-like mental state — destruction must be the purpose, not a foreseeable or even practically certain side effect. So in the Krstić litigation at the ICTY, the expulsion of a population under conditions that everyone knew would kill many of them was not enough: without the destructive aim, it was ethnic cleansing, not genocide. Meanwhile the ICJ, in its 2007 Bosnia judgment, extended the same mental-state requirement to the state itself, held that a finding of state responsibility for genocide requires proof of intent at a standard more exacting than the ordinary criminal one, and insisted the inference to intent be the only reasonable one available. On top of that sits the substantial-part doctrine: the men of one region have been accepted as a substantial part of a people, and some of the reasoning treats the destruction of a group's leadership class as likewise destroying a substantial part — a parthood relation that is partly numerical but, in the leadership case, clearly functional.

Here is what puzzles me. The doctrine demands an individual-style mental state, but attributes it through collective evidence — speeches, patterns of conduct, coordination — to entities that do not have minds, and it does so while a single offense's definition makes the perpetrator's motive (targeting the group as such) constitutionally relevant, even though domestic criminal law almost never punishes motive as such. Lemkin's own conception of genocide, as I understand it, was of a synchronized process of destruction carried out through many techniques at once — political, economic, cultural, biological, moral — which sits oddly under a doctrine that turns on locating one aim in some mind or other. And the practice seems to treat the finding of genocide as uniquely staining in a way that findings of crimes against humanity are not, even when the body count is the same. My supervisor, an orthodox legal positivist, tells me the doctrine is just a rule, that rules need no metaphysics, and that I should get back to the elements; I suspect that the rule only looks coherent because a philosophy of collective agency is quietly doing the work underneath it.

So this is what I want from you, since you can't get the case law and I don't need a survey of it: reconstruct the implicit theory or theories the doctrine presupposes. What account of collective intentionality would make sense of attributing a destructive purpose to a state through pattern evidence while insisting on an individual-style aim? Is that attribution a legal fiction, and if so, what work is the fiction doing? Does the substantial-part element presuppose an account of what social groups are such that they can be destroyed in part — and does the leadership reading commit the law to a functional rather than numerical conception of parthood? Where is the doctrine underdetermined, such that several rival theories of the wrong (harm to the group as an entity, a wrong to humanity as such, an expressive harm in the naming) would each license the same rules? And finally: if the aim-based model were replaced by a structural, process-based model of genocide — identified by coordinated patterns of destruction rather than by anyone's purpose — tell me what would have to change, in the elements, in the elevated standard of proof, in the line between genocide and crimes against humanity, and in what the naming of an atrocity as genocide is then taken to do. If some parts of the practice would not need to change at all, I would like to know which, and why.
