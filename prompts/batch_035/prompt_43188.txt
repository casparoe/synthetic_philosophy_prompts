i cover science and ideas for a mid-size outlet and my inbox is a disaster. every few weeks some press release announces the "real" Atlantis has been found (off Spain, under Antarctica, in the Andes, take your pick), or that some megalithic site "couldn't possibly" have been built by the people archaeologists say built it, and my editor wants a take, fast. i've been winging it, and i want to stop. write me a procedure i can actually run on a claim like this before i decide whether to cover it, debunk it, or just ignore it: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a passing answer to each, where the procedure stops and says "not your call, get an actual archaeologist on the phone," and where it says the claim is fine but boring vs. wrong. ground it in what the philosophy of science actually has on demarcation — falsifiability, the difference between a fringe theory and a merely new one, the way these claims tend to be built so that nothing could ever count against them, the "they laughed at Galileo" move — but it has to be runnable in an afternoon by someone who is not a philosopher.

then run it on three real cases: (1) Atlantis as a real historical place, given that it first shows up in Plato as a story told by characters in a dialogue, (2) the ancient aliens line about the pyramids or Pumapunku or whatever site is that week's example, and (3) Fomenko's new chronology, the one where huge stretches of ancient and medieval history are supposedly duplicated and everything before roughly 1000 AD is basically invented.

and one more case where i want the procedure to be uncomfortable: a hypothetical where a fringe-ish claim eventually gets vindicated — say, someone insisting a site way older than the mainstream date, the way Troy went from "just a story in Homer" to an actual dig site, or Knossos before Evans. where would my checklist have wrongly said no, what does that tell me about which steps are real method and which are just my priors dressed up as procedure, and how should i weight the possibility of being that wrong? flag honestly which parts of the procedure settle things and which parts are judgment calls all the way down. and if the checklist can't distinguish "crackpot" from "annoying but right," say that too, dont paper over it.
