I am putting together a graduate seminar unit on Leibniz's doctrine that this world is the best of all possible worlds. Before we evaluate any particular theodicy, I want the class to have the full specification in hand, so that disagreements about particular defenses can be traced back to disagreements about what a defense owes us. Please write that specification.

Purpose, so you can calibrate the standard: the theory needs to be strong enough to answer a critic who charges that the doctrine is either vacuous (compatible, for all it says, with any world whatsoever) or false (refuted by the horrors this world contains). The setting is a scholarly one in which classical logic and standard modal machinery are assumed — not a sermon, not a work of devotional literature.

Write the desiderata: what any adequate defense of the best-possible-world claim must deliver, with the case or consideration behind each, and with an explicit marking of each as non-negotiable or negotiable. Cover at least the following dimensions, and add any others you think genuinely belong on the list (as opposed to prejudices of one camp):

1. Non-vacuity. The claim must exclude something. The theory must say what it would be for a candidate world to fail to be best, so that the doctrine is not compatible with every possible state of affairs. Motivating case: a world with the same laws and one fewer instance of intense suffering and no compensating loss — the theory must say whether such a world is better or worse, and if worse, why God did not choose it.

2. Existence of a maximum. The value ordering over possible worlds must actually have a best element. Motivating case: if the value scale is unbounded above — for any candidate world, a better one remains possible — then there is no best world, and the doctrine fails for a structural rather than a moral reason. The theory must bound the scale, cap it by finite compositional resources, or otherwise show why a maximum exists.

3. Aggregation and distribution. The theory must say how goods and evils across persons and kinds of value combine into a world-total, and it must respect the distinction between a world's being best and each life in it being best for the one living it, without collapsing the two. Motivating case: a world of fewer and happier persons versus one of more and less happy persons — the theory may not simply refuse the comparison, since the best-world claim is supposed to do work, but neither may it let an aggregate verdict silence the complaint of the individual whose own life is far from best for them.

4. Explained choice without necessitation. The choice of this world must be fully explicable in line with the Principle of Sufficient Reason, yet not necessitated: if the sufficient reason is so complete that God could not have done otherwise, the view slides toward necessitarianism, the outcome Leibniz labored to avoid, and divine wisdom collapses into the logic of the laws; if the reason is too thin, the choice looks arbitrary and the doctrine loses its explanatory point. The Leibnizian device of moral necessity, an inclination that determines the choice without metaphysically necessitating it, is the obvious candidate for satisfying this pair — assess candidly whether it does, or only renames the tension.

5. Modal status. The theory must locate the claim: is this world's being best contingent, and if so, on what? It must not make the claim analytic — supplied by the meanings of the terms alone — on pain of falling back into vacuity under item 1.

6. Consistency with the rest of the package. The defense must not purchase the best-world claim at the price of doctrines the system needs elsewhere — in particular, it must not solve the problem of evil by erasing creaturely spontaneity, since the division of labor on which evil enters through creatures while God wills only the good requires that creatures be genuine sources of their acts.

Two required analyses beyond the list itself. First, an explicit section on joint satisfiability: state which subsets of the desiderata you suspect cannot be satisfied together — my own suspicion is that items 1, 4, and 5 pull against one another — and adjudicate which desideratum should give way, treating the impossibility result itself as a finding of the paper rather than as an embarrassment to be buried. Second, a brief scorecard, kept short since it is the least important part: evaluate three candidate strategies against the list — an optimality reading on which best means the greatest variety and harmony of phenomena under the simplest laws; a moral-necessity reading on which God's choice is settled by the overall good without being metaphysically compelled; and a constrained-maximum reading on which best means best achievable given composibility limits on which substances can coexist, there being no unbounded scale of worlds to maximize over.

Constraints on your answer: write in American English; refer to positions by descriptive labels rather than by author-and-year citations; include no bibliography and no reading list; do not reproduce any passage verbatim from any text. The deliverable is the requirements list with its motivating cases — put the weight there, not on the scorecard.
