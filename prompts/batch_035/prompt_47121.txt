I'm a high school junior in my school's philosophy club, and I'm putting together a moderated discussion session on whether we learn to be good by reading about good people. For our reading we did Plutarch's life of Aristides and a medieval saint's life, and I noticed something that surprised me: my classmates split on the cases in ways that didn't match the positions they claimed to hold when we discussed theory directly. One friend who insisted biography is indispensable for moral education got squeamish the moment the biography turned out to contain invented episodes; another who called hagiography "propaganda" happily admitted she'd modeled real decisions on an idealized portrait of a person she knew almost nothing factually about. So I want an instrument that sorts people by what they actually say about cases, not by which labels they like the sound of.

Please act as a professional philosopher with expertise in ancient biography, virtue ethics, and the literature on exemplars and moral exemplarism, and think carefully before writing anything — I'd like you to brainstorm the underlying structure of the debate first, then build the instrument from that structure rather than from a list of famous names.

Here is what I need, in two parts:

Part one: before writing the quiz itself, identify the three or four diagnostic questions that carve up the logical space of views on moral learning from lives — I suspect they include something like (a) whether persons rather than principles can be the primary vehicles of moral education at all, (b) whether the biographical content must be factually true, (c) whether idealization — smoothing away or downplaying the subject's flaws — is corrupting or is precisely what makes emulation possible, and (d) whether admiration is a trustworthy guide to what is genuinely admirable — but revise or replace these if you think the space is cut differently. From those diagnostic questions, construct a taxonomy: the combinations of answers they permit, the actual positions located in that space, and any combinations nobody seems to hold. For each unoccupied combination, tell me whether it's unoccupied because it's incoherent, because it's unattractive, or just because nobody has explored it.

Part two: turn the taxonomy into a "which view do you actually hold?" quiz for my classmates (ages 15-18, no philosophy background beyond our club). Ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options. The cases should include, at minimum: a figure you long admired who turns out to be morally mixed in a serious way, and what happens to the moral lesson retroactively; a biography that contains flattering invented episodes that clearly did motivate readers to live better; a virtue you can admire but structurally cannot emulate, because the circumstances that made it available (war, poverty, sainthood) aren't yours; and one of Plutarch's small, almost trivial details about a subject's habits or manner, of the sort he includes because he thinks character shows in such details. No question may simply ask the reader to self-identify with a position — every question must be answerable by someone who has never heard of any of the views. Finish with a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto the following leaves: the exemplarist view that grounds moral concepts in admired persons; the Plutarchan method of comparing lives, vices and virtues side by side, from which we learn by contrast and juxtaposition rather than by flat idealization; the hagiographic view that an idealized portrait can edify even where it invents; the historian's objection that false or cherry-picked lives corrupt moral judgment, so only accurate biography can teach; and the skeptic's view that lives teach us trivia and moral luck, and that moral learning has to run through rules and principles instead. Where your quiz cannot actually separate two of these leaves — say, the exemplarist and the Plutarchan view — flag it honestly rather than pretending a question separates them.

Please keep the language accessible to my classmates, and after the scoring key, add a short paragraph on the single case in the quiz that does the most sorting work, and what a dissatisfied quiz-taker on each side of that case would complain about.
