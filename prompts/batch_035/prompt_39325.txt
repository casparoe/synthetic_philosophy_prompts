I teach an undergraduate module in democratic theory, and next term I'm devoting two weeks to sortition: the case for filling legislatures or advisory assemblies by lot, with material on Athenian practice, the recent wave of citizens' assemblies (the Irish convention on the Eighth Amendment, the French climate convention), and the standard cluster of worries — competence, accountability, and manipulation of the selection machinery. To open the unit I want to set Shirley Jackson's short story "The Lottery" alongside the theoretical case for lottocracy, and I'd like your help working out what to do with the pairing.

Here is the tension I want to explore. Advocates of sortition treat the equal chance as a great leveller: randomness strips out the advantages of wealth, incumbency and ambition, distributes political power as a common possession, and dissolves the incentive structure that produces career politicians, since nobody can run for what will be drawn. Jackson's lottery also distributes something by equal chance, and the villagers treat that equality as part of what legitimates the ritual: nobody is singled out, everyone's slip went into the box, the Hutchisons had the same odds as anyone. Tessie's protest, on the natural reading, is not that the lottery is unjust in principle but that this particular draw went against her — she wants the draw re-done, not abolished. So the story seems to dramatise a darker face of the same coin: randomness as a machine for diffusing responsibility (nobody chose this; it was the lot), and equality of odds as a substitute for equality of anything else.

I want a reading that takes both the story and the sortition debate seriously, and that says honestly where the analogy breaks. Some places you should weigh: the lottery selects a victim rather than a ruler, so the randomness is not a means to fairness but the very form the cruelty takes; the drawing is administered — Mr Summers keeps the box, conducts the ritual, and has replaced the older wooden chips with paper slips, so the supposedly random mechanism is curated and maintained by an entrenched local figure, which is precisely the contemporary objection about who controls stratification and recruitment in a citizens' assembly; and the story contains its own reform debate, with the Adamses reporting that some villages have given the lottery up and Old Man Warner answering that abandonment invites trouble — a tidy image of the argument from tradition whenever constitutional reform is proposed. Also worth thinking about: term limits. The anti-career-politician strand of the sortition case assumes the damage is done by people who seek and hold office; in the story the offices are effectively stable (Mr Summers year after year, Mr Graves beside him) while the deadly role of victim is rotated by lot. What does that inversion suggest about which roles in a political system we ought to want random, and which we ought to worry about being permanent?

The deliverable is an essay-length reading I can use to structure the seminar, giving: the strongest version of the claim that the story is a critique of randomness-as-legitimation; the strongest version of the reply that the pairing is a category error — that sortition confers power while the lottery takes life, and that ritual, community and scapegoating are doing work no selection mechanism does; the scenes I should point students to in support of each reading; and what, if anything, the story adds to the sortition debate that the standard arguments on both sides do not capture. If your conclusion is that the story resists the reading, say so plainly — a seminar that opens with a forced analogy is worse than one that opens by testing the analogy and finds it breaking at philosophically interesting joints. Please don't pad the essay with quotations; I want scene-level reference and argument, not quotation extraction.
