At last night's meeting of our borough's infrastructure committee, we had to choose between two safety projects, and two of my colleagues argued past each other for an hour about the principle at stake. I have to second one of the proposals on Thursday, and I still can't work out which of them was right.

The background: we have budget for one project this year. Option A is the immediate repair of a footbridge in Marsh Green estate. The structure is visibly failing; our engineers say that if it stays unrepaired, within five years it will almost certainly collapse under someone, killing or gravely injuring that one person. An elderly resident who crosses it daily has petitioned us twice. Option B is a redesign of the Ashgrove junction, which our modelling suggests would reduce each of the borough's roughly 300,000 residents' annual risk of being killed in a collision by a very small amount — small enough that no individual's chance changes in any way they could notice, but adding up, over the same five years, to about four expected deaths averted.

Councillor Ashworth argued for the bridge. She said that the small risk reductions to each resident cannot simply be added together and set against a near-certain death: no one has been harmed by four expected deaths, because there is no person who suffers them, whereas there is a real, identifiable person who will be killed by a bridge we knowingly chose not to repair. She appealed to a principle she paraphrased from a philosopher whose name escapes me — that a policy is permissible only if no one could reasonably reject it — and said that no resident could reasonably reject the bridge repair over a junction redesign, since what each of them loses is an imperceptible change in their odds, while what the bridge user loses is everything.

Councillor Bhatt replied that this reasoning proves far too much. On Ashworth's logic, he said, the council could never chlorinate the water supply, never run a vaccination campaign, never build anything at all — every public project imposes tiny risks on many to avert serious harm to a few, and Ashworth's principle appears to forbid trading small scattered risks against concentrated ones in either direction. He added that the four people whose deaths the junction redesign would prevent are just as real as the person on the bridge; we merely don't know their names, and he found it disturbing that our morality should hinge on whether a victim has happened to petition us.

Ashworth answered that this trades on an ambiguity. Imposing a tiny risk on each of thousands is not the same as harming any one of them: each individual's claim against us is weak precisely because what was done to them was so slight, so it is consistent to allow the water chlorination while denying that many weak claims add up to outweigh one strong claim. The person on the bridge, by contrast, has a claim nothing else on the table matches.

Bhatt's final word was that this concession swallows the principle. If many weak claims can never combine, the council may knowingly choose the option under which more people die, whenever the deaths are diffuse — we could defund road maintenance entirely and redirect everything to visible hazards with names and faces attached, and Ashworth's theory would applaud us. He said a morality that cannot even register the arithmetic of scattered deaths is a defect in the morality, not a revelation about death.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: for each of the four positions as actually stated, say whether it is sound, merely rhetorically effective, or mistaken, and why. Then tell me who is more right overall, and — since I suspect both are missing something — identify what, if anything, neither of them has put their finger on.
