For a Descartes seminar I offered an argument meant to extend the cogito's certainty to perception, and the conclusion now strikes me as proving far too much. It runs as follows. (1) The cogito's certainty is performative: any attempt to doubt that I am thinking is itself a case of thinking, so the act of doubting makes true the very thing it purports to doubt. (2) Whatever is certain in this way is beyond even the First Meditation's deceiver, since to be deceived about whether I am thinking, I would have to be thinking. (3) Seeming to see a table is itself a kind of thinking, in the broad Cartesian sense covering whatever is immediately present to the mind. (4) Therefore, when I try to doubt that I seem to see a table, the doubting episode includes a seeming to see the table, and so confirms what it doubts; my report that I seem to see a table is accordingly as certain as the cogito itself. (5) But seemings are not inner images that merely resemble tables: the notion of a mental intermediary was introduced to explain the possibility of error, and where no error is possible — as (4) establishes — no intermediary is needed, so in seeming to see a table my mind stands in direct relation to the table itself. (6) Hence the existence of the table I seem to see is exactly as certain as my own existence, and the skeptical doubt about the external world never gets going. I cannot accept (6), since it would collapse the whole architecture of the Meditations after the second one; yet (4) looks like nothing more than the cogito's own logic applied to a new case, and (5) is the one premise I half believe on independent grounds. Which step should I give up, and what does giving it up cost?
