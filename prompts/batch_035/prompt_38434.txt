Our national sailing federation is rethinking how it certifies instructors, and I need a decision memo drafted for the committee's spring meeting. Current rules require every instructor candidate to hold a current racing skipper certificate — proof, in effect, that they can still perform the maneuvers they would be licensed to teach. A pending proposal would drop that requirement and instead test candidates through a demanding written and oral examination covering seamanship theory, the aerodynamics of sail and rig, right-of-way rules, and teaching method, followed by a supervised practicum in which the candidate explains each maneuver to novices while a qualified skipper demonstrates it. The proposal's backers note that some of our best examiners have not raced in years, and that some of the best racers make poor teachers. Opponents insist that you cannot teach what you cannot do, and that we would be licensing instructors who could talk students into situations they could not get them out of.

I suspect the dispute has a conceptual layer we keep talking past. Some members hold, roughly with Ryle, that a skill is not a stock of true propositions plus rehearsal — that there is a real difference between being able to state the procedure for a gybe in a sudden squall and knowing how to gybe in one. Others reply, in the spirit of the intellectualist rejoinder, that if a candidate knows in the right way what a good gybe consists in and how to bring one about, nothing testable remains untested: whatever an examiner could point to is either propositional knowledge or the ability to demonstrate, and the demonstrating skipper covers the latter. Neither side has addressed a third wrinkle: our recency rule appears to conflate knowing how with currently being able. A master racer sidelined by injury plainly still knows how to race, which tells against the current rule; yet it also does not follow that everyone who once could do something still knows how, which tells against simple grandfathering.

Please draft the memo I will circulate. It should recommend a concrete course of action — approve the proposal outright, reject it, or approve it with conditions you specify — and give full weight to the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than restating the rulebook. In particular, it should address: whether teaching a skill requires possessing it; whether knowledge-that plus generic teaching competence can substitute for performance ability, and what, if anything, our proposed examination could not detect; how the distinction between retaining a skill and currently being able to exercise it should shape whatever recency or requalification requirement we adopt; and what observable failure by a licensed instructor would count as evidence that the committee chose wrong. Write it in American English, and keep it short enough to be read before the vote.
