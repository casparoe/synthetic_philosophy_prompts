I sit on the grantmaking panel of a mid-sized foundation that funds work on reducing long-term technological risks. At our next meeting we must decide on a proposal requesting a substantial three-year grant for public advocacy on preventing worst-case outcomes from advanced AI. What divides the panel is not the cause but the centerpiece argument of the applicants' outreach materials. They present their case as an explicit wager: whatever the probability of catastrophe — even absurdly small — the loss if it occurs is effectively unbounded, so expected-value reasoning obliges any rational agent to support the work, and weighing the grant against other philanthropic uses of the money is simply irrational. One panelist finds this airtight. A second says it is precisely the reasoning by which a street mugger persuades you to hand over your wallet on the strength of an unverifiable but enormous threat, and that endorsing it would license every future applicant to substitute imaginativeness about magnitude for evidence about likelihood. A third raises a further wrinkle: rival organizations describe different catastrophes with mutually incompatible remedies, each claiming the same dominance over all other considerations, so the argument seems to support anything whatever and therefore nothing in particular. Draft the decision memo I will circulate before the meeting. It should recommend a concrete funding decision, with any conditions you think warranted stated specifically enough to be written into a grant agreement, and its reasoning should be framed so the full panel can act on it. In the course of the memo, assess the wager-style argument itself: whether a tiny probability times an unbounded loss really swamps every other consideration, which assumptions about decision theory, about the value at stake, and about the fungibility of money would have to hold for that to be true, and what exactly distinguishes the applicants' reasoning from ordinary catastrophic-risk insurance, which the foundation already funds without controversy. Treat the strongest version of the losing side's position seriously enough that a panelist who holds it could sign the memo.
