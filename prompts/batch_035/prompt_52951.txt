My department runs a small seminar on collective decision-making, and for the past year two of its members have been arguing past each other about the discursive dilemma without either conceding anything. The first — a committed conclusion-driven majoritarian — holds that when a group settles a set of connected propositions, the only legitimate procedure is to put each proposition to a majority vote and let the chips fall where they may: if majorities of a three-judge panel find breach and causation while a majority rejects liability, then the panel has spoken with several voices, and that is simply what groups do; demanding a single consistent collective judgment, she says, is to smuggle in the fiction that the group is one mind. The second — a committed premise-driven proceduralist — holds that the entire point of collective judgment is to form one coherent position, so the group must vote on the premises and derive the conclusion by its own lights; a majority that accepts the premises while rejecting what follows from them has failed to take responsibility for its own inferential commitments, and this failure is a defect of the procedure, not a charming feature of groups. Neither has moved the other in twelve months, and the rest of us suspect they are at least partly talking about different things.

Draft the joint statement these two could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want it structured as follows: a numbered section of claims both accept — candidates include the dilemma's arising for any aggregation function meeting natural-looking conditions (universal domain, anonymity, proposition-wise independence), the structural parallel to the lottery paradox, and the fact that the judges' individual judgment sets can each be perfectly coherent while every aggregation method on the table misfires on some profile; then a disagreements table in which each row states one residual disagreement twice, once in wording the majoritarian would actually sign and once in wording the proceduralist would actually sign, so that neither side is described in the other's loaded terms; then a section on cruxes. I suspect the cruxes include at least these, though you should revise the list as you see fit: whether collective consistency is a genuine requirement or an artifact of treating the group as a unitary agent; whether the choice between premise-based and conclusion-based aggregation is epistemically consequential or purely procedural; and whether the dispute partly dissolves once we ask how propositions are individuated — specifically, whether "the conclusion" is a separate object of judgment or just the closure of the premises under the group's own logic, and if the latter, whether much of the fight is verbal.

For each crux, give the case, computation, or argument both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. At least one crux should be tested against a Condorcet-style model: suppose each committee member's judgments are generated by a noisy but better-than-chance process over the premises, with the conclusion a deterministic function of them — as the committee grows large, which aggregation method better tracks the true conclusion, premise-based voting or conclusion-based voting, and does the answer depend on whether members vote sincerely on every proposition or only on the premises? State what each party predicts before the model is run. If you conclude that a portion of their dispute is verbal — an artifact of equivocating between the conclusion as a proposition and the conclusion as an inference — identify exactly which portion and why the rest is not verbal with it. Do not rely on citations or attributions; name only what both parties would know from working through the examples themselves, and keep the tone of a document both would genuinely be willing to put their names on.
