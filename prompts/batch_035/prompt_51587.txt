The stock objection to the soul-making theodicy is that if suffering is permitted because it builds character, we'd seemingly have reason to leave people in it — and everyone, including Hick-style defenders, insists that's a non sequitur. Fine. But I suspect the free will defense doesn't have the same exposure: when one person's will is being used to grind another person down, stepping in looks like exactly the channel that theodicy itself says providence works through (humans checking each other is part of the design, not a violation of it). So the two might actually come apart in practice, not just on paper. I want to test that.

Here's the job: construct a thought experiment where the free will defense and the soul-making theodicy give opposite verdicts about wether a bystander should intervene. Constraints: everyday scale — a realistic workplace or family situation, nothing like genocide or terminal illness; no stipulated outcomes (the case can't just declare that the suffering made the person better, that would be rigging it); and the verdicts have to fall out of each theodicy's actual logic about what God permits and why, not out of your own moral views about the situation. Keep the scenario itself to one paragraph.

Then afterwards: walk me through what each theodicy says about the bystander's choice, identify which single feature of the case is doing the work, and tell me what happens if you remove that feature. And the part I care about most — give me your honest, committed assessment: does the case actually show the two theodicies can come apart in a way that matters, or does the soul-making defender have a good reply available (say, that the intervention itself is soul-making for the intervener, or that the theodicy only ever explains permission, never prescribes it)?
