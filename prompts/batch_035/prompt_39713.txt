I teach an elective on animal ethics and moral status to fourteen third-year undergraduates, and our next session is eighty minutes long. The unit has reached the question of whether brains themselves carry moral significance: my students have absorbed, mostly from effective-altruist blog posts, the view that moral weight plausibly scales with neuron count or cortical neuron count, and the opposing intuition that this is a bureaucratic way to decide who matters. I'd like them to hit the problem themselves rather than watch me lecture it, so I'm looking for a structured allocation game. The setup I have in mind: the class splits into committees of four or five, each acting as the board of a foundation with a fixed pool of "welfare credits" (fictional, no real money) that must be divided among a set of patients. Each patient is described only on an index card listing brain mass, total neuron count, and cortical neuron count — nothing about species, behavior, or biography at first. In round one the committees allocate the full pool across a mundane list (something like mouse, chicken, dog, pig, macaque, dolphin, elephant, human) and must write down, in one sentence, the principle they used. In round two they receive a second, stress-test list engineered to embarrass whatever principle they wrote: a raven and a grey parrot whose forebrains hold roughly as many neurons as some monkeys' despite brains the size of a walnut; an elephant whose brain is several times the mass of a human's yet whose cortex has far fewer neurons; a documented case of a human with microcephaly whose brain is smaller than a chimpanzee's; and a sperm whale with the largest brain of any animal. Committees that keep their round-one principle but can't stomach its round-one-to-round-two implications should feel that squeeze directly.

Please design the full activity: precise rules, materials (what exactly goes on each card), a round-by-round timeline that fits eighty minutes with the debrief included, and some internal pressure mechanism that doesn't rely on real money — perhaps committees earn credibility points for consistency across rounds, or lose them when another committee demonstrates their principle yields an allocation they themselves refuse to endorse on a forced-choice vote. I want the disagreement to be discovered, not announced, so the participants shouldn't be told what philosophical issue the activity targets until the debrief.

The debrief is the part I care most about: it should map specific moments in the game onto the actual intellectual terrain — the total-versus-cortical neuron count divergence, the worry that brain size matters only as a proxy for whatever we really care about (and the question of what that thing is), the sentience-first and personhood alternatives to any brain-metric view, and the live question of whether a small-brained human or a large-brained whale is a bug or a feature for each account. Please also include an honest section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (my guesses: committees refusing to put a human on the card list at all, students smuggling in personality judgments via pets they've owned, and a general slide toward "cuteness-weighted" allocations — but tell me what you actually anticipate and how to handle each), and one paragraph on what this activity fundamentally cannot reproduce — I suspect that bullet-biting is far too cheap when the welfare credits are fictional and nobody will ever actually carry out the allocations, but I'd like your candid assessment and any partial fixes. Please write the whole answer in American English.
