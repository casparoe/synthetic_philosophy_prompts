My best friend — raised in England, now working in Berlin — and I (an American, currently studying in Paris) spent most of a long dinner arguing about church–state arrangements, and we ended exactly where we began: she declared the American settlement obviously superior, I muttered that it wasn't that simple, and neither of us could say precisely what "separation of church and state" even requires. The occasion was this: I complained that French public schools send home girls who won't remove their headscarves, which struck me as the state coercing religious expression — the opposite of separation. She replied that at least France separates in earnest, unlike England, where bishops sit in the House of Lords as of right and the monarch is Supreme Governor of the established church. I then pointed out that Germany, which everyone treats as a normal secular state, has the tax office collect a church tax as a surcharge on income tax from registered church members and passes it to the churches, and runs confessional religious instruction in ordinary state schools. At that point she fell back on the phrase itself: there's "a wall of separation between church and state," she said, as though that settled which of our four countries is on the wrong side of it. We never got past the phrase.

What I'd like from you is a disambiguation, laid out as a menu I could take back to her rather than a single gloss. Please set out the distinct precise claims "separation of church and state" might stand for — from the trivially true to the substantive to the false — and for each reading say whether it is a platitude, a defensible but contentious thesis, or simply incorrect. Then test each reading against the four systems we fought over: English establishment with, by many accounts, little practical religious coercion; French separation that actively regulates religious expression in the name of the secular state; German non-establishment joined to state collection of church taxes and confessional schooling; and American disestablishment. Where you can, note which regimes satisfy which reading, and flag any readings on which France comes out less separated than England, or Germany more separated than the United States — those were precisely the cases that made our argument go in circles.

Finally, tell me which reading my friend was actually invoking when she used the phrase to rank these countries, and whether that reading survives the four-way comparison. My honest suspicion is that the phrase does its work in arguments like ours by sliding between readings — pick whichever one makes the favored country look good — and I'd like to know whether that suspicion is right, and which single reading, if any, is doing most of the real work whenever people repeat the phrase.
