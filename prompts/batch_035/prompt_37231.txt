I spent thirty-one years as a field biologist — the last twenty studying foraging and parental investment in birds — and since retiring I have been working through the philosophy of rational choice with a reading group at the university here. Our current topic is the sunk cost fallacy, and one member has produced an argument that has been bothering me for two weeks: every step looks unimpeachable, yet I am certain the conclusion is wrong. I want a diagnosis, not a survey of the literature.

Take the role of a professional philosopher specializing in decision theory and rational choice, and please think carefully and systematically. Before committing to a verdict, brainstorm at least two candidate readings of the premises that might matter to the argument, state which readings you are working with, and only then give your diagnosis. Write for a reader who is mathematically literate but not trained in philosophy.

The argument runs as follows:

(1) A rational agent chooses among options by comparing their expected utilities.
(2) If a consequence will obtain no matter which option the agent chooses, then it contributes equally to every option's expected utility and so cannot affect the comparison between them.
(3) Sunk costs — the money, time, and effort already invested in a project — will be borne whether the project is continued or abandoned.
(4) Therefore sunk costs can never rationally tip the balance between continuing and abandoning a project.
(5) But everything that is already the case — every observation made, every experiment performed, every failure recorded, every datum accumulated over an entire career — will equally be the case no matter which option the agent now chooses.
(6) So, by (2), nothing that is already the case can rationally affect the comparison between any options.
(7) Therefore rational choice must be governed exclusively by what is not yet the case; an agent's whole evidential history is rationally irrelevant to what she ought to do.

Now (7) strikes me as not merely false but self-undermining: the expected utilities in (1) are computed from probabilities, and those probabilities are fixed by exactly the already-the-case items that (6) dismisses. An agent who obeyed (7) could not so much as apply (1). My colleague replies that this only shows we must be more careful about what counts as a consequence, and that his argument is simply the standard one pursued with unusual consistency.

The reason I cannot dismiss this as a trick is that in field biology we lived it. I remember when the ethologists took up what they called the Concorde fallacy — whether an animal that has already invested heavily in a mate, a nest, or a litter keeps investing beyond what the prospective returns warrant. The consensus line was that natural selection should render animals immune, since selection rewards only future reproductive returns. But there was an awkward possibility several of us noticed: if past investment is correlated with future returns — because heavy investment signals a viable brood, a defensible territory, a high-quality mate — then a rule of thumb keyed to sunk costs could be favored, not as sentimental bookkeeping but as evidence. And what selection can key on, I take it, a rational agent can key on too. My colleague insists that this evidential use of the past is precisely what his argument forbids, and I cannot see which premise he is entitled to.

So: which step do you reject, and exactly why? In particular, does (2) survive if the item in question alters not the payoffs of the options but the probabilities with which those payoffs arrive? If that is where the argument turns, say so explicitly, because I then want to know whether the classic anti-sunk-cost conclusion (4) survives as well, or whether it falls together with (6). If you think the fault is instead an equivocation on some term, name the term, give both senses, and say which premises are true on which sense. Finally, tell me what it costs: if rejecting (2) outright is the price of saving the evidential past, can a defender of expected utility theory pay it, or is there a repair that saves both? For my part, I am confident (7) is false, and I would much rather give up one of (2) through (6) than give up (1).
