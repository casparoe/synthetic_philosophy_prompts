My two friends got into it at dinner last weekend and i got drafted to referee, except I couldn't. It started because Dana was explaining why she'd just signed up for a big monthly donation to some charity after reading Peter Singer's famine piece. She brought up the shallow pond example: if you can save a drowning child right in front of you at the cost of ruining your shoes, you obviously have to wade in, and the only real differences between that and not sending money to save kids dying far away are distance and numbers, which shouldn't matter morally. So most of us are failing a basic duty of rescue unless we give until giving more would cost something comparable.

(2) Rob said no way, the pond is nothing like the charity case. In the pond you're the only one who can help a particular child right there, you know with near-certainty the rescue works, and you see the whole thing. With donations you're one of millions, the money gets spread over statistical lives, you can't trace your dollars to any specific kid, and the counterfactuals are murky (does that bednet exist because of you?). The intuition to save the nearby child is correct, it just doesn't transfer.

(3) Dana said that's exactly the effect philosophers have documented with tweaked versions of the cases: people say they'd wreck an expensive car to save one stranded hiker nearby, but won't send the equivalent money to save several kids abroad, and when you vary the scenarios so the only thing changing is vividness and proximity, the intuitions flip around. That suggests our intuitions are tracking salience, not morality -- so pointing at the difference between pond and charity doesn't show the difference is morally real, it just shows we're wired to respond to what's in front of our faces.

(4) Rob answered that intuitions flipping under tweaked cases cuts both ways: maybe it shows the pond intuition is pumped up by salience and the real duty is much weaker than the pond suggests, not that the charity duty is as strong. He also said the structural stuff is genuine, not psychological: drop-in-the-bucket problems and overdetermination are real, and it's telling that lots of legal systems actually impose a duty of easy rescue (you can be prosecuted for not helping at the scene) while none impose a duty to donate to distant strangers. The law isn't morality, but it's not nothing either.

(5) Dana: if the law's refusal to impose a duty to give tracked real moral facts, it would have to be for the reasons Rob gave in (2), and she'd already answered those. And Singer's principle itself -- if we can prevent something very bad without sacrificing anything of comparable moral significance, we must -- just doesn't care about any of that stuff.

(6) Rob's last word: the principle is exactly what's in question, you can't cite it against the counterexamples. And even if he granted most of it, "you're the only one who can help" isn't just salience -- it goes to whether your omission makes you responsible for a death versus merely not rescuing someone, a distinction even people who think the killing vs letting die thing is overrated take seriously in some contexts.

They both walked away sure they'd won and I honestly couldn't say. Can you adjudicate move by move -- who's right about which specific point, where each of them concedes too much, and whether there's something both are missing? Please lead with your overall verdict in the very first sentence and then give the reasoning, no preamble. And since a lot of this territory is genuinely fought over by people who work on it -- whether the act/omission distinction does any work here, how much weight to give intuitions that shift under tweaked cases, whether the demandingness objection is fatal or just a cost -- flag which parts are settled and which are contested, rather than presenting one side's take as the consensus.
