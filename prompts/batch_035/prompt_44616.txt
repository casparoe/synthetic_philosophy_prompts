I write literary fiction, and I have grown uneasy about a device I rely on more than I like to admit. Call it borrowed hindsight: I let the reader watch a character act while withholding, for several chapters, a piece of the character's history that would recast the act. The reader therefore judges the character on incomplete grounds, and my real payload arrives later, when the withheld fact surfaces and the reader is made to feel their own earlier judgment was hasty. I am not trying to revise the reader's opinion of the character so much as to make them distrust the speed of their own judging. A friend who works in behavioral ethics calls this manipulation: the device works, she says, precisely because the reader cannot see, at the moment of judging, that they are being set up to judge. I am not convinced. Readers know in general that novelists stage delayed revelations; nobody consents scene by scene, but everyone consents to the genre.

I would like you to argue for the following position, and to argue for it properly, not as a formality. The position is this: a persuasive technique counts as manipulative only if it would lose its power when the audience knows the general kind of thing the persuader is doing — the kind, not the specifics of this deployment. Applied to my device, the claim is that a reader who knows novelists sometimes withhold motive-bearing information for exactly this effect still reads the early chapters with full force, perhaps with greater force; the knowledge does not defuse the device. By contrast, a technique such as quietly reordering a feed to exploit the audience's inability to notice the ordering fails the test, because naming the kind of thing being done destroys it.

Then confront the strongest objection to the position, which I take to be this: the test seems too weak, since some techniques retain their power under exposure and still bypass deliberation — flattery works on people who know they are being flattered. You should say whether the position can absorb this objection, perhaps by tightening the notion of "kind," or whether it must be weakened into something I would then have to accept: that my borrowed hindsight, on the amended test, no longer comes out clean.
