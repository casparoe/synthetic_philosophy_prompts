I'm a graduate student writing a term paper for a seminar that pairs early modern rationalists with contemporary philosophy of mind, and my topic is what Leibniz would have made of the Gödelian anti-mechanist arguments associated with J.R. Lucas and, later, Roger Penrose. I have a rough sketch of an answer, but I want something far more careful and textually grounded than what I can produce on my own, so please take the role of a historian of early modern philosophy who also knows the philosophy of mathematics and the computability literature well. Before committing to any verdict, brainstorm the relevant Leibnizian material and organize your reasoning; do not simply dump everything you know about either Leibniz or the Lucas–Penrose debate and hope the two lists connect.

To fix the target: in outline, Lucas's original paper from the early 1960s and Penrose's later books argue that no formal system can capture human mathematical understanding, because for any consistent machine we humans can construct its Gödel sentence, recognize its truth, and thereby do something the machine provably cannot do. Leibniz is a fascinating test case because his system pulls in apparently opposite directions. On the one hand, he built mechanical calculators, dreamed of the Characteristica Universalis, and famously suggested that rational disputes could one day be settled by sitting down and calculating. On the other hand, his Mill argument in the Monadology insists that perception and consciousness cannot in principle be explained mechanically, and his doctrine of infinite analysis holds that even contingent truths are demonstrable only through a process of analysis too long for any finite creature.

Please do the following, in order. First, inventory the Leibnizian commitments that actually bear on the question — truths of reason versus truths of fact, nominal versus real definitions, infinite analysis and the divine intellect, the status of mathematical truths as analytic in principle, and what you take to be the strongest reading of the Mill argument — and flag the ones that are load-bearing. Second, reconstruct the anti-mechanist argument premise by premise and predict Leibniz's verdict on each: would he grant that we see the truth of a Gödel sentence, given his view that every necessary truth is in principle provable by analysis? Would he accept the identification of minds with formal systems in the first place, or regard it as a category error for reasons that have nothing to do with Gödel? Third, confront the uncomfortable possibility that Leibniz would embrace the anti-mechanist conclusion while rejecting the Gödelian route to it — his grounds being the Mill argument about perception rather than anything about formal provability — and say what that would do to his calculation ideal: does the Characteristica survive as a fragment of reasoning, or does the whole project need rethinking? Fourth, say how he would likely respond to the standard mechanist rejoinders: that humans are themselves inconsistent, that we only see the truth of the Gödel sentence on the unargued assumption that the machine is consistent, and that the argument threatens to prove too much about our own soundness. Finally, separate the parts of your reconstruction that are well grounded in his texts from those that are genuinely speculative, and identify any tension within Leibniz's system that this exercise exposes — for instance, between his optimism that reasoning can be mechanized in principle and his insistence that apperception cannot. Where you have to choose between rival readings of Leibniz, state the choice openly rather than silently assuming the reading that makes your prediction easier.
