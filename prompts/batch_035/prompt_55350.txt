I'm in a reading group working through Plutarch's Life of Pericles (the Dryden-style translation, we're not reading Greek), and I got stuck on a bit in the middle of the life and honestly the group's discussion of it went nowhere, so I want to actually understand it properly before next session.

Context, since I'm paraphrasing rather than quoting from my copy: after Pericles starts using public money and festivals and building projects to keep the common people of Athens content, Plutarch raises a question that seems to come out of nowhere. He asks whether all this -- the jury pay, the festivals, the temples and public works on the Acropolis -- should be counted as a noble statesman's policy that served the city, or as a demagogue's trick for buying off the people. And then he more or less says he'd rather conclude it was for the common good than concede that Pericles was flattering and corrupting the masses, "like a nurse stuffing a child with sweets" (that last image is roughly his, I don't have the exact wording in front of me). He also passes along, apparently without endorsing it, the report that some people -- he mentions Aristotle among others, as I recall -- thought Pericles deliberately invented the distributions as a way of dealing with persistent unemployment, keeping idle and potentially troublesome poor people busy and paid with public funds.

Three things I can't untangle and would love help with:

1. What is Plutarch actually doing with the demagoguery charge? It reads like he sets up the accusation in order to immediately refuse to adjudicate it -- he picks the charitable reading basically because he prefers it. Is that sloppy special pleading for a personal favorite, or is there a principled reason within his project for resolving doubt about a man's motive in favor of the better interpretation?

2. The part that really bugs me: the Aristotle-style reading (pericles as managing unemployment) isn't obviously the uncharitable one! Managing unemployment sounds like good governance to us. So why does Plutarch present it in the neighborhood of the corrupting-the-people charge, as though a calculated policy of pacifying the poor with payouts is somehow worse or at least more suspicious than the vague charitable reading he prefers? Is there an assumption about motives vs. outcomes, or about what a free citizen's relation to the state should be, that makes "he did it to keep them busy" a slur in a way "he did it for the common good" is not, even if the observable behavior is identical?

3. In the preface to the Life of Alexander, Plutarch says he's not writing histories but lives, and that the smallest thing -- a gesture, a joke, a saying -- can reveal character better than battles where thousands die. How is the scene I described supposed to be doing that kind of work? If the outward acts admit of two opposite interpretations and Plutarch just picks one, what exactly have we learned about Pericles the man, as opposed to about Plutarch's fondness for him? Or is the point of the passage actually to show us something about Plutarch's method of moral learning from lives -- like, the reader is supposed to notice what he's doing and think about which reading of a statesman's conduct we owe to the statesmen we admire?

Also, if it helps, our group read the Life of Antony a couple months back, and I noticed Plutarch lets Antony's crowd-pleasing read much less charitably -- so if you can say anything about what makes Pericles's version of feeding the people virtuous and Antony's version damning in Plutarch's eyes (if it's anything more than who he happens to like), that would help me a lot too.
