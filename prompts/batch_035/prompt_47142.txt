I'm teaching an intro political philosophy class and we just finished Isaiah Berlin's two concepts of liberty plus Philip Pettit's republican idea of freedom as non-domination. For next week I want to use One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest as the test case — most students know the movie, some have read the Kesey novel, so feel free to work with whichever you know better and say so up front. Read the ward through that debate. Nurse Ratched barely ever interferes with anyone directly: some of the patients are there voluntarily and could sign themselves out, and her control mostly operates through the threat of interventions, the threat of keeping someone committed, little schedule changes, the group sessions where patients police each other. But nobody on that ward is free in any way that seems to matter. Is this exactly the non-domination point — that living under an unchecked power makes you unfree even when the power rarely has to act — or does the story push further, suggesting domination gets inside people in a way neither Berlin's negative liberty nor Pettit's non-domination quite captures? I'm thinking of Chief hiding in the fog, insisting he isn't big, and the way the patients vote against their own interests at the group meetings.

Also, where does McMurphy fit? On paper he's a negative-liberty hero, the guy who won't be boxed in, but his actual project — the World Series vote, the fishing trip, breaking the window — looks republican: making the ward a place where one person can't arbitrarily rule everyone else. And the vote scene bugs me in an interesting way: he raises his hand after the vote is technically closed, and I can't decide if that's a failure of the formal channels (no contestation without real power behind it) or a demonstration that the channels were fake all along. Then the ending: is Chief's escape freedom, or just exit from a regime that stays fully intact for everyone left behind, and is there anything in the republican framework about exit being a poor substitute for actually being able to contest power? Also, honestly, McMurphy himself dominates the other patients constantly — is the book aware of that, or is that something the theory sees that Kesey doesn't?
