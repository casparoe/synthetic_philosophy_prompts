I'm a PhD student in philosophy whose dissertation defends a fitting-attitude analysis of final value, and I've run into a worry I can't seem to dissolve. As I understand the literature, the wrong kind of reasons problem is supposed to show that we sometimes have decisive reasons to hold attitudes for considerations that have nothing to do with the attitudes' fittingness — for instance, the stock case where a demon threatens to torture you unless you admire her, so that you seem to have a reason to admire her that has nothing to do with her admirability. Here's my problem: if that's right, then we can have good reasons to believe things for entirely non-evidential considerations too (the demon could just as well demand belief), and it seems to follow that evidentialism about belief is simply false and that the pragmatist tradition won this debate long ago. My advisor keeps writing "different problem" in the margins, but I honestly can't see the difference: in both cases we appear to have genuine reasons for attitudes that don't track how the world actually is, so whatever supports the one case should support the other. What am I missing about how the wrong kind of reasons problem is supposed to be a problem about the analysis of value rather than a discovery about what justifies attitudes? And is there any principled way to accept the demon cases in the fitting-attitude literature while still insisting that only evidence can justify belief? I'd be grateful for a careful explanation, written in continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — as I'd like to incorporate it into a chapter draft.
