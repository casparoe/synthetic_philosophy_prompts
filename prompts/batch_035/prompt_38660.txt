I coach a Saturday math circle for ten strong students aged fourteen to sixteen — comfortable with induction and basic logic, no game theory, no epistemology. Next session I have forty-five minutes and a whiteboard, and I want to teach them common knowledge of rationality: not just common knowledge (everyone knows, everyone knows that everyone knows, and so on), but the stronger thing epistemic game theory trades in — the assumption that this mutual awareness extends indefinitely and that everyone reasons flawlessly from it. My plan was to build the session around one of the classic announcement puzzles, but I keep going back and forth on which one, on what exactly the public announcement adds when every child can already see the fact announced, and on how honest to be about the idealizations (perfect reasoning, perfect truthfulness, common knowledge of each other's rationality). Rather than an explanation, I want a plan for the explanation.

Specifically, I need from you:

1. A dependency-ordered sequence of ideas, with the reason each must come after its predecessors — in particular where the announcement belongs, and whether students must first see that the naive counting argument fails or can be led to discover that.
2. A single carrying example, chosen from among the familiar candidates (the muddy children, the blue-eyed islanders, and a generals-trying-to-coordinate variant) or a modification of one. Say what the chosen example must have (e.g., a fact everyone already sees, a public event, a deduction chain with a determinate length) and what it must lack (private signals, deception, ambiguous payoffs). Explain why you rejected the alternatives.
3. The misconceptions this audience will predictably form, in the order they'll surface. I anticipate at least: "common knowledge is just everyone knowing it"; "the announcement tells them nothing, since they all saw it already"; and "the infinite tower is impossible, so common knowledge never really occurs." For each: the point in the sequence where it will arise, the move that heads it off, and the student remark that would tell you it's taken root anyway.
4. A closing question I can ask that would reveal whether they've grasped what the announcement actually adds — the change in the depth of mutual awareness — rather than merely learned to run the counting argument.
5. What to openly decline to cover: I'm thinking the formal fixed-point treatment, whether common belief suffices where common knowledge is demanded, and anything like agreement theorems. Tell me what to say when I set each aside, so it sounds like a boundary rather than a dodge.

One caution that matters to me: some of what surrounds this topic is textbook and some is a live dispute — for instance, whether real agents can represent or act on anything like an infinite hierarchy, and whether the idealized puzzle result survives when the idealizations are relaxed one at a time. I don't want the plan to smuggle a contested position in as settled background.

Please deliver the plan as a JSON object with exactly this schema:

- "session_title": string
- "duration_minutes": number
- "sequence": array of objects, each with "step" (integer, 1-based), "idea" (string), "depends_on" (array of step numbers, empty for the first), "board_work" (string, what actually goes on the whiteboard), and "minutes" (number)
- "carrying_example": object with "chosen" (string), "must_have" (array of strings), "must_lack" (array of strings), and "rejected_alternatives" (array of objects with "example" and "reason")
- "misconceptions": array of objects with "misconception", "arises_at_step" (integer), "countermeasure" (string), and "failure_signal" (string)
- "check_question": string, plus "what_a_good_answer_contains" (string)
- "explicitly_omitted": array of objects with "topic" and "boundary_statement" (the one or two sentences I should say aloud)
- "settled_vs_contested": array of objects with "item" (string), "status" (exactly one of "settled" or "contested"), and "note" (string, one sentence on the substance or the dispute)

Every field of every object is required; no null values. If a misconception needs more than one countermeasure, pick the strongest and fold the rest into "countermeasure" as a single coherent move.
