I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background, though I've ended up reading a fair amount about collective decision-making because of work. Here's the thing I'd like you to assess.

On my team we've been arguing about whether to make more of our technical decisions by simple majority vote among the engineers rather than by whoever owns the component deciding. A colleague who's pushing for the voting scheme keeps invoking the Condorcet jury theorem, and he explains it in terms I find natural: he says a majority vote among many voters is exactly like what we do in signal processing when we average many noisy but unbiased measurements of the same quantity. Each voter is a sensor with some error probability; the truth of the proposition is the signal; as you add voters, the errors wash out statistically, roughly like noise falling off with the square root of the number of samples. He points out that the jury theorem actually gives something even better than that square-root decay — the probability that the majority is wrong drops fast, exponentially, as the group grows — so long as each voter is even slightly better than a coin flip and their errors are independent. His conclusion: with, say, twenty engineers each a bit better than chance on average, the vote is close to infallible, so majority rule is the rational default for our decisions, and we should be more comfortable deferring to team votes in general — at work and politically.

I have a nagging feeling the sensor-averaging picture is doing more work than it's entitled to. But I can't quite articulate where it breaks.

Please audit the analogy thoroughly. Give me an explicit correspondence table — what plays the role of the signal, the sensor, the noise, the number of samples, the averaging operation — and then go through where the mapping breaks down. I have some candidate disanalogies; tell me which of them actually matter for his conclusion and which don't: (a) sensors measuring the same quantity share a fact of the matter, whereas many of our decisions are about what we should do, where the voters may not share a "signal" at all; (b) sensor noise is at least idealized as independent, while our errors presumably come from shared training, the same documentation, the same senior engineer's opinion — the errors are correlated in a way averaging can't fix; (c) his own model: averaging measurements is not the same operation as taking a majority vote, even though both supposedly beat individual noise; (d) the requirement that each voter be better than a coin flip, which in the sensor picture is the requirement that the sensor be unbiased — and a sensor with a systematic bias averages into a confident, wrong answer. I'd also like to know whether there's a better analogy available — something from my world, maybe ensembles of models or redundant hardware — that captures what's right about the argument while making the failure modes visible.

Most importantly, take a committed position: once the disanalogies are on the table, does his conclusion survive in some restricted form, and if so, exactly what conditions does the voting scheme impose on us for it to work — and where he's wrong, say plainly what part of his argument the analogy was silently carrying.
