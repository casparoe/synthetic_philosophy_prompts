Two members of my research group have spent two years arguing about the moral status of experience at the fundamental physical level, and we have finally persuaded them to run a structured adversarial collaboration. I need the joint statement that collaboration should produce.

Call them the Pansentientist and the Functionalist. The Pansentientist holds that phenomenal experience may be ubiquitous — that panpsychism, or at least panexperientialism, cannot be assigned negligible credence — and that if experience is everywhere, then, since valence (pleasure and suffering) is plausibly intrinsic to experience rather than a later evolutionary add-on, the total amount of suffering in the universe may be dominated by processes nobody counts: thermal jostling, stellar interiors, the substrates of large-scale computation. She takes this to have practical implications under moral-uncertainty reasoning: even a modest credence in pansentience, multiplied by the stakes, could bear on whether we should welcome high-energy experiments, fusion power, or certain kinds of computing infrastructure. The Functionalist holds that valence requires a specific functional organization — something like a system that represents its own states as better or worse and can act on that representation — so that even if experience is ubiquitous, suffering is vanishingly rare, occurring only where nociception-like architecture exists. He accuses the Pansentientist of running a moralized Pascal's mugging: attributing suffering in a way that no possible observation could undercut, then demanding that expected-value reasoning take the attribution seriously.

Both argue in good faith, both know the metaphysics literature, and both concede that the ethics has been far less explored than the metaphysics. The dispute has nonetheless hardened into a familiar loop, which is why we want a written joint product rather than another seminar.

Draft the document their collaboration should issue. It should contain:

1. A numbered section of claims both can sign — including, if they honestly can, claims about what neither knows, about the epistemic predicament of agents who cannot interrogate fundamental physics for the presence of valence, and about which parts of their dispute are merely verbal (for instance, whether they are using the word suffering univocally at all).
2. A disagreements table: each residual disagreement stated twice, once in wording the Pansentientist would accept as an accurate rendering of her view, once in wording the Functionalist would accept as an accurate rendering of his.
3. The cruxes — sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party. My hypothesis is that the cruxes include: whether valence can come apart from consciousness itself, or whether experience without valence is not even intelligible; whether expected-choiceworthiness reasoning under moral uncertainty is appropriate when the welfare attributions are empirically shielded, that is, when nothing we could learn would revise them; and what, if anything, would count as evidence of valence in systems that cannot report on themselves — here they have in mind the kinds of findings that get cited in debates about the moral status of organisms without neurons, such as avoidance behavior and responses to anesthetics in simple creatures. Test my hypothesis against what their positions actually imply, and correct it where it is wrong.
4. For each crux, at least one concrete case, experiment, or argument both parties would accept as bearing on it, with each party's prediction about the outcome recorded in advance — including, where a party predicts that no evidence could bear on the crux at all, an explicit statement of that prediction and its implications.

The work I care most about is in the drafting: formulations that are neither vacuous nor slanted toward either side, and a clean separation of the verbal residue from what is genuinely at stake. If part of their disagreement reduces to a difference about which question they are answering, say which part and why.
