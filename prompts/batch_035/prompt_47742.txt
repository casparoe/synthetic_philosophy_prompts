I have no philosophy training, I fix elevators for a living, but for the last couple of years I've been obsessed with this thing philosophers call the causal exclusion problem (got into it through a pop book on consciousness, then fell down a wikipedia hole). The argument as I understand it: if my decision to raise my arm is a mental thing, and there's also a complete physical story about neurons and muscles that fully accounts for the arm going up, then the mental decision seems to get squeezed out — either it does nothing (epiphenomenalism) or it's just the neural story described in mental words. And I've come to a position on this that I actually believe: I think the whole argument secretly depends on treating causation like a conserved quantity, like there's a fixed amount of causal "oomph" and if the physical state claims all of it, nothing is left for the mental state. But causation isn't energy! Nothing says an effect can only have so much cause. My pet version of this: causes only compete with each other when they're at the same grain. The mental state is coarser-grained than any particular neural configuration — "deciding to raise my arm" covers a whole family of physio states, any one of which would've done the job — so the mental cause isn't racing a specific physical cause for the finish line, it's more like a generalization that stands above the details. Coarse causes don't exclude fine causes because they're not in the same race. At least that's how it seems to me, and my brother-in-law (a neurologist, insufferable) thinks it's hand-waving.

Tell me what would actually count against my view. Not just the standard objection that I'm secretly saying the mental cause does nothing the physical one doesn't already do — though tell me if that objection is right — but the full map: which thought experiments, which intuitions about cases, which things from neighboring areas (like what we say about levels of explanation in other sciences, or about whether "the match caused the fire" and "the burning tip of the match caused the fire" can both be true) my position stands or falls with. Which of these would someone committed to my view have to concede really hurts, and which could they reasonably wave off? And be honest with me: has my position been padded with enough "well, it's just a different level of grain" moves that nothing could ever refute it? Because if so I'd kind of like to know before I keep boring people at Thanksgiving with it. Rank the problems from fatal to merely awkward, and if you think the grain idea has been tried by actual philosophers and failed, say how and why, I won't be offended.
