I'm a third-year graduate student, and this past spring I served for the first time on our department's fellowship panel. We had three formal criteria a proposal had to satisfy: that the project was significant, that the applicant could realistically complete it within the award period, and that it fit the funder's stated priorities. One applicant ended up in a strange position — a majority of the panel judged each of the three criteria satisfied, yet a majority ranked her below the funding line, because the overlapping majorities were made of different people. When I described this at the time, a senior colleague waved it off as an artifact of our voting order: we scored criteria after reading the files, rather than voting on each applicant's overall merit first. That gesture at the agenda is what I want to work on, but I now see the standard treatments don't quite address it. The usual debate asks which aggregation rule a group should adopt — premise-driven procedures versus outcome-driven ones, with the impossibility results showing that no rule satisfies everything we want — but that debate assumes the rule is chosen, explicitly, by someone. In actual committees like ours, nobody chooses a rule; the rule is fixed by the order of business, which is fixed by whoever chairs the meeting and by procedural inertia. So the standard fixes quietly relocate the question rather than answering it.

Turn this into a proposal for a one-semester term paper, roughly 6000 words. The question as I'd like it stated: when the collective judgment a panel reaches depends on the sequence in which its questions are taken up, is that agenda-dependence a defect of the procedure that should be eliminated, a cost to be distributed fairly (whoever controls the agenda controls the outcome, which is a real assignment of power), or an unavoidable feature of any multi-member body making linked judgments? Make sure the question is stated so it has a determinate answer, and explain why the existing positions leave it open — the standard defense of premise-based procedures doesn't tell us who gets to decide which propositions count as premises, and the impossibility results establish a dilemma without ranking the ways of living with it. For the method, say what I would actually do, month by month: reconstruct the fellowship-panel case formally, work through how the answer changes under at least two different accounts of what the panel is for (reaching the best verdict, versus producing a decision the members can each endorse in public), and test the candidate answers against hard cases where changing the agenda would flip a result the members care about. State the possible conclusions and which considerations would decide among them, be honest about what would count as the project failing — including the possibility that the notion of "agenda control" can't be made precise enough to do the work — and give a candid assessment of whether this is too much for one semester. Don't include citations or a reading list; I'll supply those myself. The response should begin with the proposal itself, with no preface or commentary beforehand.
