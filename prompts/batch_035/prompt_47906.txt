I've been reading (and occasionally commenting in) the identity-adjacent threads on the rationalist corner of the internet for well over a decade, and I've finally admitted to myself that my object-level verdicts about the Ship of Theseus and its many cousins don't fall under any single principle I can state clearly. I'd like you to do the reflective equilibrium for me.

Take the role of a professional metaphysician who works on persistence and identity over time. Please think carefully, and before committing to anything, brainstorm several candidate principles — spatiotemporal continuity, continuity of form or function, causal-historical continuity, some best-candidate or closest-continuator rule, a sortal-relative account on which "same ship" and "same band" answer to different criteria — and test each against all of my verdicts, not just the first few.

Here are my verdicts:

1. Theseus's ship, planks replaced one by one over years of ordinary maintenance: same ship. I'm not moved by the observation that no original plank survives.
2. Hobbes's twist, where the discarded planks are gathered and reassembled into a second ship: my gut says the continuously sailing vessel is the ship and the reassembled one is a replica — but I wobble when I notice the reassembled one holds all the original matter. Treat this as my least stable verdict.
3. Grandfather's axe with two new heads and three new handles (the old Trigger's broom gag): same tool.
4. My car, after a crash, rebuilt onto a new chassis with only 30% original parts carried over in a single month-long repair: same car, though less confidently than case 1.
5. A rock band, forty years old, with no original members but an unbroken touring and recording history: same band.
6. The tribute act made up of the founding members playing the old songs: not the same band.
7. A codebase refactored line by line over fifteen years until nothing of the original remains — what my colleagues call the Ship of Theseus antipattern: same software, same license, same warranty obligations.
8. A big-bang rewrite: a new team reimplements the identical spec from scratch in a year: different software, even though it does the same job.
9. A hiking trail re-routed a hundred meters uphill after erosion, same blazes and same guidebooks: same trail.
10. A restaurant in its third generation of family ownership, menu and cuisine entirely changed: not really the same restaurant, though the same business. Note that 5 and 10 pull in opposite directions and I can't articulate why.
11. Me, after the usual turnover of matter: same person, obviously.

Deliver a principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause, plus verdicts on cases I haven't listed — say, a violin restored with 70% new wood, a constitution replaced clause by clause over two centuries, and a marriage after fifty years in which every habit, friend, and belief has changed. Then tell me honestly which of my verdicts your preferred principle cannot save, and whether I should drop the verdict or complicate the principle.

Begin with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning — no preamble, no restating my cases back at me.
