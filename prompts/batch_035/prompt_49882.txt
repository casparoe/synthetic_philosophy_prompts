The exam question was: "Present the dogmatism paradox, using Sosa's parking-lot version of it. Then explain what responses are available, and finally say whether the reasoning the paradox targets is the same phenomenon as bootstrapping arguments about reliability, or whether they should be kept apart."

The student wrote:

"The dogmatism paradox, from Harman's train example but best known in Sosa's parking lot version: I parked my car this morning and haven't been back since. I know my car is in the lot. I also know that if my car is in the lot, it hasn't been stolen. So by closure of knowledge under known entailment, I know my car hasn't been stolen. But it seems wrong to say I know that — I haven't checked, and ordinarily nobody would claim to know their car wasn't stolen without checking. Hence the paradox: closure, together with the knowledge I plainly have, generates knowledge I intuitively shouldn't have.

What the paradox really shows is that closure is false. Since we can't know the car wasn't stolen, but we do know it's in the lot, the only way out is to deny that knowledge is closed under known entailment. That was Harman's own response and it has become the standard one in the literature.

As for bootstrapping, that's just the same trick in a different setting. In the gas-gauge case you reason: the gauge reads full; if the gauge is reliable, the tank is full; the tank is full; so the gauge was right on this occasion — and then, repeating the pattern, you conclude inductively that the gauge is reliable. Since the dogmatism paradox likewise lets you conclude something (that the car wasn't stolen) without any independent evidence, they are the same phenomenon, and whatever dissolves one dissolves the other."
