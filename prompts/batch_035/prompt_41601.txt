At seventy-four, after a career in condensed-matter physics, I have lately been attending a philosophy reading group that meets fortnightly in a member's sitting room. We recently spent an entire evening on Newcomb's problem, and two members argued past each other for so long that I lost the thread, though I was asked to summarize next time. I would rather come with a clear map of who is right about what. Allow me to reconstruct the exchange as faithfully as I can.

To recall the setup: a predictor who has been right in every recorded case has placed a million dollars in a closed box if and only if it predicted you would take only that box; the other box is transparent and holds a thousand dollars regardless. You may take both boxes or only the closed one. The disputants were our host's son, an actuary by training, and a retired psychiatrist who joined us last spring.

(1) The actuary opened by saying the case is trivial: the boxes are already filled before you choose, nothing you do now can alter their contents, and taking both therefore gets you a thousand more than taking one, in every physically possible state of the world. Declining the extra thousand, he said, is like refusing to pick up a coin you are walking past.

(2) The psychiatrist answered that a theory of rational choice which instructs you to walk out with a thousand dollars, every single time, while the people who ignore it collect a million, has forfeited its title. On any run of this experiment, she said, the one-boxers win; a definition of rationality that systematically loses money is a bad definition.

(3) The actuary replied that the correlation the psychiatrist keeps citing is a common-cause artifact: the predictor's earlier prediction influences both what is in the box and, through your character, what you now do. Correlation of that sort is exactly what he spent his working life discounting, and by the psychiatrist's reasoning, he said, you ought to treat a barometer as a lever on the weather. He added a medical example he seemed to think damning: if a genetic condition disposes a person both to smoke and to develop cancer, it would be absurd to quit smoking on the ground that abstention improves the odds.

(4) The psychiatrist conceded the genetic case but denied its relevance: the lesion is indifferent to your deliberations, whereas the predictor is a machine built to model precisely those deliberations. Before the boxes were filled, she said, you had it in your power to become the sort of decider the predictor would read as a one-boxer, and rationality plausibly concerns the selection of one's whole decision procedure, not just the final act.

(5) The actuary's rejoinder was that by the moment of choice everything is fixed, so the psychiatrist's position either smuggles in backward causation, with the present choice reaching back to shape the prediction, or collapses into fatalism, in which case deliberation is idle theater. He said her talk of deciding what sort of decider to be was psychological packaging around one of those two absurdities.

(6) She closed by suggesting that choice and prediction might instead be joint downstream effects of an upstream fact about the decider, and that the actuary's dilemma assumed too narrow a menu of causal structures. Neither would yield, and the rest of us had run out of evening.

I confess my sympathies drifted toward the actuary, since I was trained for decades never to mistake correlation for causation, and yet I cannot shake the feeling that a person who leaves the room with a thousand dollars has done something wrong. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: say, for each of the six numbered steps, whether the speaker is right, partly right, or mistaken, and why. Then tell me who is more right overall, and identify anything of substance that both of them missed. I would ask that your reply be continuous prose only, in the manner of a letter, without headings, bullets, bold text, or tables of any kind.
