At a recent dinner, two friends of mine — a physics postdoc and a graduate student in history of science — became locked in a disagreement about whether the Scientific Revolution actually happened, and I would like someone to referee it. Let me lay out the exchange as it actually unfolded, since I honestly could not tell at each step who had the better of it.

Daniel opened by saying that the Scientific Revolution is the most consequential event in the history of human knowledge: the moment when Europe replaced appeals to ancient authority with systematic observation and mathematical demonstration, running roughly from Copernicus in 1543 to Newton's Principia in 1687. He allowed that the label is somewhat arbitrary but insisted the underlying transformation is as real as anything in history.

Priya replied that the label is not merely somewhat arbitrary; it is a historiographical construction of the twentieth century. She pointed out that the phrase in its modern sense was popularized by historians like Alexandre Koyré and Herbert Butterfield, who wrote evocatively about the period's importance, and that earlier generations simply did not conceive of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries as a unified happening called a revolution. Her conclusion: since the concept was invented after the fact and imposed on scattered developments, the Scientific Revolution never actually occurred, and continuing to speak of it perpetuates a myth.

Daniel objected that this reasoning proves far too much. The books exist; the people existed; Copernicus really did publish on the motions of the heavens, Galileo really did point telescopes at the sky, and Newton really did produce his mathematical physics. Saying the Scientific Revolution didn't happen because the name came later, he argued, is like denying the fall of the Western Roman Empire happened because later historians chose to treat 476 as a boundary. Events do not come pre-labeled; periodization is how historians make the past intelligible, and it is no less legitimate for that.

Priya conceded that events do not come pre-labeled but pressed the disanalogy. The fall of Rome, she said, at least corresponds to a datable political event with an identifiable before and after. The so-called Scientific Revolution stretches across a century and a half, involved a small number of people who mostly did not know one another, and proceeded in fits and starts with abundant continuity — the period's practitioners were, after all, educated scholastics, and many of the crucial ingredients, from the medieval universities to the recovery of ancient texts, predate it entirely. Calling this a revolution, she argued, smuggles in a political metaphor implying a sudden, coordinated, and deliberate overthrow of an old order, and that metaphor is false to the historical record.

Daniel answered that the metaphor need not carry all that freight. The Industrial Revolution is routinely described as a revolution, though it unfolded over many decades, affected regions unevenly, and involved no coordinated conspiracy to seize power. If gradual, uneven, but epochal change can still count as a revolution there, the same courtesy should extend to the sciences. He added that whatever we call it, the cumulative outcome was a genuinely new kind of knowledge-making, which is more than most centuries can claim.

Priya's final move was to turn the dispute about words. Perhaps, she said, if revolution is defined loosely enough, the label can be made to fit; but then the term has been drained of its distinctive content, and the only remaining question is whether it misleads more than it illuminates. In her view it misleads: it suggests a clean break from a supposedly benighted medieval period, which is doubly fabricated, since the Middle Ages are themselves a retrospective category invented to serve exactly the kind of before-and-after story she is complaining about. The whole apparatus, she concluded, is a convenience of textbook writers, and conveniences of textbook writers should not be mistaken for features of the past.

Daniel closed by noting that she had now expanded the target from one period label to all of them, and that this concession cuts against her original claim. If every period boundary — antique, medieval, early modern — is drawn partly by later interest, then drawing such boundaries is simply what historians do, and the question is whether the Scientific Revolution is drawn worse or better than its neighbors, not whether it is uniquely fake. He suspected that her real complaint was with triumphalist narratives about science rather than with the periodization itself, and that she had wrapped an objection to the storytelling into an objection to the category.

Neither would budge, and the table broke up around them. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each step in the argument as I have recorded it, say whether the speaker is right, partly right, or mistaken, and why. Then tell me who is more right overall, and identify anything of substance that both of them missed. I would especially like to know whether the dispute is, in the end, merely verbal, or whether something substantive hangs on which of them is correct.
