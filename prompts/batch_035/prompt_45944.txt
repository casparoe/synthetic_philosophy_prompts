As part of my job at a mid-sized AI lab, I've been assigned to write the internal case for moving our evaluation stack away from outcome-based supervision — checking final results, reward on task completion — and toward process-based supervision: monitoring and approving each step of a system's reasoning and actions as they happen. Having spent a month on this, I've come to hold a specific view: that process-based supervision cannot deliver the safety it promises, because somewhere down the line every process check must itself be validated by outcomes, so the pressure toward reward hacking and specification gaming isn't eliminated but merely relocated — now applied to producing plausible-looking step descriptions rather than to the final result — while the added overhead degrades capabilities enough that labs under competitive pressure will quietly hollow the process checks out. Tell me what would count against this view. I want the observations, arguments, and formal results that should move me, sorted by how decisive they would be — including things that have arguably already happened in deployed systems or in published process-supervision work. Distinguish what a committed holder of my view would concede as counting against it from what they would dismiss, and say whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability: is "the gaming pressure just moves elsewhere" the kind of claim that can survive any evidence, and if so, is that a defect for a view of this kind or just what you should expect of a claim about incentive relocation?
