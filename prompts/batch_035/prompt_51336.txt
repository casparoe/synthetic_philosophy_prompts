I teach an upper-level seminar on early modern political thought — fourteen students, one 75-minute session — and we've just finished the Pascal unit. The text we worked through is the fragment where he argues that justice without force is helpless, because the just are always gainsaid by offenders, while force without justice is tyrannical and condemned, so that justice and might must be combined; and, since we have been unable to make what is just strong, we have made what is strong just. On the page this reads as either a cynical aphorism or a piece of proto-Hobbesian sociology, and my students split accordingly: half find it obvious, half find it repugnant, and neither group seems to feel why Pascal thought both halves were needed — why the trouble with might is not merely that it can be abused, and the trouble with justice not merely that it can be ignored. I want them to run into that themselves, in a structured activity, before the fragment is discussed as a whole. They have read the fragment, so I'm not hiding the topic from them; what I'm hiding is the diagnosis, and the point at which their own society in miniature starts producing it.

I'd like a three-phase society game, designed so that each phase enacts one limb of the fragment. Phase one should be an unenforced order: groups produce and trade scarce goods under agreements that are purely verbal, with a payoff scheme in points (no real money — redeemable at most for something trivial, like choice of the final seminar topic or a box of pastries) and a scoring structure that makes quiet defection profitable, so the order collapses through their own choices rather than through my staging. Phase two should introduce a single Enforcer — one student, or me — with real sanctioning power over arbitrary rules the players had no hand in making, and the phase should be built so that compliance evaporates the moment enforcement lapses: I want a window where the Enforcer's attention is elsewhere and players can defect with impunity, and the game should reward them for catching it. Phase three should attempt legitimation: the same arbitrary rules, now wrapped in something — a fabricated founding story, an unverifiable claim that the rules are ancient, some ritual or status marker — and the phase should be scored so that participants who comply even when unobserved do better than participants who comply only when watched, without my ever telling them that unobserved compliance is what's being measured. I'd need the full apparatus: group roles and counts, the materials (I'm assuming index cards, tokens, a compliance ledger, whatever else), the concrete rules and payoff tables for each phase, a minute-by-minute timeline fitting the 75 minutes with room for the debrief, and a specification of how I should behave as facilitator in each phase — including what I fabricate in phase three and how I keep it unverifiable.

Then the debrief, and I want it to do three things. First, map the moments: which phase-one defection stands for justice without force being gainsaid, which phase-two rebellion stands for force without justice being condemned, and the phase-three pattern of unobserved compliance for the making-strong-just move — with students, ideally, discovering from the ledger that the phase-three rules were the phase-two rules with a story attached, and being asked whether the story or the sanctions was doing the work. Second, tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong on a first run and how to handle it: I anticipate at least that phase one may fail to collapse because fourteen seminar-mates who know each other import their real trust into the game, that the Enforcer role can be uncomfortable or too much fun, and that philosophy students may be so primed to unmask ideology that phase three never produces the compliance it needs — but tell me what you actually expect and what the fixes are. Third, be frank about which feature of the fragment the activity cannot reproduce and I must supply in the debrief. My own worry is that the game can only ever deliver the sociological Pascal — might needing a story — while the fragment's deeper claim is that there is a true justice which we are merely unable to enforce, a claim about what we can't reach rather than about what we tell ourselves; if the game can't stage that, I need to know exactly where the handoff to lecture has to happen.
