I'd like a careful explanation of a dispute I've run into concerning the Allais paradox — specifically, whether the famous preference pattern actually violates the independence axiom, or whether it violates only a misapplied version of it.

Here is what I think I understand so far, and where I lose the thread. In the von Neumann–Morgenstern setting, the independence axiom is stated over lotteries with objectively given probabilities: if you prefer lottery A to lottery B, you should also prefer any mixture that swaps in A for B as a common branch, since the branches where they differ are the only places preference can come from. The Allais pattern — taking the sure thing in one pair while taking the riskier gamble in the other, where the two pairs share a common branch in the relevant sense — seems to flout this. But I've read that there's a subtler story in the Savage framework. There, probabilities aren't built into the objects of choice, and the analogue of independence is the sure-thing principle over states of the world. Savage apparently suggested reframing the Allais choices as a two-stage decision — first a shared random event that may render your choice moot, then the choice itself — and on that framing the two options differ only on some states and coincide on the rest, so the sure-thing principle appears to forbid the Allais preferences outright. Yet this reconciliation is contested, and I can't reconstruct exactly why on my own.

Please explain, at a level that builds genuine intuition rather than just reciting the formalism: (a) what the independence axiom and the sure-thing principle each say, and in what sense they are supposed to be analogues across the two frameworks; (b) why the Allais pattern violates the mixture-based version; and (c) the strongest form of the two-stage argument that the pattern is irrational, together with the strongest replies — for instance, that the agent may legitimately treat the stage-one event as probabilistically dependent on the stage-two outcomes, or that the mixture-based axiom presupposes a reduction of compound lotteries that the agent need not accept, or that regret and disappointment can be accommodated by describing outcomes more finely. To test my grasp, please also construct a small worked example in which the shared event is correlated with the later outcomes in a way that makes the Allais pattern rational, so I can see precisely where the sure-thing argument breaks.

Throughout, please clearly separate what is settled among decision theorists from what remains genuinely contested — for example, whether the two-stage reconciliation succeeds, and whether redescribing outcomes to include regret rescues expected utility theory or merely trivializes it by making it unfalsifiable.
