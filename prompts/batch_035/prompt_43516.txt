I have a set of verdicts about what can and cannot be passed along by testimony in the aesthetic domain, and I would like to know whether any single principle captures them. Here are my gut reactions, honestly reported even where I suspect they are inconsistent.

A friend whose taste has tracked mine for years tells me the new film by a director we both love is a masterpiece. I believe her before seeing it, and I act on the belief — I buy tickets, I recommend the film to others. Yet I feel I have not acquired whatever it is one acquires from seeing it, and I would not say I have experienced its being a masterpiece.

A respected architecture critic writes that a building I have never visited is a forbidding and alienating piece of design. I believe this, cite it in conversation, and feel no unease at all about doing so.

I taste nothing wrong with a wine, but a sommelier I trust tells me it is corked. I believe the wine is flawed. This feels entirely fine — in fact it feels like a paradigm of learning from an expert.

A friend describes a sunset to me, and though I believe her that it was beautiful, I balk at saying I know the sunset was beautiful, in a way I do not balk at saying I know the exhibit was controversial when she tells me that. The asymmetry feels real to me even though both claims reached me by the same route.

Someone tells me a joke is funny. I hear the joke and do not find it funny at all. I nonetheless believe it is funny — funny to its intended audience, funny in the teller's mouth. This feels like ordinary belief from testimony, not a degraded or secondhand version of anything.

Here is the tension I keep circling. The corked wine and the beautiful sunset both seem to be claims whose truth depends on how things strike someone. Yet I accept the wine verdict from the expert without reservation while withholding something — knowledge, entitlement, I am not sure what — in the sunset case. And my tentative principle makes it worse: I want to say testimony transmits whatever is available to common observation but not claims whose truth depends on the subject's own response. The wine seems to fall on the wrong side of that line, and the forbidding building, which I happily accept on the critic's word, may fall on the wrong side too, since whether it alienates seems to depend on the observer as much as whether a sunset is beautiful does.

So: is there a principle that captures these verdicts, or must some go? Candidates I can half-see involve a distinction between claims about how things are for an expert observer, claims about how things are for me, and purely descriptive aesthetic claims — but I cannot make that tripartite division do the work without the joke case collapsing into the wrong category. Please state the principle, or a small set of candidates, as precisely as you can, say which verdict motivates each clause, tell me what it predicts about two further cases I have not mentioned — someone telling me a poem is a sonnet versus telling me it is haunting, and someone telling me a meal was delicious versus that it was over-salted — and be explicit about which of my verdicts your preferred principle cannot save, since I suspect at least one of them deserves to be sacrificed.
