i teach democratic theory at a mid-size state school -- upper-level seminar, 18 students, one 80-minute session. next week is the boundary problem: who counts as the demos, and why that question is so much harder than it looks. i don't want to lecture it at them. i want an activity where they manufacture the problem for themselves before they know it has a name.

here's my half-formed idea, feel free to fix or replace it: the seminar has to make a series of real decisions with real stakes (candy i bring in, plus one genuinely binding decision like how the pizza budget for the final session gets split), and the twist is that each decision also determines the franchise for the next round. so round one might be voted on by everyone, but the outcome of round one reallocates votes for round two -- maybe people who "won" round one lose their vote, or a subgroup gets enfranchised that wasn't before. i want them to run into three things without me prompting: (1) that "everyone affected" and "everyone subject to the decision" pick out different groups, and they'll start fighting about which principle to use at exactly the moment the choice of principle changes the outcome, (2) that the demos feels totally natural while it's given and falls apart the second anyone has to justify it, and (3) the regress -- whoever gets to decide who counts is itself a group nobody authorized, and they should notice that on their own.

give me the full design: rules, materials (no tech, nothing i need to buy beyond candy), the payoff scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline that fits 80 minutes, and the debrief. the debrief should map specific moments from what actually happened onto the standard positions (all-affected, all-subjected, rolling or issue-specific constituencies) -- but only after they've lived it, they shouldn't hear the term "boundary problem" until the last 10 minutes. also tell me what will predictably go worng the first time i run this -- i suspect someone will immediately propose universal suffrage for every round as a fix, and i want to know whether to let that ride or how to complicate it. and be honest about which part of the boundary problem no in-class activity can reproduce (my guess: the sheer scale and historical path-dependence of real boundaries, but you tell me) so i know what i have to just say at the end.
