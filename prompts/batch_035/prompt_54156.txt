I've become convinced that causation is best analyzed interventionistically: roughly, the claim that C causes E is the claim that an idealized surgical intervention on C, holding the appropriate background fixed, would change the probability or the value of E. I arrived at this partly out of dissatisfaction with the counterfactual analyses in the Lewis tradition — the similarity ordering among possible worlds always seemed to me to do the real work while remaining unexplained, whereas the interventionist account at least tells you something operational about how to find out whether a causal claim holds. I'd now like a map of the position's exposure. What would actually count against it?

I'm aware of several objections and would like your assessment of them, but I'd also like to know what I've missed:

1. Causation by omission and by absences — where there appears to be no variable to intervene on (the gardener fails to water the plants, and they die).
2. The circularity worry: an intervention is itself a causal notion, so perhaps the analysis presupposes what it was meant to analyze.
3. The dependency on how variables are carved up: it looks as though the framework presupposes a great deal of structure — which variables exist, which are exogenous, which are modular and independently manipulable — that it was supposed to illuminate.
4. The assumption of acyclicity and modularity: what happens with genuine feedback loops, or in systems where intervening on one variable necessarily disturbs the mechanisms connecting others?
5. Token causation: the framework seems naturally suited to type-level, general causal claims, while singular, actual-causation judgments (who caused the crash) appear to need extra machinery bolted on.

For each: would a committed interventionist concede it as a genuine cost, or insist it rests on a confusion? Which of these, if pressed hard, would force a revision of the view rather than a further refinement — and which refinements are principled rather than ad hoc? I'm particularly interested in whether the position risks becoming insulated: able to accommodate any apparent counterexample by adjusting what counts as the right variable, the right intervention, or the right background conditions, so that nothing could ever count against it. If it is so insulated, tell me whether you think that is disqualifying for an analysis of causation, or just the normal condition of an analytical framework in metaphysics.

Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say plainly which you think the strongest case against the position is, and note anything the position predicts or forbids that its rivals do not — cases where interventionism and the counterfactual view of possible worlds genuinely come apart, not just where they use different vocabulary for the same verdict.
