I'm on the board of a small institute that can fund roughly eight researchers for four years on conceptual work about machine sentience and digital minds — philosophy, decision theory, and formal modelling, not empirical interpretability or neuroscience. We have to choose an agenda, and I want a defended prioritization rather than a survey: which open questions in this area most deserve those researcher-years, in what order, and why.

To make the task concrete, here are the clusters we've been weighing, though I'd welcome additions or omissions. First, boundary questions: which functional or computational properties, if instantiated, suffice for morally relevant experience — and in particular whether properties like recurrence, self-modelling, or a certain kind of integration can be type-identified across radically different substrates. Second, attribution questions, which I suspect are more neglected: when a morally relevant computation is embedded in a larger system, does the patienthood attach to the whole, to the sub-process, or somehow to both, and what happens when one such process is run many times or split across machines. Third, cardinality and individuation, a cluster I've seen almost no serious work on: does instantiating the very same computation twice, at different times or on different hardware, create two episodes of experience or one, and does a set of trained weights sitting motionless on a disk instantiate anything at all absent a forward pass? Fourth, decision-making under severe uncertainty: how a funder or a developer should act when the probability that a system matters morally is unknown but not zero, including whether there's a principled middle ground between ignoring the possibility and treating every optimization process as a patient. Fifth, the epistemology of detection: what would even count as evidence about inner states in systems built to report on themselves, given that training can produce reports untethered from whatever else is going on.

For each question you prioritize, I want the reasoning, not just the placement: why it's upstream of the others, whether it's tractable with conceptual tools rather than empirical ones, how neglected it is relative to its importance, and what developments over the next few years — new model architectures, court cases, public attention — would move it up or down the list. I'm especially interested in whether you think the attribution and individuation questions are genuinely load-bearing for practice or merely fascinating, since our instinct is that boundary-drawing has soaked up attention that the messier questions about parts, copies, and multiplicity actually deserve, and I'd like that instinct either confirmed or taken apart. I also want you to be explicit about the criteria you're using and where they conflict — if the most important question is also the least tractable within four years, say so and tell me what you'd fund instead.

One constraint on the format of your answer: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. Write it as continuous prose, the way you'd write a long memo to the board, with the recommended ordering and its justification woven through the paragraphs rather than presented as a list.
