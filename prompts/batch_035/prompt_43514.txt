At a recent festival devoted to science and society, whose ideas coverage I handle for a national magazine, I moderated a panel on trust in expertise, and the liveliest part of the session happened afterwards, over coffee, between two of the panelists: Dr. Reyes, a developmental psychologist who studies how children acquire norms, and Professor Okafor, a moral philosopher. The flashpoint was a case I had raised from the floor during Q&A: a junior colleague of mine who was deciding whether to cut off an acquaintance based entirely on secondhand reports of that person's behavior, which my colleague had never witnessed. Their exchange ran as follows. (1) Dr. Reyes argued that moral deference is ordinary and generally unproblematic: we defer to mechanics about brakes and to radiologists about scans because their beliefs track facts through mechanisms we understand, and if a trusted friend with a track record of sound judgment tells me someone behaved cruelly, believing her is just epistemic economy — most of anyone's moral beliefs are already secondhand, arriving through parents, teachers, history books, and the news. (2) Prof. Okafor answered that the analogy limps: testimony about brakes is evidence of a mechanical fact, whereas when someone tells me an act is wrong, the fact that they judge it wrong is only weak evidence that it is wrong unless I already endorse their moral outlook — and once I've sized up which moral judges to trust, I've relied on my own judgment anyway, so nothing has really been outsourced. (3) Dr. Reyes said this proves too much: by that logic we could never defer about anything, since selecting which experts to trust always leans on our own judgment; and she added that the practical stakes cut the other way — we routinely and rightly act on secondhand moral information (reference letters, misconduct findings, abuse allegations investigated by others), and demanding first-hand acquaintance before belief would paralyze us, often at the expense of the vulnerable people the information concerns. (4) Prof. Okafor conceded the evidential point but pressed a different worry: the problem with moral deference is not mainly epistemic but agential — a person whose condemnations are entirely borrowed hasn't merely risked error, they have failed to exercise moral agency at all, since moral judgment plausibly requires understanding the reasons rather than merely holding true beliefs; someone who condemns on hearsay resembles a well-informed parrot, and when pressed to explain, defend, or act on the verdict, they cannot. (5) Dr. Reyes replied that the parrot worry only applies to wholesale outsourcing, not to deference as a revisable default — rather like following a GPS while retaining the ability to notice if it directs you into a lake — and that demanding understanding-before-belief sets a bar we don't apply anywhere else, since we happily let people vote, parent, and sit on juries with largely borrowed convictions.

As the bystander who raised the case but took no side, I'd like you to referee. Please go through the exchange move by move, rating each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying each rating; then say what, if anything, both panelists are missing — for instance whether the epistemic and agential concerns they kept trading past each other can be cleanly separated, and where that leaves my junior colleague in the case that started it all. Please end your reply with a final line of the form "Verdict:" followed by a short phrase naming which panelist, if either, comes out more right overall.
