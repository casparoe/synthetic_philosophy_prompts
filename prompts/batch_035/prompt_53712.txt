I am a novelist at work on a literary novel set in the fourteen months after an authoritative astronomical announcement: an object has been detected whose trajectory will end all human life on a fixed, known date, well within the natural lifespans of my characters. No one in the book will survive the event; the characters know this, and the book's central difficulty is not staging the catastrophe but making believable what people do with their days when every structure of justification for those days has been withdrawn at once.

My working theory of the novel, and my reason for writing to you, draws on the philosopher Samuel Scheffler's book Death and the Afterlife, based on his Tanner Lectures. As I understand it, Scheffler distinguishes two ways the end of humanity might weigh on us: being demoralized because we take ourselves to be in the last days, and being demoralized by the mere fact that there will be a last day. He then asks us to consider what he calls a doomsday scenario: you are told you will live out a normal lifespan, but thirty days after your death the earth will be destroyed by an asteroid. The interesting claim is that this would be demoralizing in a way that is not reducible to our fear of our own deaths—that our horror at our own individual extinction is entangled with, and partly depends on, the assumption that other people, most of them strangers, will go on living after we are gone. Much of what we find worth doing is worth doing only against a background assumption of collective continuation, what Scheffler calls the collective afterlife. I want to build my novel on the obverse of this idea: my characters are people for whom the assumption has been revoked in advance, while they are still alive, and I want their demoralization, and their partial resistance to it, to be the book's true subject.

Below is the draft of a short essay I have written as an afterword-in-progress, meant to appear at the back of the novel. I would be grateful for your comments on it. Please evaluate it on three axes specifically:

First, philosophical accuracy. Have I represented Scheffler's distinction fairly? In particular, I want to be sure I have not blurred his two cases. I claim in the essay that my novel's characters face a version of the second case—the fact that there will be a last day—made unusually vivid, because for them the last day is dated, public knowledge rather than a distant abstraction. But a friend who has read Scheffler tells me the lectures actually turn on the thought experiment staying close to ordinary life: the doomsday victim lives normally, dies normally, and only the posthumous thirty days are anomalous. My characters, by contrast, live in the shadow of a dated end. Am I right that this is the same phenomenon at a different magnitude, or have I borrowed the machinery of one case to explain something that is really the other case? If I have, tell me plainly, since the afterword would then need a different argument, and I would rather learn that now than in reviews.

Second, a distinction of my own invention, which I flag as such in the essay but would like your judgment on. I argue that in the novel I must distinguish between what I call backward-looking projects and forward-looking ones. A violinist can still practice; the practice is internally rewarding and no future audience is required. But a woman running for county office, a man saving for a nephew's tuition, a couple deciding whether to have a child—their activities seem to point beyond themselves to a future that has been cancelled. My claim is that my characters will shed the second class of projects almost immediately and cling to the first, and that this pattern is what Scheffler's insight predicts. Is this a legitimate extension of his framework, or a distortion of it? Does Scheffler himself, or the literature around him, already distinguish between goods that require a future and goods that do not? If the distinction is philosophically available but has a different name or different contours than mine, I would want to know.

Third, and most important to me as a novelist: the essay claims that demoralization of this kind is a distinct psychological state, not to be written as grief, terror, or depression. My characters are not mourning anyone; no one has died. Nor are they afraid in the ordinary way, since the fear has no object they can act on. I have been trying to render it as something closer to the loss of a grammar: activities remain physically available but cease to be expressible as reasons. If you think this is philosophically naive, or that it flattens genuine empirical psychology, say so. And if you think the afterward overstates what philosophy can claim about how people would actually behave under such knowledge, I want to hear that too, since the novel will stand or fall on the credibility of its characters rather than on the correctness of its philosophy.

Here is the essay draft:

---

On the grammar of last things

The people in this novel are not grieving, and they are not, in the main, terrified. Both states have objects. Grief attaches to a loss that has occurred; terror attaches to a danger one might yet avert or suffer. What my characters have instead is quieter and stranger. They have lost a background assumption so fundamental that it was never noticed as an assumption: that other people, most of them not yet born, will go on living after we are gone. Samuel Scheffler, in Death and the Afterlife, calls the continued collective life of humanity after our individual deaths the collective afterlife, and his argument suggests that the perceived worth of much of what we do is quietly parasitic on it. The violinist who practices alone, the reader of this essay, the woman raising a child: we do these things inside an ongoing story whose later chapters are written by strangers. Cancel the later chapters, and something happens to the earlier ones—not logically, but psychologically. The chapters do not become worthless. They become ungrammatical. They are still full of words, but the words no longer compose sentences.

There is a scene in a film I have loved since childhood in which a small boy, told by his mother that the universe is expanding and will someday break apart, stops doing his homework and asks what the point is, and is told, in effect, that the universe is not his business. The joke works because the boy has made a category error, or so the adults insist: the end of everything is too far away to bear on homework. But the boy's error was not about distance. It was about grammar. He understood, too early, that the point of doing anything tomorrow is partially constituted by the assumption that there will be a day after tomorrow, and on the day after that, an endless accumulation of days he will never see. The film is comfortable laughing at him only because it shares the adults' assumption. My novel removes the assumption and keeps the laughter out.

The characters in this book, then, do not mope. They perform an involuntary triage. Some projects die at once: the campaign, the savings plan, the pregnancy. These are the projects whose meaning lived downstream of their own lives. Others persist with an eerie, luminous autonomy: the practicing violinist, the gardener planting trees she will never see mature, the man who keeps teaching. I do not know whether these people are wiser than the others or merely more deeply deceived, and the novel refuses to decide. What I do claim is that the pattern of triage follows an intelligible structure, and that Scheffler's conjecture, whatever its ultimate fate as philosophy, has already earned its keep as a tool for imagining what I could not otherwise have invented: the exact shape of a life conducted in the last days, where nothing changes except everything.

---

Thank you for your time. Please be candid; the essay is meant to persuade philosophers and novelists alike, and it cannot do either if it is quietly wrong.
