I am a third-year PhD student in philosophy, writing a prospectus on the norms of assertion. Before our committee meeting, each of us circulates a short statement of the commitments the dissertation will presuppose, so that the committee can see where to push. I have drafted mine and would like it audited before it circulates. What I want is not a survey of the debate but a consistency check: which of these commitments pull against each other, which apparent conflicts dissolve on inspection, and what the cheapest repair would be for each genuine one, ranked by severity — and, at the end, which single revision would clean up the most. The commitments, as they will appear in the document:

1. The norm of assertion is knowledge: it is proper to assert that p only if one knows that p, and the norm is constitutive of assertion itself rather than a convention a community could have adopted otherwise.
2. Assertions of the form "p, but I don't know that p" are improper, and their impropriety is my principal reason for preferring the knowledge norm to truth-based and belief-based rivals.
3. Assertions of the form "p, but I don't believe that p" are improper as well, but for a purely pragmatic reason: such an assertion defeats itself, in a way that has nothing to do with which norm governs assertion.
4. Whether one knows that p does not vary with what is practically at stake. My ground is that stakes change neither one's evidence nor which counterfactual alternatives count as close: safety, and therefore knowledge, is stakes-invariant.
5. In the bank case, where a deposit must clear by Monday or the company defaults, asserting "It's open on Saturdays; I was there last Saturday" on that basis alone is improper and deserving of criticism — even though the same assertion on the same basis seems perfectly proper when nothing hangs on it.
6. Lottery propositions are unknowable: in a fair million-ticket draw, one cannot know that one's own ticket will lose.
7. Ordinary knowledge is robust: returning from two weeks away, one knows one's house is still standing; waiting at the gate, one knows one's flight will depart. I have no patience for skepticism about such propositions and will not concede that one merely believes them. If there is a difference between these cases and the lottery, it strikes me as obvious rather than in need of theorizing.
8. Hedged assertions are unproblematic: "I don't know that the pharmacy is still open, but I believe it is" is a perfectly proper thing to assert.

I have reread the list several times and cannot decide which parts are quietly inconsistent and which merely sound awkward together. Since the document invites exactly this scrutiny, I would rather learn where the real pressure falls now than discover it in the meeting. Please be specific about which pairs are in tension, how badly, and what each repair costs me elsewhere in the list.
