Six weeks ago I was appointed to my town's public works and safety committee, a body I had never given a moment's thought to before, and I have discovered that nearly every dispute that reaches us is at bottom a dispute about the precautionary principle — though not one of my colleagues ever calls it that. Residents invoke it when it suits them and forget it the moment it cuts against them, and I have caught myself doing the same. Rather than continue on impressions, I have tried to record my actual verdicts on the cases we have faced and to find the rule behind them. Here is what I have so far.

The utility proposed trialing a new corrosion-control additive in the drinking water. There is no evidence of harm, only the absence of long-term studies, and meanwhile our ageing pipes are leaching lead into the water right now — every month of delay is a month of known exposure. I voted to wait for further toxicology anyway. It felt obviously right at the time, and still does, even though on any simple reading the known harm was on the side of acting.

The school district proposed replacing its diesel bus fleet with electric buses, and a group of residents objected, citing battery fires. I thought we should approve immediately: the fire risk struck me as a bare possibility, not worth prolonging children's daily exposure to diesel exhaust. Structurally, this case looks identical to the first — a new technology, an uncertain risk, a harmful status quo — and I gave the opposite verdict without hesitation.

A telecom carrier wants to place a small tower four hundred metres from my daughter's elementary school, and a parents' group demands it be denied pending studies of health effects. I found the demand illegitimate, and I note that I hold this view with some feeling, since the case concerns my own child. Yet it rests on the same epistemic basis as my first verdict: unknown effects of something new.

The lake association asked to treat an invasive weed with a species-specific herbicide before it spreads further through the shallows. Waiting has costs of its own — the weed does not wait — and I said treat now, monitor closely. Precaution on the side of action, apparently.

Finally, a member asked that the summer fireworks festival be postponed pending a study of its effect on migrating birds. I thought that was perfectly reasonable: a small burden on the organizer, so why not ask. Yet I bristle at the parents' tower demand, which asks for exactly the same kind of study-before-permit.

My tentative principle is something like this: where an intervention carries a possibility of serious and irreversible harm, hold off until there is reasonable evidence of safety, unless holding off perpetuates a comparable harm. But I can see this principle does not fit me. The escape clause decides the water case against my own verdict, since lead exposure is surely a comparable harm; and the herbicide case, which the clause should also push toward immediate action, I did decide that way, while in the chemically identical-in-structure water case I did not. So either my principle is wrong, my verdicts are, or the relevant difference between the cases is something I have not yet named — perhaps who bears the risk, or whether the feared harm has a plausible mechanism (the tower fears have none, the chemical fears arguably do), or whether the proposed study is cheap to demand.

What I would like from you, then, is this. State, as precisely as you can, the principle or small set of candidate principles that best captures these verdicts, with the case that motivates each clause. Tell me what the best principle predicts for two cases now pending before us: whether to remove the derelict dam on the creek, which blocks fish passage but whose removal might stir up sediment-bound contaminants downstream; and whether to permit a company to pilot sidewalk delivery robots in the town centre. And be honest with me about which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate — I want to know not only which ones it overturns, but whether the ones it overturns are ones I should be glad to lose.
