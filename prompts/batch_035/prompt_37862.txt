I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background — everything I know about this stuff comes from a misspent twenties reading LessWrong and arguing on internal Slack. Our office runs a fortnightly internal talk series, mostly technical deep dives, and I've volunteered to give a forty-minute session, whiteboard only, on coherent extrapolated volition: the idea that instead of hand-specifying what an advanced AI should want, you point it at what humanity would want under some idealisation — roughly, what we would endorse if we understood more, thought things through longer, and were in some sense better versions of ourselves, with the further claim that these idealised wants converge enough to be usable.

The audience will be about thirty engineers, mostly backend and ML-adjacent: sharp, impatient, and allergic to anything that smells of mysticism or of hand-waving dressed up as depth. Several of them build preference and ranking systems for a living, which I suspect cuts both ways.

Please plan the talk rather than explaining the idea to me. In particular:

1. Ordering and dependencies. What has to be in place before the idealisation step can even seem motivated? My instinct is to open with "hand-specifying values fails, here are the hilarious failures", but I worry that leads the room straight to "so you learn values from labelled data", which is a different and much more comfortable conversation for them. Where should the talk actually begin, and what does each later step need from the earlier ones?

2. The carrying example. One concrete case to build the whole session on. My thought is an individual-level case first — a person whose stated wants diverge from what they would endorse after genuine reflection — before scaling to humanity. But I need an example that survives an audience that will immediately try to formalise it, poke at edge cases, and ask where the ground-truth labels come from. What must the right example have, and what must it not have?

3. The misconception map. What will this specific audience get wrong, and where in the sequence should each error be pre-empted? Candidates I can foresee: reading "coherent" as the technical consistency of a preference database (we have people who maintain exactly those and know how brittle they are — this could either derail everything or be my best asset, I can't tell which); mapping the whole proposal onto "a better reward model, then"; assuming extrapolation means literally simulating future people and polling them; assuming the output is a fixed specification rather than an ongoing process.

4. The aggregation wall. What happens when idealised wants conflict — between people, or within one person — is where I expect to lose the room, and "it's called *coherent* extrapolated volition because Yudkowsky hoped it would cohere" feels like a thin answer. Should I head this off early, treat it as the closing problem the talk has been building toward, or something else?

5. A closing question I can ask that would distinguish genuine understanding of the idea from polite nodding — ideally something they can't answer by pattern-matching to the nearest ML concept.

6. What to openly refuse to cover at this level and say so out loud. I assume the underlying metaethics is out of scope, but tell me where exactly to draw the line and how to say so without sounding like I'm dodging.

Finally, and this matters: flag anything in my own framing above that is subtly off. As I said, I have no formal background, and the thing I'm most afraid of is passing on a distortion I picked up second-hand and don't know is one.
