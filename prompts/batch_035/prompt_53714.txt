I work on the empirical study of moral character — background in social psychology, now finishing a philosophy PhD — and I have a single postdoc (about four years) to build a research program on virtue ethics after the situationist critique. I need to decide where to go deep, and I want more than a survey of live questions: I want a dependency-aware prioritization of the field's open problems, defended in terms of what my time and skill set can actually move.

Some context on where I think the debate stands. The critique, running from Mischel's work on the modest cross-situational consistency of trait-relevant behavior, through the classic experimental literature — Milgram's obedience paradigms, Asch's line judgments, Darley and Batson's seminary study, the bystander research — up to Doris and Harman's philosophical systematization, held roughly that global character traits enjoy little empirical support, so virtue ethics rests on a false picture of the person. My sense is that the first round is over: nobody defends pure globalism anymore. What is unsettled is whether anything recognizably Aristotelian survives, and which of the surviving projects is worth whose effort.

Here are the candidate questions I can see; you should add, split, or reject entries as you see fit:

1. Interactionism. People plausibly differ in how situationally susceptible they are, and situations interact with person-level dispositions. Is interactionism a real home for virtue ethics, or does it quietly reduce virtue to a statistical abstraction with no distinctive normative payoff?
2. Situation management as a virtue. On one response to the critique, the admirable agent is one who engineers her own circumstances — avoiding temptation-dense environments, building supportive routines, pre-committing. Is that a genuine recovery of a broadly Aristotelian idea about practical reason, or an admission that internal character was the wrong target all along, collapsing virtue into ordinary prudence?
3. Re-scoring the critique after the replication crisis. Several canonical situationist findings rest on small samples, questionable analytic choices, or failed or weakened replications. Does this re-open the empirical question enough to matter, or do converging field results carry the critique on their own?
4. Moral trait expression in the wild. Laboratory situations are short, artificial, and staged; diary studies, experience sampling, and longitudinal field work might paint a different picture of cross-situational and cross-temporal consistency.
5. Factitious and credit-based accounts. There are proposals on which attributing a virtue to someone can be self-fulfilling — the attribution helps make it true — and on which acting from virtue should be understood via earned moral credit rather than robust inner traits. Do these rescue anything of the traditional theory, or relabel the concession?
6. Formal and computational models of trait attribution, consistency, and person–situation interaction, including agent-based simulations of how communities ascribe character under realistic noise.

Two constraints on my side: I can run studies, work with longitudinal and experience-sampling data, and build formal models, but I have limited access to philosophy-department mentorship and no comparative advantage in ancient texts.

For the deliverable, please:

- Rank the questions you think most worth pursuing, for someone with this profile and this budget, and defend the ordering explicitly: which criterion (importance, tractability for me specifically, neglectedness, or how many downstream questions hang on it) is doing the work at each step, and where those criteria conflict.
- Say which questions are upstream of which — e.g., whether anything useful can be said about situation management before the interactionist modeling question is settled, or whether the normative-autonomy move in (5) and (6) makes the empirical agenda largely irrelevant.
- Say what evidence arriving over the next five years would move items up or down the list — a large pre-registered longitudinal study of trait stability, a wave of failed replications of person–situation interaction effects, a formal no-go result for trait ascription, new field data on temptation management in everyday life.
- Be explicit about what you would deprioritize even while granting it matters, and why: because it is intractable with my toolkit, because too many people already work on it, or because its answer would not change the downstream normative or applied picture (moral education, character measurement in organizations, virtue-talk in policy).
- Finally, tell me if my framing of the endgame is wrong: if the interesting post-situationist questions are no longer about whether character exists but about something else entirely — say, about which trait concepts to keep and which to retire, or about how agents should allocate their own self-improvement effort between building traits and building situations — restructure the agenda accordingly.
