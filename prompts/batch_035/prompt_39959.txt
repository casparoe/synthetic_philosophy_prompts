I write literary fiction, and for the past two years I have been translating my third novel into Spanish in collaboration with a native-speaker friend; my Spanish is serviceable, hers is better, and we work through the manuscript line by line. The experience has left me with a pattern I cannot find a name for. English lets me leave an astonishing number of things undecided on the page: my narrator can mention a brother without saying whether he is the elder or the younger, a visitor can be addressed without placing the relationship between speaker and addressee, and an event can be narrated without committing to whether it was finished or still underway. Spanish rarely lets me get away with any of this. Every chapter, the grammar presents us with a small queue of forced decisions — elder or younger brother, familiar or formal address, completed or ongoing — and no single decision feels momentous, yet each one settles something my English prose had left genuinely open, and after a few chapters the Spanish version of my narrator is a measurably more definite person than the English one. I do not experience this as loss; some of the decisions are improvements. But here is the part that fascinates me: when I reread my original English chapters now, I can no longer inhabit their vagueness. The open questions read as evasions, as though the English had been declining to commit, whereas before the translation I experienced those same sentences as open-handed rather than cagey. A choice made under grammatical duress in one language has followed me back into the other, and the other language has not forgiven it.

People I've described this to keep saying that it is just the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis, but it seems to me the wrong shape. The famous claim, as I understand it, is about limits: what a language permits you to think. My experience is not of a limit but of something more like a debt — Spanish does not stop me thinking anything; it bills me for things English was quietly carrying on credit, and once I have paid, I cannot un-pay even upon returning to English. Nor is it the translator's ordinary lament about what gets lost, because nothing is lost; something is decided, and the decision leaks backwards into the source text. What I would like to know is whether there is an established concept, in linguistics or the philosophy of language, for precisely this configuration: grammar functioning as a system of obligatory commitments that a writer working in another language can defer, where the commitment, once made, transfers across languages and retrospectively hardens the very text that allowed the deferral. If nothing established fits exactly, I should be grateful for a coined term with a careful definition, some boundary cases (for instance, does the phenomenon require bilingualism, or does it also cover a monolingual writer whose attention to her own indefiniteness is sharpened merely by having it pointed out?), and an account of how near it stands to the respectable modern descendants of Sapir and Whorf, if theirs is even the right lineage. A bare label will not satisfy me; I want to know what the thing is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
