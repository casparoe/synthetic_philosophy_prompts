I'm the thermal and energy engineer at a mid-size colocation data center, and at least once a month I sit through a pitch or design review where somebody invokes "information is physical" to justify something. Last quarter a vendor told us that deleting our cold archive would meaningfully reduce heat output in the storage rows because erasing bits carries a thermodynamic cost, and a colleague countered that the real energy cost of storage is idle spin, controller overhead, and cooling inefficiency, not the physics of erasure at all. I was a physics minor a long time ago and have forgotten most of it, but I know just enough about Landauer's principle, the Szilard engine, and Bennett's resolution of Maxwell's demon (the demon's memory has to be reset eventually, and that's where the entropy bill comes due) to suspect that both of them are gesturing at something real while using it at scales where it can't possibly matter -- or maybe I'm wrong and I'm the one gesturing. My VP has also started quoting reversible computing at me as the reason our long-term energy budget can be optimistic. Please turn the actual physics and philosophy of information into a procedure my team can run in ten minutes during a design review: an ordered list of questions to ask of any thermodynamics-of-information claim, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, at which step the procedure should stop with a verdict like "this is not a physics argument, it's an engineering-economics argument," and where it has to hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values or business priorities rather than physics. Please mark honestly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then run the procedure on three claims we've actually heard: (1) deleting cold data releases heat we should account for in our cooling budget; (2) compressing logs before writing them to disk saves a meaningful fraction of our write energy; and (3) reversible or adiabatic logic will eventually let our compute energy drop toward zero, so we should factor that into five-year planning. For (3) especially, I want the procedure to distinguish what the second law actually permits from what's just an engineering aspiration -- I dont want to concede more to the demon than physics requires, but I also dont want to wave off a real ceiling if there is one.

Please give it to me as a single JSON object with these fields: 'procedure', an array where each step is an object with 'step_number', 'question', 'sufficient_answer', 'insufficient_answer', 'judgment_call' (true/false), and 'if_failed' (what to do when the answer doesn't clear the bar); 'applications', an array with one object per claim above, each having 'claim', 'stopped_at_step', 'verdict', 'reasoning', 'what_the_physics_actually_says', and 'numbers_used' (the actual quantities you're comparing, e.g. the Landauer cost of erasing a bit at room temperature versus the real-world energy per bit of writing or storing one, with rough values -- if you can't pin a number, say so there instead of faking it); and 'limits', an array of strings describing what the procedure cannot settle and where a human just has to decide. Keep the reasoning strings plain enough that I can read them out loud in a design review without losing the room.
