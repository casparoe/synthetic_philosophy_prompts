In my distributive justice seminar we recently covered Parfit's distinction between egalitarianism and what he called the priority view. Afterwards two classmates, Lena and Marcus, kept the argument going in the hallway, and I honestly couldn't tell who was winning.

Lena defended the priority view: a fixed benefit matters more when it goes to someone worse off, and that's the entire view — it concerns people's absolute level, not how they stand relative to anyone else. Marcus said this was just egalitarianism trying to dodge the leveling-down objection: if you think it's bad that some are worse off than others, you seem committed to a pro tanto reason to eliminate the inequality even by making the better-off worse off while benefiting no one — which is absurd — and relabeling the concern as priority merely hides the commitment. Lena replied that this is exactly what the priority view avoids: it never gives any reason at all, however weak, to make someone worse off, so it passes the leveling-down test precisely where egalitarianism fails it, while still capturing the intuitions that drew us toward equality in ordinary cases.

Marcus then shifted ground: fine, he said, but the view has a blind spot of its own. Take two people both at a high absolute level — comfortably well-off — one somewhat richer than the other. Any serious egalitarian can say the gap itself is regrettable; the priority view, by contrast, has to say the gap as such is nothing, that the only thing that could count is a benefit to the person slightly worse off, and that since both are doing splendidly it counts for almost nothing. It cannot register that it is bad, in itself, that some have much more than others. Lena answered that this is a feature, not a bug: intuitions that gaps are intrinsically bad typically trade on envy, or on a sense of unfairness that is really about how the inequality arose — unearned advantages, rigged procedures — rather than about the inequality itself; where the inequality is deserved or innocently produced, most people's conviction that the gap per se is bad evaporates, and the priority view matches that, while egalitarianism does not.

Finally, Marcus pressed on the weights themselves. Why should the very same benefit count for more simply because its recipient is worse off? Egalitarians have an answer: it is bad, as such, that some are worse off than others, and the worse off are the ones bearing that badness. A prioritarian who refuses that answer is left asserting an extra weighting as a brute moral fact with no rationale — one can always fit a curve to one's case judgments, but a view that is merely a fitted curve is not doing any justificatory work. Lena's reply was that every non-derivational view bottoms out somewhere: utilitarians say suffering is bad, period, without offering a further reason, and are not accused of curve-fitting for it; demanding a deeper justification for the priority weighting while exempting other first principles is special pleading.

They eventually gave up and turned to the rest of us, and I couldn't referee it. Please go through their exchange move by move and say who is right at each stage. In particular, does the priority view really escape the leveling-down objection as cleanly as Lena claims — and if it does, does that escape cost it anything of independent moral importance?
