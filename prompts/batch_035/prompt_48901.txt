I came to linguistics as an undergraduate a true believer, and the poverty-of-the-stimulus argument is what converted me. The chain went like this: children acquire the structure-dependent rules of their language — the classic case being question formation, where the auxiliary of the main clause, not the first auxiliary in the string, moves to the front — on exposure to finite, noisy, and heavily degraded input. The input underdetermines the generalization, since a linear-rule account fits the observed data just as well; children never appear to entertain the linear hypothesis on their way to the structure-dependent one; the caregiver corrections needed to rule it out are famously absent or unreliable; and all of this unfolds on roughly the same schedule across wildly different environments and levels of parental speech. The conclusion I drew, and still hold, was that something substantive and language-specific has to be built in, and that what I was really studying was the innate endowment rather than learning proper.

The problem is that I now work as an ML engineer on pretraining, and a colleague of mine — sharp, not a crank — keeps telling me that large language models have refuted all of this. His claim is that a model trained on nothing but next-token prediction over raw text, with no built-in grammar and no correction signal, ends up with impressive command of auxiliary inversion, agreement, embedding, and long-distance dependencies, so the nativist inference from poverty of the stimulus to innate linguistic structure has simply failed. My stock replies — that these models see orders of magnitude more data than any child, that matching a corpus distribution is not acquiring a grammar, that what matters is acquisition from child-directed-scale input on a human timeline, and that the interesting evidence about children was never about the sheer existence of a learnable input-output mapping but about uniform, rapid, error-pattern-constrained acquisition — all feel to him like moving the goalposts, and I can't fully convince myself he's wrong about that. So: what would actually count against my position? I want the defeaters laid out and ranked from fatal to merely awkward, including the live ones. What would a serious proponent of innate structure concede as genuine evidence against them — say, a modest-scale model acquiring structure dependence from a corpus matched to what a child actually hears, or solid evidence that parental feedback is doing more work than the classic literature assumed — and which alleged defeaters would they rightly dismiss, and why? And in particular, I want an honest accounting of whether the position has by now been qualified into unfalsifiability, with each retreat (data quantity, then data quality, then the very definition of acquisition) making the empirical claim safe again, and whether that insulation is a fatal defect in a claim of this kind or just what you should expect of a claim about a species-level capacity that our models only approximate from the side.

For what it's worth, what would move me most: either a demonstration that the gap between child and model data budgets can be closed without any linguistic prior doing the work, or an argument that my colleague and I are not actually disagreeing about the mechanism but are using acquisition, grammar, and learning to pick out different things — and that once the words are disambiguated, there is a single well-posed empirical question underneath. If your diagnosis is the second, I'd want to know what that question is, and what result would settle it. Please be willing to conclude that the nativist position is genuinely well insulated and to say whether that should lower my confidence in it.
