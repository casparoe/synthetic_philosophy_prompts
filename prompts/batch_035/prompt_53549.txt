During my doctoral work in decision theory I keep colliding with a family of cases where my verdicts refuse to organize themselves into a theory, and I would like help extracting the principle, if there is one, that underwrites them — or being told honestly which verdicts have to go.

The cases are all one-shot, no communication possible, and the stakes are real money. My verdicts:

1. Prisoner's dilemma against an anonymous counterpart drawn at random from the population, about whose reasoning I know nothing: defect. This one feels settled.
2. Same dilemma against a counterpart I have strong reason to believe thinks about these matters much as I do — similar training, same arguments read, same intellectual temperament — though I cannot verify anything about them: I lean cooperate, but I could not defend the lean, and it would not take much to push me back.
3. Same dilemma against my identical twin, whose reasoning I know from long experience to track mine: cooperate, without hesitation.
4. Newcomb's problem with a predictor I am told is 99% accurate: one-box.
5. The twin case again, except a trusted intermediary has sealed both our moves in envelopes and informed each of us only that the other has already sealed: I defect — and yet I cannot articulate what the sealing ceremony changed, given that in case 3 my twin had presumably also already decided by the time I reasoned it through.
6. The twin case with an asymmetric matrix, where my defection saves me a little and ruins them: I still cooperate. But in the mirror version, where my cooperation costs me greatly for a small gain to them, I waver, and I am not sure in which direction.
7. Voting: I hold that a citizen in a close election has reason to vote, on the ground that their decision evidentially correlates with the decisions of like-minded voters — but I deny that a citizen has reason to cooperate in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma with a randomly selected fellow citizen, even though the two arguments appear to run on the same engine, and I do not know how to make that denial principled rather than ad hoc.

Standard causal decision theory captures 1 and 5 and loses 3 and 4; Hofstadter-style superrationality, or something evidential in the neighborhood, captures 3 and 4 but seems to me to overgenerate, pulling me toward cooperation in 2 and voting in 7 in ways I keep resisting. My working guess is that the operative variable is something like the degree and epistemic accessibility of the correlation between our decision processes — case 4's 99% versus case 1's unknown near-zero — but I cannot get that variable to behave in case 5, where the correlation with my twin is undiminished and I defect anyway.

So, three requests. First, state the principle, or a short list of candidates, precisely enough that each clause is motivated by a specific case above. Second, apply it to at least two cases I have not listed: a counterpart who will be told my move in advance and instructed to copy it (where the correlation is manufactured rather than natural), and a counterpart who is a duplicate of me created last week with my memories (where the correlation is natural but recent). Third, tell me plainly which of my seven verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and which should go: I would rather overturn a verdict I can be shown to be wrong about than patch the principle with epicycles.
