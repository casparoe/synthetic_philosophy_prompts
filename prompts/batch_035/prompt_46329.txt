I've been reading up on how we actually decide someone is dead, and my gut verdicts don't obviously add up to one principle. Trying to figure out if they do. Here's the set:

Someone with total irreversible loss of all brain function, brainstem included, kept on a ventilator so their heart keeps beating and they stay warm and pink: dead. That one feels rock solid to me.

A patient in a permanent vegetative state, cortex destroyed but brainstem chugging along, breathing on their own, sleeping and waking: not dead, even though the person everyone knew is gone. Also feels solid, though I notice some philosophers would say this one IS death if they go for a consciousness-based definition.

A guy whose heart stops, no resuscitation attempted, doctor waits, pronounces him dead, and then 3 minutes later he gasps and sits up on his own (this actually happens, it's called the Lazarus phenomenon): never died. It's not that he died and came back, it's that the doctor was wrong.

But an organ donor whose heart stops, the team waits two to five minutes for it to be sure it won't restart on its own, then declares death and takes the organs (donation after circulatory death): dead at that point, and I'm fine with that, it seems right.

A hypothermic person pulled from an icy lake, no pulse, no brain activity visible on the monitor, basically frozen, but they rewarmed slowly and came back: never dead either.

A brain-deatd patient where it turns out the posterior pituitary is still secreting antidiuretic hormone, so not literally every cell of the brain is off: still dead, I'm not going to flip on that one over the hypothalamus.

And an anencephalic baby born without most of the brain but with a working brainstem: not dead.

So my picture is something like death is the irreversible loss of all brain function, with irreversibility doing the work in the hypothermia case (loss is real but reversible, so not death). But then I hit the pair I can't square: the Lazarus guy and the DCD donor. If autoresuscitation can happen within a few minutes of circulatory arrest, and we say the guy who sits up at minute three never died, in what sense was the DCD donor, declared dead at minute two, actually dead? And the hypothermia case makes it worse, since with cold bodies people have come back after much longer than that.

Is there a principle that saves all of these, or close? Maybe something about what would happen with versus without intervention? And I'd want to know what it says about a case I haven't made up my mind on: someone in deep hypothermic cardiac arrest put on ECMO, where the machine is circulating and oxygenating their blood while they're rewarmed — during that window, are they dead, alive, or somehow indeterminate? Also happy to hear which of my verdicts you'd throw out if the clean principle requires it.
