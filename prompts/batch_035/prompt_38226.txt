I'm writing an essay on Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation, on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, and I want to pair it with Woody Allen's Midnight inParis as a test case. Read the film through Nietzsche's tripartite scheme: monumental history, which summons the great deeds of the past as models and encouragement for action; antiquarian history, which reverently preserves and honors origins and local inheritance; and critical history, which drags the past before a tribunal, condemns it, and suffers to break from it. The premise seems made for this. Gil, a Hollywood screenwriter nostalgic for the Paris of the 1920s, is whisked there at midnight, and when he and Adriana go a step further back to the Belle Époque, they find Toulouse-Lautrec and Gauguin scorning their own era and pining for the Renaissance — which looks like a reductio of antiquarian reverence as such, and Gil's realization that every generation's golden age is someone else's suffocating present seems to be the film's Nietzschean moral. But I want a reading that is faithful in both directions, and I have doubts at three points. First, does the film ever distinguish antiquarian from monumental history, or does it collapse all reverence for the past into the pathology it diagnoses, in a way Nietzsche — who thought a measured dose of antiquarian piety and monumental inspiration was indispensable to life — would reject as flat? Second, what exactly is Gil's final choice to remain in his own time and walk off in the rain: critical history, which involves condemnation of the past and the pain of severance, or merely the unhistorical power of forgetting, or even the suprahistorical stance Nietzsche attributes to those who regard all ages as fundamentally alike and stop caring about the process? Third, where does the film resist the reading — it is, after all, itself a sumptuous, museum-like tour of a Paris lovingly curated for the viewer's consumption, and its caricature of learnedness, Paul the pedantic academic, trades on an equation of erudition with deadening history that Nietzsche's own complaint (against the overeducated, history-saturated student) both invites and complicates. Give me the strongest version of the reading, an honest account of where it strains, and a judgment on what the film adds to the essay — for instance, whether the Belle Époque sequence dramatizes something Nietzsche only asserts: that antiquarianism is structural rather than a personal failing, each era metabolizing the last into its own nostalgia.
