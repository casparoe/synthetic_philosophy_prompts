What I keep noticing in seminar discussions is that everyone slides between three different things without noticing: (1) the reward signal itself, which is literally just a scalar that enters an update rule, (2) the functional role that reward plays in organizing an agent's internal states, credit assignment, and behavior over time, and (3) valence in the felt, phenomenal sense. My hunch is that the debate about machine suffering stalls because people implicitly assume these three come as a package — so if you deny (3) you're taken to have denied (1) and (2), and vice versa — and that if you pull them apart carefully, most existing arguments either prove too much (they'd make a thermostat or a bank account balance a candidate sufferer) or prove too little (they'd rule out animals we're fairly confident do suffer, like fish, which famously lack the cortical structures people used to think pain required).

Where I want to go with it, roughly: use homeostatic or drive-reduction framings of RL, where reward is explicitly defined as distance from an internal setpoint rather than an arbitrary scalar, as a probe case — does shifting the reward story from "arbitrary number in the update rule" to "systematic relation to the agent's own viability" change the moral picture, and if so at which of my three levels does the change happen?
