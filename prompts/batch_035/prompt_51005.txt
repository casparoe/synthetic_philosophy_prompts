Please compare David Lewis's counterpart theory with the traditional doctrine of transworld identity as accounts of de re modality. Rather than rehearsing the standard textbook contrasts, focus your comparison on the following three questions.

First, how does each view handle de re modal claims about ordinary artefacts whose persistence conditions are vague or convention-dependent — for instance, a wooden ship whose planks are replaced one at a time until none of the originals remain? In a possible world where a slightly different ship exists, there may be several equally good candidates for being transworld-identical with our ship, and equally several equally similar counterparts of it. Does the multiplicity of counterpart relations cause a deeper problem for Lewis than the multiplicity of candidate identity relations causes for the transworld identity theorist, or do the two views face structurally similar difficulties here?

Second, Kripke famously objected that on Lewis's view it is not really Humphrey who might have won the election; rather, someone else — someone merely resembling Humphrey — wins, which he took to be the wrong way to capture what we mean when we make such claims about a person. Assess this objection: does it expose a genuine defect in counterpart theory, or can the counterpart theorist preserve everything that matters in de re modal discourse by insisting that the resemblance relation plays the semantic role that identity plays for the rival view?

Third, consider whether the two positions are substantive metaphysical rivals at all. A defender of transworld identity might argue that counterpart theory is merely a notational variant: a different but intertranslatable regimentation of the same modal facts. Meanwhile, the counterpart theorist might reply that the rival view is committed to a mysterious relation of cross-world identity that has no non-question-begging criterion. Weigh these arguments against each other.

In your comparison, reach a reasoned verdict as to which view better captures our modal thought, and say explicitly what would have to be true for the opposite verdict to be correct.
