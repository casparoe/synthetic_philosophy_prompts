Question (20 points): Suppose you divide your credence between two moral theories -- say 60% utilitarianism and 40% some Kantian view. (a) Explain how decision-making under this kind of "moral uncertainty" differs from ordinary empirical uncertainty. (b) Explain why the simple rule "act as if the theory you find most probable is true" is unsatisfactory. (c) Present the "maximize expected choiceworthiness" (MEC) proposal and explain the main difficulty it faces.

And here is the student's answer, copied out:

"Moral uncertainty and empirical uncertainty are alike in that the agent acts without full information, but the source differs. Empirical uncertainty is about contingent facts -- what will actually happen if I do X -- and in principle more evidence can shrink it. Moral uncertainty is uncertainty about which moral theory is true. Since observation doesn't settle which theory is true, evidence in the ordinary sense can't reduce it, though arguments can shift your credences, so you can still have probabilities over theories, the way you have probabilities over scientific hypotheses.

The 'act as if your most probable theory is true' rule fails for two reasons. First, asymmetric stakes. If option A is slightly better than B on utilitarianism, but B is much better than A on the Kantian view, then with 60/40 credence the rule tells you to pick A even though most of the weight of what matters favors B. Second, it ignores dominance: if one option is at least as good as the others on every theory you have any credence in, and strictly better on one of them, you should choose it whatever your probabilities are, and the rule can go the other way.

MEC handles both problems. Treat the theories like states of the world in ordinary decision theory: for each act, work out how choiceworthy it is according to each theory, weight by your credences, and pick the act with the highest probability-weighted choiceworthiness. Asymmetric stakes get captured by the weighting, and dominance falls out automatically.

The main difficulty for MEC is that different theories measure choiceworthiness in different units -- hedons versus whatever the Kantian equivalent would be -- so you need a common scale to do the weighting at all. But I don't think this is a special problem for MEC. Ordinary decision theory already compares utilities across different persons whenever a policy helps one group at another's expense, and nobody concludes from that that expected utility theory is inapplicable. So the comparisons are available and MEC goes through. One further point: MEC has a nice conservative feature -- if a theory you have any nonzero credence in forbids an act, MEC won't recommend it, so the morally uncertain agent is pushed toward acts that are permitted by all the theories she takes seriously."
