Exam question: The conceivability–possibility thesis says, roughly, that what is conceivable is thereby possible, at least as a defeasible guide. Explain the thesis and the most serious threat to it, using Kripke's case of water and H2O as your illustration. Then explain the best available reply to that threat on behalf of the thesis, and assess whether the reply succeeds. (Suggested length: one to two paragraphs.)

Student's answer: The conceivability–possibility thesis holds that if we can coherently conceive of something, then it is possible. Descartes was the first to state it carefully, and he distinguished between positive conceivability, which he characterized as the ability to form a clear and distinct mental image of a scenario, and negative conceivability, which is the absence of any contradiction in the scenario. The clearest threat to the thesis comes from Kripke's a posteriori necessities. Before chemistry established that water is H2O, it seemed perfectly conceivable that water was some other substance — a simpler liquid, or a compound with a different formula. If conceivability guaranteed possibility, then it should have been possible that water was not H2O. But since identities, once true, are necessary, there is no possible world in which water is not H2O. So the thesis appears refuted. The defender of the thesis can reply that what was really conceivable before the chemical discovery was only something weaker: that some clear, drinkable, ocean-filling liquid was not H2O. That remains possible, and is indeed actual in worlds where a different liquid plays the water role. No world contains water that is not H2O, but many worlds contain watery stuff that is not H2O, and the pre-chemists were conceiving only of the latter. On this reply, the thesis survives, because the appearance of conceiving water-without-H2O was an appearance of merely epistemic possibility, not genuine metaphysical possibility. It is worth noting, though, that since all of Kripke's necessities are a posteriori, and a posteriori truths cannot be established by conceiving, conceivability can at most ever reveal a priori possibility, never metaphysical possibility — so the thesis, even if not refuted, is much weaker than it first appeared. This still leaves the thesis with important applications: for example, if zombies are conceivable, then consciousness is not identical to any physical state, since whatever is conceivable is at least epistemically possible, and whatever is epistemically possible is metaphysically possible unless ruled out by an a posteriori necessity, and no a posteriori necessity could rule out a hypothesis about the absence of consciousness. So the thesis survives in a restricted form.
