Quick bit of background: I'm a retired marine microbiologist who spent three decades mostly worrying about whether the methane was coming from the sediment or the water column, and since retiring I've fallen hard into philosophy — mostly aesthetics, which surprised me too. I help run a monthly philosophy circle at our local U3A, about ten retirees, sharp people, no formal philosophy at all, ninety minutes around a table with coffee. Next month it's my turn to lead, and the topic I picked is aesthetic testimony — the question of whether you can really come to know aesthetic stuff secondhand, the way your friend telling you the soup is salty hands you knowledge straight off. Don't explain the debate to me; I've read enough at this point to hold my own. What I need from you is a plan for the session.

First, the ordering. There are several moving parts here — the difference between aesthetic and non-aesthetic properties, the fact that ordinary testimony works so smoothly we never notice it, the intuition that aesthetic stuff somehow has to be experienced firsthand, and the business about aesthetic judgment maybe being an ability you acquire rather than a fact you're told — and I keep going back and forth on which one has to go in front of which. Which dependencies actually matter for a group like mine, and where does the sequence break if I get it wrong?

Second, the carrying example. I'd like one concrete case to build the whole ninety minutes around. My instinct is something like a friend raving about a dish at a restaurant you haven't tasted — but I want you to think about what the example needs to have and what it needs to lack. Everyone in the literature trots out Hume's wine bottle with the key tied to a leathern thong at the bottom, and I feel like it's gotten stale; if you think a version of it is actually the right vehicle anyway, tell me why. Something with an expert's verdict would let me rope in critics and sommeliers, but I worry it drags in a bunch of issues about expertise that aren't the point.

Third, the misconceptions. I can already predict two: half the table will say it's obviously impossible because beauty is subjective, full stop, and the other half will say it's obviously possible because people follow movie reviews all the time, look, it works. I want to know exactly where in the sequence each of those should be headed off rather than left to fester until the last twenty minutes. But I'd also bet there are misconceptions I'm not predicting — one thing I've learned from teaching grad students is that the misunderstanding you don't anticipate is the one that eats the session. Tell me what mine will be and where to catch them.

Fourth, a check. Some question I can throw out near the end that would tell me whether they've actually grasped the question — as opposed to just having confirmed whatever they walked in thinking, or having decided the whole topic is a bunch of fuss over wine snobs.

And fifth, what to leave out. I know there's this whole adjacent war about testimony in general, reductionism versus anti-reductionism, Hume and Reid and Coady and the rest, and my instinct is to not even gesture at it — but tell me whether any scrap of it is load-bearing for my session, and say explicitly what I should announce I'm refusing to cover so nobody feels cheated.

One more constraint: this is a chatty group, they'll tolerate a bit of abstraction but not much. If a piece of the plan only works with a whiteboard and handouts, say so and I'll come prepared.
