I'm a lay member on a hospital research ethics committee (the kind where a few of us aren't doctors or scientists, we're there to represent the community/patient side). We meet monthly and I probably see six to ten consent packages per meeting, with maybe 20-30 min per one before we have to vote, and the stakes are real but not dramatic — most of these are low-risk studies, the occasional chemo trial, surveys, chart reviews, biobanking. The doctors on the committee know the regulations cold. What nobody has is any actual account of what makes consent VALID, as opposed to merely obtained and documented. So every month we have the same three fights:

1. someone says a payment is "coercive" and someone else says no, coercion is a threat, a payment is just an offer, and we never resolve it
2. a study withholds some info on purpose (to keep a placebo effect clean, or to not cue participants' answers) and half the room says that's fine as long as we debrief after, half says it's deception, whatever we call it
3. a surgeon wants to recruit her own patients for her own study and insists the relationship makes consent easier, and the rest of us suspect it makes it worse but can't say why in words

What I want from you is a decision procedure I can actually run in that 20-30 minutes, per application, grounded in whatever the philosophy of consent actually says — the real distinctions people have worked out, like the difference between being misled and just not being told everything, between a coercive threat and an attractive offer, between having the capacity to consent and actually understanding what you agreed to — but translated so a committee member with no philosophy background could follow it. Questions in order, what a sufficient answer to each looks like, where the procedure terminates in a no, and where it stops and hands the decision back to the room because the question is genuinely a values call that no procedure can settle. Be upfront about those handoff points rather than hiding them in the middle of some step.

Also include: some way to handle the cases where consent isn't given by the participant at all — a stroke trial where patients are enrolled unconscious under emergency rules with consent "deferred" to family later, and a dementia study where a legally authorized representative signs for someone who is partly there, partly not. I don't need the procedure to solve those, I need it to tell me honestly whether they're consent problems or something else wearing consent's clothes.

Then run the procedure on three applications so I can see it work:

(a) a placebo-controlled trial of a new topical pain gel that intentionally doesn't tell participants there's a chance they'll recieve gel with no active ingredient, with a full debrief after
(b) an interview study about money stress and medication skipping, paying participants the equivalent of two days' wages, recruiting through a food bank where the interviewer is a familiar face
(c) the surgeon recruiting her own patients, mentioned above, for a study that's basically safe but involves her testing a follow-up protocol she herself designed

For at least one of these the procedure should land somewhere I won't like (I'm fine with it being one of the ones I'd personally wave through), and I want you to say which one and why the procedure gives that answer anyway.

And end with what the procedure can't do — where a committee following it faithfully will still wave through something that's wrong, or block something that's fine. Don't sugarcoat it. If the honest answer is that a 30-minute procedure mostly just standardizes which things we argue about rather than reducing the arguing, I'd rather know that going in.
