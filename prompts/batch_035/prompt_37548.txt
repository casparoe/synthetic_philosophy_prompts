A dispute from my philosophy of science seminar has hardened into an argument I can no longer see through, and I would like to know exactly where it fails, if it does. The argument runs as follows.

(1) Take any theory T that we hold on the strength of the evidence: our account of electrons, of continental drift, of how a vaccine works. For any such T I can construct a rival T* that is empirically equivalent to it — T* agrees with T about every observation that has been made and every observation that could ever be made, while disagreeing about the underlying structure of the world. T* might say that electrons are as T describes them except that a field has been masking their real nature all along, or that T is right about all observables but wrong about the unobservables in a way that happens to leave every observable trace untouched, or simply that the regularities T records will change their behavior tomorrow. These rivals are gerrymandered, but nothing in logic rules them out.

(2) If two hypotheses are empirically equivalent — if they agree on every possible observation — then no possible evidence discriminates between them; whatever the world shows us, it confirms or disconfirms both to exactly the same degree.

(3) So for every theory T we believe, there is a rival that our total evidence supports exactly as well.

(4) Rational belief in T requires that one's evidence favor T over the alternatives to it; if the evidence leaves T in a dead tie with a rival that denies what T says, then believing T rather than the rival is not rational.

(5) So we are never rational in believing any theory over its rivals: not about electrons, not about the distant past, not about tomorrow's sunrise, since for each of these there is a constructed alternative supported by all the same evidence.

(6) And the argument generalizes past science. My memories are evidence for there having been a past, but there is a rival on which the world came into being five minutes ago, complete with every trace and every memory, and that rival fits all the evidence I will ever have just as well. So I am not rational in believing there was a past.

(7) Finally, if all of that is right, then I am not rational in believing the premises of this argument either, since they too have rivals that no possible evidence can exclude.

My TA says this proves too much and that the rogue step is in there somewhere, but he would not say where. My own suspicion is that (4) is the weak premise — that rational belief does not require beating every constructible rival, only the rivals worth taking seriously. But when I tried to say that, he asked what makes a rival worth taking seriously, and the only answers I could give were either circular (a rival is serious if the evidence does not rule it out, which is what I was trying to establish) or dogmatic (we just know that drift is serious and the masking field is not), and I found I had no reply. Meanwhile a friend in the seminar took the opposite route: she keeps (1) through (5) but bites the bullet only for unobservable posits — she believes that a theory fits all possible observations while suspending judgment on whether the unobservable structure it describes is real, and she claims this costs her almost nothing. I cannot tell whether she has found a cheap exit or has simply stopped believing science.

So tell me where the argument goes wrong, if it does. If you think there is a slip between the premises, name the step and explain why it looked fine and why it fails only on a reading the rest of the argument does not support. If you think the argument is valid, tell me which premise is least costly to give up and what the cost is, and whether my friend's exit is genuinely cheaper than rejecting the lot. I would like to keep (1), because I do think underdetermination is a real feature of scientific reasoning and not just a philosopher's toy, but I would rather be told the rot starts there than keep it by hiding a mistake in (4). Please write your answer as continuous prose, in full sentences and paragraphs, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way one would write a long, careful letter.
