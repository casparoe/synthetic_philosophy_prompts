I am preparing a handout for an interdisciplinary graduate seminar that mixes philosophy students and economics students, on the question of how the welfare of future people should be weighed against welfare now. Please write a decision tree rather than an essay.

The tree starts from a single commitment everyone in the room accepts — that the welfare of people who will exist later counts for something — and each internal node should be a question a student could answer without knowing which position it leads toward. The first few nodes should do the sorting I most care about, and that students most often blur together: whether the temporal position of a benefit is itself a reason to discount it, as opposed to discounting that flows from the benefit being less likely to arrive (extinction risk, institutional decay) or less needed given expected growth; whether a discount rate appropriate for a private investor's portfolio has any business in an intergenerational social welfare function; and whether the far future differs from the near future in degree or in kind.

Each leaf should carry the name of the position — or an honest 'unnamed' — together with one sentence on its most awkward commitment. At minimum the tree must locate: the Parfit-style view that temporal distance is morally on a par with spatial distance; the exponential discounter with a positive rate of pure time preference; the discounter who refuses pure time preference but discounts for uncertainty about whether the future's beneficiaries will exist at all; the instrumentalist who sets the effective social rate by opportunity costs in capital markets; and whatever leaf deserves the label 'presentist' — the view that only people who exist now generate claims in the first place.

Two further requirements. First, build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of famous names: set out the branch questions, work out which combinations of answers they make available, then say where the actual positions fall, and note any unoccupied combination with a sentence on whether nobody holds it because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because it is unexplored. Second, flag honestly any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate — I know, for example, that questions about uncertainty and about growth may not distinguish someone who discounts for the falling marginal utility of a richer future from someone who discounts the wellbeing of the future people themselves, and I would rather students see the tree's blind spots than trust it too much. Keep the whole thing to something that fits on two sides of a page.
