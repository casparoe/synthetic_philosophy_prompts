For the past year my monthly column, which covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, has been circling the far future: climate trajectories, the demographic transition, and lately the arguments longtermists make about how many people there should be. The final column of the year takes up population ethics, and I would like to close it not with another argument but with a villanelle on the repugnant conclusion — the result, first named by Derek Parfit, that on total views of welfare a world containing an enormous number of people whose lives are only barely worth living can rank as better, strictly by the numbers, than a world with fewer people whose lives are very good. I want the form to do philosophical work rather than decorate the theme. The two refrains should carry the two horns of the problem: one refrain voiced by the totalist's arithmetic, that adding lives worth living, however thin, always adds to the sum; the other voiced by the intuition that something has gone badly wrong in the world of Very Many. A reader who knows the literature should be able to tell which refrain is which, and a reader who doesn't should still come away with a good poem about lives and arithmetic. The middle stanzas should stage the attempts at escape — the person-affecting restriction, lexical thresholds, averaging — with the villanelle's built-in property that the refrains return no matter what the middle stanzas try, mirroring the way each proposed escape gets dragged back by a variant of the same argument. And the closing couplet, where the two refrains are finally forced into the same breath, should convey two convictions that refuse to reconcile, not a tidy resolution; my column should not pretend the field has one.

Since my readers include working philosophers and I don't want the column to smuggle in a verdict, please append a short note in plain prose flagging where the poem leans on contested ground: which escapes you chose to gesture at in the middle stanzas and whether any of them are still considered live options; how you read barely worth living, given the standard distinction between a life barely worth living and one barely above the point of no longer mattering to anyone whether it continues; and, above all, that it remains genuinely disputed whether the repugnant conclusion refutes total utilitarianism, should simply be accepted, or is an artifact of how the question is framed. In that note, please mark clearly what counts as settled among people who work on population axiology and what remains open — in particular, do not present any one escape route as the accepted solution, because as far as I can tell there isn't one.
