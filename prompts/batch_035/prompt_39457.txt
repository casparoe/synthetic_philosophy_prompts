In the midterm for my upper-level seminar on philosophy of mind, I posed the following question:

"Clark and Chalmers argue that cognition can extend beyond the brain into the environment. In their 1998 paper 'The Extended Mind,' they write that a part of the world counts as part of a cognitive process when it functions in a way that would be counted as cognitive if it happened inside the head. What does this criterion amount to? How does the argument for it proceed — in particular, what role do parity considerations and the Otto/Inga thought experiment play? And how does their 'active externalism' differ from the content externalism associated with Putnam and Burge?"

One student's answer reads as follows. Please evaluate it.

"The extended mind thesis says that the mind is not just in the brain but includes the tools we think with. Clark and Chalmers's criterion is that whenever some external resource does something that, done in the head, would count as cognition, that resource is genuinely part of the cognitive process. So the mind extends into the environment whenever external elements drive cognition, and they put it bluntly: cognitive processes ain't (all) in the head. Their argument runs through a thought experiment involving Otto, who suffers from Alzheimer's, and his wife Inga. Otto carries a notebook everywhere; when he needs to remember something, he looks it up, whereas Inga just consults her biological memory. Clark and Chalmers say the notebook entries are Otto's beliefs, because in ordinary circumstances Otto trusts the notebook's accuracy more than his own memory. The notebook is therefore literally part of Otto's mind. The right way to read this is on analogy with Putnam and Burge's externalism about content: their point was that meaning just ain't in the head, and Clark and Chalmers extend this by showing that the vehicles of mental states, not just their contents, lie outside the organism. This distinguishes their view from classic externalism, which located mental states' meanings in distal causes. Their conclusion is that self and mind extend together into the world, and that anything reliably coupled to the brain — a notebook, a smartphone, a calculator — is literally part of someone's mind."
