I keep reading one-line dismissals of the surprise exam paradox, and before I accept any of them I want to see what a resolution is actually supposed to deliver. Please write me the desiderata: the requirements any adequate solution to the surprise exam paradox must meet, with the motivating case behind each. Treat the paradox in its standard form — an announcement that an exam will occur on exactly one of the next n days, and that on the morning of the exam the students will not know (or be able to deduce) that it is that day — plus the backward-induction argument purporting to show the announcement cannot be fulfilled. The requirements I have in mind include at least these, but add, prune or reweight as you see fit, and tell me why each is a genuine desideratum rather than a prejudice of one camp:

(1) It must locate a definite failure in the induction — say precisely which step is invalid and why, rather than gesturing at the argument's "circularity" or self-reference.
(2) It must explain the asymmetry between the small cases and the large ones: for n = 1 almost everyone agrees the announcement is straightforwardly self-defeating, yet for n = 50 the teacher can plainly hold the exam on Tuesday and genuinely surprise the students; a solution that collapses this asymmetry has explained something away rather than explained it.
(3) It must tell us what, if anything, is wrong with the student's position on the penultimate evening — in particular whether "the exam must be tomorrow, but then I would know it was tomorrow, so it cannot be tomorrow" is a Moore-paradoxical belief, a rational blindspot, or something else again, and what the difference amounts to.
(4) It must handle perturbations of the set-up without ad hoc repairs: the iterated announcement (the teacher re-announces the exam each evening for the remaining days); the vague announcement ("sometime this term"); and the probabilistic variant in which the teacher picks the day uniformly at random and surprise is redefined so that the students' credence, on waking, that the exam is today never exceeds one half — where the backward induction appears to go through just as before, yet the teacher can evidently comply with high probability.
(5) It must not rely on principles with independent costs — e.g. unrestricted knowing-that-one-knows — whose abandonment elsewhere in epistemology would be too expensive; and it should say whether its diagnosis generalises to the kindest cousins of the paradox (lottery reasoning, hangman) or whether the solution, to be adequate, must instead explain why it does not.

Mark each requirement as non-negotiable or camp-relative, and flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — if the list itself is unstable, I want to see that, since it would be a result in its own right. Then, briefly and without ceremony, score the main families of solutions (the dissolve-the-announcement line descending from Quine; the epistemic-blindspot treatment; the revision-of-what-the-student-knows-at-each-stage line; and any others you take seriously) against the list. The criteria are the part I care about: keep the weight there, not on the scorecard.
