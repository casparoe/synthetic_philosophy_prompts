I'm taking an epistemology course this semester and we just got to the unit on peer disagreement, which I was really looking forward to. Before the lectures I listened to a podcast episode on higher-order evidence to get oriented, and honestly it made decent sense to me, but now that we're in the weeds in class I'm second-guessing some of what it said. The host's summary went roughly like this: "Epistemic akrasia is when you believe something you know you shouldn't believe — it's literally the epistemic version of weakness of will, and like weakness of will it's always irrational, and philosophers are basically in agreement on that. The clearest case is peer disagreement. If someone just as smart and informed as you disagrees with you, then you know one of you is wrong and you have no way to tell which, so you're rationally required to suspend judgment. Anyone who digs in and keeps their belief after hearing about the disagreement is guilty of akrasia. That's what higher-order evidence is: information about your own evidence and reasoning, and it always trumps first-order evidence, the same way you should always trust the meta-level over the base level."

At the time I nodded along, and parts of it still seem right to me, like the weakness of will analogy. But in class the professor mentioned that some philosophers actually defend akratic belief as rational in certain cases, which sounds like it contradicts the "philosophers are basically in agreement" part, and nobody in the class could reconstruct exactly why. Also the more I think about it, the more it seems like the podcast glided from "you should take the disagreement seriously" to "you must suspend judgment" pretty fast, and I'm not sure those are the same thing. And I have a nagging feeling the claim that higher-order evidence always trumps first-order evidence is too strong, though I can't put my finger on why — maybe something about the disagreement itself not telling you which of you is the mistaken one?

So I guess my question is: how fair is this summary? Can you go through it piece by piece and tell me which parts a mainstream epistemologist would accept, which parts are simplifications, and which are just conflations or overstatements? In particular I'd like to know (a) whether believing something after finding out about peer disagreement really amounts to akrasia as opposed to something else, and (b) what the actual pro-akrasia arguments look like, the ones my professor was hinting at. Also, if the summary is wrong somewhere, does the conclusion that you're "rationally required to suspend judgment" survive once you fix it, or was that conclusion resting on the error all along?
