Give me your credence, with full decomposition, that the strategy-stealing assumption will hold over the coming fifteen years — that is, that at some point within that window, AI systems will be able to do everything humans currently do to advance AI, at least a few times faster, assuming no deliberate restrictions on AI development. Two readings of 'everything humans currently do' must be kept apart, so give a separate number under each: the strict reading, on which it includes the physical and institutional work (building datacenters, procuring chips, arranging power and financing), and the loose reading, on which it covers only the cognitive work of research, engineering, and coordination. Then decompose, with a rough probability and a one-line justification for each term: (a) the probability that the remaining gaps in current models — long-horizon reliability, research taste, tacit know-how — will be closed by further scaling and training rather than being architectural limits; (b) the probability that, even given the cognitive work is done by AIs, the pace is set by bottlenecks with timelines of their own (chip fabrication, energy buildout, permitting), so that 'a few times faster' fails regardless of what the models are capable of; (c) any further term you think belongs in the decomposition. For calibration: a colleague of mine puts this at 0.8 on the grounds that compute is the only binding constraint, while I sit nearer 0.5. Close by telling me where our disagreement most likely lives, which term you are least confident in, and what evidence arriving within the next five years would move your number the most.
