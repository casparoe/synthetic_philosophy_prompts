I'm a novelist working on a secondary-world epic where one of the POV characters is a court historian, and I've realized I need to know what theory of history my world actually runs on before I write her chapters — but every time I try to pick between cyclical and linear history I just end up restating my own gut feelings. So here's what I want: a "which view of history do you actually hold?" quiz, built as a decision tree, that I can also give to my beta readers to see what assumptions they bring to the book (a lot of them read fantasy expecting empires to rise and fall forever, and I'm not sure my world works that way).

The tree should start from things a reader can answer about concrete cases — a generation of peace followed by a generation of ruin, a conqueror's dynasty going soft by the third generation, a famine that forces a whole people to reorganize themselves, a technological breakthrough that seems to change the rules — and each branch node should be a question someone can answer without knowing where it leads. Branches should be labeled by the claim that splits them: does history have a direction at all, is decline inevitable or can it be answered, does the cycle (if there is one) come from inside the society — factional drift, cohesion decaying, elites getting lazy — or from outside shocks, does the death of one civilization mean anything beyond the next one taking its place. The leaves should be the named positions: Polybius' rotation of constitutions, Ibn Khaldun's group-solidarity cycle with its rough four-generations span, Spengler's civilizational lifespan where a culture is basically an organism with a fatal destiny, Toynbee's challenge-and-response where breakdowns are failed answers rather than scheduled deaths, the linear-providence or progress views that treat the cycle-talk as an illusion, and Vico's return-if-not-repetition if he fits somewhere. Also mark which combinations of branch answers land on no named position, and say whether nobody's there because the view is incoherent or just because nobody got around to defending it. If two of the big names can't really be seperated by any question a normal reader could answer, say so instead of inventing a fake distinction.

One more thing: I'd like the diagnostic questions phrased as things that could happen in my world as easily as in ours — that's deliberate, since I want to reuse the tree as a character sheet for my fictional historians too. So no questions that only make sense if you already know 20th century Europe happened. Ten-ish questions, each with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto leaves, and a short note on which leaf is most likely to be an artifact of question phrasing. Start directly with the first question — no intro, no explanation of what you're about to do, that can come after the scoring key if you really think I need it.
