I'm taking an intermediate game theory course, and this week we moved into epistemic game theory — common knowledge, the Aumann agreeing-to-disagree stuff, and then the centipede game. In section we used a small version that I'll just reproduce here so we're all on the same page: two players alternate moves, and at each move the current mover can either Take (ending the game right there, with the mover getting the bigger pile) or Pass. The piles grow over time like this: if player 1 takes at her first move, the payoffs are ($2, $1); if she passes and player 2 takes, ($1, $4); if he passes and player 1 takes at her second move, ($4, $3); if she passes and player 2 takes at his second move, ($3, $6); and if he passes too, the game just ends with ($6, $5) — nobody gets to move after that.

Backward induction then unwinds the whole thing: at his last move player 2 compares taking ($6 for him) with passing ($5), so he takes; knowing that, player 1 takes at her second move rather than let him get $6; knowing that, player 2 takes at his first move; knowing that, player 1 takes the $2 at move one. Fine, it's mechanical. But at office hours a group of us got into an argument our TA couldn't settle. My friend thinks common knowledge of rationality makes the move-one take airtight — everyone knows everyone is rational, everyone knows that everyone knows, and so on, so the induction is just logic, and passing is definately a mistake, period. I kept pushing back that the argument kind of eats itself: for player 1's reasoning to work, she has to assume player 2 would choose rationally at a node that, by the very reasoning being used, is never reached, and it's not clear what "player 2 is rational at that node" even means once reaching that node would already contradict the common knowledge assumption. If node 3 is somehow reached, something in the premises has already broken, so what exactly grounds player 1's beliefs about player 2 there?

Write a dialogue between the two of us — call her Maya (team common-knowledge: the induction is basically a theorem, deal with it) and me, Dan (team the argument is self-undermining at unreached nodes). Make us both genuinely good at this: Maya should have real replies, not just "it's math, look it up," like maybe distinguishing what's assumed along the actual path from what's assumed counterfactually, or pointing out that working game theorists use backward induction constantly without anything collapsing. And Dan should have to face the hard parts of his own view — like whether he can say anything determinate about what player 1 should expect at all if he refuses counterfactual rationality attributions, and whether forward-induction style reasoning (reading things into a surprising earlier move) could ever justify passing here. Have us actually respond to each other's points rather than trading monologues, and let it end somewhere honest, even if unresolved.
