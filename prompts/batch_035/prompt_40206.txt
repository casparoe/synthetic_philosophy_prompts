I teach a mixed graduate seminar that leans heavily on history of science and history of ethics, and over the last few years I've watched a certain reflex spread among my history-minded colleagues and students, which I would like a name for if one exists. Everyone now knows the old sin: writing the past as a steady march toward our present opinions, treating earlier thinkers as failed approximations of us. But the correction has overshot in a distinctive way, and I keep running into it. Last spring I said, in passing, that Priestley had been simply wrong about combustion, and a student objected that this "imposes our standards on his conceptual world" -- as though "wrong about combustion" were an invidious comparison rather than an ordinary evaluation. A colleague reviewing a dissertation insisted that the chapter describe Aristotelian mechanics not as an early and inaccurate account of motion but as a "coherent framework in its own terms," and when I asked whether it was nonetheless less accurate, she said the question was "not a historical question." In another case a student defended a reading of a nineteenth-century moral theorist on the ground that it would be "anachronistic" to notice the theorist's argument was invalid. In each case the move was the same: some legitimate methodological point about understanding past thinkers on their own terms gets quietly converted into a refusal to make any evaluative claims about them at all.

What intrigues me is that this is not the relativism it superficially resembles, because the people doing it are perfectly comfortable asserting that our current theories are true, that Lavoisier really did have the better of it, that some contemporary moral arguments are sound and others not. The refusal runs in one direction only: toward the past. It is also not the ordinary methodological caution about anachronism, which, done properly, governs what you must attend to in explaining a past view -- the questions it was answering, the standards internal to its time -- without saying anything about whether the view was correct. The people I have in mind have converted a rule about explanation into a rule about evaluation, and have done so selectively. Nor is it exactly the old sin in reverse: writing history as progress toward the present at least lets you say the past was worse; this reflex protects the past from comparative judgment altogether, and I cannot tell whether that is a substantive epistemology (past conceptual schemes are somehow incommensurable with ours), a moral scruple (the dead cannot defend themselves), or just a nervous tic that has become fashionable.

Does this overcorrection have an established name in the historiography literature -- something that would be to the sin of Whig history what Whig history is to naive chronicle? I know the standard concepts in the neighborhood: the Whig interpretation itself, presentism, anachronism, and I am aware that the sociology-of-knowledge tradition said things about explaining true and false beliefs symmetrically, though I have never worked through whether that is the same thing or only looks like it. What I want is the carving, not the label: precisely what the phenomenon is, how it differs from each of the nearby concepts, and where its edges are -- in particular, whether there is a defensible version of the refusal (perhaps when past and present frameworks genuinely do not share enough machinery for comparative evaluation) that my student and my colleague are garbling, or whether every version collapses into the indefensible asymmetry I have described. If nothing established fits, please say so, coin a term for the asymmetrical refusal itself, and give me the boundary cases: views we can now safely call wrong, views where the comparison genuinely breaks down, and views where the only remaining disagreement is about which of those two categories they fall into.
