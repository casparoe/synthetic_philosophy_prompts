Last night my oldest friend and I ended, or rather failed to end, a long-running argument about Berkeley. He is a game developer, and he believes he has idealism's number. On his telling, Berkeley's doctrine amounts to the claim that reality behaves like a modern video game: the world renders only what a perceiver perceives, and what nobody perceives is simply not drawn. From this he draws two conclusions in a single breath — first, that Berkeley was advancing a lazy-rendering hypothesis about the physical world, and second, that the hypothesis is refuted the moment you walk back into an empty room and find the furniture where you left it. When I objected that Berkeley has a standing answer to the empty-room point, namely that sensible things exist continuously because they are perceived by an infinite mind rather than by us, he waved this off as the server keeping the state — which struck me as conceding the point while appearing to win it. Late in the evening, at his weakest, he also reached for the old line that one cannot conceive an unconceived tree, since conceiving it is conceiving it; I accused him of confusing the act of thinking with its object, and the argument dissolved there. We parted unresolved, and I would like an audit of his analogy, because I suspect it is doing more work than either of us admitted.

Please provide the following.

1. A correspondence table with exactly these columns: (a) element of the video-game picture; (b) its putative counterpart in Berkeley; (c) whether the mapping holds; (d) where it breaks; (e) whether that break matters for the specific use my friend put the analogy to, namely refuting Berkeley via the empty room. The table should include at least these rows: unrendered objects; the rendering event; the game engine or server; the player; the saved, persistent game state; and the hardware on which the whole thing runs.

2. A verdict given as a JSON object with precisely these fields:
- "friend_conclusion_survives": one of "yes", "no", or "survives only after repair", followed by a one-sentence justification;
- "load_bearing_breaks": an ordered list, most important first, of the disanalogies that actually bear the weight of my friend's conclusion, each with a clause explaining why it is load-bearing rather than incidental;
- "smuggled_features": a list of features of the game domain that the analogy quietly imports into Berkeley — a material substrate, an implementation that could in principle be discovered from inside, the player's curious position of being both within the world and outside it, or anything else you identify;
- "honest_use": what, if anything, the analogy can legitimately be used to teach about Berkeley;
- "better_analogy": a replacement analogy, or the string "none available";
- "master_argument_note": a brief statement of whether the conceivability line he reached for belongs in this audit at all, or whether it is a separate argument that only looks like part of the same picture.

One consideration I want kept in view throughout: Berkeley appears to have intended esse est percipi as something like a thesis about what existence claims concerning sensible things amount to, not as an empirical hypothesis about rendering behind the scenes, and he denied ever needing objects to blink in and out of existence. If the analogy makes him look absurd only by attributing the blinking to him, that should be said plainly. Please be even-handed: if my friend's conclusion survives the audit, say so. I would rather lose the argument correctly than win it by the same trick he used.
