I teach an intro course that covers the economics of colonialism, and every year I do a unit on Niall Ferguson's Empire vs. Shashi Tharoor's Inglorious Empire, basically the strongest versions of "the Raj was on net a good deal for India" and "the Raj was a catastrophe, full stop." Ferguson puts railways, the telegraph, the English language, a common law system, and huge capital flows on the credit side; Tharoor counters with the drain of wealth, the wrecking of Indian textile industry, and the famines, plus his favorite stat about India's share of the world economy collapsing from something like a quarter to a few percent under British rule. And here's the thing: my students and I have now run this debate maybe four years running, and it never moves. The Ferguson side says the GDP share fell because the West industrialized, not because Britain looted India — the counterfactual India without the Raj might have done even worse. The Tharoor side says the counterfactual is a dodge, you don't get to charge famine victims for their railway tickets, and the "would India have industrialized anyway" question is unanswerable so it's doing all the work. Everyone talks past everyone else.

My working theory, which I'd like you to actually evaluate rather than just nod along to, is that this isn't really one disagreement at all but three stacked on top of each other: (1) a factual-ish dispute about counterfactual history that is probably in principle unresolvable, (2) an accounting dispute about what even goes on the balance sheet — do institutions and a lingua franca count as "benefits" in the same currency as millions of famine deaths, and who gets to decide the exchange rate, and (3) a deeper moral dispute about whether a balance-sheet framing is legitimate at all, since Tharoor at his most rhetorical isn't saying "the costs outweighed the benefits," he's saying the ledger itself is obscene. If I'm right that these are three different disagreements wearing one trenchcoat, then the participants keep thinking they're losing arguments when actually they're just playing different games, and no amount of new data about, say, rail freight rates or famine mortality is going to change anyone's mind.

So: diagnose this for me. Is my three-layer theory right, or is it really one substantive disagreement (maybe about the counterfactual, maybe about something else) that's just hard? Which of the three layers, if any, is verbal or ill-posed rather than substantive? And given your diagnosis, tell me honestly whether anything could count as progress here — a research finding, a conceptual distinction, a reframing — or whether this debate is structurally fated to be eternal, and I should just teach it as an example of an unresolvable argument instead of a live one. I'd rather not burn another semester on it if it's genuinely stuck.
