I'm a PhD student in political theory writing a dissertation on republican liberty, and for the past year I've also served as bylaws reviewer for the housing cooperative I live in — about 180 units, an elected seven-person board, and an amendment process in which every proposed change passes through me for a first read before it goes to a membership vote. Lately the board has been sending a steady stream of governance amendments, and I've noticed my written comments have degenerated into vibes: I call a rule "a bit heavy-handed" or "seems fine" without any systematic account of why. Since non-domination is supposed to be the organizing concept of my dissertation, I'd like to hold our bylaws to the standard my thesis defends rather than to my mood on a given Tuesday.

The distinction I want the procedure built around is the republican one between interference and domination: a rule can constrain a member without dominating them, provided it isn't arbitrary — provided it tracks interests the constrained members share, and provided those subject to it can contest its application and get decisions reviewed — whereas a rule vesting an official with open-ended discretion dominates the member even if the discretion is never once exercised, because the member lives at the official's mercy. The co-op setting supplies the natural materials for testing this: who proposed the rule, on what terms, what counts as compliance, and what recourse a member has after a decision is made.

Please give me a screening procedure I can run on any proposed amendment in about ten minutes, expressed as exactly five numbered steps, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. Make the order matter — a proposal should be able to fail at step two without my having to work through step four. Weave a brief application of each step into at least one of the two live proposals I'm reviewing this month: (a) the board may enter any unit on 48 hours' notice to inspect for documented maintenance concerns, with the inspection report appealable to the membership; and (b) the board may levy discretionary fines of up to one month's fees for conduct it deems "uncooperative," with no appeal specified. Make the fifth step the one that identifies where judgment cannot be eliminated, and be honest about which kinds of proposals the procedure will wave through despite the fact that a republican theorist ought to be suspicious of them.
