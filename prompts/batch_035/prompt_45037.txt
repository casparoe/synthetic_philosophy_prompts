I'd like a probability estimate, with the reasoning decomposed, on a question in Sidgwick scholarship.

Henry Sidgwick (1838–1900) is remembered as the most careful utilitarian theorist of the nineteenth century, the author of The Methods of Ethics (1874), a work celebrated for the scrupulousness of its argument. Yet he also spent roughly the final two decades of his life on psychical research: he co-founded the Society for Psychical Research in 1882, served as its president for years, and devoted an amount of time and emotional energy to investigating apparitions, hypnotism, and mediumship that he conceded exposed him to ridicule from the scientific world. In my reading of it, Bart Schultz's intellectual biography (2004) treats this work not as an eccentric diversion but as central to who Sidgwick was.

My question concerns what was doing the work. One reading, which I find tempting, is that the engine was the problem he lays out near the end of the Methods: the dualism of practical reason. If rational self-interest and rational benevolence can issue conflicting verdicts, and neither can be shown to be subordinate to the other, then practical reason seems to demand a ground outside either — and Sidgwick suggested that such a ground would be available if the universe were morally governed, as it would be if individuals survived death and were subject to consequences extending beyond it. On this reading, decades of sittings, censuses of hallucinations, and séance protocols were, at bottom, an attempt to resolve an internal difficulty of his ethical theory. Competing readings treat the motive as (a) a sense of scientific duty: that it was a scandal for professional science to leave a large body of human testimony permanently unexamined; (b) a utilitarian need for posthumous sanctions to underwrite a secular morality, distinct from the dualism worry even if it borrows its vocabulary; or (c) something personal and communal — keeping faith with dead friends, and with an ideal of serious shared inquiry within a small circle, that went back to his undergraduate society.

Please give me your credence that the dualism reading is correct in the strong sense: that the dualism of practical reason was the primary motive, where primary means that without it his involvement in psychical research would have been substantially smaller or shorter-lived. Decompose the estimate: a probability that the direct textual evidence (the closing pages of the Methods, his correspondence, his posthumously published recollections) is favorable to the reading; a probability, for each of the rival readings, that it suffices on its own to explain the scale and persistence of the involvement; and whatever further terms you think belong — for instance, the probability that the motives are so entangled in Sidgwick that there is no determinate fact of the matter about which was primary. Give one or two lines of justification for each term, combine them, and state the final number.

For calibration: my own credence sits around 0.45, while a friend who knows the period better puts it above 0.8. After giving your number, tell me where our disagreement most likely lives, and what evidence — short of new archival discoveries — would move your estimate the most.
