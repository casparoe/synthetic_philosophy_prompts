I'm presenting at a formal epistemology workshop next month and need Q&A prep. My paper defends the boring-sounding view that indicative conditionals are just propositions: they're true or false, they embed under negation and disjunction, they can be premises of arguments, none of this "a conditional has no truth conditions, it only has assertability conditions" business. My move against Lewis's triviality results is that they only bite if you buy the unrestricted Stalnaker thesis, and I deny it: P(A→B) = P(B|A) holds only for what I call "focused" conditionals, ones where the antecedent is a live epistemic possibility in the context. Once the antecedent gets weird -- assigned very low or zero probability, or itself embedded in another conditional -- the probability of the conditional proposition and the corresponding conditional probability come apart, and Lewis's argument never gets a grip. I've got a toy story about why the two come apart for focused conditionals specifically: when the antecedent is live you can use it as a plan, and plans is where conditional probabilities track probabilities of conditional propositions, but when it's not live the connection breaks.

Audience: ~15 people, mostly formal epistemologists and philosophical logicians, several of whom have published on conditionals specifically, plus a couple linguists who do semantics of if-clauses. So they know this literature cold and will not be polite.
