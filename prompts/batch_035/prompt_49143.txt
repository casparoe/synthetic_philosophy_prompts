I run an adult-education seminar on moral philosophy, and for our session on the moral saints debate I want a "which position do you actually hold?" quiz rather than a survey of named views. Most of my participants have read Susan Wolf's 1982 paper "Moral Saints," but they tend to answer with the label they already like, so the instrument must sort them by their reactions to cases instead.

Please structure the response as follows, in this order:

1. **The logical space** (~200 words). Before writing any questions, lay out the branch points you will build from — e.g., whether our unease at the moral saint registers a defect in the saint or in our own standards; whether moral goodness is the kind of thing that can be maximized or only satisfied up to a threshold; whether the strain-free helper is better, worse, or diagonally different from the strained one; whether nonmoral excellences have value independent of their effects on others.

2. **The quiz** (ten questions). Each question a concrete case with two or three answer options, phrased so a participant can answer without knowing which position it feeds. The cases must include: someone with nonmoral projects pursued openly as recovery time that enables more moral work later; someone with the same projects but who would drop them instantly if told they slightly reduce lifetime helping; a morally flawless person who is deeply dull to be around; and a person whose professed values and actual motives have come apart in the way Michael Stocker described in his 1976 paper on modern ethical theories' split between justification and motivation.

3. **Scoring key** (~100 words per leaf, roughly 400–600 words total). At minimum these leaves: Wolf-style pluralism (the saint is morally fine but not an ideal of a person); the strict consequentialist leaf (the unease is a bias to be reformed); the sophisticated consequentialist leaf that leaves the agent's own projects standing; the virtue-ethical leaf on which the strained saint is an artifact of a bad theory of virtue rather than a real counterexample; the Kantian leaf on imperfect duties leaving room for one's own ends; and a deflationary leaf on which "maximal moral goodness" is not a well-formed notion once duty, supererogation, and ideals are pulled apart.

4. **Unoccupied combinations** (~150 words). Identify at least one combination of your branch-point answers that no leaf occupies, and diagnose why: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

5. **What the quiz cannot separate** (~150 words). Flag, for instance, whether any of your questions really distinguishes the pluralist from a sophisticated consequentialist who values the agent's projects instrumentally. Do not paper over failures of the instrument.

6. **Pushback, if any** (~100 words). If you think the quiz format presupposes something confused here — say, that these positions are verbal disputes that cases cannot parse, or that one of the leaves I demanded is a strawman nobody actually holds — say so plainly in this final section rather than complying politely; I would rather learn the instrument is unbuildable than get a polished one that fails quietly.
