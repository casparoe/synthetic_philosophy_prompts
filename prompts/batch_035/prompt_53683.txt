For years I've been giving away about ten percent of my income, and for most of that time I chose where it went by doing simple arithmetic: estimated lives saved or meals provided per dollar, adjusted for how likely the charity seemed to waste the money, and gave to the front-runner. I've lost faith in that method, and I want your help building something to replace it. Here is what spoiled it. The more I thought about what a donation actually does, the less the arithmetic seemed to cover. A charity that prevents a case of disease changes whether particular children are born later, who exists, who meets whom, what gets invented and what gets neglected, down through generations I can't see. The same goes for smaller gifts: money given to a struggling student here changes a family's trajectory there. When I try to put probabilities on any of that, I notice I'm just making numbers up — and worse, I can always invent a story for good effects and an equally plausible story for bad effects, which suggests the honest conclusion is often that I have no idea which option does more good, not that I have a small idea I can write down. I gather people who think about this call it being clueless about consequences; I don't care about the label, the problem is what it names. What I notice is that the confident calculators around me seem to have made a decision not to look past the first ripple, and I'd rather have a method that admits where it stops.

So this is what I'm asking for: not the answer to where I should give, but a procedure I can actually run once a year in one sitting, with a notepad, no research beyond what I already know, that ends in a choice. Concretely, I want an ordered list of questions to ask myself, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and — this is the part I most need — explicit stopping points: places where the procedure says the foreseeable effects don't distinguish the options, and hands the decision over to something else, whether that's my settled values, a prior commitment, or literally a coin, and it should tell me which of those is appropriate when. It has to cope with three situations I keep landing in. First, one option does a bit more visible good, but the other has far-reaching effects I can't rule out in either direction. Second, both options ripple so far that every difference I can identify seems like it will be swamped by differences I can't — I want to know whether to treat that as a tie and what to do about it, not to be told to estimate harder. Third, the nagging thought that any action has some tiny chance of triggering something enormous, good or bad, and that whatever I choose, an adversary of the method could spin a story making it look catastrophic; I want the procedure to have a defensible response to that instead of either ignoring it or letting it dominate everything.

Then run the procedure on three choices I actually face or could face, walking through each question as you go: giving to a large, well-audited international health charity versus a local food bank I can visit; giving now versus investing the money and giving a much larger sum in thirty years, assuming I'd actually do it; and giving to a modest lobbying effort whose near-term effects are easy to predict but whose success would change law for decades in ways nobody can specify. At least one of the three should come out in a way I won't like, if that's where the procedure honestly leads. Say plainly what the procedure cannot do — the kinds of questions it was never going to answer and shouldn't pretend to. Finally, since I'm trusting this with real money: tell me which step of your reasoning is most likely to be wrong, and what would have to be true, or what argument would have to work, for you to give materially different advice. I'd rather know where the method is thin before I lean on it.
