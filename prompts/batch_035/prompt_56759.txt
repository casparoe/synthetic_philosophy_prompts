So here's my situation: I'm a journalist who mostly writes about science and ideas, and I've got a percolating peice on Judgment at Nuremberg, the 1961 Kramer film — Spencer Tracy as the American judge, Maximilian Schell as the German defense counsel, Burt Lancaster as the dignified defendant judge who, in the end, wants one thing from Tracy: that the world be told it wasn't a political trial. I rewatched it at a retrospective last month and this time what grabbed me was the legal philosophy humming underneath it, which the film deliberately keeps at ghost level. The defense's strongest card is never innocence; it's retroactivity. His clients applied the law as it stood, punishing without a law was invented afterward, by the winners. And the film keeps the victors' justice worry alive on its own side too: by 1948 the Americans want Germany as an ally against the Soviets, pressures come down from Washington to go easy, and the closing titles tell us almost nobody sentenced stayed in prison for long.

What I want is a reading of the film through that lens specifically — the no-crime-without-a-law maxim, ex post facto, victors' justice — plus one thing the film never once mentions: Tokyo. The Tokyo tribunal sat at the same time, also run by the winners, and the asymmetries there were uglier: commanders prosecuted for mistreating prisoners while the victors' own firebombing of cities and the two atomic bombs were never on the table, and the emperor quietly exempt. Does the fact that the film only interrogates the Nuremberg side — and frankly flatters the American judge — make it an honest dramatization of the rule-of-law problem or a comfortable one?

A few scenes I'd want you to work through rather than around. The atrocity-footage montage in court: is it doing legal work or rhetorical work, and does the film know the difference? The sterilization witness (Montgomery Clift's character) and the cross-examinations where Schell turns each witness's fear into complicity — the film seems to hold that fear explains but doesn't excuse; can the law actually draw that line? The defense's counterattack about America's own forced-sterilization laws and segregation — the film gives him the best legal arguments in the whole picture and the worst motives; is that honesty or deck-stacking, and where else does it stack the deck (remember who wrote it and when, the same year the Eichmann trial began)? And the final cell conversation, where the judge in effect tells the convicted judge that the guilt began long before anything he was charged with — is the film's verdict on victors' justice that it was justice anyway? Is that the film's conclusion or just the judge's, with the release epilogue undercutting it? I'd also like you to say plainly where the film resists my reading, and to end on what it adds to the retroactivity debate that the law-review back and forth doesn't — I've read enough of that stuff to fill a notebook and none of it ever made me feel the deadline pressure those closing titles do.
