I am a retired radio astronomer who spent thirty-five years measuring the 21-centimetre line of neutral hydrogen, and in the years since I retired I have been working through philosophy of science, mostly Popper, Lakatos, and their critics. I am now drafting an essay on the problem of verisimilitude, and I would be grateful for candid comments on whether my argument is sound, whether my characterization of the literature is accurate, and whether the central move is interesting or merely a physicist's naivety dressed up as philosophy.

The essay's working thesis is that the debate about truthlikeness went astray by treating closeness to truth as a logical relation between the contents of theories, when working scientists instead regiment it as a relation of approximation between quantities. My sketch runs as follows. We all want to say that Newtonian mechanics is closer to the truth than Ptolemaic astronomy, even though strictly speaking both are false; Popper proposed a definition of verisimilitude in the early 1960s in terms of truth content and falsity content, and in the mid-1970s Tichý and Miller independently showed that his definition had the perverse consequence that, as a matter of logic, no false theory could come out more verisimilar than another false theory — which empties the notion of almost all its intended use. I gather that later work, by Niiniluoto and others, rebuilt truthlikeness using similarity measures over possible worlds or over the consequences of theories, and I plan to describe that work briefly and fairly, though I may have the details wrong and would welcome correction. The move I want to defend, however, is that this whole tradition models the wrong relation. In physics we already possess an exact, quantitative notion of one false theory being closer to the truth than another: Newtonian mechanics is recovered as a limiting case of general relativity when gravitational fields are weak and velocities are small, and the Newtonian parameters are limiting values of the relativistic ones. So closeness to truth, I want to argue, is best analysed as convergence in a space of measured quantities — an error-bound notion of the sort every observational paper already uses — rather than as any comparison of which sentences a theory entails. On this view, what Tichý and Miller exposed was not a technical glitch in Popper's definition but a symptom of asking a logical question where the real relation is metrical.

I am aware of two objections I must confront, and I would like your view on whether my answers to them hold up. First, the limiting-case relation seems hostage to a choice of coordinates or parametrization, and it does not obviously preserve reference: the caloric theory of heat yields accurate functional predictions about heat flow while its central kind fails to refer to anything, and phlogiston chemistry was predictive in a similar way — so does my quantitative criterion wrongly certify such theories as truthlike, and can I answer that by separating closeness of functional form from closeness of ontology, or does that distinction simply reintroduce the logical notion at a higher level? Second, it may be said that Popper was after an epistemic appraisal — a standard for saying that science is making progress even though every theory is false — and that my reconstruction, being about the mathematical relation between two formalisms, says nothing about whether either formalism is nearer the truth in the sense Popper cared about. Is that objection fatal, or can the essay legitimately reply that the epistemic question inherits whatever the metrical relation settles? I would also appreciate comments on the overall structure: should the caloric and phlogiston material come before or after the Newton–general relativity case, and is the essay at risk of equivocating between closeness of predictions and closeness of theories without flagging the distinction? Please do not supply a bibliography or reading list; I only need judgments on the argument, the accuracy of what I have written above, and where it is weakest.
