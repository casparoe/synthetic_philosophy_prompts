I've been auditing a seminar on conditionals, and I've been keeping a notebook of my gut verdicts about indicative conditionals — the "if it rained, the match was cancelled" kind, not the "if it had rained..." kind. I'd like to know whether my verdicts add up to a single coherent view, and I suspect they don't, in which case I want you to take a side rather than hand me a menu.

The verdicts. First, a fair die is about to be rolled, and I'm confident to degree exactly 1/3 that if it comes up even, it will come up six — the same degree to which I'm confident it will come up six given that it comes up even. So it seems to me that the probability of an indicative conditional just is the conditional probability of the consequent on the antecedent. Second, I can also make perfectly good sense of an indicative conditional being flat-out false: if the butler did it and the maid was asleep in her room, then "if the butler did it, the maid saw him" is false, not merely improbable, and once the forensics show the butler did do it while the maid slept through everything, I'd retract the conditional outright rather than just lower its probability. Third, indicative conditionals enter into valid inferences: modus ponens and modus tollens with them feel as valid as anything in logic. Fourth, yet nested conditionals trip me up. In a three-way race between a Republican incumbent expected to win, a fellow Republican, and a Democrat, I accept "if a Republican wins, then if the incumbent doesn't, the other Republican will" — it seems to follow just from the arithmetic of a three-way race — and I accept that the incumbent won; but I do not accept "if the incumbent doesn't win, the other Republican will," which is what modus ponens with a conditional conclusion hands me, and which looks plainly wrong given that the Democrat was polling a strong second. Fifth, after the die has been rolled and I've seen it came up three, I find myself unable to say whether "if it came up even, it came up six" is true or false, though before the roll I had no trouble betting on it at 1-in-3.

My dim sense is that some of these cannot all be held together — I've read that if indicative conditionals are ordinary propositions whose probability always equals the corresponding conditional probability, then everything collapses into everything else, so that the probability of any conditional would have to equal the probability of its consequent, which my die case flatly contradicts. So here is my request: pick the view you actually think is right, state precisely what it takes the probability and the truth value of an indicative conditional to be, and tell me which of my five verdicts it has to overturn and why those rather than the others (I can feel pressure on holding the first together with the second, and on holding the third together with the fourth). Then tell me what the resulting view predicts about the case I keep going back and forth on: a conditional like "if the test was rigged, the results came out 2-to-1," asserted about a test everyone now knows was clean — true, false, or somehow moot, and what exactly my confidence in such a conditional should be after the clean bill of health comes in. Don't lay out the options and leave me to choose; commit to one and defend it against the verdicts it has to bite.
