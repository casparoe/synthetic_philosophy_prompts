I'd like to test a prominent real-world episode against the philosophical position it is usually taken to embody, and see how much survives the test.

In 1947, as the UN Commission on Human Rights was drafting what became the Universal Declaration, the American Anthropological Association, under its then-president Melville Herskovits, sent the Commission a "Statement on Human Rights" warning against a worldwide declaration of rights. Drawing on the relativism of the Boas school, it argued to the effect that an individual's capacities and even his very identity are constituted by his culture, that respect for differences between cultures is a precondition for any viable international order, and that because standards and values are relative to the cultures from which they derive, a declaration built on the beliefs and moral codes of one culture would to that extent have diminished applicability to mankind as a whole. The episode's context is also worth weighing: Herskovits's Committee for International Cooperation in Anthropology, then the dominant professional association of anthropologists, had grown wary of American and European moralizing abroad, partly because of the field's encounters with colonial administrations. Then, in 1999, the same association effectively reversed itself: its Committee for Human Rights published a "Declaration on Anthropology and Human Rights" affirming that there is no conflict between respecting cultural difference and a commitment to international human rights, and repudiating the way the 1947 statement had been used to license indifference to abuse.

My question is where moral relativism, as a philosophical position, actually stands in this sequence. First, map the 1947 argument onto the standard metaethical taxonomy. The famous line about individuals being "who they are" only within a culture reads as a constitutive claim from the philosophy of social science; the closing inference from cultural relativity to the inapplicability of a universal declaration reads as a metaethical claim. Are these two claims separable? I want to know whether the inference works at all: does it require the strong descriptive thesis that cultures are self-contained moral systems that no actual society satisfies, or merely the weak thesis that values differ, which almost every moral realist grants? Does the statement need what philosophers would later call agent-relative normative reasons, or does it run on a shallower confusion that its critics (who were, to be fair, often as careless as its defenders) failed to name? Then tell me how the two claims behave in the 1999 reversal, and what each implies about which version of relativism is actually at stake in the human-rights debate. Also say where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak: does either statement supply a clear answer about the moral status of the Universal Declaration itself, which had by 1999 become the reference point the anthropologists were accused of resisting?

A few constraints on the answer. Do not treat either the 1947 or the 1999 text as sacred: the task is to state what the correct verdict is, all things considered, not to balance the two documents against each other. And do not resolve the question by labeling one side "relativism" and the other "universalism" and leaving it there — those labels hide almost everything of philosophical interest in this episode, and part of what I want is an account of which distinct positions are being conflated when it is described that way.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — in this case, your overall verdict on the charge as leveled against the 1947 statement — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
