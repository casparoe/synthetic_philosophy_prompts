Quick background: I'm on a team of devs, no philosophy people, and yesterday our teammate Dana ran a thing on us at lunch that she got from a behavioral econ book — the Ellsberg urn thing. 90 balls in a box, 30 of them known to be red, the other 60 some unknown mix of black and yellow. First she asked who'd rather bet $100 on red coming up vs. black coming up. Basically everyone said red. Then she asked who'd rather bet on red-or-yellow vs. black-or-yellow. The same people (me included, oops) said black-or-yellow. She said we'd all just contradicted ourselves, and she and Marco argued about it for the rest of lunch. I couldn't tell who was winning and I want a proper scorecard.

Their exchange, as best I remember it:

(1) Dana said our two choices are irrational, full stop. If you'd rather bet on red than black, then adding yellow to both options shouldn't flip your preference — it's the same bet plus the same extra outcome. And she said someone whose preferences flip like that can be milked for money with a couple of side bets, like an arbitrage against yourself.

(2) Marco said it's not irrational, it's worst-casing. The black/yellow split is unknown, so for each bet you assume whatever split hurts you most. Betting red guarantees a 1/3 chance. Betting black could be near zero. Betting black-or-yellow guarantees 2/3, while red-or-yellow could be barely 1/3. The exact pattern the table chose is what maximin predicts, and he said refusing to trust an unknown quantity you have zero data on is just good engineering — like pinning dependency versions before a release instead of grabbing whatever the registry happens to serve.

(3) Dana said that's incoherent: to prefer red over black you have to assume there's hardly any black in the box, and to prefer black-or-yellow over red-or-yellow you have to assume there's tons of black. Same box, opposite assumptions, depending on which question she asks.

(4) Marco said he doesn't assume anything about the split — that's the whole point. Worst-casing a bet isn't a belief about the balls, it's a policy about where he refuses to be confident. And he asked why, if the choices are so obviously irrational, nearly everyone at the table made them, including people who've never taken an econ class in their lives.

(5) Dana said fine, but you don't need some exotic theory to make sense of Marco — he's just risk averse, and risk aversion fits into ordinary expected utility without any trouble: put a prior over how many of the 60 are black, crank the numbers, done. Marco said no — with anything like a uniform prior on the split, red and black come out exactly even, so expected utility theory says he shouldn't prefer either one, which to him means the framework is missing something real, not that he's broken.

They gave up when we had to get back to the sprint. Can you go through it move by move and tell me who's right at each step, and also whether there's something both of them are missing?
