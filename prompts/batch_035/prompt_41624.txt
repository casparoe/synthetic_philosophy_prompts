I am a high school senior writing an extended essay for my economics course on the socialist calculation debate, and for the historical background I have been reading a popular history of economic thought that my school library owns. I have chosen not to name the book because my question is really about whether its account is accurate, and I would rather have the evaluation stand on its own. The relevant chapter presents the debate as a three-round fight between Mises, Lange, and Hayek, and it reads as confident and coherent, but I do not yet know the primary literature well enough to tell a fair compression from a distortion, which is exactly what I need to do before I build my essay on top of it.

Here is my summary of the author's account, clause by clause. He says that Mises, writing in 1920, argued that socialism is impossible because without private ownership of the means of production there are no genuine market prices for capital goods, and that without such prices a planning authority has no way of calculating whether any given production plan uses resources rationally, so that a socialist economy could not merely be less efficient than a market economy but could not allocate resources rationally at all. Lange, we are told, answered by proposing that a central planning board determine correct prices by solving the system of simultaneous equations that formal economic theory says characterizes a general equilibrium, computing the equilibrium prices directly and then issuing them to factory managers. Hayek, in his 1945 essay, is presented as having changed the subject rather than answered Lange: his point was that the information needed to set prices is dispersed among millions of individuals, exists largely as tacit know-how, and could never be gathered by any board, which the author treats as a different and deeper objection than the calculation problem Lange had addressed. The chapter closes by saying that although Hayek won the theoretical exchange, the real reason socialism failed in practice was motivational: without private profit and loss there is no incentive to work hard or to innovate, and that is why Lange's elegant model never stood a chance, a conclusion the author attributes to the combined lesson of the whole debate.

Something about this account feels off to me, though I cannot pin down what. My instinct is that the framing of Hayek's contribution as a change of subject rather than a continuation of the same argument does some quiet work, and I also wonder whether the closing point about incentives belongs to the calculation debate at all or is being smuggled in as its conclusion. Please go through the account and tell me which parts a historian of economic thought would accept, which are defensible simplifications, and which misrepresent the positions involved — I am particularly interested in whether the description of Lange's proposal is right, and in whether the book's final conclusion actually follows from anything in the debate as the book itself describes it. Since my essay will be evaluated partly on fairness to the historical positions, I would be grateful if you were specific about which of Lange's and Mises's actual claims the author is compressing or conflating, rather than just telling me the summary is mostly fine.
