I'm the teaching assistant for a mid-level course on nineteenth-century political thought, and I've just started grading our take-home essay on Mill and empire, which has become a more pressing task than I expected — the instructor is ill, the grades are due in two days, and this first paper is a borderline case I have to justify carefully in my comments. The paper concerns Mill's double life: by day he spent his entire career, from age seventeen in 1823 until the Company's abolition in 1858, at the East India Company's offices in London — for much of that time heading the Examiner's office, which drafted official dispatches to India — while by night he wrote the liberal works we all know. The question we set the students was the following:

"Mill's On Liberty (1859) was published one year after the East India Company was abolished and Mill lost the post he had held for thirty-five years. Some scholars read the essay as, in part, a response to this event: an argument that the Company's system of rule by a trained official class in London was good for India precisely because Indians were not yet fit for self-government, and that the transfer to the Crown was a mistake. Assess this claim, drawing on what you know of Mill's life, his father's writings on India, and the political theory of On Liberty."

The student's essay, in full:

"On Liberty appeared in 1859, a year after the East India Company was wound up, and in my view the timing is the key to the essay. Mill worked for the Company from 1823 until 1858, and his father James Mill had written the standard work on India, the History of British India (1817), which was so respected that it practically got both Mills their jobs. James Mill thought Indian society was backward and needed rational administration by Britain, and John Stuart inherited this view: he believed Indians were not yet ready to govern themselves, so a benevolent despotism ruled by experts at a distance was, for the time being, the arrangement that best served their interests. This is why the timing of On Liberty matters. When the essay says that the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilised community is to prevent harm to others, we should read it as a defense of the Company: the Company exercised power over India precisely to prevent harm, since India, being uncivilised in Mill's terms, fell outside the essay's protection. In fact the whole essay can be read as an argument that the Company's officials should be left alone to do their work, free from interference by the Crown and by Parliament — that is what the tyranny of the majority amounts to in the colonial context. There is even a deeper point. James Mill thought one could judge India better from a desk in London than by going there, and in the same spirit On Liberty argues that distant, disinterested experts see a society's needs more clearly than the society itself does; so On Liberty is not really about individuals at all but about how societies progress, which is the theme Mill first set out in his treatise on the progress of human societies. In short, On Liberty is best understood as the working philosophy of a lifelong imperial administrator, and its famous principles are the fruit of Mill's day job."

I'd like you to grade this. Please structure your response in four sections, in this order: "Overall assessment" (one short paragraph); "What the essay gets right" (roughly 150 words); "Specific errors and distortions" (the heart of the response, roughly 300 words); and "Grade and marginal comments" (a suggested letter or percentage grade plus the individual comments you would write in the margin next to the offending passages). Bear in mind that some of the student's claims are defensible, some are half-truths, and at least two are subtly wrong — for instance, I'd want you to check carefully whether On Liberty actually says what the student paraphrases it as saying, and whether the work the student calls "his treatise on the progress of human societies" is really a separate publication the student has misdescribed.
