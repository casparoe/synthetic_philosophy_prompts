I've been reading the rationalist blogosphere since the Overcoming Bias days, and lately the discourse around AI-driven automation has gotten repetitive in a way I'd like to pick apart rather than just argue with. The standard move — which I've seen from economists and econ-literate commenters perhaps a hundred times now — goes like this: someone worries that this time machines really will take the jobs; the reply invokes the lump of labour fallacy, gestures at two centuries of evidence since the Luddites (automation destroyed old occupations, created new ones, and real wages rose), and concludes that the correct policy response is purely transitional: retraining programs, wage insurance, perhaps a UBI, with the productivity gains understood as an unambiguous good that merely needs to be distributed. The debate always stops right there, as though the rebuttal were self-evident. For a blog post I'm drafting, I want to examine what this whole discursive practice presupposes rather than whether its conclusion is true.

My hunch is that the rebuttal is only a valid inference under a specific theory of value and of the worker's interest — something like: what matters to workers is the wage, the wage tracks marginal productivity, and automation's gains diffuse to labor through the ordinary working of markets, so that if aggregate employment and average real wages eventually recover, the workers are, by definition, made whole. As best I can tell, Marx in the machinery chapters of Capital, volume 1, never needed the lump of labour to be true in the aggregate for his argument to run: the mechanism he describes is that machinery transforms the composition of the working class, deskills, swells the reserve army, and permanently shifts bargaining power — the harm lives in the transformation and the power relations, not in a permanent shortfall of jobs. And in the Grundrisse, in the long stretch that people in these discussions keep calling the Fragment on Machines, he seems to push further: once machines do most of the producing, labour time ceases to be a sensible measure of wealth, so a system that still metes out claims on wealth through wages is left sitting on an open contradiction. If that's right, the standard rebuttal may be answering a complaint Marx never made, while leaving his actual one untouched.

So please structure the response in the following sections, in this order. First, Statement of the Practice (~150 words): lay out the rebuttal move concretely, including the standard follow-through about retraining and redistribution, in a form an economist would sign off on. Second, The Implicit Theory (~350 words): reconstruct the assumptions about value, wages, and worker welfare that turn the rebuttal from a non sequitur into a sound inference. Third, What the Rebuttal Leaves Untouched (~400 words): assess whether the Marxian mechanisms — deskilling, the reserve army, bargaining power, and the Grundrisse point about labour time as both measure and distributor of wealth — are compatible with the very historical record the rebuttal cites. Fourth, Underdetermination (~200 words): say which parts of the practice the rebuttal strictly requires, versus which rival views (Keynesian demand management, a straightforward human-capital view, even a fully Marxian one) could equally license the retraining-and-redistribution follow-through. Fifth, If Marx Were the Presupposition (~250 words): describe how the policy conversation would have to change — what would replace retraining and UBI as default recommendations if the implicit theory were the Marxian one instead. Throughout, be hard-nosed about where the rebuttal is genuinely strong: my readers will not forgive me for a strawman of the econ side, and I would rather publish a nuanced concession than a clever-sounding gotcha.
