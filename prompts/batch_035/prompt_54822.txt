my roommate and i run a film-and-philosophy night, one old movie plus one text per month, and this time it's Network (the 1976 Lumet film, Chayefsky's script) paired with Kierkegaard's critique of the press and the public — the stuff from the Two Ages review, usually printed as The Present Age. i'm leading the discussion and want to actually do this well instead of hand-waving, so give me a real reading of the film through that lens.

plot refresher in case you need it: Howard Beale, an aging news anchor, announces on air that he'll shoot himself on his final broadcast, then instead has an on-air breakdown and starts ranting. ratings explode, so the network gives him a pulpit, then basically a variety show — his sermons share a program with a fortune teller and a segment built around fugitive radicals. by the end his doom-preaching has tanked the numbers and he's been turned (by the corporate chairman, in one scene) into a preacher of money and markets; when even that fails, executives have him assasinated live on air for the sweeps. the programming executive, Diana, treats all of it — his breakdown, his rage, his death — purely as content.

now the lens, and where i want you to take it seriously:

- Kierkegaard's public is passionless, abstract, a phantom of spectators that levels everything down. but Network's audience gets whipped into a genuine frenzy. is frenzy the same as passion for him, or is it spectacle — and would he say rage-as-entertainment is leveling's final form, passion itself absorbed into reflection? does the film agree with him or correct him?
- the scene where Beale tells viewers to shout out their windows that they've had enough, and the whole city does it. is that a crowd, or the public imitating a crowd? something assembled and dispersed by the medium, fake by construction?
- everything passionate in the film gets converted into programming, including the radical fugitives. is that leveling in his sense, or just capitalism doing its thing? Kierkegaard has no economics of the press — does the film need him at all, or does it need Marx? or does he explain the part Marx can't: why the audience wants it?
- the ending. Kierkegaard predicted a public too passionless to ever actually do anything. the film gives you a public that is passionate enough to watch anything and does nothing, and the one man with a mission gets scheduled and then killed for ratings. vindication or update?

give me a reading that's faithful in both directions — where the lens illuminates specific scenes (point me to ones to show clips from), where it honestly breaks down, and what the film shows that Kierkegaard's own text can't. that last bit is what i want to close the evening on.
