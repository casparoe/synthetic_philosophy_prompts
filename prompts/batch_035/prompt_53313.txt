I teach an upper-level module on the ethics of the welfare state, and I'm building a unit on conditionality — the practice of attaching behavioural requirements to benefits — and the older logic it inherits from the poor laws, including the old principle of "less eligibility" (relief must remain less comfortable than the worst wage). My students can recite that sanctions and work requirements exist without feeling how strange the underlying reasoning is. Deservingness research suggests that when people judge whether someone merits help, they roughly ask five questions: was the person in control of the cause of their need; are they willing to reciprocate; are they "one of us"; how severe is the need; and do they show the right attitude — effort, deference, gratitude. I want a satirical artifact that smuggles this logic into view rather than lecturing about it.

Write a parody of an official government FAQ page, roughly 700 words, issued by a fictional agency — call it the Ministry of Conditional Support — under the title "Do I Deserve Help? Frequently Asked Questions." The register should be deadpan bureaucratic: warm, plain, scrupulously helpful, the way good government guidance actually reads — never snide, never winking. It must contain exactly five numbered questions, in this order: (1) "How do I prove my need is real?"; (2) "What if my situation is my own fault?"; (3) "Do I have to give something back?"; (4) "Am I one of the people this scheme is for?"; (5) "What happens if I don't comply with my conditions?" Each answer, about 130 words, should quietly enact exactly one of the five deservingness criteria above, so that an attentive reader can reconstruct the whole test without it ever being named. The humor must come from real features of conditionality rather than invention: the answer on need should reveal that need is the least decisive criterion (a well-documented catastrophe you could have avoided actually counts against you); the answer on fault should show the criterion expanding until every poverty traces back to some choice; the reciprocity answer should treat help as a debt that gratitude never fully amortizes, mentioning a "community contribution credit"; the identity answer should lean on the contributory fiction that some claimants "paid in"; the compliance answer should include a sanctions schedule in which the penalty for missing one appointment exceeds the amount at stake, plus a "hardship payment" that turns out to be a loan. You may give the page a title and a single invented epigraph — one reassuring sentence from a fictional minister — but nothing before or after the five items.

Two constraints on fairness. First, the target is the deservingness logic and the institutions that encode it, never claimants: every applicant should be addressed with impeccable fictional politeness, and the piece should leave a social-policy scholar thinking you have read the conditionality literature and a claimants' advocate thinking it is cruelly accurate — the satire should work as a reductio of locally plausible rules that are collectively indefensible, not as a joke at the expense of poor people. Second, keep it denationalized: no real country, party, politician, or named programme should be identifiable, and use a generic currency, so the piece reads as a reductio of the logic itself rather than a hit on any current government.
