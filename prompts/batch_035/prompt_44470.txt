I write about science and ideas for a general readership, and over the past few years, while reporting pieces on everything from clinical screening thresholds to how mapmakers decide where one region's name ends, I have kept running into a pattern I don't have a word for. It concerns the borderlands of vague concepts — the stretch where nobody can say where red stops and orange starts, where a town gives out and a city begins, where tall ends. But the thing I keep noticing is not the blur itself; it is something inside the blur. Even in the no-man's-land where individual calls cannot be settled, certain combinations of those calls seem flatly unavailable, and everyone treats them as errors rather than as one more opinion.

Three instances, all from my notebook. First: a materials lab I visited grades runs of samples along a continuum from red toward orange. The middle samples get honestly marked borderline, and nobody objects. But if the grading record jumped back and forth — sample 42 called red, sample 55 called not red, sample 68 called red again — the lab would treat that as a procedural failure and redo it, even though each of those calls is exactly as defensible as its neighbors, since none of them is defensible at all. Second: in sports reporting, two prospects measured a day apart at 6'3½" and 6'3⅝"; a story that called the shorter of the two tall and the taller one not tall would read as a mistake, not as a dissenting verdict about a borderline pair. Third, and strangest: a mid-sized municipality can be described as neither a city nor a town, and that sounds like sober description; but describing it as both a city and a town sounds like a contradiction. Both sentences place it in exactly the same unresolved middle, so why is one of them off-limits?

The neighborhood is obviously vagueness — borderline cases, the sorites paradox, and so on — but everything I can find under those headings is about where the line is, or whether there is a line. What I am pointing at is what stays forbidden after the line has been given up: guardrails that operate inside the gray zone, so that the unsettleable cases are unsettled only individually and not in combination. So, three questions, and let me say up front that a bare label will not help me; I need the carving. Does this phenomenon have an established name, and if so, what exactly does the name cover? How does it relate to the sorites paradox in particular — is the familiar premise that removing a single grain cannot turn a heap into a non-heap one of these in-between constraints, or is it a different kind of animal? And if no established concept captures it, please coin a term, define it precisely enough to decide contested cases — in particular, does it extend to rules that hold only relative to a purpose, such as a pollution reading that counts as elevated for one regulatory regime and not for another? — and mark where its boundary lies with ordinary context-dependence.
