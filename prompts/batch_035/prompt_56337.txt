I chair the student council at my school, and I have run into a problem I have since learned has a name. We are about thirty students who meet fortnightly for one hour, and we decide things by majority vote: budget allocations, which club proposals to fund, whether to take positions on school policy. Last term we voted on a motion to cut funding for the school greenhouse project, and something odd happened. I will simplify, but roughly: the motion depended on three premises — that the greenhouse is underused, that the money could do more good elsewhere, and that redirecting funds is within our gift. A majority of the council accepted each premise when we voted on them separately. Yet when we voted on the motion itself, a majority rejected it, because the majorities were made up of different people each time. We then had a furious argument about which vote "really" counted: the premise votes or the conclusion vote. My maths teacher told me this is called the discursive dilemma or the doctrinal paradox, and that social choice theorists have written a good deal about judgment aggregation, though she could not tell me how any of it survives contact with a real meeting of teenagers.

I would like you to turn whatever that literature actually teaches into a procedure I can run as chair. Specifically, please write a document with the following sections, in this order and at roughly these lengths:

1. "The rule I recommend" (about 300 words). Commit to a single default rule for when my council should vote premise-by-premise and when it should vote on the conclusion directly, and defend it. Do not give me a survey of every aggregation rule on offer and leave the choice to me; take a position and say why it beats the obvious alternatives, including any conditions under which you would concede the default should be overturned.

2. "The procedure" (about 500 words). A step-by-step checklist I can actually follow in the chair's seat during a one-hour meeting: what to ask in what order, how to tell in advance whether a motion is the kind where premise voting and conclusion voting could come apart, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure should stop and hand the decision to a debate or a person because the question is one of values rather than of consistency, and what I should write in the minutes so the outcome is not reopened at the next meeting.

3. "Worked cases" (about 450 words). Apply the procedure to three motions: (a) funding a late bus on concert nights, which most of us personally support; (b) a proposal to stop funding a club whose activities most members privately think are a waste of money but which some members belong to; and (c) a motion to endorse a change to the school's homework policy, where our council's view carries no authority and is mainly symbolic. At least one of the three should come out a way I will not like — say so plainly rather than softening it.

4. "Where the procedure misfires" (about 200 words). An honest account of the situations the procedure gets wrong or cannot settle, including anything about strategic voting by members who work out the rule and vote their preferences on premises they do not hold.

Please answer in British English. I should say that our members have no background in logic or social choice theory, so whatever technical distinctions the procedure rests on need to survive translation into language a fifteen-year-old can follow in the moment — but do not let the simplification quietly change what the theory actually claims. I would rather have a definite rule I can be argued out of than a balanced overview.
