I have just been appointed director of a new character-education program at a public secondary school, and in a month I must deliver the opening address at the staff workshop that inaugurates it. The appointment has become philosophically awkward for me. The program's prospectus to parents leans heavily on the language of virtue: we will, it promises, cultivate honesty, courage, and compassion as stable traits of character. In preparing my remarks, I have been reading the situationist literature, and it has unsettled me more than I anticipated — Hartshorne and May's finding in the late 1920s that a child's honesty on a classroom test barely predicts whether she cheats at a game of marbles later the same afternoon; Darley and Batson's Good Samaritan study, in which a seminarian's being in a hurry mattered more to whether he stopped to help than anything about his dispositions; Milgram's obedience results; the bystander experiments. If cross-situational consistency of character is largely an illusion, then a curriculum promising to build it is promising the wrong thing, and a staff that half-knows this will smell it at once — several of our teachers trained in psychology.

Please write the address: roughly ten minutes, delivered to a mixed room of skeptical psychology-trained teachers, enthusiastic donors seated at the back, and colleagues who mainly want practical guidance. It must take situationism seriously rather than deflect it — I want it to concede the Hartshorne and May result explicitly and openly, and then to argue that the program survives the concession. What I would like the speech to make real is a reorientation I have been considering: that we teach character partly as situation-design, so that students learn to audit the pressures of a scene — hurry, diffusion of responsibility, whatever norms are most salient — and to alter those pressures, meaning that Aristotelian habituation survives situationism by treating the situations themselves as among the things habituated. I want that idea actually argued, not merely gestured at, but in language a tired teacher at four in the afternoon on an inset day can follow; and I want the speech to end with the room convinced that the program is more honest, not less ambitious, for the concession.
