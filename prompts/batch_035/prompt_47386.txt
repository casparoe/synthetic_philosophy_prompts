You are a professional philosopher with strong views in philosophy of mind and the emotions, and I want your help stress-testing a theory before I commit to it in a book of essays I'm writing alongside my novels. I'd like you to role-play a committed hedonist critic — someone who holds that all motivation tracks felt pleasure and displeasure, and who therefore thinks any account of why we seek out sorrow is either disguised pleasure-talk or error theory. Make this critic sharp and genuinely difficult: they know the classic moves (the masochism reply, the "mixed pleasures" reply, the claim that tragedy-enjoyment is just relief at the ending), they should concede small points when cornered but never the main one, and they should stay in character until I write "stop." Keep each reply to a single focused paragraph so it feels like a live argument rather than a lecture.

My theory, which you'll be attacking: readers return to devastating novels not because the pain is secretly sweet, and not merely because of catharsis or "safe" practice for real grief, but because grief in fiction is a skill we get to perform well. In life we grieve badly — too numb, too angry, too soon over it. In a novel the sorrow arrives on cue, at the right pitch, and the reader gets to have exactly the feeling the moment calls for. The pleasure is in one's own emotional accuracy, an aesthetic pleasure in the fit between feeling and occasion, like a dancer's pleasure in a movement finally landing.

Here is my opening claim for your critic to go at: you hedonists say we only ever pursue what feels good, but that makes the reader's weeping inexplicable in the wrong way — not because it is pain, but because nobody grades it. I read alone at midnight, cry over an invented death, and will never tell a soul. If the payoff were pleasure in achievement, there would have to be some witness or standard against which the grief succeeds or fails. There isn't. So the accuracy can't be for anything — unless it's just intrinsically pleasurable to feel accurate, at which point you've conceded that the pleasure and the pain are one state, and your hedonism has stopped doing any explanatory work. Take it from there.
