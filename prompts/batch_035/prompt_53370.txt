so here's a weird thing that happened to me and I want you to turn it into a short story, because I can't get it out of my head and maybe fiction is the only honest way to deal with it.

I'm a novelist. last month I killed off a supporting character in the book I'm working on — a midwife named Dagmar, a hundred-something pages in, she'd grown a whole backstory I never planned, a dead husband, a way of clicking her tongue before she said anything kind. I cried writing her death scene. like actually cried, at my own desk, over a person I made up out of nothing. and then a few hours later I was fine, wiped my face, went to dinner. it's obviously connected to the old paradox of fiction — readers (and apparently writers) have real emotions about people they know perfectly well don't exist, and that seems to either mean the emotions aren't real or our beliefs aren't doing the work we thought. but nobody tells you what it feels like from the inside, being both the person who invents the grief and the person who suffers it.

here's what I want: a short story, first person, from a writer in roughly my position. but I don't want the story to be about the paradox, I want it to *do* the paradox — the reader should catch themselves feeling something for Dagmar (or your equivalent character, make her your own, change everything except the fact that the narrator invented her and mourns her) at the exact moment the text is reminding them she's fabricated. so like: give us real scenes of her, real texture, let her be as alive as you can make her, and then have the machinery of the writing intrude — the narrator noticing she only exists when the narrator is thinking about her, that her memories change between drafts, that a line of dialogue she "said" was actually written, deleted, and rewritten eleven times. the reader's emotion for her should be built up and then be shown its own foundation.

a couple of specific asks:

- somewhere in there, a scene where the narrator tries to explain the death to someone (a partner, a therapist, another writer) and the only way to make the grief legible is to tell it as if Dagmar were real — and it works, the listener tears up — and the narrator hates that it works
- late in the story, the narrator rereads an old draft where Dagmar doesn't die, and feels nothing at all, and that scares them more than the crying did. use that to gesture at whatever you think actually resolves or dissolves the paradox (pretend emotions, make-believe emotions, irrationality, whatever you find most true) — but gesture at it through the narrator's experience, don't have anyone lecture about philosophy. if a philosopher's view shows up it should be worn by a character, half-remembered and slightly wrong, the way real people half-remember ideas
- the ending should not resolve clean. I want the reader to put it down unsure whether they were ever really feeling anything, and then to notice they're still a little bit sad about Dagmar, and to have no idea what to do with that

length-wise: long enough to actually build the attachment, this doesn't work in 400 words. no metafictional trickery for its own sake — the intrusions should hurt a little, not be clever. and one more thing: the narrator should be a slightly worse person than me, someone who maybe uses the fictional dead as a way to not deal with the real ones. I suspect that's the part that'll be uncomfortable to write, it's the part I can't do myself.

small note: don't name the paradox anywhere in the story itself. if you put the phrase "paradox of fiction" in a character's mouth I will know you took the easy way out.
