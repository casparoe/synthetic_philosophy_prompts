I run a student philosophy society at my university — about 14 people, mostly first- and second-years who've done a bit of free will in intro but nothing deep, one 90-minute evening slot. I want to do Galen Strawson's Basic Argument with them, but with a twist I care a lot about: they shouldn't hear Strawson's name or see the argument written out until the very end. The whole point is that the structure of the argument — you do what you do because of how you are; to be truly responsible for what you do you'd have to be responsible for how you are; but that would require an earlier act of self-shaping, done with the way you already were, and so on forever — has to ambush them through the rules of the game, not through anything I say.

Rough shape I've been imagining (push back on this freely, I've never run it): Round one, everyone gets a "character sheet" they didn't choose — dispositions dealt out by dice or card draw (impatient, scrupulous, conflict-averse, generous-to-a-fault, etc.) — and then they play some short decision game where the sheet visibly drives what they do. Maybe a group negotiation where sheets constrain moves. Afterwards the group hands out blame and credit for how it went. Round two, they get to "edit" their sheet, but only using what's already on it — like, you can only temper your impatience using whatever degree of scrupulousness you were already dealt — and then the blame/credit round happens again and (I'm hoping) explodes into a fight.

Design the full thing for me: rules, materials (nothing that costs real money), a timeline, and the debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto specific stages of the argument. Make the calls and defend them, don't give me three variants to pick from.
