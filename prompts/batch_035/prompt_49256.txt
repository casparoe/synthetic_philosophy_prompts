I host a small conversation series at my neighborhood library, and next month's guest is a cognitive scientist who holds that an ant colony, taken as a whole, genuinely has a mind — beliefs, memories, something like intentions — while no individual ant has any of these things. I have no academic background; I got interested after reading the dialogue in Gödel, Escher, Bach where an anteater describes his friendship with a conscious colony called Aunt Hillary, and after something I saw myself: the ants in my kitchen found the sugar jar again, along the exact same route, months after I had moved it, even though none of the ants that first discovered the jar could possibly have still been alive. Please draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond asking him to restate the view. At least one should press him on where a colony's memory could be stored when its neurons, so to speak, keep dying and being replaced — is anything carried over besides the pattern of traffic itself? At least one should ask what observation, in principle, would count as evidence against colony-level minds rather than merely for smarter ants. And at least one should take up the strangest part of the Hofstadter dialogue: the anteater eats individual ants while remaining the colony's devoted friend, so I want a question about whether a whole can flourish while its parts are regularly consumed — and whether the guest thinks such a mind could care about its own ants, or only about the anthill. For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give.
