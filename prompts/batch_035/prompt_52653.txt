I teach a seminar on philosophy of history, and this fall I'm building a unit on periodization — the question of whether things like "the Middle Ages," "the Renaissance," and "the Scientific Revolution" name real stretches of the past with real essences, or whether they're retrospective constructions that historians keep having to apologize for. We'll do the standard material: Petrarch more or less inventing the "dark age" so he could stand at the dawn of a new one, Haskins finding a renaissance in the twelfth century (1927, before you ask), and Shapin, whose book on the Scientific Revolution famously opens with "There was no such thing as the Scientific Revolution, and this is a book about it." You get the vibe.

For the final session I want to do something a bit weird, and I'd love your help thinking it through: Mark Twain's A Connecticut Yankee in King Arthur's Court, read as a primary source in the history of the *idea* of the Middle Ages. Hank Morgan, a factory superintendent from 1880s Connecticut, gets conked on the head and wakes up in sixth-century Britain, which he treats exactly the way a Gilded Age foreman would treat a badly run plant: as a backlog of ignorance, superstition, and inefficiency to be cleared. The famous scenes — the eclipse he "predicts," the knights wandering around with sandwich-board advertisements, the tournament, the Church's interdict, the finale where a handful of boys behind electric fences and Gatling guns annihilate the assembled chivalry of England.

Here's what I want a reading of. On the one hand, the novel is a perfect specimen of the "Middle Ages as the negative image of modernity" construction: medieval means ignorant, priest-ridden, romantic, and the plot exists to show that modernity (science, engineering, newspapers, marketing) is simply *better*. On the other hand, the "modernity" Hank carries is itself extremely periodized — patents, wage labor, factories, telephones, mass circulation newspapers. It's not a neutral standard of rationality; it's Manchester capitalism plus the American self-image of the 1880s. So maybe the novel accidentally demonstrates the constructivist point: "medieval" is less a description of the sixth century than a label for whatever the 1880s wanted to feel superior to, and "modern" is whatever flattered the 1880s about itself. And there's a third layer, which is that Twain is really satirizing the romantic medievalism of his own century — Sandy and the tournaments are digs at Walter Scott, whom Twain elsewhere half-jokingly blamed for the Civil War — so you have one nineteenth-century construction of the Middle Ages fighting another one, with the actual Middle Ages nowhere in sight.

So: does the novel dramatize the period-skeptic's thesis better than the arguments do, or does it resist that reading? I want you to take seriously the places where it resists. Is there anything Hank actually encounters that reads as genuinely period-specific rather than projected — Catholic church authority, hereditary hierarchy, personal violence as a dispute-resolution mechanism? If someone in class pushes back and says "the medieval/modern contrast the novel draws is real, look at what actually changes between the sixth and nineteenth centuries," what's the honest periodization-skeptic reply, and what does the skeptic have to concede? And conversely, does the ending — where Hank wins every battle and still loses, poisoned by the rotting dead, longing for Sandy and the "soul" of Camelot in the frame narrative — complicate the idea that the novel is confident about where the period break falls?

I'd like this usable in seminar: give me the reading, plus two or three discussion questions I could actually hand out, and flag the scenes I should tell them to reread closely. Keep it honest — if the constructivist reading flattens the book somewhere, say so, because a student of mine will find the spot where it does.
