I'm drafting a paper on suffering-focused ethics and, before I engage any particular theory, I want to fix the standard of adequacy first — otherwise every objection I write will just smuggle in my own prejudices about what counts as a failure. Please respond as a professional philosopher working in population ethics and normative theory: think carefully, brainstorm candidate requirements before committing to a list, and then deliver the specification.

The target family is broad: negative utilitarianism in its strict and moderated forms, lexical or threshold priority views on suffering, suffering-focused consequentialism, and the asymmetric views in population ethics that treat suffering and happiness unequally. The deliverable is the list of desiderata any adequate theory in this family must meet — with the motivating case behind each requirement. To get you started, here are the pressures I already suspect a specification must register, though I don't know which of these are genuine requirements and which are artifacts of how the debate has been run:

- The asymmetry that started it all: declining to create a happy life seems permissible, creating a miserable life seems wrong, and a theory should either vindicate that asymmetry or explain it away rather than stipulate it. But a strict reading quickly generates trouble.
- The world-destruction problem: if only suffering counts disapprovingly, a painless universal annihilation looks like an improvement — or even a duty — and most people regard that as a reductio. Is it a non-negotiable desideratum to avoid this conclusion, or is that our naive positive-welfarism talking?
- The pinprick problem: on some lexical versions, eliminating a single pinprick outweighs any amount of happiness. A theory must either endorse that with a straight face, block it with a principled threshold, or explain why the threshold isn't ad hoc.
- Ordinary trade-offs: we take a child to be vaccinated, climb mountains, run marathons. A theory that forbids any imposition of suffering for the sake of later joy is a non-starter; one that permits all of it has said nothing. The desideratum has to mark where and why.
- Camouflage failure: a suffering-focused view must differ in kind from classical utilitarianism with the zero point shifted, or from critical-level views with a baseline at the neutral point — otherwise the distinctive label is decoration.
- Population axiology: consistency with, or a principled stance on, the repugnant and sadistic conclusions and the non-identity problems that arise once future people enter.
- Decision guidance under uncertainty: a theory that weights bad outcomes must say something sane about tiny probabilities of astronomically bad ones, without collapsing into either paralysis or fanaticism.

For each requirement: state it precisely, give the case that motivates it, and mark it as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp within the family. Then do the three things I actually care most about: flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied; say which single requirement does the most to exclude otherwise-live candidates; and say whether, if some subset really is jointly unsatisfiable, that fact is itself the headline result — the family being defined by demands no theory can meet at once. Skip scoring the existing theories against the list; I'll do that in the paper.

One last thing, since I'll be building on your answer: as you go, indicate how confident you are in each major claim, and at the end single out the one point you are least sure of — especially whether any requirement you've marked non-negotiable is a genuine adequacy condition or just the consensus of a particular moment in the debate. I'd rather know where the ground is soft now than find out from a referee later.
