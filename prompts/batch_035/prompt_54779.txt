I'm taking a philosophy of mind course and we're in the emotions unit right now. My professor has this annoying (kind of great) habit: she won't tell us which theory she actually holds until the last lecture, but she'll happily rule on any case we bring her — just a yes/no on whether some state counts as a genuine emotion. Her official menu is that emotions are either evaluative judgments, or perceptions of value, or feelings of bodily change. A few of us have been keeping a log of her rulings, planning to reverse-engineer her theory before the reveal, and next week she's judging three new cases, so naturally there's a betting pool. The log so far: (a) someone terrified of a dog they fully know is harmless, who admits their fear is irrational — she ruled genuine emotion; (b) someone who took too much cold medicine and got a free-floating sense of impending doom, with no opinion whatsoever about anything actually being dangerous — not an emotion, "just a body state," she said; (c) a climber on beta blockers who feels zero bodily disturbance on an exposed ledge but registers the danger and gets off fast — genuine emotion; (d) someone crying over a novel, who knows full well nobody real is suffering — genuine emotion again.

The three new cases for next week: Case A, the detached sentry — a guy whose perception is working perfectly, he sees the rockslide coming, can calmly report it's dangerous, normal adrenaline and everything, but because of some lesion he feels absolutely no urge to move and just watches with interest (perception plus arousal minus any concern). Is his state fear? Case B, the hypnotized subject — under hypnosis he genuinely believes the rubber snake is deadly, but he's totally calm, no racing heart, no urge to run, just the bare belief sitting there doing nothing (judgment minus perception, feeling, and motivation). Emotion or not? Case C, the anticipatory one — someone lying perfectly safe at home who works themselves into real terror just thinking about a surgery scheduled next month. She dropped a hint that this one is "easier than we think," whatever that means.

Can you help me prep for the pool? Predict her verdict on A, B, and C; tell me which rules actually fit her track record (the trichotomy may not be the whole story — the bodily-feeling view looks definately dead after (b) and (c), the judgment view looks pretty stretched after (a) and (d), and I suspect there's at least one rule outside the official menu that fits everything she's said); say what each live rule predicts on the new cases; give your best guess at what she's running, with reasons; and finally invent one further case that would cleanly split the two best-fitting rules. Please put it all in a single JSON object with these fields: "predictions" — an array with one object per case, each having "case" (A, B, or C), "verdict" ("emotion", "not an emotion", or "torn"), "confidence" ("high", "medium", or "low"), and "why" (a sentence or two); "candidate_rules" — an array of objects, each with "name", "statement" (one line saying what emotions are, on that rule), "fit" (how it explains her four rulings so far, including any strain), and "predictions" (an object with the keys A, B, C and the verdict each rule gives); "best_guess" — an object with "rule", "why", and "confidence"; and "tiebreaker" — an object with "case" (your invented scenario, a couple sentences), "rule1_says" and "rule2_says" (the verdicts of the top two rules on it). Don't feel locked into her trichotomy if something outside it fits better — that's kind of the whole point of the exercise.
