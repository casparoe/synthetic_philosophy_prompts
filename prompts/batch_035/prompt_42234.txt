been reading a lot of Keynes lately, both the Treatise on Probability and his economics writing, and somewhere along the way I seem to have collected a bunch of views that might not actually fit together. want to sanity check the whole package before I embarrass myself at my reading group. here's what I've signed up for:

(1) Probability is basically a kind of logic. the probability of a hypothesis given some evidence is an objective relation between them, like entailment but weaker. so if two people share the same evidence and get different probabilities, at least one of them is just wrong — it's not a matter of taste.

(2) Keynes's later point about the big economic questions is right: where will the economy be in twenty years, will there be a war, etc. — for stuff like that there just isn't a probability to be found. no relation, no number, and pretending to calculate one is basically a sophisticated way of fooling yourself.

(3) I'm pretty persuaded by Dutch book arguments though. if your degrees of belief don't obey the probability axioms, a bookie can take you for a sure loss, so rational agents should have sharp credences over everything, even weird far-future stuff.

(4) I buy Keynes's weight of argument distinction as a description — two evidence sets can give the same probability but one rests on way more data — but I don't think weight should do any work in rational decision making. once you've got the probability, act on it; caring about how much evidence backs it is just psychology.

(5) the whole Knight risk vs uncertainty thing seems overblown. what he calls uncertainty is just risk with very thin evidence, there's no genuine third category, and I also think Ellsberg-style ambiguity aversion (paying to avoid bets where the probabilities are unknown) is basically irrational sentimentality.

so — does this hang together at all, or have I assembled a frankenstein? which pairs are in genuine tension and which only look like it? I can kinda see (1) and (3) clashing but maybe objective Bayesians get to have both? and (2) feels like it must be fighting somebody here but I honestly can't tell who. for each real tension, how bad is it and what's the cheapest way to fix it, and at the end tell me which single revision would clean up the most mess. also I'd want to know what the fixed-up package then says about a case like: should a policymaker in 1930 put a probability on "a major war within a decade" — yes, no, or it depends on something I haven't listed?
