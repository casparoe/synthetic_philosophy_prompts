I'm a novelist working on a secondary character — a self-made architect, as it happens — who espouses Objectivism and deploys it at dinner parties to the discomfort of the other guests. I want her to be formidable rather than a caricature, so I need to know how far I can lean on the popular picture of Rand's ethics before it becomes a strawman. The popular biography I read as background summarizes her view of altruism roughly as follows: 'Rand's central claim was that selfishness is a virtue and altruism a vice. Since altruism means any concern for the welfare of others, she held that charity, compassion, and helping the needy are all moral failings — a doctrine she said had been invented by religion and by collectivism to enslave the able. Only the pursuit of one's own happiness is moral, and on this view one man's good never requires another's sacrifice. In practice this meant admiring businessmen without qualification and despising the weak, which is why critics have always read her ethics as a hymn to greed.' Something about this strikes me as too tidy, but I can't locate the seam. Go through it claim by claim: which parts would a careful Objectivist accept, which are subtle misrepresentations, and where does the summary trade on an ambiguity Rand herself was at pains to police — for instance, between altruism as a moral doctrine and simply helping people, or between selfishness in her technical sense and in the ordinary one? I'd also like you to distinguish what Rand actually asserted from what the summary infers from her assertions, especially the concluding slide from her ethics to despising the weak. Finally, the practical question I most need answered: if I arm my character with the corrected doctrine rather than the summarized one, do I lose the drama — or does the accurate version, shorn of the easy target, make her if anything harder to dismiss?
