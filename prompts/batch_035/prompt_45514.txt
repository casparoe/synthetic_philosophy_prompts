I'd like you to build a Frankfurt-style case to unusual specifications: the agent must be praiseworthy, not blameworthy, for the action she couldn't have avoided.

Some background on the difficulty, so the constraints make sense. Frankfurt cases are supposed to show that moral responsibility doesn't require alternative possibilities: the intervener would have ensured the outcome had the agent been inclined otherwise, but never has to act, so the agent acts on her own reasons while lacking any alternative. The standard cases involve wrongdoing, and many suspect the strategy doesn't generalize: blameworthiness can look like an agent's own self-directed failure, but praiseworthy action often seems to involve credit for going beyond what was ensured, which an intervener standing ready might undercut. Your case must make the lack of alternatives compatible with genuine praiseworthiness.

Specifications:

1. The scenario must be realistic and mundane — a workplace, a family, a community setting — no neuroscientific gadgets, no guaranteed prediction machines. The intervener must be an ordinary human who would have intervened through ordinary means (persuasion, authority, a practical arrangement) had the agent wavered, and who never acts.
2. The agent must act for the right reasons, on her own motivation, and her doing so must be something a reasonable observer would call praiseworthy rather than merely correct or obligatory. The intervener's readiness must not itself be part of the agent's situation in any way that explains her action — no deterrent effect, and she never knows about it.
3. Confront the dilemma defense head-on: the PAP defender argues that either there is a reliable prior sign of the agent's choice (in which case a flicker of alternative possibilities remains, since the agent could have shown the other sign), or there is none (in which case the intervener couldn't know when to act). Say explicitly which side of the dilemma your case is on and why that costs the least.

After the scenario — which should be no longer than one paragraph — do the following, in this order: (a) state what the Frankfurt-style reasoning yields, deriving the verdict that the agent is praiseworthy despite lacking alternatives from the case's features rather than stipulating it; (b) state the strongest response available to a defender of the principle of alternative possibilities, choosing between the flickers-of-freedom line and the dilemma defense, and explaining which feature of your case it targets; (c) identify which single feature of the case is doing the most work, and what happens to the verdicts if it is removed; and (d) give your own judgment, with a one-sentence reason, on whether the case succeeds against the principle.

The whole response must be at most 600 words. A case that stipulates the praiseworthiness rather than generating it from the described facts fails, so make sure the facts on the page support the credit you claim for the agent.
