I'm putting together a short position piece on trust and moral knowledge for a graduate seminar, and before I circulate it I'd like a proper consistency audit, because I have a nagging suspicion I'm holding views that don't fit together. The topic is moral testimony — whether and when it's okay to form moral beliefs on the say-so of others. Here is my package of commitments, stated as plainly as I can manage:

(1) I'm a testimonial anti-reductionist in general epistemology: absent specific defeaters, believing what a sincere, competent speaker tells you is epistemically permissible, and testimony functions as a basic source of justification rather than something that always needs to be backed by independent evidence of reliability. (2) I'm a moral realist: there are moral facts that don't depend on our attitudes. (3) I'm a moral testimony pessimist: for a mature, epistemically responsible adult, forming a moral belief purely on someone else's say-so is normally defective — not always wrong, but defective — because while testimony can transmit moral knowledge, it cannot transmit moral understanding, and understanding is what we should be after in the moral case. (4) I'd give two rationales for that pessimism if pressed: the understanding-based one just stated, and also a reliability one, namely that unlike scientists, would-be moral experts lack the kind of track record that makes deference sensible. (5) I think deference to scientific experts is not merely permissible but often epistemically required — I defer to climatologists about climate, and I think I'm right to, even though their testimony obviously doesn't hand me scientific understanding. (6) I think there are cases where accepting someone's testimony about their own experience of degradation or injustice is not just permitted but obligatory — where declining to believe them, or demanding they first prove it to me, is itself an epistemic failing, even though that testimony standardly carries moral claims with it (that a practice is degrading, that an institution is unjust). (7) I see no problem at all with moral education proceeding through testimony and example — children come to believe that hitting is wrong, that they shouldn't lie, largely on the basis of what they're told, and I'd never call that epistemically defective.

What I'd like from you is a careful audit rather than a survey of the literature. Go through the pairs and triples and tell me: which of these are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and how severe each genuine tension is? For each one, say what the cheapest available repair would be and what it costs elsewhere in the package. A few things I specifically want you to address: does my general anti-reductionism (1) leave any principled room for the moral exception in (3), or am I just being inconsistent there? Can both of my rationales in (4) survive alongside (5), given that the understanding-based rationale seems like it should apply to scientific testimony too? And is there a defensible line between the deference I condemn in (3) and the deference I make obligatory in (6), or does (6) quietly swallow (3)? Finally, if you had to recommend one single revision that would clean up the most problems at the least cost, which would it be — and what would I lose by making it? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and don't pull your punches about which of my commitments is doing the most damage.
