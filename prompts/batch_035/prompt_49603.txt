I coordinate a small working group on AI welfare, and the recurring argument about whether reinforcement-learning agents can suffer has stalled in a way I want diagnosed rather than adjudicated. The ML people on the group point out that in a standard RL setup the reward is just a scalar that nudges weights: there is no persisting valenced state over time, nothing the system 'wants' in a counterfactual sense, and calling negative reward 'suffering' looks like a pun on the word. The welfare researchers reply that if valence is anything at all, it is a signal that biases learning away from certain states, and that our own affective system was built by evolution on just such a signal — so either that functional story of valence is wrong, or RL agents are at least candidates for suffering, and the ML side is just refusing to run the inference they accept everywhere else. Both sides accept the same facts about the architecture, and nobody has moved in a year. My hypothesis is that the disagreement is verbal: 'suffering' is being used phenomenally by one side (there being something it is like) and functionally by the other (there being a certain kind of learning signal), so each is answering a different question under one word. But I'm unsure, because the phenomenal side may be making a real substantive claim — that no system lacking some further feature (integrated state over time, a self-model, a capacity for the signal to linger rather than vanish at the next gradient step) could realize valence at all — and if that claim is substantive, the debate isn't merely verbal. Diagnose the disagreement for me: verbal, substantive, or two questions under one heading. Ground the diagnosis in what each side is actually committed to, not in a survey of the field. Then say what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a conceptual distinction, an architectural development, an agreed test — and be honest about whether the debate in its current form is productive or just a ritual restatement of prior commitments about which systems matter.
