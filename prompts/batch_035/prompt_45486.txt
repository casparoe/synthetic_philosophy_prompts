I'm putting together a philosophy of mind unit on functionalism and multiple realizability, and instead of another round of the standard thought experiments, I want to pair the theory with a work of fiction: Ted Chiang's novella The Lifecycle of Software Objects (2010). In it, a company called Blue Gamma sells "digients" — digital entities raised by human trainers in the shared virtual world of Data Earth — and the story follows Ana, a former zookeeper, and Derek, one of the digients' designers, over more than a decade as they rear digients like Ana's Jax into something like persons. On its face the novella is a gift to functionalism: minds defined by what they do rather than what they are made of, realized in silicon and virtual bodies as readily as in neurons. And the story keeps staging versions of the multiple realizability intuition — digients migrate to new platforms and, in one scene, borrow robot bodies in the physical world, at which point Ana reflects that Jax's code is running on the network and the robot is merely an attached peripheral. What survives these transfers, if functionalism is right?

But the more I reread it, the more the novella seems to push back. The digients cannot be instantiated with an adult functional profile straight away; they have to be raised, in real time, through years of play, schooling, mistakes, and social entanglement, and their minds are shaped by the particular bodies and physics they developed in. Ana and the other owners also resist treating digients as copyable or restorable files — a backup or rollback seems to them to destroy someone rather than preserve him — which suggests that what matters is not an abstract causal organization but a specific developmental history. So I'd like a reading that takes both sides seriously: does the novella dramatize multiple realizability, or does it complicate it, perhaps by showing that functional roles are not specifiable in advance and can only be acquired through a contingent embedding in a realizing medium and environment? Where does the story genuinely vindicate the functionalist, and where does it resist the reading — and where would an honest critic have to concede that the novella is trading on details of its fictional technology rather than on anything the philosophy itself settles?

For context: my students will already have covered the basic machinery — functional states defined by their causal roles, Putnam's machine functionalism, the arguments for and against multiple realizability, and the usual objections like the China brain and the charge that functionalism is too liberal or too chauvinistic. So you can assume the theory. What I want is criticism rather than exegesis: a claim about what this particular work does to the debate, which scenes carry the philosophical weight (the platform migrations, the robot peripheral, the disagreements about how the digients may be modified or monetized), and — most importantly — whether the novella adds anything to the functionalism debate that the standard counterexamples don't. If your conclusion is that the story is ultimately neutral, or that it quietly sides with embodiment and developmental systems against functionalist orthodoxy, fine; just make the case from the text's plot and choices rather than from what a story about artificial minds is presumed to be about.
