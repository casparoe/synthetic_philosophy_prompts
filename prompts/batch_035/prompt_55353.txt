I'm a final-year philosophy undergraduate writing a dissertation chapter on whether perceptual experience can justify belief, and my supervisor produced an argument at our last meeting whose conclusion I think must be false, even though each premise looked fine as she stated it. I have to adjudicate it in the chapter, and I can't currently see my way out. Here is the argument, reconstructed into numbered steps as faithfully as I can manage (she spoke freely, so the wording is mine, not hers):

(1) If your background beliefs, expectations, and learned categories partly determine the content of your visual experience — so that what you see owes as much to what you already think as to what is in front of you — then your experience is biased in the relevant sense and cannot serve as an independent reason for belief; its evidential force should be discounted accordingly.
(2) Learned categories do partly determine the content of visual experience. The novice looking at a chest radiograph sees a confusing grey fog; the trained radiologist sees a mass, a fracture, a device in the wrong place — and the difference is not one of judgement arrived at after looking, but of what the image experientially presents, which is why the radiologist can still be surprised by what she sees.
(3) So trained perception is biased in the relevant sense, and its evidential force should be discounted.
(4) The better trained the observer, the greater the discount; a fortiori, where a trained and an untrained observer disagree about what is there, we should, other things equal, favour the untrained report.
(5) But in every domain we do the reverse — and the studies of trained observers that persuaded us of (2) were themselves collected and interpreted by trained observers, so (4) applies to them too, and the argument saws off the branch it is sitting on.
(6) Therefore either trained perception is less rational than untrained perception, or (1) is false.

I cannot accept the first alternative in (6). But when I try to reject (1), my supervisor asks whether I would say the same about the anxious patient who is convinced the mole on her shoulder is cancerous, looks at it, and experiences it as obviously malignant: if belief-shaped content does not discount the experience, on what ground does the patient's experience justify her belief any less than a dermatologist's? And when I try to reject (2), she replies that surviving surprise is supposed to be evidence of penetration precisely because effects of post-perceptual judgement are not supposed to survive it.

Please answer in exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: the first point should name the single step you reject and give your diagnosis of what goes wrong in it; the second should explain why that step looked fine — what in it is true under one reading and false under the reading the argument needs; the third should state what your diagnosis costs elsewhere in epistemology, namely which principle you have had to qualify or abandon and where else it will be missed; the fourth should deal with the mole case — say whether, on your diagnosis, the anxious patient's experience justifies her belief, and if not, why the case does not push you back towards (1); the fifth should state in one sentence whether my supervisor is nonetheless onto something that the rejected step conceals.

Commit to one diagnosis throughout and defend it; do not set out the available positions and leave the choice to me. If you think the argument is valid and the real question is which premise is cheapest to give up, say so, and then give up the cheapest one anyway. Please write in British English.
