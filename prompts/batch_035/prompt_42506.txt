I'm working through Adam Smith's *Theory of Moral Sentiments* and have hit a stretch in Part IV (the chapter on the beauty that the appearance of utility confers on the productions of art) that I can't reconcile with the sympathy machinery set up in Part I. Here are the two paragraphs I'm stuck on:

"But that this fitness, this happy contrivance of any production of art, should often be more valued, than the very end for which it was intended; and that the exact adjustment of the means for attaining any conveniency or pleasure, should frequently be more regarded, than that very conveniency or pleasure, in the attainment of which their whole merit would seem to consist, has not, so far as I know, been yet taken notice of by any body."

"The poor man's son, whom heaven in its anger has visited with ambition, when he begins to look around him, admires the condition of the rich. He finds the cottage of his father too small for his accommodation, and fancies he should be lodged more at his ease in a palace. He is displeased with being obliged to walk a-foot, or to endure the fatigue of riding on horseback. He sees his superiors carried about in machines, and imagines that in one of these he could travel with less inconveniency. He feels himself naturally indolent, and willing to serve himself with his own hands as little as possible; and judges, that a numerous retinue of servants would save him from a great deal of trouble."

I'd like you to help me understand this, thinking as a scholar of eighteenth-century moral philosophy. Please first unpack precisely what Smith is claiming, then address three specific puzzles. (1) What is the mechanism supposed to be? It looks like Smith is saying the spectator's pleasure in the aptness of the means gets transferred to, or confused with, the agent's own interest in his wellbeing — but it's unclear to me whether the deception operates on the poor man's son himself or on the spectators whose sympathy he is chasing. (2) How does the passage relate to the unnamed "ingenious and agreeable philosopher" praised earlier in the chapter (Hume, I take it, on utility)? Is Smith extending Hume's observation or quietly correcting it? (3) Later in the same chapter, after this excerpt, Smith suggests that nature saddles us with this illusion about the happiness of the rich precisely so that we will toil, and thereby populate and improve the world. I can't decide whether to read that as sly irony or as a serious teleological claim; which reading is better supported by how Smith uses the impartial spectator elsewhere in the book? Please reason carefully through the plausible interpretations before settling on one, and don't simply assert a conclusion — reconstruct the argument.
