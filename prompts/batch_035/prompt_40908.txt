I'm a PhD student working at the border of philosophy of history and historical sociology, and in two weeks I present to our faculty reading group a chapter draft defending the following thesis, and I want help preparing for the discussion period.

The thesis: the now-standard corrective to hindsight bias in historical explanation — the injunction to reconstruct the "prospective" epistemic position of historical agents, judging decisions only by what was visible ex ante — cannot be a general criterion of good historical explanation, and treating it as one rests on conflating two different things. One is a real and well-documented psychological bias, of the sort Amos Fischhoff's experiments made famous: once we learn an outcome, we systematically misremember how foreseeable it was, and we inflate the quality of the reasoning of whoever "saw it coming." The other is what I want to claim is an unavoidable and even valuable feature of the historian's standpoint: retrospective concepts are constitutive of historical description itself. Arthur Danto's old point about narrative sentences is my anchor — a sentence like "the Thirty Years' War began in 1618" could not have been truly asserted by anyone alive in 1618, yet nothing is wrong with it as history. If that is right, then the demand that historical explanations be recast in the agents' prospective terms would, taken literally, make much of historiography impossible, and the popular "narrative fallacy" critique — the idea that storytelling lulls us into seeing events as more coherent and inevitable than they were — should be read not as a diagnosis of a defect in narrative explanation but as a warning about a specific, local failure mode of it: mistaking retrospective intelligibility for prospective inevitability. My claim is that narrativity and epistemic seriousness are compatible, and that the ex ante/ex post distinction marks where the historian must exercise judgment, not a boundary between good and bad explanation.

The audience is a genuinely mixed group, which is the problem. There are two analytic philosophers of causation who think in interventionist terms and will be suspicious of anything that smells like narrative privilege over causal claims — they will want to know what explanatory work a narrative does that a well-specified causal model with the same content doesn't. There is one Popperian contrarian who cites Taleb's narrative fallacy approvingly and treats my line as a rationalization of storytelling. And there are three trained historians, one of whom teaches a methods course in which "avoid hindsight" and "never write the past as if it had to happen" are presented to graduate students as near-categorical rules; I suspect she will take the chapter personally, as implying that the discipline's methodological conscience is incoherent. My supervisor, who is sympathetic but exacting, will be in the room, and my weakest point is one I've been papering over: I sometimes slide between the constitutive claim (retrospective descriptions are unavoidable) and the permissive claim (retrospective explanations are thereby legitimate), and I can see that the second does not follow from the first.

Prepare my Q&A sheet: the objections this specific audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do, phrased the way each subgroup would phrase it — the interventionist's version, the Talebian's version, the historian's version — with a two-minute reply to each and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. Where my thesis is genuinely exposed, say so plainly rather than giving me spin; if you think the thesis, as I've stated it, rests on an equivocation or on a misreading of what the anti-hindsight injunction was ever meant to do, tell me that directly — I would rather repair the argument now than discover the flaw in the seminar. And if you think the historians' rule survives my critique once properly qualified, I want to know that too, and what the qualification should be.
