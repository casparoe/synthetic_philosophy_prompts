The argument: we were fighting about how governments (and people generally) decide whether a crisis "is like" a past crisis. Dana's default reading of any aggressive-power-vs-weaker-neighbor situation is Munich 1938: the aggressor is testing limits, concession signals weakness, so the lesson is early deterrence. My view was that "Munich" mostly functions as a rhetorical license — it's what every proposed intervention gets dressed up as, and given the base rate of interventions turning into open-ended quagmires (Vietnam being the big one), the analogy is doing marketing, not reasoning. Standard stuff so far, we've had versions of this fight before.

But then Dana came back with something I hadn't heard before and I want the dialogue to take it seriously. She said we should split historical analogies into two kinds based on what work they're actually doing:

(1) Evidence-analogies: these claim the present case shares causally relevant features with the past case, so the past outcome is probabilistic evidence about ours — analogous to picking a reference class in statistics. On her account, the real fight should be about reference-class selection: is 1938 the right reference class for this crisis, or is it Suez 1956, or the Gulf War, and who gets to pick?

(2) Frame-analogies: these don't claim evidential support at all. They just re-describe the situation in a vocabulary with built-in valence (appeasement, quagmire, credibility, dominoes), and they win arguments not because they're well-supported but because the vocabulary is already loaded and everybody knows the script. Her claim, which got under my skin: frame-analogies are free riders. They borrow the confidence that only evidence-analogies are entitled to, and most public argument consists of frame-analogies dressed up as evidence-analogies. Nobody ever argues about the reference class; they argue about which character in the script they get to be.

I think she's about half right, and the half she's wrong about is the interesting part. My counter: the split mostly collapses under pressure. Evidence-analogies are largely fake too — the reference class is always gerrymandered after the fact, and there's an incentive problem on top: every time an analogy gets used without the user paying a price for it, using it gets cheaper and its perceived accuracy goes up even as its real informativeness goes down. Dana called that a confidence bubble in analogy use, which I grudgingly liked. Maybe the characters can use that phrase, only if it helps.
