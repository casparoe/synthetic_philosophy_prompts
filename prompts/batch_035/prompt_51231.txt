In my seminar on language and social norms we have been reading workplace speech codes alongside the content policies of large social-media platforms, and I have noticed they share a three-part structure. First, a list of designated words is prohibited outright, regardless of whether the speaker intends anything derogatory by them. Second, there are exemptions for quotation, testimony, and classroom discussion — the word in inverted commas, or in the mouth of a reporter, is not the same violation. Third, use of the same words by members of the group they target is either permitted outright or placed in a different, milder category. What semantic theory of slurs does this structure presuppose? If the derogatory content belongs to the word's standing meaning, invariant across speakers, then the third clause licenses exactly the speech the first prohibits; if content instead varies with the speaker's identity, the code seems to presuppose something like an indexical or perspectival semantics — yet then the quotation exemption is puzzling, since on such a view it is the speaker's voice, not the mention/use boundary, that should carry the weight. Might the practice track something other than meaning — expected harm, or standing to derogate — and could a purely non-semantic theory explain why the codes are written as word-lists in the first place? Please reconstruct the implicit theory the three clauses presuppose, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely tolerates, and say which clause ought to give way if the presupposition proves untenable.
