Been arguing with my brother about what we owe real dead people when movies dramatize them, and I've started keeping a list of my own gut verdicts to see if they hang together. Shakespeare turning Richard III into a child-murdering hunchback: fine, no notes. Amadeus inventing that Salieri killed Mozart: fine, I love that movie. A war movie "based on true events" folding three real soldiers into one invented composite character: fine. But a biopic that invents an affair for an actress who is still alive: not fine. The Crown writing invented screaming matches between royals who are alive and will definately watch it: not fine. A docudrama about a long-dead journalist that shows him fabricating quotes he never fabricated, shot documentary-style so you'd believe it: not fine. And a movie that shuffles a dead musician's timeline around so his career peak lands right before his diagnosis for a better third act: fine honestly, though I can't explain why that one gets a pass and the docudrama doesn't. My brother says the principle is just living vs dead, but that can't be right, since the journalist's been dead a century and I still say not fine. So: is there a principle here? Maybe something about whether audiences walk away with false beliefs they'll actually act on, or about whether anyone is left to be hurt? Give me a principle (or two candidates) that fits most of these, tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to drop, and tell me what it says about the case I keep flip-flopping on: a biopic about a woman dead twenty years, whose adult kids are alive, that invents a secret she kept from them her whole life. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble and no restating my question.
