my roommate has gone full Benatar lately and last night he tried to convert me with an argument that ends somewhere I really don't want to go. it started as the standard asymmetry thing, which I actually half buy: when you compare someone's existence with their never existing, (1) the pains in their life are bad, (2) the absence of those pains, had they never existed, is good even though nobody is around to enjoy that goodness, (3) the pleasures in their life are good, but (4) the absence of those pleasures is not bad, because there's no person who is thereby deprived. so never existing beats existing whenever life contains any pain at all, which is always. fine, says I, grim but coherent, and not obviously crazy given that we all accept it's wrong to create a life of misery while nobody thinks we're obligated to create a happy one.

then he went further. (5) he said the real engine of the asymmetry is a single principle: a benefit requires a beneficiary, but an avoided harm doesn't -- a harm avoided is good even if there is no one for whom it is good, while a benefit foregone is bad only if someone is deprived of it. (6) now take my grandmother: 89, happy, mildly arthritic, loves her garden. compare her future if she keeps living (some pleasures, some pains) with her future if she were to die painlessly in her sleep tonight (no further pleasures, no further pains). (7) he claims this is structurally the same comparison as (1)-(4): pains present versus absent, pleasures present versus absent, and in the death scenario, just like in the never-born scenario, there's no one in that future being deprived of anything -- she's not in it. (8) so by the asymmetry, painless death tonight is better for her than continued life. (9) and if that holds for her it holds for everyone at every moment, which is the pro-mortalism conclusion that even Benatar himself insists his view doesn't have to accept.

I got as far as saying "the cases are different because grandma exists now, so her death deprives HER, whereas the never-born child was never anyone to deprive" and he came back with: deprived of what? the future pleasures -- but you just granted in (4) that absent pleasures aren't bad without a subject, and in the world where she dies tonight there is no subject either, at least not after tonight. if absent pleasures can be bad for the grandma who existed right up until the death, why can't they be bad for the possible child, whose non-existence also just consists of the world not containing them? and honestly I stalled out. I'd like to keep the asymmetry itself if I can, since its the only explanation I know of for the misery/happiness intuition in the first paragraph, while flatly denying that a painless death benefits a happy person. where exactly does his chain break, and what does breaking it there cost me? is it (7), the claim that the two comparisons are structurally identical, or is the problem already sitting in (5), the way he compressed the asymmetry into that one principle about beneficiaries? and if its (5), can the asymmetry survive without the principle, or does it turn out the asymmetry was never doing the work I thought it was?
