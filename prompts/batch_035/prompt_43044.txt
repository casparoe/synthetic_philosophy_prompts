You are a philosopher of science with expertise in the metaphysics of interlevel relations in complex systems, and I would like you to write a careful comparative analysis. Before answering, take a moment to brainstorm the key possible lines of comparison and say briefly which ones you take to matter most; then develop the ones that do.

The two accounts to compare are Mark Bedau's account of weak emergence and Erik Hoel's account of causal emergence. By Bedau's account, developed in the 1990s philosophy of complexity literature, a macrostate of a system is weakly emergent just in case it is derivable from the system's microdynamics and initial conditions, but only by brute simulation: there is, as a practical matter, no shortcut that lets you predict it without step-by-step iteration of the micro-level dynamics. On Hoel's account, developed in the 2010s at the intersection of theoretical neuroscience and information theory, a system exhibits causal emergence when a coarse-grained description of it — grouping micro-elements into macro-elements and micro-states into macro-states — has greater effective information than the fine-grained description, where effective information measures, roughly, how much the description of a system's present state constrains its possible futures, assessed by intervening on the present state over its full support. Causal emergence then comes in two flavors: the coarse-graining can either wash out noise (deterministic causal emergence, where the macro is more predictable than the micro) or broaden power (degenerative causal emergence, where the macro can do more things than the micro can be seen to do).

A colleague of mine claims that Hoel's framework is, at bottom, a relabeling of Bedau's, because in both cases the macro-level advantage is always relative to some practical limit: in Bedau's case the limit is on derivations, and in Hoel's case the limit is on how much irrelevant micro-detail we are willing to carry around. On her reading, both accounts are epistemic rather than ontological: neither shows that the macro level is doing anything the micro level could not in principle do, and Hoel has merely replaced an inference-limit with a measurement-convenience. I want you to adjudicate this dispute.

Specifically, I would like you to address the following questions:

1. Is the colleague's equivalence claim right? Consider, for instance, whether a system could be weakly emergent in Bedau's sense without exhibiting causal emergence in Hoel's sense (or vice versa). It would help to construct a concrete toy example in each direction — say, a cellular automaton whose macro-behavior cannot be predicted except by simulation, versus a system with a small number of micro-elements whose coarse-graining trivially increases effective information. Does the fact that Hoel's measure is defined over the full support of the interventional distribution — so that counterfactuals that would never actually occur still count — drive a real wedge between the two accounts?

2. One standard worry about Bedau's account is that it makes emergence hostage to the state of our computational and mathematical technology: a macro-property is emergent until someone finds a shortcut, at which point it stops being emergent. Does Hoel's account inherit an analogous dependence on the choice of coarse-graining map? That is, when we search over possible coarse-grainings and pick the one that maximizes effective information, are we discovering structure in the system, or imposing it? And is the answer different in kind from the way Bedau's account depends on what our best mathematicians have managed to derive?

3. Does either account succeed in being more than epistemic? Bedau himself advertises weak emergence as compatible with, indeed a consequence of, micro-physicalism; he is explicit that nothing metaphysically novel appears at the macro level. Hoel, by contrast, has suggested that the level with the greatest effective information is the one that most fully exists, or at least the one at which nature can be said to act. Take a position on whether this stronger reading of causal emergence is defensible, or whether the notion of existence-by-effective-information collapses under pressure into a claim about which level of description is most useful for a certain kind of intervention.

4. Finally, consider a hybrid objection that cuts across both frameworks: both accounts can be accused of rewarding the wrong thing. On Bedau's account, mere computational intractability is treated as a mark of emergence, so a system whose macro-behavior is unpredictable simply because it is an incompressible mess of noise risks counting as emergent. On Hoel's account, the analogous worry is that effective information can be maximized by coarse-grainings that simply average away noise — so the framework may reward discarding detail rather than finding new structure. Assess how serious each of these objections is, and say whether the two frameworks have principled resources for answering them (Bedau's later appeals to the explanatory role of macro-properties, for instance, or Hoel's insistence that the coarse-graining must preserve the causal structure, not just the statistics).

Please conclude with a verdict: on the evidence you have laid out, are weak emergence and causal emergence two characterizations of the same phenomenon at different levels of abstraction, or genuinely different notions picking out different features of complex systems? And should my colleague's reduction of the one to the other be accepted, qualified, or rejected? Be precise about the sense in which any given claim you make is conceptual rather than empirical — I am interested in the structure of the two accounts, not in a survey of who published what.
