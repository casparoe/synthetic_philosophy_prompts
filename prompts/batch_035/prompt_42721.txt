I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for a good while now — since the Overcoming Bias days, in fact — so most of my philosophy of mind comes second-hand: Chinese Rooms, zombies, Mary and her colourless room, experience machines, all filtered through the local fondness for dissolving questions rather than answering them. A recent forum post I read offers a summary of Dennett's views on intuition pumps, and I would like to know how far I can trust it, because I don't have the background to check it myself and it will inevitably shape how I treat thought experiments in future arguments. Here is the substance of the post's summary:

'Daniel Dennett coined the term "intuition pump" in his 2013 book to cover thought experiments in general — the Chinese Room, Mary's Room, the trolley problems, the philosophical zombies. His central claim is that no thought experiment is really an argument at all: what it does is get you to picture a scenario vividly enough that an intuition fires, and intuitions are just gut feelings, products of a brain evolved for middle-sized objects and everyday stakes, with no authority over hypotheticals far outside that regime. Dennett demonstrated this most clearly with the Chinese Room: the systems reply he endorsed shows that once you attend to the man, the rulebook and the room as one implementation of a larger system, the intuition that the system doesn't understand Chinese dissolves, which refutes Searle. His verdict was that misfiring pumps should be retired from serious philosophy altogether, since they trade on vividness rather than proof. The practical upshot is that thought experiments about zombies, uploads and sentient simulations carry no evidential weight, because the intuitions they elicit track the imagery of the story, not the truth of its conclusion.'

I am inclined to accept this, since it flatters the forum's usual scepticism about armchair epistemology, but a few things nag at me. The post credits the coinage to a 2013 book while simultaneously treating the whole apparatus as a discredited toy, which seems odd if the tools were as worthless as all that; and I vaguely recall the Chinese Room still being argued about decades on, which sits badly with the breezy claim that it has been refuted. Please go through the summary claim by claim and tell me which parts a careful defender of Dennett would accept, which are conflations or overstatements, and whether the final practical moral — that intuitions elicited by strange thought experiments carry no evidential weight — actually follows from the parts of the summary that survive scrutiny. I would also like to know what Dennett's own recommended practice with intuition pumps was, if it was not simply to retire them. Please write your reply in British English.
