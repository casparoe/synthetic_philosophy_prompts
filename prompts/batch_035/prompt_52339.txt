I've been asked to chair my department's annual "Above and Beyond" award committee, and in drafting the criteria I've discovered I have no worked-out account of what going beyond duty actually is — only a handful of verdicts I feel strongly about. Here they are. A colleague who donates a kidney to a stranger clearly qualifies. A lifeguard who rescues a drowning swimmer at serious risk to herself does not, however admirable — it's her job. A colleague who quietly donates ten percent of her salary to effective charities, every year, for twenty years, qualifies, even though no single year's donation seems especially heroic. A colleague who did something equally costly but had promised to do it — he had pledged a kidney to his ailing brother — does not qualify in my eyes, since he had an obligation, even one he took on voluntarily. And I'm genuinely torn about a colleague who performed an enormous, unrequired sacrifice but was open that he did it partly because it would look good on his record for promotion. I'd like you to tell me whether some one principle captures these verdicts — in particular, whether "beyond duty" should be read in terms of exceeding obligation, or in terms of sacrifice borne, or something else entirely — and to state your preferred principle precisely enough that I could apply it to next year's cases. Then tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, whether my treatment of the twenty-year donor versus the one-off hero forces a choice between treating supererogation as a property of acts or of lives, and what it implies about the case I keep going back and forth on: a colleague who did something enormously costly and unrequired, but did it because, in her words, she couldn't have lived with herself otherwise.
