For a seminar on rational choice (14 students, 75 minutes), design a game in which participants experience satisficing as a rational strategy rather than a failure of nerve. The setup I have in mind: a sequential search task with costly draws — say, sealed envelopes containing point values drawn from a distribution the participants don't initially know — where each additional draw costs points and players must commit to setting a "good enough" threshold before they start. Some players should be forced to play as optimizers (they may draw until they've computed or approximated the expected-value-maximizing stopping rule), others as free satisficers, and at least one group should be asked to set their aspiration level jointly and revise it mid-game, so the class runs into the underexplored question of how a satisficer should adjust the threshold when the environment turns out better or worse than expected. Include rules, materials, a payoff scheme that needs no real money, and a timeline. The debrief should map specific moments in the game onto Herbert Simon's distinction between substantive and procedural rationality and onto his "scissors" metaphor — the claim that bounded rationality is jointly produced by mind and environment — and should pose, rather than settle, whether satisficing is a normative recommendation or merely a descriptive verdict about what minds like ours can achieve. Tell me what will predictably go wrong on the first run (I expect some satisficers to accidentally outperform the optimizers because of search costs, and I want to know whether that's a bug in my payoff scheme or the point), and be explicit about which feature of satisficing no such game can reproduce — I suspect it is that in real decision-making there is no facilitator who knows the underlying distribution, so participants can never confirm whether "good enough" was well calibrated.
