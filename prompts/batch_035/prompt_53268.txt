I'm a retired geophysicist — thirty-eight years of fitting inverse models to seismic and gravimetric survey data — and in my retirement I have been working through Bayesian confirmation theory, which I had naively assumed would feel like home. Mostly it does. But I have hit the literature on the so-called problem of old evidence, and I cannot tell whether it is a genuine difficulty or a philosopher's rediscovery of something my colleagues and I were disciplined about for decades without naming it so grandly. The problem, as I understand it: the most celebrated confirmations in the history of science often involve evidence that was already on the table before the theory arrived — the advance of Mercury's perihelion was a known anomaly for decades before general relativity accommodated it — and yet standard conditionalization cannot register this as confirmation, because an agent who is certain of the evidence has P(E) = 1 and P(E|H) = 1, so the likelihood ratio is vacuous. The proposed repairs involve counterfactual assignments: the probability you would have given the evidence had you not already known it, or some similar retrospective reconstruction.

Restate this whole debate in the vocabulary I actually used for a career — training sets and holdout sets, cross-validation, data leakage, double-dipping, post hoc model selection, in-sample versus out-of-sample error, effective number of parameters, the difference between fitting and predicting — as faithfully as you can, and then give me the residue report. My working suspicion, which I want tested, is that the old evidence problem is simply what we would call leakage or data snooping: a model that was selected for its fit to a dataset gets no honest credit from that same dataset, and the counterfactual-prior maneuver is precisely what a referee would flag when a paper claims predictive success on data the model-builder had already seen. But I suspect the translation is not clean. The statistical vocabulary is essentially static and ensemble-flavored — a dataset exists, and you carve a holdout from it — whereas the philosophers' worry seems irreducibly diachronic and first-person: it is about one agent's learning history, about what a particular scientist knew at a particular time and what they would have believed otherwise. Whether that residue is a defect of my vocabulary or a sign that the philosophical problem is less substantive than it looks, I genuinely cannot say, and that judgment is the part of the answer I most want.

One specific thing I would like included: does the accommodation-versus-prediction distinction the philosophers argue about — whether a theory that was adjusted to fit known data is somehow worse confirmed than one that predicted it in advance — have an exact counterpart in the statistics I know, or only an approximate one? In my world the answer was always operational (penalize model complexity, demand out-of-sample validation), never about the epistemic state of the person who built the model. If the philosophical version cannot be reduced to that operational machinery, I would like to know precisely where the reduction fails.
