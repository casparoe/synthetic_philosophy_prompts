I have been keeping a list of my own verdicts on questions of material constitution and persistence, in the hope that they exhibit some pattern, and I have come to suspect that they do not. I would like you to tell me what principle, or what small set of principles, best captures them. Where a principle must be bent into artificial complexity to save a verdict, I would rather you overturn the verdict than complicate the principle, but I leave that judgment to you, as long as you are explicit about which verdicts you are discarding and why.

Here are the cases, with my verdicts.

1. A statue and the piece of clay it is made of, shaped at the same moment: I say there are two objects on the pedestal, not one. The statue cannot survive being squashed into a ball; the clay can; and one thing cannot both survive and fail to survive the same squashing.

2. Yet I am confident that I am one thing with this living body — not a person standing in some relation to a human animal, but the animal itself. I hold to this even though I also believe that if my cerebrum were transplanted into another body and the rest of me kept alive on machines, I would go with the cerebrum. That second belief looks like it forces two things here as well, exactly as in the statue case, and I cannot see how to stop it without giving one of them up.

3. A ship whose planks are replaced one at a time, in the ordinary course of repairs, until none of the original planks remain: the same ship, and I would not budge on this.

4. A ship dismantled into planks in a warehouse, shipped abroad, and reassembled from all and only its original planks a year later: also the same ship, I say — it merely existed for a while in a scattered state.

5. My grandmother's wedding ring, melted down and cast into a new ring, using all of the original gold: not the same ring. I am surprised to find that I hold this, given (4), since the matter is preserved in both cases and the form is interrupted in both.

6. The Nile: the same river, even though not a single water molecule in it today was in it a century ago.

7. My chess club: when its membership turns over gradually, one resignation and one election at a time, it remains the same club. But when the entire membership resigned at once and a new group took over the name, the weekly schedule, and the rules, I judge the new club a different club, however similar its conduct.

8. Yet I take France to be the same nation across revolution, conquest, and the replacement of essentially its entire population, which looks structurally like the club case I refuse in (7). Perhaps nations differ from clubs, but I want to know what the difference is supposed to be, and whether it can be stated without simply naming the cases it is meant to fit.

My tentative principle is something like this: a thing persists so long as its function is continuously realized in matter appropriately arranged; neither identity of matter nor unbroken continuity of form is required; and gaps are bridged whenever the thing's structure is preserved in its parts or in a settled plan for restoring it. This seems to fit (3), (4), and (6), and possibly (7), but I do not know whether it can accommodate (5) and (8), and I cannot see what it says about the combined case, in which the replacement repairs of (3) proceed while the discarded planks are reassembled as in (4) — in that scenario I genuinely do not know which, if either, vessel is the original, and I want your view.

What I would like from you, then: a precise statement of the best principle or principles, with each clause tied to the verdict that motivates it; what your principle says about three cases I have not listed — a university that merges with another, a sandcastle destroyed by the tide and rebuilt by hand the next morning, and a jazz standard that persists through performances sharing no notes; and an honest accounting of which of my verdicts the principle cannot keep, together with the order in which you would overturn them and what my corrected set of verdicts should look like.
