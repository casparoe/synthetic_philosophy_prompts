I teach philosophy now, but I came up as a quant, and I've honestly never been able to read Pascal's wager without hearing it in portfolio terms. Restate the whole thing for me in the vocabulary of finance — positions, payoffs, hedging, diversification, mutually exclusive assets, position sizing, liquidity — as faithful as you can, with belief as the asset you take a position in. Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about: what maps cleanly, what has to be distorted to fit, and what can't be said in the finance vocabulary at all.

I already have suspicions about two spots. The infinite payoff looks like it breaks every allocation rule I ever used — Kelly sizing and mean-variance both go incoherant once expectation is undefined, and "the expected value dominates regardless of the probability" starts sounding less like a theorem and more like an artifact of the math. The many-gods objection looks to me like a problem about mutually exclusive assets with anticorrelated payoffs, where diversification is simply off the table. But the spot I want you to take seriously is the buy-in: finance assumes you can open a position at will, and Pascal seems to assume the same about belief — that living as if you believe is buying the asset and waiting for the price to move. Is that a gap in my vocabulary (finance just has nothing to say about how positions in belief get opened), or a gap in the wager itself, or neither?

And at the end, tell me straight: once the translation is done, is there any residue that constitutes a real philosophical disagreement, or is what's left over just an edge case in expected-utility arithmetic that happened to get a famous name attached to it?
