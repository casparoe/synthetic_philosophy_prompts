In December 2013, thirteen-year-old Jahi McMath suffered catastrophic post-operative bleeding and cardiac arrest following surgery for sleep apnea at Children's Hospital Oakland. She was resuscitated but never regained consciousness, and on December 12, 2013 she was declared dead under California's neurological (whole-brain) criteria; a court upheld the declaration and California issued a death certificate. Her family, on religious grounds, refused to accept the finding, and eventually moved her — mechanically ventilated, with a beating heart — to New Jersey, the one American state whose law allows families to reject neurological criteria for death on religious or moral grounds. She was maintained there for four and a half years, during which, according to her family and their experts, she showed somatic development, including the onset of puberty, and responses her mother interpreted as reactions to her presence and to commands. In June 2018, after complications from surgery, her heart stopped and could not be restarted; she was declared dead under circulatory criteria, and New Jersey issued a second death certificate, dated June 22, 2018, citing liver failure alongside the hypoxic brain injury.

Analyze this case through the definitional debate about death — the standoff between the whole-brain and circulatory standards, and the background question of whether death is a single biological event that our criteria track more or less accurately, or a partly institutional fact that our criteria partly constitute. Treat the case as a natural experiment no bioethicist could have arranged: one body, two death certificates, and a four-and-a-half-year interval in which the occupant of the bed was legally a corpse in one state and a living patient in another. Some questions to discipline the analysis: What exactly, on the whole-brain view, was being maintained in that New Jersey bed — a dead body that nevertheless matured, or a living organism that had lost the person? If the latter, what does that concede about the claim that whole-brain death simply is the death of the organism? The somatic development is standardly dismissed as compatible with brain death on the ground that it is driven by hormonal and mechanical support rather than neural integration — say honestly whether that reply marks a principled boundary on what counts as integrated functioning or is an ad hoc patch, and whether a defender of the circulatory standard has the better of the argument precisely here. Then confront the symmetry the case forces: if death is what the circulatory view says it is, the California certificate was issued more than four years too early; if death is what the whole-brain view says it is, New Jersey prolonged a legal fiction in which a dead patient was fed, treated, and operated on. Is there a coherent reading on which both certificates were correct — one on which death is indexed to a jurisdiction's criteria rather than to the body — and if so, has death effectively become a legal status in the way that citizenship or marriage is, rather than a biological fact? Where do the three standard positions (whole-brain, circulatory, and the higher-brain view that ties death to the permanent loss of consciousness rather than to the loss of all brain function) each gain ground in this case, where do they lose it, and where are they simply silent? I want a disciplined mapping of the case to the theories and back, not a retelling of the litigation. End with a final line of the form: Verdict: [a short phrase naming which of the three views this case most damages, or the single most important thing the case establishes about the definition of death.]
