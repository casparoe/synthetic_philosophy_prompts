I'm preparing a position statement on rational choice under uncertainty for a graduate seminar, and before I circulate it I'd like a consistency audit. Here is the package of commitments I plan to defend:

(1) The ideal of rational choice is Bayesian expected-utility theory: an ideally rational agent holds sharp degrees of belief satisfying the probability axioms, updates them by conditionalization, and chooses so as to maximize expected utility relative to them. Departures from this ideal in real agents are biases — regrettable even when humanly understandable.

(2) The Allais preferences are a clear instance of such a bias. Preferring a certain payoff over a gamble that includes it, and then reversing that preference once every option becomes a gamble, violates independence, and I'm confident this is an error, not merely a divergence from an arbitrarily chosen model.

(3) The Ellsberg preferences, by contrast, strike me as reasonable. Faced at matched odds and stakes with a bet on drawing red from an urn known to contain 50 red and 50 black and a bet on red from an urn containing 100 balls in an unknown proportion, I think preferring the known urn is not a bias, and I would resist any account of rationality on which an agent must be indifferent between the two urns. The distinction between risk, where the odds are known, and ambiguity, where they are not, seems to me to track something normatively real.

(4) I hold that rational preference is transitive, and that susceptibility to being money-pumped is a genuine mark of irrationality, not just a practical inconvenience.

(5) I also hold that an agent who refuses favorable bets on the ambiguous urn at any odds — who won't take red on the unknown urn even at twenty to one in their favor — is irrational, roughly as irrational as someone who refuses the same terms on the known urn.

(6) Finally, on public policy under uncertainty: when a government must decide whether to fund, say, a program whose efficacy is known only to lie within a wide band, I think it should form its best point estimate and maximize expected value accordingly, treating ambiguity exactly as it treats risk.

I can't tell whether these hang together, and I'd rather find out now than in the Q&A. Please go through the set and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look to be, and rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing everything as equally bad. For each real tension, say what would have to give and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the set. Please write in American English; lead with your bottom line in your very first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question. And since I'll have to defend whatever I keep: for each verdict you reach, tell me what consideration would most likely change it — where your own reasoning is most likely to fail, and what I would need to establish to show that a commitment you've told me to drop can in fact be retained.
