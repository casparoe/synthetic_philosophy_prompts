Quick context: I'm presenting at a philosophy of economics workshop in a few weeks, and I want a Q&A prep sheet. My thesis, roughly: the whole fight over Friedman's 1953 essay -- the "as if" line that a model's unrealistic assumptions don't matter so long as its predictions come out right -- gets misframed as one question when it's actually two. For what I call closure exercises (comparative statics, where all you need is that the model's derivatives point the right way), the F-twist is basically fine -- nobody cares that firms aren't literally profit maximizers if the model gets the sign of the response right. But when the same model's assumptions get used to pin down structural parameters that then feed into welfare claims or counterfactual policy analysis, the unrealisticness isn't doing a simplifying job anymore, it's quietly deciding the answer before the data get a say. So my line is: idealization tolerance should depend on what the assumption is being used for downstream, prediction-adjacent use gets a pass, identification-and-welfare use doesn't.

The room: about fifteen philosophers of economics, most of whom think the Friedman debate was either settled decades ago or was never that interesting; plus two macroeconomists who actually build and estimate DSGE models and will treat my distinction as either obvious or naive, not sure which yet. One of the philosophers is a known Musgrave partisan, so expect the "negligibility vs. domain vs. heuristic assumptions already handles this, you've just relabeled it" objection early.

What I want from you: the eight or so objections this specific audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way a philosopher of economics would phrase it (and, where it's the macro people talking, the way they would). For each, a two-minute reply I can actually deliver, plus an honest note on where the reply is soft. Flag which objections I should concede parts of up front rather than fight. And include the question I'm hoping nobody asks -- you know the one, the "so what, name a concrete case where your rule changes what anyone does" version -- and tell me if there's any decent answer to it, because I'm not sure there is.
