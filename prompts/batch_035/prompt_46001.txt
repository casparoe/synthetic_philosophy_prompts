I've been reading a popular book on the history of ideas -- the author is a journalist by training -- and it has a chapter arguing that philosophy never really gets anywhere. Summarizing it roughly: after two and a half thousand years, philosophers are still arguing about the questions the ancients argued about, while in science the disputes of one century become the textbooks of the next; no philosophical question has ever been closed, every classic position still has able defenders, and today's debate about the mind and body is just the one Descartes started, conducted with more footnotes. Where philosophy does produce settled results -- formal logic, say, or the very idea of a scientific method -- the book says this only proves the point, because those questions were then handed over to mathematics and science, and only stopped being philosophy once they started producing real answers. The conclusion is that philosophy is a practice of perpetual rehearsal: valuable for training people to argue well, but not a way of finding anything out.

I have no philosophy training, and I found myself half-persuaded and half-irritated without being able to say exactly why. Is this fair? Please go through it and tell me which parts a professional philosopher would accept as at least defensible, where it slides between claims that ought to be kept apart (it seems to move from "philosophers still disagree" to "nothing has been settled" to "nothing has been discovered," and I don't know if those are the same complaint), and whether the line about results leaving philosophy for science supports the author's conclusion or actually cuts against it. I'd also like to know whether the comparison with science is as flattering to science as he assumes.
