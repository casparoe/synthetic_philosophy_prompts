In an introductory epistemology course I am auditing, the instructor wrapped up a lecture on G. E. Moore's "Proof of an External World" with a compact summary, which I copied down as faithfully as I could:

"Moore stood before the British Academy in 1939, held up his two hands, and said, as he gestured with the right hand, 'Here is one hand,' and then, gesturing with the left, 'and here is another' — thereby giving a valid argument from premises he claimed to know with certainty to the conclusion that external things exist, which he took to refute Kant, who had complained that it was a scandal to philosophy that no such proof had ever been given. The argument is certainly valid; nobody disputes that. But it's question-begging: the premise that he is holding up a hand is exactly what the skeptic doubts, so Moore's proof assumes the very thing it was supposed to establish. Moore himself effectively conceded the failure when he admitted that he could never prove that he knew he had hands. Some contemporary epistemologists have revived the strategy under the name 'Moorean' arguments, but on the consensus view these work only if you help yourself to the assumption that perception is trustworthy — an assumption the skeptic will not grant. So what Moore really gave us was a declaration of confidence in common sense, not a proof — a point he had been building toward since his 1925 'A Defence of Common Sense.' If you like, the proof also settled, as a bonus, that we see material objects directly rather than sense-data, but that's a sideshow, not the main event."

I am inclined to accept most of this, but a few things nag at me. If the premise really were identical to the conclusion, the argument would be circular in a way that Moore, a careful logician, would plainly have noticed, so I suspect the instructor is sliding two different charges together. And I vaguely recall that Kant himself never doubted the existence of external things, so 'refuting Kant' seems like an odd description of what Moore was doing.

Please audit the lecture's account claim by claim. Specifically: (1) Is 'he took himself to refute Kant' a fair description of Moore's target, given what the Kantian 'scandal' was actually a scandal about and what Moore himself said about it? (2) Is the instructor's definition of question-begging — that the premise is 'exactly what the skeptic doubts' — the same as the charge that the proof assumes its own conclusion, and is Moore's argument guilty of either? Bear in mind that Moore laid down explicit conditions that a proof must meet; does his argument meet them as he stated them, and where exactly would the serious objection have to bite instead? (3) Did Moore's admission that he could not prove that he knew he had hands amount to conceding that he did not know it, or that the proof failed — and what was his considered view about the relation between knowing something and being able to prove it? (4) Does the contemporary revival of Moore's strategy really require assuming in advance that perception is trustworthy, or does it claim something subtler about how perceptual experience justifies belief — and does the instructor's appeal to a 'consensus view' hold up given how that debate actually divides? (5) Was the direct-realism 'bonus' a claim Moore made, one he denied, or one about which he was officially agnostic — and how does his chosen definition of 'external things' bear on this? Finally, sort the lecture into what is accurate, what is an oversimplification a defender of Moore would tolerate for the sake of exposition, and what is a genuine misrepresentation; and say whether the instructor's bottom line — a declaration of confidence, not a proof — is a fair verdict, or whether it stands or falls with the missteps above.
