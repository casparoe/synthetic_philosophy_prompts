I hold a version of negative utilitarianism with a lexical threshold, and I would like a rigorous map of what could count against it.

The view, in my formulation: below some intensity of suffering, pleasure and pain are commensurable in the ordinary utilitarian way — they can be traded off, aggregated, and maximized over. But any episode of suffering above the threshold is lexically prior to everything below it: no amount or arrangement of positive experience, however vast or however distributed, can compensate for a single instance of it, and no quantity of sub-threshold suffering can compensate for it either. Two refinements of mine matter for the objections. First, I index the threshold to the intensity of the episode itself, not to duration-weighted totals, and I deny aggregation across the threshold: a million pains just below it do not sum to something that outranks one pain just above it. Second, I intend this as a claim about the intrinsic badness of experiences, not directly as a decision procedure, though I am not sure how long that wall holds once probabilities enter.

Tell me what would count against the view, sorted from fatal through serious to merely awkward. In particular, I want your honest treatment of at least the following, plus anything I have missed:

1. The arbitrariness of the threshold. If I place it at some intensity T, the view implies that an episode at T plus some vanishing increment cannot be compensated by any amount of pleasure, while an episode at T minus that increment can be compensated by quite a lot. Is the discontinuity a defensible feature, a cost to be offset by the view's other merits, or a symptom that intensity cannot do the work I am assigning it? And if I retreat to a vague or fuzzy threshold, have I bought flexibility at the price of no longer saying anything determinate?

2. Collapse worries in both directions. If the threshold is set low, the view approaches strict negative utilitarianism with its familiar costs, including the notorious implication that a painless world would be acceptable even at the total elimination of worthwhile experience. If it is set high, the view approaches standard utilitarianism in all practical cases, and the lexical structure becomes idle machinery. Is there a principled stopping point between, or is the position a way station with no stable resting place?

3. The dominance of the threshold under uncertainty. Once super-threshold suffering is lexically prior, any nonzero probability of it seems to dominate every gamble that risks it, however favorable otherwise. Does the view, made decision-theoretic, become action-paralyzing or fanatical in a way that should count as a reductio, or is this a manageable cost that afflicts lexical views generally?

4. The empirical and phenomenological defeaters. Work in pain science complicates the idea of a single intensity dimension: chronic pain, dissociative analgesia, pain asymbolia, and the dissociation between intensity and unpleasantness all suggest that what I am calling intensity may not carve the phenomenon at a joint. If the badness of pain is not well ordered by any one measurable quantity, does my threshold fail for want of something to be a threshold of?

5. The pristine-world comparison. On my view, a future containing ten billion lives of untroubled, even ecstatic happiness plus one hour of super-threshold suffering is worse than an empty future. I take it this is the sort of thing a defender should wear openly rather than hide, but tell me whether it functions as a principled bullet-biting or as a defeater that reasonable people should treat as decisive, and why.

For each point, distinguish what a thoughtful defender of the view would concede as genuine pressure from what they would resist, and say where I am likely fooling myself by treating a serious objection as merely awkward. Finally, assess the insulation question directly: given the refinements available to me — vagueness in the threshold, the intrinsic-badness versus decision-procedure distinction, restrictions on aggregation — could this position be qualified into something that no possible observation, intuition, or argument could threaten, and if so, is that a defect in a view of this kind, or simply what a foundational axiology should expect?
