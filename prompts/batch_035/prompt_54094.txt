My brother has spent eleven years and most of his savings restoring the 1920s wooden sailboat our grandfather left him — most weekends, two loans, and eventually a marriage that ended partly over the project. A marine surveyor has now told him that the remaining work will cost more than the finished boat could ever sell for, and his new partner has made continuing a live issue between them. He refuses to stop, and his defense is not just sentiment: he says that quitting now would turn the eleven years into a pure waste, and that people who abandon things the moment the arithmetic turns against them become the kind of people who never finish anything — a trait, he argues, that has its own costs in a life. My instinct is that he is being irrational, but I cannot tell which parts of his defense, if any, are doing real work — for instance, whether the half-restored boat itself now counts for something in the decision, even though the years and money already gone presumably do not. Where exactly is he right, where is he fooling himself, and is there a principled line between the considerations that legitimately bear on what he should do next and the ones that are just his past costs talking him into further costs? Please keep separate what is genuinely settled about how to reason in a case like this from what remains contested among people who study decision-making, and don't present either side of that line as the consensus.
