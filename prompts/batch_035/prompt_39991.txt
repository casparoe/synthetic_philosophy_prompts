My grandmother has had dementia for about six years now and it's bad. My brother has been reading Dennett for years, and at Thanksgiving he laid this on us: the self is like a center of gravity. Nobody thinks there's a little dot inside a rock; physicists just posit a center of gravity because it makes prediction easy, and it's a perfectly respectable thing even though it's an abstraction. Same, he says, with grandma: the her we knew was never a thing in her head, it was a posit all of us computed together — a center of narrative gravity — and the narratives stopped cohering years ago. So the person we're mourning is already gone; visiting her is like visting a landmark where the gravity isn't anymore, and honestly, he says, the hard questions (how much to do to keep her body going, and for whose sake) should be answered for our sake, not hers, because there's no her left for anything to be for.

Half of me thinks hes right and half of me thinks he's doing a trick with the word abstraction. Can you audit the analogy for me? Give me the mapping first — what plays the role of the physicist, the object, the predictions, the usefulness. Then where it breaks: a center of gravity never fades gradually, can't be harmed or benefited, and nobody wonders whether it's still in there — and I want to know which of those gaps actually matter for what he's using the analogy to conclude, versus which are just scenery. Then the real question: once the disanalogies are on the table, does his conclusion — no patient left, so the decisions are ours and not hers — survive, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? And if the epistemic half of his claim (she's already gone) dies but the normative half (the decisions are for us) limps on, tell me which half was doing the work. I don't want comfort, just an honest picture of what's in that room.
