I'm teaching a freshman seminar called "Minds, Machines, and Freedom" (fun course, chaos to grade) and next week I want to do Groundhog Day alongside something I stumbled on called sphexishness, and I'd like help thinking the reading through.

In case you don't know the term: the sphex wasp lays her eggs on a paralyzed cricket she's stashed in a burrow. When she drags the cricket to the entrance, she leaves it at the threshold, pops inside to check the burrow, comes back out, and pulls it in. But if a experimenter nudges the cricket a few inches away while she's inside, she comes out, re-locates it, drags it back to the threshold... and goes in to check again. Move it again, she does the whole check again. She'll do this dozens of times with no sign of catching on. Hofstadter coined "sphexishness" for this kind of thing -- behavior that looks intelligent from outside but turns out to be a fixed routine running on rails, totally indifferent to how the world deviates from the script it expects. The interesting philosophical question is what the opposite of sphexishness even is, and whether we have more of it than the wasp.

Now Groundhog Day. Here's my rough thought, poke at it: Phil starts the film as arguably sphexish in the everyday sense -- a scripted, cynical routine he runs on everyone around him. Then the time loop traps him in literal repetition. But then something odd happens: the loop is also the only thing that ever breaks him out. Same day, same people, same events, and his responses to them keep changing until eventually he's a different person, and the loop releases him. So the movie seems to be running an experiment: strip away every novelty in the world, and see what in him still counts as flexible. Is the film's answer that Phil's eventual "freedom" is just reason-responsiveness under perfect determinism -- which is basically compatibilism as a rom-com? Or is the film sneakily showing the opposite, that his final transformation is just another groove, a better script (the improvised piano, the small-town saint routine), and the movie dresses it up as growth because it needs a happy ending?

Some things I'd want your take on specifically:

- Where exactly does Phil's sphexishness break? Early loop Phil exploits the day mechanically (the robbery, the endless Rita-seduction attempts that are literally him rerunning a script until it outputs yes). Is retrying-with-persistence-until-success flexible or is it peak sphexish? I genuinely can't decide.
- The suicides. He kills himself a bunch of times and the day just resets. Does the film use that to define what sphexishness can't fix -- you can't script your way out of a loop by escalating -- and is that a philosophically respectable point or just a plot beat?
- The hardest part for my reading: Phil's change seems to be driven by despair and then by wanting Rita, not by reasoning his way anywhere. If flexibility means responding to reasons, is Phil flexible at all, or did the world just grind him into a different shape? Is being ground into a better person free?
- And where does the reading strain? The film is a fantasy -- no real loop, no day that actually repeats for anyone -- and it cheats (he remembers, the world doesn't). Does that cheat break the analogy to determinism, or does it not matter?

I'd like a reading that takes both seriously: the wasp story as a real puzzle about what behavioral flexibility amounts to, and the film as something with its own position, not just an illustration I staple the puzzle onto. If you think the movie actually adds something to the sphexishness debate that the philosophy lit on its own doesn't have -- some third option between "ran a fixed program" and "responded to reasons" -- say so and say what it is. Bonus if you can suggest 2-3 specific scenes I should screen for the seminar and why each one does different philosophical work.
