My brother runs our fantasy football league, and this year, instead of a trophy, he put up $1000 of his own money for a "claim game." The rules: between noon and midnight last Friday, any of the 14 of us could text him the word CLAIM. If exactly one person texted, they won the whole grand. If nobody texted, or if two or more did, nobody won, and the money got rolled into next year's trophy fund.

Before the window opened, Theo, who's deep into old Hofstadter columns, announced in the group chat that nobody should claim. His reasoning: we all got the same speech, we all have the same info, so if you can talk yourself into claiming, so can everyone else, and then we all claim and all lose. The only way anyone ever wins this thing is exactly one person breaking symmetry, and there's no principled way to be that person, so stay out. He said Hofstadter ran basically this exact setup in Scientific American back in the 80s (a 20-person version), and that when the magazine later ran a lottery where every entry shrank the prize, readers buried them in postcards claiming googolplexes of entries each, and the prize collapsed to a fraction of a cent. That's what happens, Theo said, when everyone follows the "obviously I claim" logic.

Priya called that mysticism. We're 14 different people, not one algorithm with 14 copies, and for each of us, not claiming is dominated: sit on your phone and you're guaranteed $0; claim and you've got a live shot at a grand. Whatever the other 13 do, claiming is never worse for you.

Theo: that's the trap — if claiming is right for you, it's right for all of us, we all claim, and we all lose. An argument that's right for each person separately and a disaster when everyone follows it isn't a good argument.

Priya (who turned out to be alot more read-up on this than I'd guessed — she'd actually skimmed the columns): fine, then randomize. There's some small probability that's optimal, and she said Hofstadter's own answer to the 20-person version was to send the telegram with probability 1/20, not to hang back — so Theo was misquoting his own guy. Her proposal: everyone who wants in rolls a d20 and claims on a natural 20, close enough.

Theo: two problems. First, the dice plan is unstable — if the other 13 are rolling at 1-in-20, your expected take from claiming for sure is over $500, versus maybe $25 if you obey your own die, so anyone who takes the plan seriously should immediately break it. Second, if we're all able to agree to roll dice, we could just as easily agree to draw straws and hand somebody the $1000 cleanly — the dilemma only exists because we can't coordinate, and coordinated dice are coordination. And anyway nobody can verify anyone's roll.

Priya: this is Newcomb handwaving. In Newcomb there's a predictor; here nothing I do makes anyone else claim. Correlation isn't causation, and "same computation, same output" is doing all the work in that sentence.

Theo: it's not causal, it's logical. You're not making them claim; you're one of fourteen people running the same decision, and if the argument that moves you moves everyone it moves, the group outcome is the thing you're choosing, like it or not.

What actually happened: three of us claimed — Priya, who insists her d20 came up a natural 20 so the system worked and the dice just came up unlucky overall, plus two others — so nobody won and the grand went to the trophy fund. At the bar afterward, one of the other claimers said his only mistake was that two other people also claimed, and that if they'd been as smart as him he'd be up $1000. Theo declared the whole thing a vindication of Hofstadter. Priya wouldn't concede anything. They eventually asked me to referee, and I honestly couldn't.

Theo's history of the Hofstadter stuff is secondhand, so if you know the actual columns, tell me where his summary goes wrong.
