I teach an intro free will unit and this year I want to pair the Dennett-Caruso book Just Deserts with Groundhog Day, and I want a reading of the film that actually does some philosophical work rather than just gesturing at "time loop = self-improvement."

Here's what I want explored. The obvious reading of the film is retributivist: Phil is a jerk, the loop is punishment, he suffers until he's reformed, and we the audience enjoy watching it. That's basically the "he got what was coming to him" intuition. But I suspect the film, read carefully, is closer to Caruso than to that intuition -- if you take seriously his line that punishment should be forward-looking (deterrence, rehabilitation, protection, his quarantine analogy) rather than backwards-looking desert. Phil's transformation happens not because suffering purifies him but because the loop removes every consequentialist reason to behave well: no reputation, no legal consequences, no tomorrow. So the only reasons left to act well are, well, the actual reasons to act well. That reads to me like an argument that moral improvement is circumstantial and self-modifying rather than deserved, which is the Dennett/Caruso joint project in a weird way -- Dennett with his compatibilist "you're the kind of agent who can reprogram yourself" story, Caruso with the denial that jerk-Phil ever deserved the loop in the first place.

But then there are complications I want you to take seriously rather than wave away:

1. Nobody imposed the loop. No punisher, no judge. Does the absence of an intentional punisher let the film dodge the desert question entirely, or does it just launder it -- the screenwriter is the punisher, and the audience's satisfaction at Phil's misery is doing the retributive work?

2. The old homeless man who dies no matter what Phil does. That scene seems to break the "effort + improvement = results" logic, and it's the one thing Phil cannot fix by becoming better. What does the film think that tells us? Does it have anything to say about the innocent suffering that no amount of self-cultivation addresses?

3. The suicide montage. He kills himself, a lot, and it never sticks. Is the film playing suffering for laughs in a way that a just-deserts-free picture of punishment would find queasy, or does the fact that it's consequenceless (for everyone but him) make it fair game?

4. When Phil exits the loop, is he the same agent who entered it? Rita falls for the post-loop Phil partly on the strength of loop-Phil's accumulated work -- piano, French, ice sculpture. Is there a question here about whether moral credit, like moral blame, has a persistence problem? (I'm honestly not sure this one goes anywhere, tell me if it doesn't.)

I'd also like you to say where the reading strains: is there a version of the film that really is committed to deserts -- the idea that Phil has to *earn* his way out, that the loop is exactly proportioned to his jerkishness -- and if so, defend it properly, dont just knock it down.

What I want at the end is a verdict on what the film adds to the Dennett vs Caruso debate that the book itself can't do: something about how it *feels* to watch the desert intuition get satisfied and then quietly dismantled over ninety minutes. If you can point me to specific scenes and sequences that would work as clips in class, that would be great too. No need for quotes, just the scenes.
