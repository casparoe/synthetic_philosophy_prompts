I'm a PhD student in political philosophy, and my dissertation defends a contractualist justification of public health and safety regulation, but the aggregation problem is threatening to sink the chapter, so before I write myself out of the tradition I want to see whether my actual verdicts add up to a principle I could live with. Here is my track record on cases. A surgeon may not kill one healthy visitor to harvest organs for five dying patients: wrong, no hesitation. A driver may divert a runaway trolley away from five people onto one: permissible, also no hesitation, and yes, I feel the pull of the objection that these two verdicts can't both stand. A city chlorinates its water, thereby imposing on each of a million residents a minuscule risk of a fatal reaction, in order to prevent a handful of deaths from waterborne disease: permissible, and more than permissible. If I can either relieve one person of a day-long migraine or relieve a million people each of an hour of mild headache, I should relieve the million. Yet if I can save one person from death or spare a thousand people a week of severe pain with no lasting injury, I must save the one. And when the choice is between saving one person and saving two, each facing an equally painful death, I genuinely cannot decide whether to say the two must be saved or that each individual deserves an equal chance, with the numbers counting only through the lottery weights. What I want from you is a principle, or a small set of rival candidates, stated precisely enough to generate verdicts on cases I haven't listed: something in the neighborhood of Scanlon's reasonable rejection test constrained by the idea that reasons must be compared person by person, or a threshold view about when numbers count and when they don't, or something else entirely if you think my verdicts point elsewhere. Tell me which of my verdicts are load-bearing, which the best principle cannot accommodate and must therefore overturn, and what the resulting principle predicts about at least one case I haven't mentioned where I am not going to like the answer. Then apply it to the case I keep going back and forth on: a transport ministry deciding whether to fund a barrier upgrade that would, statistically, prevent about three deaths a year on a dangerous road, at a cost that would otherwise cut every commuter's daily travel time by ten minutes for a decade, benefiting roughly two million people. I also want your honest judgment on whether the principle you extract is still contractualism in any recognizable sense, or whether it smuggles in the very aggregation across persons that the tradition was supposed to forbid, since that determines whether my dissertation survives. Please reply in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write a long letter to a colleague.
