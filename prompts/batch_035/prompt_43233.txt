I'm a PhD student in coastal engineering, and I've landed on our department's student advisory panel for a decision that has quietly divided the faculty for two months now, and I have to write a recommendation memo by next week. I'd appreciate a serious, philosophically careful reading of the situation, because the arguments I keep hearing strike me as both forceful and somehow beside the point.

Here is the situation. For the past six years, our department has operated a small network of tide gauges and storm-surge sensors along a stretch of coastline that is prone to rapid-onset flooding in certain villages. The network feeds an automated warning system; when surge conditions develop, sirens sound and people evacuate. The program was originally funded by a grant that has now expired, and the faculty must decide whether to keep the network running out of general departmental funds or to redirect those funds to a new wave-tank facility that would substantially improve our doctoral training and research output. The equipment is ours; we installed it, we maintain it, and there is no other institution positioned to take it over on any realistic timeline. If we shut it down, the warning system goes dark, and everyone agrees that in a serious surge event, people in those villages would very likely die who otherwise would have gotten out. The villagers have come to plan their lives around the sirens.

One side of the faculty argues roughly as follows: the storms, the tide, and the geography are what threaten these people, not us. We stepped in six years ago and, at some cost to ourselves, put a mitigation in place. Nothing obligates a department to keep funding a rescue apparatus indefinitely when the money could do enormous good elsewhere — good that will also, over decades, plausibly save lives through better engineering. If we had never built the network, no one would dream of accusing us of killing anyone. The other side replies that this is too fast: once you have built the thing and people have reorganized their lives around it, shutting it off isn't like declining to rescue; it's more like switching off a machine you know is keeping people alive, and the fact that the deaths will be traceable to a natural process rather than to our hands shouldn't matter as much as it seems to. A third colleague muddies things further by proposing a middle course: keep the network for one final storm season while publicly announcing that it's the last, on the theory that a clearly telegraphed withdrawal of support is different from an abrupt one.

What I need help with is the following. Is there a principled line between the harms we would be doing and the harms we would be merely allowing in this case, or is that distinction doing no real work once you press on it — in which case the debate should just be a straightforward weighing of expected lives saved in the villages against the expected value of the wave-tank program? And I want to get straight on the device my colleagues keep reaching for: both sides keep asking what would have happened "if we had never intervened at all," as if the moral verdict should track the nearest world in which we were never involved. That strikes me as an unstable way to reason, but I can't articulate why. Finally, does the "one more season with advance notice" compromise actually occupy a defensible middle ground, or is it just a way of making an abandonment feel like an agreement? I want to walk into the panel meeting with something more than intuitions, so please be as rigorous as you can — I'm less interested in being reassured than in understanding which of these arguments actually holds up.
