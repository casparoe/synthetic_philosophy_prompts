I'm a second-year graduate student in computer science taking a philosophy of science course as an elective, and I need to write a term-paper proposal on something at the intersection. The topic I've settled on is the family of impossibility results in algorithmic fairness — the results showing that when base rates differ between groups, you cannot simultaneously have a calibrated risk score and equal error rates across groups. What bothers me is the prevailing mood in the literature: these results get presented as if they expose a fundamental contradiction in the very idea of fairness, so that "fairness is mathematically impossible" becomes a slogan. My half-formed suspicion is that this reading conflates two different things these results could be showing. On one reading, they reveal an incompatibility among genuinely fairness-related desiderata. On another reading — the one I find more plausible — they reveal something about the semantics of scores: there's a tension between treating a risk score as a probability estimate that means the same thing across groups and treating it as a decision variable that is used the same way across groups, and the impossibility results are a theorem about that tension, which only looks like a theorem about fairness because we implicitly assume both roles for the score at once.

I want to develop this into a proper proposal. The paper would reconstruct the impossibility results carefully enough to identify exactly where each assumption enters the proof — base rate differences, calibration, error rate parity — and then ask which of these are really about fairness and which are about what it means for a score to be calibrated in the first place. My tentative claim is that the interesting question is not "which fairness criterion should we abandon" but "what is a risk score even doing when groups have different base rates," and that answering it dissolves some of the despair: what's impossible is a specific combination of measurement and decision commitments, not fairness as such. But I'm aware this could be wrong in an embarrassing way — maybe the distinction I'm drawing collapses under pressure, or maybe it's already implicit in how careful people discuss the results.

Please turn this into a term-paper proposal of the sort my instructor would approve: a precise statement of the research question, an explanation of why the standard readings leave it open, a description of the method (what I'd actually do, week by week over a fifteen-week semester, given that the formal reconstruction should come first), the possible conclusions and what would decide among them, and an honest assessment of what could make the project too big or too thin for a term paper. Don't include a bibliography or attribute specific results to specific authors by name — I'll fill all of that in myself; just refer to "the calibration-based impossibility results" and the like.
