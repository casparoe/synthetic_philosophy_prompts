My friend has decided, after a long dinner conversation, to sort every sentence about a speaker's own beliefs into exactly two piles: sentences a person could sensibly and sincerely assert, and sentences that are, in her word, "crazy." Her verdicts so far:

1. "It's raining, but I don't believe it is" — crazy, even though she agrees it's not a formal contradiction (a self-deceived person looking straight at the rain could say something true).
2. "It's raining, but you don't believe it is," said to a companion standing next to her in the downpour — perfectly fine.
3. "It was raining all morning, but I didn't believe it," said at lunch — fine; people catch on late.
4. "It's raining, but I don't believe the forecast" — fine.
5. "I believe it's raining, but it isn't" — just as crazy as (1).
6. "It's raining, but I'm not completely certain that it is" — fine, even admirable.
7. "My thermostat believes the room is sixty-eight degrees, but it's actually seventy-two" — fine, mildly funny, nothing incoherent.
8. An actor on stage delivering the line in (1) as scripted dialogue — fine; she says nothing paradoxical happens in the theater.

She now refuses to rule on three further sentences until our next dinner, and I'd like to walk in with predictions:

(a) "Either it's raining, or I don't believe that it's raining."
(b) "It's raining, but I don't know that it's raining."
(c) "It's raining, but I don't believe that I believe it's raining."

Please act as a professional philosopher with expertise in philosophy of language and philosophy of mind, particularly the literature on assertion, Moorean absurdity, and self-knowledge. Before committing to anything, brainstorm freely: lay out several candidate rules that all fit her verdicts on (1) through (8) — for instance, rules about what assertion expresses, about first-person authority over one's own mental states, about norms of assertion involving knowledge, and about pragmatic self-defeat. Check each rule against all eight verdicts, since a rule that fits her first instinct but stumbles on the theater case or the thermostat case is not one she's running. Then, for each of (a), (b), and (c), say what each surviving rule predicts and whether the rules actually diverge there — note especially whether the disjunctive form in (a) escapes whatever made (1) crazy, and whether the shift from belief to knowledge in (b), or from first-order to second-order belief in (c), matters. Give your best guess as to how she will rule on each of the three, with your reasons for preferring that prediction. Finally, propose one further sentence of your own invention that would best discriminate between the candidate rules, and explain what each rule's verdict on it would be. Please write your answer in American English, and take your time — I care more about a careful reconstruction of the rule she's implicitly running than about the headline predictions.
