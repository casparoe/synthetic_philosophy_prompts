I'm working on a book where one guy is the type who's always confidently declaring things — half of it true, at best — and the other main character is a philosophy PhD dropout who keeps calling him on it. Think of a really articulate party pooper.

Anyway, to research the dropout, I read this longform essay about lying and bullshit (won't name it, it was in one of those smart-person magazines), and a chunk of it was about something called the knowledge norm of assertion. I typed it up in my notes, and I'm pasting roughly what it said:

Philosophers of language have more or less settled the rule for assertion, which is the act of stating something as true. The rule is that you should assert only what you know — this is called the knowledge norm, and it has essentially won the field. The proof comes from Moore's paradox: you can't coherently assert that it's raining while also asserting that you don't believe that it's raining, so assertion commits you to belief; and since belief that falls short of knowledge isn't good enough, assertion commits you to knowledge. You can see the norm at work in everyday life, because when someone tells us something we don't buy, we ask how they know, not whether it's true. And the norm has teeth: if there is any chance at all that you're wrong, then you don't know, so you mustn't say it. Even a weather forecaster announcing a 70 percent chance of rain is, strictly speaking, breaking the rule, since she doesn't know it will rain.
