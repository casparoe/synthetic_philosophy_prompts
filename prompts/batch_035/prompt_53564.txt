I've been collecting my gut verdicts on self-referential sentences and I want to know if one account of truth can capture them all. Plain liar, "this sentence is false": I say it's not true, and also not false. Truth-teller, "this sentence is true": indeterminate, nothing settles it. Curry, "if this sentence is true, then pigs fly": pigs don't fly and I refuse to believe the whole conditional, but I can't find the flaw in the two-step argument that concludes pigs fly, and giving up modus ponens feels off the table. Revenge, "this sentence is not true": it seems not true, but that makes it true, so whatever I said about the plain liar apparently can't just be repeated here. A blackboard pair, "A: B is false" and "B: A is true": both indeterminate like the truth-teller, though A is about another sentence which bugs me for some reason. Is there one story — gaps, gluts, revision, whatever — that gets all of these, and what does it do with the case I keep flip-flopping on: "this sentence is either false or indeterminate"? Answer as a numbered list of exactly five points, each a sentence or two, no intro or conclusion — I'm imagining one point for the principle, one per hard case, and one for the verdict you'd overturn, but you do you.
