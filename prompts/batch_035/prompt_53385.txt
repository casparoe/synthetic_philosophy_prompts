I am reading a popular history of twentieth-century philosophy, which I would prefer to leave unnamed, and its chapter on the decline of logical positivism compresses W. V. Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" into a single paragraph. I will reproduce that paragraph exactly as it appears in the book:

"Quine's celebrated paper is usually remembered as the one that refuted logical positivism. His target was the distinction between analytic truths, which hold in every possible world and can be known independently of experience, and synthetic truths, which merely record how the world happens to be. Kant introduced the distinction, and Carnap built his entire system on it. Quine demonstrated that the distinction cannot be drawn. He showed that every attempt to explain analyticity — as truth by definition, or as truth in virtue of synonymy — runs in a circle, since synonymy can itself be explained only through analyticity. He then attacked the second dogma, the empiricist thesis that all knowledge derives from sense experience, and concluded that our statements are answerable to experience only collectively, never one at a time. The upshot, as Quine himself allowed, is that any statement whatever can be held true whatever happens, provided drastic enough adjustments are made elsewhere in the system; even the law of non-contradiction could, in principle, go the way of phlogiston. In the end Quine proved that there are no analytic truths: logic and mathematics are empirical generalizations, confirmed in just the way physics is, and revisable in the light of further experience. The irony is that by demolishing empiricism's dogmas, Quine demolished empiricism itself — an irony he never acknowledged."
