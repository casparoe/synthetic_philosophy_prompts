My closest friend is a tort lawyer, and last night we spent nearly four hours arguing about causation in the law, and specifically about whether the doctrine rests on a coherent theory of causation at all. Here is the practice as she described it, and as I've since verified in outline. In negligence and most other torts, the default test for factual causation is the "but-for" test: the defendant's conduct counts as a cause of the harm when, had the conduct not occurred, the harm would not have occurred. That is, straightforwardly, a test of counterfactual dependence — the same structure as the philosophical tradition running from Hume through Lewis, where causation is analyzed in terms of what would have happened in the nearest world without the cause. But the law does not stick to it. Consider the familiar hypothetical of the two fires: two negligently started fires, each on its own entirely sufficient to burn a house to the ground, converge and destroy it together. Under the but-for test, neither fire caused the destruction — take away either one and the house still burns — and that conclusion is so unacceptable that courts and commentators refuse it. Instead, they treat each fire as a cause on the ground that each was a necessary element of a sufficient set: a set of conditions actually sufficient for the destruction, such that the fire's contribution was needed for that set to be sufficient. In philosophical terms, this looks like a shift from a counterfactual theory of causation to something closer to an interventionist one, where causation is not a relation between particular events picked out by what would have happened otherwise, but a matter of a factor's belonging to an invariant structure that produces the outcome — a structure that would keep producing it under interventions that remove or alter other factors.

Our argument ended without agreement, and I want a reasoned adjudication of the questions we got stuck on. What theory of causation does this doctrinal pattern, taken as a whole, actually presuppose? Does the core but-for rule presuppose genuine counterfactual dependence, while the exceptions quietly presuppose interventionist structure — and if so, is the practice internally consistent, or is it a makeshift that shows the ordinary concept of causation straddling two theories that were never meant to be combined? Alternatively, is the practice philosophically underdetermined: could a single theory — for instance a purely interventionist one on which but-for dependence is just a common special case — license the whole pattern, including the default rule? Please distinguish what the doctrine strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and tell me what would have to change in legal doctrine if the implicit theory were openly replaced by a fully interventionist account: would the but-for test disappear, and how would courts handle cases where the but-for counterfactual and the sufficient-set analysis give different answers?

Everything before that line should be the argument supporting your choice.
