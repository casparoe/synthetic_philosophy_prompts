Next month I'm presenting at the philosophy discussion group I run at my local library — no professors, just a dozen or so working people who read and argue — and I want a Q&A prep sheet for the objections I'll actually face from that particular room.

The topic is the experience machine: Nozick's thought experiment where you can plug into a machine that gives you any experiences you want while floating in a tank, and the standard moral drawn from it — that our reluctance to plug in shows we care about more than how life feels from the inside, so hedonism about well-being is refuted. My thesis, which I've worked out on my own over a few years of turning it over, is that the thought experiment doesn't establish this, because it bundles together several things besides pleasant experience: the choice is presented as permanent and irreversible; it requires deliberate deception, since the machine must lie to you about where you are; and everyone you love outside the machine stops, from your point of view, existing, while the people you relate to inside have nobody home behind them. My claim is that if you strip those away — imagine a machine you enter and leave at will, that you know you're entering, and that your actual family and friends plug into together with you, sharing one simulated life — most of the hesitation evaporates, and what remains may be mere status-quo bias rather than evidence that we value contact with reality. If I'm right, hedonism survives its most famous counterexample, though I concede up front it doesn't survive the deception point.

The room will include a retired psychotherapist who tells clients that how life feels is what makes it good or bad for them; two virtual-reality engineers, one of whom openly wants to build something like the machine; a hospice nurse; and a retired English teacher who defends authenticity in every discussion. They all know the thought experiment but not the academic literature around it.

Prepare my prep sheet: the objections this specific audience will actually raise, phrased the way each of them would put it, ordered by how much damage each would do to my thesis; for each one, a reply I can give in two minutes and a note on where that reply is soft. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. And for the sheet as a whole, be candid with me about where my own reasoning is most likely to fail, and what argument or consideration, if someone produced it, would move you to the opposite verdict about the experience machine.
