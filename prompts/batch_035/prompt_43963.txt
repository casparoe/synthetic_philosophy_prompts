I teach philosophy of social science (third-year, ~18 students, one 75-minute session) and we're coming up on the "can culture be a cause?" unit — Weber's Protestant ethic and the general worry about explaining anything with values. I want a classroom activity where they run into the problem from the inside instead of me just lecturing about it, since on the page "values can't be causes" sounds like pedantry and "values can be causes" sounds like common sense, and neither feels like anything.

Here's the set-up I imagine, but rework it as you see fit. Each group gets a dossier on a fictional case: two regions of an invented 19th-century country, say the Grand Duchy of Arvel. In the north, followers of a minority ascetic sect (invent your own names for the sect and the majority church, keep them obviously fictional so nobody thinks they're getting real history) show systematically higher literacy, higher savings, earlier adoption of double-entry bookkeeping, more schools per capita; the south, adherents of the majority church, lags on all of it. The dossier should contain maybe 8-10 concrete facts of your own invention, deliberately mixed: some that fit a culture story, some that cut against it — e.g. the north also got a canal 40 years earlier, or the sect only ever took root on the one soil type where dairy farming was profitable, so the regions were already different before anyone had any values about work. I don't want the dossier to secretly favor one side.

Then the structure. Assign different groups different explanatory mandates: one commission must explain the divergence with values, one with material conditions and incentives, one with institutions. Each commission writes up its explanation AND — this is the crux — one risky prediction: a further fact about Arvel, not in the dossier, that their explanation would get right and the rival explanations would get wrong. Then I, playing facilitator/omniscient historian, reveal what the "actual" further facts are, and this is where I need your design help: the revealed facts should be ones that trap the values-commission — the values they cite turn out to be documented only after the economic divergence (so are they cause or rationalization?), or the values are being measured by the very behavior they were supposed to explain. But it has to be a fair trap: the materialist commission should get nicked at least once too (maybe the canal turns out to have been financed by a sect-affiliated bank), so the debrief doesn't collapse into "culture bad, canals good."

The debrief should map what actually happened onto the real debate: Weber's thesis, the standard objections it attracts (circularity — values read off the behavior they explain; the epiphenomenon worry — values as post-hoc justifications; Tawney-style reversal — maybe the economy produced the religion; plain confounding), and then the harder question the game opens up: what would it even take for a value to be a cause? Something like an intervention on values holding everything else fixed, or values as intermediate variables in a chain. And be honest with me about what the activity can't reproduce — Weber's actual method is interpretive, it's about grasping meaning from the inside, and no 75-minute game with invented dossiers can do that part. Tell me exactly where in the debrief I have to supply it by hand, and say so out loud to the students.

One more thing: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this? My guess is at least one commission just writes "it's obviously both" and refuses to commit to a prediction, so tell me how to force the commitment without it looking like I rigged the game against them — because if it looks rigged, the moral they'll take away is about me, not about explanation. Also fine to flag anything else that reliably occurs with this kind of exercise (groups inventing facts that arn't in any dossier, etc.), and how to handle it on the fly.

Logistics: no real money for payoffs, whiteboards and printed handouts only, and the whole thing including debrief has to fit the 75 minutes — give me a timeline that actually adds up.
