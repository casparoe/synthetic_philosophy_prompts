I'm writing a paper on the value of original works of art, and before I engage any particular theory I want the specification settled first: what would an adequate account of the value difference between an original and a perfect copy or forgery have to deliver? Please write the desiderata—the cases such a theory must get right, the distinctions it must respect, and the results about how we actually respond to art that it cannot contradict—with the motivating case or consideration behind each requirement. The purpose matters: I want a theory that shows the premium we place on originals to be rational, not one that merely predicts that people pay it, so a requirement that only a prediction-machine could meet is not a genuine desideratum. Flag any requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied; that tension is what my paper is really about.

Cases the theory must handle, at minimum:

- Perceptual indistinguishability. A painting and a copy indistinguishable to any viewer, under any magnification, forever, still seem to differ in value. Whatever the theory locates the difference in, it cannot be located in anything that shows up in the visual experience of the work taken alone.
- Post-exposure re-evaluation. Han van Meegeren's forged Vermeers were admired and hung as masterpieces until his exposure in 1945; afterward, the very same canvases struck viewers as meretricious. The theory must say whether anything in the works changed, or whether the change was entirely in the spectators—and it must handle the awkward fact that spectators found the later judgments natural rather than embarrassing.
- Delayed revelation and retroactivity. A forgery hangs undetected for fifty years and then is unmasked. Was its value low all along, or does value depend on what people believe at the time? The theory must not collapse the distinction between the value a work has and the value it is believed to have.
- The reverse misattribution. An original dismissed for a century as a later copy is discovered to be autograph. The value rises overnight with no change in the object. The theory must explain what changed and why that change is legitimate rather than mere market psychology.
- Autographic versus allographic works. A painting is devalued by being a copy; a novel, a symphony, or a print struck from the artist's plate is not. The theory must draw this line in a principled way, and must say why performances of a score by different players do not stand to each other as forgery to original.
- The authorized replica. After Duchamp's Fountain was lost, replicas were made with his approval in the 1950s and 1960s, and museums now display them as the work; posthumous bronzes cast from an artist's original waxes command a premium over casts the estate did not authorize. The theory must explain what is preserved by authorization in cases where the artist's hand made nothing, and what is lost without it.
- Self-copying. A painter, decades on, copies her own earlier canvas and sells it as the earlier one. No one else was deceived about authorship in the sense that matters to attribution; the hand is the right hand. Is anything of value lost, and does the theory say so without ad hoc machinery?
- Material survival. Restoration and repair raise a Ship-of-Theseus question: how much of the original material can be replaced before the object is no longer the work, and can the theory state a principled threshold rather than a vague one?

Distinctions the theory must respect:

- Aesthetic value versus market value versus relic value. A lock of a saint's hair is not supposed to be valuable for how it looks, and nobody thinks that value is aesthetic. If the theory grounds the original's premium in a historical connection the object bears, it must say whether it has preserved an aesthetic difference at all or quietly conceded that the difference is relic-like—and whether that concession is a cost.
- Value that depends on the work's actual history versus value that depends on the audience's beliefs, which come apart in both directions in the cases above.
- The difference between a copy and a forgery, where the difference is deception, and between a copy and a mere duplicate, where no one intended to pass anything off.

Finally, tell me whether these three requirements are jointly satisfiable: that the value difference between original and perfect copy is genuinely aesthetic rather than economic or relic-like; that it does not supervene on what anyone believes about the work; and that it can remain unperceivable in principle, as the indistinguishability cases require. If no theory can have all three, say which one you would drop and why. Then, briefly—half a page at most—score the main families (the view that grounds value in historical connection to an act of origination, the Goodmanian view that the difference must be detectable in principle, and the deflationary view that the premium is extra-aesthetic, like a relic's) against your list. I care about the criteria, not the scorecard, so keep the weight there.
