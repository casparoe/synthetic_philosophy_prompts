I am an analytic philosopher of language by training: fluent in the standard apparatus of contextualism, relativism about predicates of personal taste, supervaluationism about vagueness, truth-maker semantics, and paraconsistent logics, and comfortable enough with Sanskrit doctrinal vocabulary as it appears in secondary literature. I have been reading about the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda — the thesis that reality and truth are irreducibly many-sided, so that any single categorical assertion captures only a partial standpoint — together with its two supporting apparatuses: syādvāda, the sevenfold predication in which every judgment is qualified (in some way it is; in some way it is not; in some ways it both is and is not; and so on up to the assertion of ineffability itself), and the doctrine of nayas, the legitimate but partial standpoints from which such judgments are made, each true relative to its own angle of consideration and false only when mistaken for the whole.

Translate these doctrines into my vocabulary as faithfully as you can, component by component. Specifically: does the sevenfold predication come out as a paraconsistent truth predicate, as a discourse-level pragmatics governing assertion rather than a semantics for sentences, as a precursor of supervaluationist "true in some precisi­fications," or as something else entirely? Does the naya doctrine come out as ordinary indexical or contextualist relativization, or as something closer to a principled partition of inquiry into complementary perspectives? Does the central anekāntavāda claim — that one-sided assertion is a failure, and that it is a failure of the assertor rather than of the sentence — have any counterpart in the contemporary debate about faultless disagreement, or does it cut across it?

Then give me the residue report, which is what I actually care about. Say what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion, and what has no expression at all in my vocabulary — for instance, whether the traditional gloss of anekāntavāda as a remedy for attachment to one's own view (a cognitive-ethical defect rather than a merely semantic error) can be said at all within truth-conditional semantics, and whether the traditional charge that it collapses into self-undermining relativism is answered better or worse by my tools than by the Jains' own. Finally, tell me which way you think the residue cuts: does it point to something the analytic debate about relativism and disagreement is missing, or to something the Jain framework cannot say and mine can? I do not want a survey of the secondary literature or a reading list; I want an honest translation whose failures are as informative as its successes.
