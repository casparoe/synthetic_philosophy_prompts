I am a novelist whose fifth book depicts, sympathetically and from the inside, a woman who sells a kidney in a legal, regulated market modeled on Iran's paid-donation system and who, a decade on, counts the transaction among the best decisions of her life. I have been invited to present the book, along with a short defense of the moral vision behind it, to a university bioethics center whose members include a Kantian who holds that selling body parts undermines the very autonomy the transaction is said to express, a political philosopher in the Satz tradition of noxious markets, a natural-law theorist, and a health economist who favors presumed-consent reforms over any market scheme. My argument, in outline: the widespread repugnance at kidney sales is not evidence of a wrong but a reaction to watching desperation converted into liquidity, and the right target is the desperation; that we tolerate compensated plasma, egg donation, and commercial surrogacy without the same revulsion suggests the objection tracks class and status rather than the sanctity of the body; and that fiction's method — sustained identification with a seller who is not deceived, not coerced, and not sorry — is a legitimate test of whether the revulsion survives acquaintance. Prepare my objection sheet: the questions these particular people will actually ask, phrased in their own vocabulary, ordered by how much damage each would do to my case; for each, a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is soft; what I should concede up front; and the one question I am hoping nobody asks, together with the best answer I have to it. Begin your response with the single most damaging objection stated in one sentence — no preamble, no restatement of my setup — and then give the reasoning behind the ordering.
