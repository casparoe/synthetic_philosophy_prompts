I've gotten really into competitive forecasting over the last few months — it started as a side thing for a project at work but now I do it most evenings, and the site I'm on scores everything with the log score. Somewhere down a rabbit hole I ended up reading about epistemic utility theory, and there was this bit about strictly proper scoring rules that said (paraphrasing) that a proper rule is set up so your own current credences are exactly the ones you expect to score best — like, by your own lights, no other credence assignment would do better in expectation. That made sense to me at first.

But then it hit me weird. If that's true, then from where I'm standing right now, changing my credence in anything should look like a worsening. Say I'm at 60% on some resolution, and I fully expect that reading the news over the next month will move me off that number. Haven't I just admitted that I expect to move to a credence I currently judge to be worse than the one I have? So on this framework, updating looks irrational, or at least something a consistent agent should resist — which sounds insane, since the whole edifice gets sold as a defense of Bayesian rationality. I assume the error is somewhere around "expected" meaning something different before vs. after you see evidence (my friend in the phil dept mumbled something about evaluating policies rather than one-off changes, but I honestly didn't follow her), but every time I try to spell it out I talk myself right back into the same trap lol. I feel like theres a clean way to state the resolution that doesn't just amount to "trust me, updating works out for the best" — where exactly am I going wrong?
