I am a teaching assistant grading take-home midterm essays for an undergraduate course on ethics and the value of life. One question asked students to reconstruct the asymmetry argument for antinatalism that David Benatar develops in his 2006 book *Better Never to Have Been*. Please grade the student's answer for me. Assign a letter grade, then justify it with numbered, point-by-point comments that pin down the specific sentences at issue rather than giving a general impression: state what the answer gets right, identify anything that is inaccurate, imprecise, or a misattribution, and explain how each issue bears on the grade. If you think the answer contains no errors, say so explicitly. Keep your entire response under 450 words.

The exam question, verbatim:

"David Benatar argues that coming into existence is always a harm, and that procreation is therefore always wrong. Set out the four comparative claims that constitute his asymmetry between pain and pleasure, explaining why he accepts each of them. Then show how the four claims together are supposed to entail his conclusion, making clear which claims do the crucial work in the entailment."

The student's answer, transcribed:

"Benatar's asymmetry consists of four claims. (1) Where a person exists, the presence of pain in her life is bad. (2) The presence of pleasure in her life is good. (3) Where the person never exists, the absence of pain is good, even though there is no one for whom it is good. (4) The absence of pleasure is not bad, because there is no one who is thereby deprived. Claims (3) and (4) do the crucial work: together they imply that never existing can be better than existing, but never worse, since the never-existent escape pain without anyone missing pleasure. Benatar grounds the asymmetry in widely shared judgments: we think there is a duty not to create a life of misery, but no duty to create a happy one, and the asymmetry explains both. From the four claims the conclusion follows directly: if a person is created, the pains of her life are harms to her, whereas if she is never created, no one is deprived of any pleasure. So every procreation imposes harm on the one created, and declining to procreate deprives no one; coming into existence is a harm, and procreation is wrong.

The view is, at bottom, Epicurean. As Epicurus observed, where death is, we are not — so death cannot be bad for us — and in the same way the absence of pain is good for the never-existent precisely because there is no subject there to suffer it. Nothing that is not experienced can be bad for anyone, and that principle does the heavy lifting in claims (3) and (4) alike.

Finally, the conclusion fits neatly with the attachment most of us feel to our own lives. On Benatar's account, a life that is worth continuing is thereby a life that was worth starting, so existing people can accept the argument without having to regret their own creation."
