I'm a third-year PhD student in social psych, and after yet another lab meeting argument about stats I've been trying to figure out what I actually believe about statistical evidence by writing down my gut verdicts on cases. The problem is I can't tell if there's a principle under them or if I'm just holding positions from different departments in my head at once. So: is there a principle that captures all of these, and if not, which verdicts have to go?

My verdicts, as honestly as I can state them:

1. Lab A preregisters N=200, runs it, gets p=.043. Lab B runs the exact same design, decides mid-experiment to keep going "because it's close," and ends up with the identical data and p=.043. I trust A way more. The numbers are the same, but I think B's result is basically worthless.

2. A Bayesian monitors their posterior after every participant and stops when the Bayes factor crosses 10, ending at the same N and roughly the same conclusion. Honestly this feels fine to me, even though it's the same peeking behavior from case 1. I half remember there's some theorem about stopping rules not mattering if you only look at the likelihood, and my gut seems to agree when it's Bayesians doing the stopping.

3. At the same time, when I talk about the replication crisis I sound like a full frequentist: preregister everything, lower alpha, more N, treat p=.043 as barely evidence at all.

4. An ESP study with a beautiful design, preregistered, p=.001: I still don't believe it. A plausible, boring finding at p=.043 from a decent design: I basically believe it. So my priors are clearly leaking into my verdicts about "the evidence," whatever I tell myself about error rates.

5. Someone measures six outcomes, reports the one that came out, p=.02. Obviously bad. Same six outcomes, all preregistered, Bonferroni-corrected, p=.048: fine, I think. But that means my objection in the first half isn't about the data at all, it's about the selection process, and I can't quite say why the process matters when the data are what they are.

6. An analyst picks an exclusion rule after noticing it helps the result: bad. Picks the same rule after noticing it hurts the result: I'm still a little uneasy but much less bothered, which feels like an asymmetry I can't defend.

7. Bayesian reanalyses of replications using fairly skeptical priors: principled, good. Using an enthusiastic prior that happens to boost your preferred conclusion: feels like cheating to me. So apparently priors are fine when skeptical and cheating when favorable, which cannot be a real principle.

The tension I keep circling is that some of my verdicts are about the data as evidence (where the stopping-rule theorem pushes toward Bayes), some are about process and verifiability (preregistration, pre-commitment), and some are just about what's plausible anyway. A friend suggested the real distinction is between what I should believe when I read a paper versus what norms a field should enforce for publishing, i.e., that maybe I'm a Bayesian as an individual reader and a frequentist as a gatekeeper. That's clever but I'm not sure it survives case 7.

So: give me a principle (or a small set of candidates) stated precisely enough to make predictions, tell me which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and which ones I should genuinely drop, and tell me what it says about the case I keep going back and forth on -- a meta-analysis of ten small unprenregstered studies versus one big preregistered study with the same total N, all reaching the same nominal conclusion. My gut says the preregistered one, but depending on which principle you pick I suspect it should tell me that's dumb, and I want to know if that's true before I say it out loud in lab meeting again.
