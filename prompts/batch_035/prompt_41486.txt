I teach an upper-level course on philosophy of religion and I'm writing the final. Give me one essay question on whether religion actually makes people better — but I want the crux to be a level-reversal puzzle, not the usual recitation of Durkheim. The setup: within most countries, more religious individuals tend to report more trust in strangers and more charitable behavior than their secular neighbors, but across countries the pattern seems to flip — the most secular societies (think Scandinavia) score highest on generalized trust and lowest on corruption, while highly religious countries often score worse on both. So the question has to force students to (a) say what "more moral" would even have to mean for the question to have an answer at all (individual behavior? societal trust? institutions?), (b) deal with the reversal head-on — does it show that religion isn't needed to sustain trust at scale, or is it just confounded, with development driving both secularization and trust so neither is causing the other, and (c) say what kind of evidence could actually settle it, given you can't run the counterfactual of secularizing a country overnight. It should be unanswerable by reciting "religion is the glue of society" or by new-atheist talking points about religious violence — either of those should cost marks. Students get about 45 minutes for it. Include a model answer of roughly 600 words and a marking scheme that can seperate an A from a B by naming the specific moves each requires.
