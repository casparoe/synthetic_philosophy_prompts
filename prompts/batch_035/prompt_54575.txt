For a feature on machine reasoning I have been writing, I interviewed two researchers about Hofstadter's notion of jumping out of the system, using the MU-puzzle from Gödel, Escher, Bach as the running example: starting from the string MI and applying the MIU-system's four rules as long as you like, you never reach MU, and the proof — that no derivable string contains a number of I's divisible by three — requires stepping outside the production rules and reasoning about the system from above.

My first interviewee, a formal-methods computer scientist, argued the whole framing is confused. What Hofstadter calls jumping out is always a move into a metasystem, and there is no reason to think that move is special: Gödel numbering is a mechanical encoding, the invariant argument about the MIU system is itself a theorem of arithmetic, and every escape lands you inside another formalism. On this view, a sufficiently capable reasoner could internalize its own meta-levels the way we implement provers that reason about provers, and nothing principled stands between a machine and that ability. The interesting question is not whether a system can jump out of itself but how cheaply it can buy the next level of indirection.

The second, a cognitive scientist, thought this missed the point by exactly one level. The lesson of the MU-puzzle, she said, is that an agent confined to the rules can crank them forever and remain structurally unable to notice the invariant — the impossibility is not practical but perspectival. So jumping out is a real cognitive act, whatever its metamathematical status: intelligence partly consists of spontaneously re-describing the game one is playing. When the formalist replied that her "jump" is then just another formal operation, one the system could in principle internalize, she conceded the point about principle but rejected its relevance: for actual agents, the question is whether the re-description happens at all, and there is no guarantee that heavy immersion in a system's rules — as in a model trained to complete patterns within one — leaves the snapping-out reflex intact. He answered that this reduces Hofstadter's dramatic notion to an ordinary, learnable skill, meta-level search, with no residue of "strange loop" about it; she answered that calling it learnable does not explain why it so often fails, in humans as much as machines, and that a skill whose conditions of failure we cannot yet state is not yet an ordinary skill.

I report this as a journalist, not a partisan: both struck me as right about something and wrong about something, and my piece needs a verdict more careful than that.
