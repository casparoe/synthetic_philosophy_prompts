Most modern written constitutions contain a rule of change — an amendment clause — and the drafting habit is to let that clause apply to itself: the procedure competent to amend everything else is, on its face, competent to amend the procedure. Yet many of the same constitutions entrench particular contents against amendment, as Germany's Basic Law does by declaring certain principles unamendable even by the prescribed supermajorities, and some courts have gone further and found unamendability in constitutions that never stated it, as India's judiciary did with the basic structure doctrine. The philosopher Peter Suber invented the game Nomic in 1982 to isolate this tangle: it opens with a small rule-set divided into mutable and immutable rules, but the protection on the immutable rules is deliberately light, since a permitted move is to transmute an immutable rule into a mutable one, after which it can be amended like any other. Suber's point was that the apparent paradox of a rule authorising its own change is one that functioning legal systems live with daily, in statute and constitution alike.

I want you to tell me what theory of legal validity and authority this cluster of practices presupposes. Reconstruct the implicit theory rather than merely labelling it. In particular: does self-applicable amendment require dealing with the sort of worry Alf Ross raised about self-referential norms — on his view a statute that declared its own invalidity, and perhaps by extension one that authorised its own repeal or replacement, suffers a self-reference failure that renders it void rather than merely changed — or does the practice quietly presuppose a Hartian picture, on which validity flows from a rule of recognition grounded in official acceptance rather than in a further rule, so that self-amendment is harmless because the amending power was never a rule of the same logical kind as the rules it amends? Does the entrenchment half of the practice presuppose a distinction between constituted and constituent power that the self-amendment half quietly denies? And can the practice really be internally consistent, given that the unamendable provisions were themselves adopted by the very amendment-authorising power they now bind — or is the Indian judiciary's stance the candid one, namely that a hierarchy of powers must at some point be asserted rather than derived?
