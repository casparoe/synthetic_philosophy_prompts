Avicenna's flying man thought experiment asks us to imagine a person created fully formed in mid-air, blindfolded, with limbs outstretched so that nothing touches them, and no sounds reaching them. He claimed that even in this state of total sensory deprivation, the person would still be sure of their own existence as a thinking thing, which he took to show that the self is known directly rather than through the senses or the body. I've been thinking about how this compares to large language models: they never see, hear, or touch anything either, they only process language, and they can describe themselves when asked. If Avicenna could be shown how these systems actually work, do you think he would conclude that they possess the kind of self-awareness he attributed to the flying man, or would he say the comparison fails because the flying man has an immaterial soul while a machine is purely material? I'm also unsure whether his argument cuts both ways here: either sensory deprivation is irrelevant to self-awareness and machines might in principle have it, or there is something the flying man has that goes beyond merely having no sensory input. Which reading of the experiment do you find more defensible, and why?
