The Carnegie Hero Fund, a private foundation established in Pittsburgh in 1904, awards the Carnegie Medal to civilians in the United States and Canada who knowingly and voluntarily risk death or serious physical injury to an extraordinary degree in saving, or attempting to save, another person's life — and it accompanies the medal with scholarship aid, disability assistance, and death benefits for the dependents of rescuers killed in the attempt. Its eligibility rules are strict. Members of the armed forces, firefighters, police, and others whose vocations require rescue work are generally excluded unless the act was clearly beyond the line of duty; the rescuer must bear no responsibility for the peril, so anyone who caused the danger is ineligible; family members of the victim are excluded except where the rescuer died or was gravely injured; and children too young to comprehend the risk do not qualify. Meanwhile, the legal background is strikingly different: common-law jurisdictions impose no general duty to aid a stranger in peril even at trivial cost to oneself, whereas several civil-law systems criminalize the failure to render easy assistance.

What theory of obligation and merit does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct its implicit account of duty and supererogation, attending in particular to: the exclusion of line-of-duty rescuers, which seems to presuppose that duty is indexed to role rather than to anything about the act itself; the family-member exception, which seems to presuppose both that special relationships generate duties and that dying or being gravely injured can push a duty-conforming act beyond duty; the extraordinary-risk threshold, which seems to presuppose a permission to weigh one's own life very heavily against a stranger's; the awarding of medals for failed attempts, which presupposes that merit attaches to risk willingly run rather than to lives saved; and the cash payments, which presuppose that society compensates sacrifice it did not require — even though it does, after all, pay firefighters for risk they were obligated to take. Where does the practice strictly presuppose one theory of these matters rather than merely fitting several, and what would have to change — in the eligibility rules, the payments, or the no-duty legal background — if we adopted instead the view that easy rescue is simply obligatory?
