I am drafting a novel whose central character is a retired sinologist dying of motor neurone disease, a woman who has spent forty years teaching the Zhuangzi and now finds that none of it is helping her. To understand what I can honestly do with her, I want a serious reading of Tolstoy's The Death of Ivan Ilyich against the Zhuangian attitude toward death — not as an essay in comparison but as a test of whether the two views can inhabit the same person. Consider, in particular, the famous anecdotes: Zhuangzi drumming on a tub and singing after his wife's death, once his initial grief has given way to reflection on the flux of qi from which she condensed and back into which she has returned; his conversation with the skull, who finds death preferable to the anxieties of life; and his instruction that at his own burial his body be simply left to heaven and earth, the sun, moon, and stars as his burial jade, since to fuss over the corpse would be to interfere with a transformation already underway. Set these against Ivan Ilyich's long resistance, his physical revulsion, his envy of the healthy, and his final hours — including Gerasim, the peasant servant whose easy, unembarrassed familiarity with death and dying is presented as something Ivan Ilyich's whole class has lost.

My questions are these. First, is Gerasim a Zhuangian figure, or is he something else — a Christian simplicity of a kind the Zhuangzi neither promises nor needs? Second, where does Ivan Ilyich's arc genuinely vindicate something Zhuangian — the idea that suffering is intensified by clinging to a false identity, that the long illness strips away an artificial person and reveals something that can let go — and where does the story refuse the reading, as with Ivan Ilyich's terror, which lasts months and does not seem to be a merely cognitive error he could have thought his way out of? Third, and most important for my novel: if a person steeped in Zhuangzi's arguments for the harmlessness of death, the arbitrariness of the self/other boundary, and the wisdom of according with change nonetheless lies awake at night in panic, what does that failure show? Is it bad faith or weak practice on her part, a defect in the Zhuangian view itself, or evidence — as some interprevers of Zhuangzi would insist — that the anecdotes are not arguments at all but performances of a freedom that can't be extracted and applied like a doctrine? I would like a reading that is fair to both texts, willing to say where the comparison breaks down, and that ends by telling me what the two together suggest a novelist can and cannot honestly depict about the death of a philosopher.
