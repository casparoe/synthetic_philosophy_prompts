You are a professional epistemologist whose speciality is the area around the ethics of belief, and you are good at telling genuinely distinct concepts apart from near neighbours that share a name. Please think carefully before answering and survey the plausible candidates rather than settling on the first one that sounds close; if what I describe fits nothing established, say so plainly rather than stretching a term to cover it.

I keep running into the following asymmetry, and I cannot find a name for it. When someone believes something ahead of sufficient evidence and turns out to be wrong, they are blamed as credulous or reckless. When someone withholds belief in the same situation and, because they withheld, misses out on something good — the trust that was needed to save a collaboration, the confidence that was needed to attempt something difficult, the early commitment to an idea that needed early believers in order to get off the ground at all — nobody is blamed. The withholder is called prudent, cautious, sensible, even when the loss traces back to the withholding just as directly as the credulous person's loss traces back to their believing. It is as though the withholding of belief were thought to be an action with no costs of its own, a neutral waiting room outside the flow of events, whereas believing is thought to put you inside the flow. But in the cases I care about, the option is forced in William James's sense: the evidence will not arrive in time, and by the time it arrives, the belief would make no difference — the friend will have taken the offer elsewhere, the attempt will not have been made. In those cases waiting is choosing, and it carries a risk of exactly the same kind as the risk of believing.

Two features seem to me essential to the phenomenon, and I want them preserved in whatever we call it. First, it is an asymmetry of appraisal, not of fact: the world punishes both parties about equally often; only the blame is distributed unequally. Second, it is not about wishful thinking — the early believer need not want the belief to be true; they may simply judge that the expected value lies with believing, and still be blamed in a way the withholder never is.

I know this is adjacent to the James–Clifford debate — Clifford's duty to believe only on sufficient evidence, and James's permission for forced and momentous options. James does remark, in effect, that a policy of thinking which would prevent one from acknowledging certain kinds of truth, were those kinds of truth really there, would be an irrational policy; but that is a complaint against a policy, not a name for the asymmetry of blame I am after. Clifford, for his part, treats the duty of inquiry as obviously worth its costs. I have also wondered whether the right home is the recent work on suspension of belief as an attitude in its own right, or the literature on epistemic versus practical rationality, or something in the moral luck discussion — the early believer who turns out right gets no credit, which looks structurally like luck. I cannot tell which of these, if any, fits, and I do not want the concept stretched to cover my case.

So, my question: does this asymmetry have an established name? If not, I would like you to coin one, with a definition precise enough to distinguish it from (a) the Jamesian permission to believe on passional grounds, (b) plain evidentialism, (c) risk aversion, and (d) the general observation that inaction is often a form of action. 2. Nearest concepts — the two or three closest existing ideas, and for each, what it gets right about my description and where it parts company from it. (roughly 300 words)

4. Proposed term — if nothing established fits, a name with a one-sentence definition, plus a note on which further cases the name should and should not be applied to. (roughly 150 words)

The name itself matters little to me; the carving does. A bare label, or a label attached to a paragraph of throat-clearing, will not be useful. If you think the phenomenon decomposes into two or three already-named things with no residue, say that, and show me where the seams are.
