I am a political philosopher who has spent most of my career defending cosmopolitanism: in print I have argued that compatriots' claims on one another derive from shared subjection to a coercive political scheme, not from nationality itself, and that whatever special duties follow from shared citizenship do not diminish the duty to assist distant strangers in serious need. The federal courthouse in my city has now asked me to give the keynote address at a naturalization ceremony, and I find myself facing a genuinely awkward composition problem.

The audience will be mixed. Roughly a hundred new citizens will take the oath that morning, along with their families — many of whom remain citizens of other countries and, in a good number of cases, depend on remittances the new citizens send home. The presiding judge and the court officials expect a warm, celebratory affirmation of what citizenship means, and I have been politely but firmly told that the occasion is not the place for anything controversial. My own colleagues, meanwhile, will read the transcript, and they will notice immediately if I smuggle in the claim that compatriots, as such, are owed more of our concern than anyone else — a claim I have spent twenty years arguing against.

So I need a speech that threads a needle. It must convey to the new citizens that what they are taking on is genuinely significant — more than a passport and a convenient tax arrangement — without asserting that their obligations to their new compatriots now outrank their obligations to their parents in Manila or their cousins in Lagos. It must celebrate the particular political community they are joining while honoring, rather than quietly shaming, the transnational ties that most of them will keep. And it must land as a welcome, not as a lecture: several listeners will be working in their second or third language, and the judge will cut me off if I turn philosophical.

Please write the speech. Aim for roughly eight hundred words in a ceremonial register, in American English. Whatever view of special obligations you end up taking, make sure the argument does real structural work — determining what the speech can and cannot say — rather than decorating the margins. If you can give me one moment that would move a judge and a cosmopolitan theorist for the same reason, so much the better.
