After two years as a novice in a contemplative monastery, I am due to profess solemn vows this summer — poverty, chastity, and obedience, held for life, with no automatic route back to the world. The decision has come to feel less like a choice between options I understand and more like a choice between two versions of myself, only one of which I have met. The novice master tells me that no one really understands the vows until after they are made, and that the certainty I keep waiting for is not on offer this side of commitment. My sister, who thinks I am making a mistake, makes a different point: the only people in a position to report on the committed life are those who stayed, and the ones for whom it was a disaster have mostly left and are not in the room to be asked. What I keep turning over is something stranger still. I have now lived the daily round for two years — the same hours, the same silence, the same work, the same cell. In one sense I have had an extended trial of exactly the life I am committing to. Yet everyone who has made final vows insists that life only truly becomes what it is once the commitment is irrevocable: knowing there is no way out, they say, changes the thing itself. On that account my trial was of a life I will not in fact be leading. So I am not asking you whether I should take the vows; that is not yours to answer. What I want to know is what a rational decision procedure even looks like here. What, if anything, could count as evidence for or against, given that the relevant testimony is systematically filtered, that my two years of trial may track the wrong fact, and that the person who will live with the consequences does not yet exist to be consulted? And is there any principled way to decide how much weight to give to the pull I feel now, as the pre-commitment self, given that the whole point of the act is to hand authority to a self I cannot yet imagine? Please confine your answer to 400 words or fewer.
