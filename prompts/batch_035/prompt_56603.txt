I write novels for a living and somehow got roped into running a ninety-minute session at a writers' retreat on what emotions actually are -- like, philosophically. The hook is that it matters for us practically: if you believe an emotion is basically a judgment (a way of thinking about the world, like "this dog is dangerous" that happens to come with heat attached), you'll write fear one way, as the character's appraisal leaking into their body. If you think an emotion is more like a perception (the body's read on a situation, which can be accurate or off, the way your eyes can be fooled), you'll write it another way, closer to how you'd write a character seeing something. And if it's basically just a feeling-state, raw and non-representational, that's a third kind of scene entirely. So the three-way fight -- judgment theories, perceptual theories, pure feeling theories -- is the thing I want to get across to about fifteen fiction writers, none of whom have read any philosophy since maybe one intro course twenty years ago.

But I don't want you to just explain the theories to me. I want a teaching plan. Concretely: what has to go first in the ninety minutes and why (I suspect you can't do perceptual theories before judgments, but I'm honestly not sure), what single carrying example I should build the whole session around -- and here's a constraint, it should be an emotion that a novelist would plausibly have to write, so maybe not the usual fake examples, though tell me if a fake example does some job a realistic one can't. The example needs to be rich enough that all three theories can get a grip on it and yield different writing advice, and ideally it should have some structure that makes the theories visibly disagree rather than just redescribe the same thing in different words.

Then the misconceptions. I can already predict one: my crowd will hear "emotions are judgments" and think I mean emotions are deliberate or conscious, that the character decides to be afraid, and the room will revolt because that's obviously wrong -- so I need to know exactly where in the sequence to block that off. Another one I'm worried about is people treating "perception" as meaning the emotion is always of something out in the world, and then getting stuck on things like shame or nostalgia where it's murky what's even being perceived. There's also the classic puzzle of recalcitrant emotions -- being afraid of flying while fully believing it's safe -- which is actually the reason I got interested in this, because that's precisely the kind of character moment we're always trying to render, but I don't know whether that puzzle should come early as the hook or late as the payoff, and I suspect it's load-bearing for the order of everything else.

I'd also like a check I can do at the end, some exercise or question where the writers show me they can now think in terms of the three theories instead of just nodding along -- ideally something that forces them to make a writing choice they couldn't have articulated a reason for at the start.

And tell me what to flat-out refuse to cover in ninety minutes, because I know myself, I will try to cram in the mind-body stuff and the whole question of whether AI characters in fiction could "really" have emotions, and someone will bring up that one novel where the character can't feel anything and I'll lose forty minutes. What do I explicitly park for another session, and do I say so upfront or just quietly cut it?

One formatting thing: whatever you write back, please keep it as plain continuous prose, no bullet points, no headers, no bold or tables -- I want to read it like a long letter, not skim a powerpoint. And it's fine to be a bit rough, I'd rather have your actual thinking than something polished into shapelessness.
