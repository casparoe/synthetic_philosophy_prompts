I'm a PhD student in political theory writing a conference paper that reads the Flint water crisis through Dewey's political epistemology -- mainly the account of publics from The Public and Its Problems plus his broader experimentalism about inquiry. You're a Dewey scholar who knows the pragmatist corpus cold, so please actually think this through rather than riffing: sketch the mapping to yourself first, then give me the disciplined version.

Here's the episode as I'm framing it (correct me if I've got the facts wrong, but I believe this is right): in 2014 Flint, under a state-appointed emergency manager put in place to cut costs, switched it's water source to the Flint River; corrosion control wasn't maintained, so lead from old pipes leached into the drinking water; residents complained about the color, smell, and health effects and were repeatedly assured by officials that the water was fine; the issue was finally forced from outside -- a Virginia Tech engineering lab tested tap water and found lead levels way above the federal action level, and a Flint pediatrician showed elevated blood-lead levels in children -- and only after months of denial did the authorities concede and switch back.

My paper's claim is that this is close to a textbook Deweyan case: indirect consequences of a transaction (a cost-saving decision whose effects fell on people outside the decision), a public that existed in fact before it existed for itself, officials mistaking a political question for a settled technical one, and the public finally recognizing itself through communication and citizen-initiated science rather than through the official expert apparatus. But I want to stress-test the reading, not just illustrate it. So: work the mapping carefully -- what exactly is the "transaction," who counts as the public and at what point, what plays the role of inquiry versus experiment -- and then tell me where the framework strains. Does governance by appointed emergency managers fit Dewey's story about how publics get eclipsed, or does it need a category he simply didn't have? Is "experimentalism" even the right word for what the outside engineers and the pediatrician did, or is that just evidence-gathering that we're flattering by calling experiment? And run it the other way too: what does Flint show about Dewey that his own theory doesn't anticipate -- does the fact that the residents were mostly poor and Black, and that the damage was to children's bodies in a way no subsequent inquiry can reverse, break the pragmatist faith that problems get dissolved once a public organizes and communicates? I don't want a hagiography of pragmatism or a hit piece; I want to know where the theory is genuinely silent rather than forced to speak.
