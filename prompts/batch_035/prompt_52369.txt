Quick background: I'm nobody special, no philosophy background, I just got to thinking. In our house I've somehow become the designated bug-and-mouse person, and the longer I do it the more it bugs me (sorry) that I have no consistent policy at all. Some things get the cup-and-postcard ride to the garden — spiders, mostly, and moths when I'm feeling patient. Mosquitos get swatted on sight. Flies get the vacuum in summer regardless of how I felt about them a minute earlier. Last month I stood over a single moth in the kitchen for like ten minutes trying to decide, and my family will not let me live it down. My wife's position is basically "kill by default, it's a bug", mine is more like "the killing needs a reason", and I'd like a procedure that settles our arguments, or at least shows exactly where it can't and we just have to pick.

So what I want is a ladder of what deserves what level of consideration, and then a set of tests for which rung something lands on, that I can actually run in ten seconds at midnight with a shoe in one hand and a cup in the other. For the ladder I've been working off my own rough criteria and I'd like you to fix them up or throw them out: does it look after its young rather than just laying eggs and leaving, does it learn from bad experiences over its life instead of only flinching, does it have eyes that point somewhere in particular (my brother in law swears front-facing eyes = hunter = smarter, side-facing eyes = basically food with legs, and honestly I can't tell if that's a real signal or a thing he made up), does it spend seconds working to escape a bad spot instead of milliseconds. And I need the procedure to be honest about where the ladder stops — I mow the lawn without a second thought and I want to know whether that should bother me or whether plants just arent on the ladder, same question for the sourdough starter honestly, and same question for our smart thermostat, which my brother in law insists "senses" the cold in exactly the way the bug senses the wall. I'm pretty sure that one's wrong but I couldn't tell you the difference in words, and I want the procedure to tell me.

Then run the thing on four real cases so I can see it work: a spider in the shower at midnight, moths that got into the flour bin, a wasp nest right above the door the kids use, and mice in the garage — that last one being where I think the procedure will give me an answer I won't like, because I use the live traps and drive them out to the field, and I've read that can be worse for them than people assume, so I want you to actually commit to whatever the procedure says there even if it annoys me. And mark clearly which steps are judgement calls dressed up as steps, because if the whole thing secretly comes down to how squeamish I feel that night I'd rather know now. Keep it at the level of what I can observe myself in the moment — I'm not doing brain surgery on a wasp to find out its rung.
