I'm a PhD student in economics with a strong background in analytic philosophy, and I've decided to spend the next three years working on decision-making under deep uncertainty rather than on empirical topics. Keynes's Treatise on Probability is now a century old, and I keep hearing two opposite things about it: that its central ideas were absorbed into the mainstream, or that they were refuted and are only of historical interest. Ramsey's early critique attacked the idea that probability is a relation we can directly perceive; Savage-style Bayesianism then built a decision theory that assumes fully comparable, numerical probabilities; and behavioral finance largely treats departures from that framework as bias rather than as evidence about the structure of uncertainty itself. Meanwhile, Keynes's own views — that probabilities are sometimes non-numerical and not always comparable across propositions, that new evidence can raise what he called the weight of an argument without changing its probability, and that his later economics leaned on conventions and confidence where expectation cannot be calculated — sit awkwardly against all three of those developments.

What I want from you is a prioritized research agenda for someone with my background and my time budget. Candidate questions I'm aware of include: whether incomparable probabilities can ground a rigorous decision theory that doesn't collapse into standard expected utility; whether Keynesian weight of evidence captures anything not already handled by imprecise-probability frameworks; whether there is a defensible middle position between treating reliance on conventions and animal spirits as a failure of rationality and treating it as the only rational response to unmeasurable uncertainty; and how much of the Treatise survives Ramsey's critique and later ones. But don't feel bound to this list — add or drop questions as you see fit.

Please structure your answer in four sections, in this order: (1) My ranking — a numbered list of the questions you'd prioritize, one sentence each; (2) Criteria — about 150 words on what you are prioritizing for, given my background and constraints, and the trade-offs between importance, tractability, and neglectedness; (3) Defense — roughly 500 words explaining, question by question, why each sits where it does, making explicit which questions are upstream of which others and which are dead ends or merely historical; (4) What would change my mind — about 100 words on what conceptual or empirical developments would reshuffle the ranking.
