Write me a dialogue between two philosophers arguing over drinks about whether Lewis's 1976 triviality proof actually kills the idea that P(if A then B) = P(B|A) — the "Equation". The two characters:

1) Dana, a truth-conditionalist: she thinks indicative conditionals are ordinary propositions with truth conditions (she's relaxed about whether they're Stalnakerian or material-conditional-ish), and she thinks the Equation is basically right as a constraint but the triviality proof shows you have to deny something else — her preferred escape is to deny that conditionalization is the only rational update rule, or to restrict the Equation to "reasonable" posteriors somehow. She should actually sketch the proof rather than gesture at it: set it up with the law of total probability, splitting on B and not-B, and show how you get P(B|A) = P(B) if the Equation holds in every posterior.

2) Mina, an Adamsian: she thinks indicatives lack truth values outright — they have assertibility conditions, not truth conditions — and that's exactly what lets her keep the Equation without triviality, since Lewis's argument needs conditionals to be propositions you can conditionalize on. Her weak spots, which Dana should press: what happens to negated conditionals, to compounds like "either the match is cancelled or, if it isn't, it'll be sold out", to conditionals embedded in other conditionals, and to the fact that people seem to make bets that get called off when the antecedent is false.

Give them a concrete toy case to argue over: a fair six-sided die about to be rolled, with A = "the die comes up even" and B = "the die comes up on 2", so P(B|A) = 1/3. Mina should use the called-off-bet structure (payoff conditional on A, stake returned if not-A) to argue the Equation is vindicated by Dutch book reasoning; Dana should counter that a bet you can't be forced to settle isn't a bet on a proposition, it's a different animal, and that Mina is quietly changing the subject from truth to utility.

Requirements: both of them are good at this — neither is a strawman, each concedes small points and lands real blows on the other. The dialogue should contain actual back-and-forth at the level of the derivations and the specific escapes, not just trading slogans. At least one point where the discussion gets down to the concrete numbers on the die (e.g., whether Mina's view assigns anything at all to "it's false that if the die is even it's a 2", and what Dana thinks that probability should be). End unresolved but with each having sharpened the crux for the reader: whether keeping the Equation is worth giving up propositionality, or keeping propositionality is worth giving up the Equation. Around 900-1200 words, natural speech, they can interrupt each other, references to Lewis and Adams by name are fine but keep the math self-contained. Also please dont make Dana win just because she has the "famous result" on her side — Mina's position was taken seriously for decades and the dialogue should show why.
