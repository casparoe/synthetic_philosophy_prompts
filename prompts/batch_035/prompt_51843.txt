I'm a software engineer, and my only exposure to philosophy has been whatever drifts into engineering blogs, so apologies if I'm rediscovering something with a three-hundred-year history under a different name. But I've noticed a recurring pattern in how my industry handles certain kinds of failed bets, and I'd like to know whether there's an established concept for it.

Concretely: early in my career I watched a company rearchitect a monolith into microservices on the promise that independently deployable services would let small teams move fast. What we got instead was a distributed monolith: cascading outages, data inconsistencies, and weeks of debugging latency across service boundaries. The striking part was not the failure but what followed. Every failure was diagnosed as insufficient commitment — we hadn't automated testing enough, hadn't adopted a service mesh, hadn't restructured the teams. Each rescue was individually reasonable, and none of them was ever counted as a cost of the original bet. Then, a few years later, the industry quietly settled on a pragmatic hybrid, and the people who had made the original bet started telling the story as a vindication: the industry now "does microservices," by which they meant the hybrid that the original bet was defined against. The strong version of the claim was simply forgotten, and its abandonment was retold as its triumph.

I've since seen the same shape elsewhere: a grand promise that approach X will render Y obsolete, a pile of failures attributed to doing X too timidly, an accumulation of individually sensible patches that never get tallied, and finally a settlement on X-plus-Y that gets presented as X all along. People who remember the original strong claims are dismissed as fighting the last war.

Here's what I want, and please don't just hand me a label and stop. Is there a name for this exact configuration — the structured forgetting, the patch accounting that treats every rescue as free, the retroactive vindication? I'm aware of Kuhn's paradigm shifts, and of things like sunk cost and survivorship bias, but none of them seems to be quite this. So, first, what are the nearest established concepts, and precisely how does each fail to fit? Second, and more importantly: a genuinely good idea also matures through failures and course corrections, so what distinguishes the pattern I've described from the healthy evolution of an idea? If there's a principled way to tell the pathological version from the healthy one — some mark observable before the fact rather than after — I'd like to know it. And if the literature has no exact match, I'd rather you said so and carved the concept out yourself: what it is, what it isn't, and where its edges lie.
