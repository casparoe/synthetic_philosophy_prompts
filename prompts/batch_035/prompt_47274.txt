I host a small philosophy podcast and next week's guest is a philosopher who works on hinge epistemology -- the Wittgenstein-inspired view (mostly from On Certainty) that all empirical inquiry rests on hinge certainties: commitments like that you have a body, that the earth existed for ages before you were born, that your name is what you think it is, which aren't held on evidence and aren't up for rational doubt because they're the backdrop against which evidence for anything else gets assessed. She's known for the stronger and more controversial step of saying hinges aren't even candidates for knowledge, since knowing something requires being able to be wrong about it in a way that matters, and she describes them instead as the axis around which other judgments turn.

I'd rather not have her just restate the view for twenty minutes, so draft me eight to ten interview questions that press where the literature actually presses. At minimum I want one question on the conservatism worry -- that a view on which some of my commitments are immune to counter-evidence just hands people a license to never revisit their political or religious convictions, and a follow-up keyed to whatever reply she's most likely to give (probably something about hinges being non-beliefs rather than beliefs). One on the conspiracy theorist: flat-earthers and cult members also have commitments that no evidence budges, so I want her to tell me what makes a hinge a *good* hinge, and a follow-up about whether that standard is itself hinge-relative. One on change over time: Wittgenstein has the image of a riverbed of hardened propositions that can shift, but the shifting isn't caused by the water of ordinary evidence, and I want to press her on how a hinge revision could ever be rationally explicable rather than just something that happens to you. And one on where hinges fit in the Agrippan trilemma -- are they supposed to be a genuine fourth option beyond regress, circularity, and arbitrary stopping points, or just a psychological description of the arbitrary stopping point?

A few more things: my co-host is going to bring up Moore-style hand-waving arguments, so one question should get her take on why the epistemologist who shouts about his own hand gets diagnosed as having mislocated the certainty rather than as having proven anything. And keep the questions concrete, no open-ended stuff like what drew her to Wittgenstein -- she's said in other interviews she finds that question tedious and I don't want to loose twenty minutes of my runtime on it. I tried skimming On Certainty myself to prep and honestly it reads like disjointed jottings, so if one of your questions can get her to say something about why it's written like that, bonus points. Total runtime is about 45 minutes so dont be afraid to make a couple of the questions meaty, with sub-parts.
