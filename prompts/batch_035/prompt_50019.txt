Two members of my probability reading group, Ayesha and Ben, spent last Thursday's session arguing about the Sleeping Beauty problem, and they have asked me to referee the exchange. I could not in good conscience crown a winner, and I would be grateful if you would do it for me: go through their exchange in order, adjudicate each move, and then answer the specific questions I list at the end. Both of them are philosophically able, so please do not assume that either has made a schoolboy error; where a move is bad, say precisely why.

The protocol under discussion, so that we are agreed on the setup: Beauty is put to sleep on Sunday evening and a fair coin is tossed. If it lands heads, she is woken once, on Monday, interviewed, and then sedated with the interview erased from her memory. If it lands tails, she is woken, interviewed, sedated and erased on both Monday and Tuesday. In each interview she is asked how confident she is that the coin landed heads. She knows the entire protocol in advance.

Their exchange, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Ayesha opened with the awakening-counting argument. Run the experiment many times, she said, and two out of every three interviews occur in tails-runs. On waking, Beauty knows only that she is in an interview, and has no more specific self-locating information than that; so her credence in heads should be one third.

(2) Ben replied that nothing happens to Beauty on Monday that she did not fully expect on Sunday. The coin is fair, the protocol is common knowledge, and the interview itself — the white room, the question, the mild disorientation — is guaranteed to occur on Monday whether the coin lands heads or tails. Credence should not shift absent new evidence, and there is none; her answer should remain one half.

(3) Ayesha pressed the point about evidence. The relevant information is not about the coin, she said, but about Beauty's own position in time: on Sunday she knew which day it was, and on waking she has lost that. Ayesha then escalated with an exaggeration: suppose tails bought a thousand interviews rather than two. Could Ben really maintain that Beauty's confidence in heads should stay at one half while she serves out a thousand indistinguishable interrogations? And if he concedes that it should fall even slightly, then the no-new-evidence principle is dead, and the only remaining question is how far it falls.

(4) Ben called this a confusion of bookkeeping with epistemology. Beauty's credence, he said, is about the coin, and the coin's long-run frequency is one half no matter how often each outcome wakes her; she can concede that most interviews occur in tails-runs without moving her credence an inch, just as she can concede that most lottery tickets are sold on Fridays without it following that Friday tickets are likelier to win. On the exaggeration, he was unmoved: if she would bet on heads before the experiment, she should bet on heads during it, however many times tails subjects her to the question.

(5) Ayesha then produced a betting argument. Imagine a bookmaker who offers, at every interview, an even-odds bet on heads — a £1 stake repaid as £2 if the coin landed heads, and nothing otherwise, with all bets settled at the end of the experiment. A halfer must regard even odds on heads as exactly fair, and should therefore advise Beauty to accept at every interview. In a heads-run she is interviewed once and finishes £1 up; in a tails-run she is interviewed twice and finishes £2 down. Averaging over the fair coin, the halfer's advice loses 50p per experiment even though every single bet looked fair at the moment it was placed. A thirder, valuing heads at one third, would refuse those terms and cannot be exploited in this way. If credences are anything, Ayesha said, they are what per-interview fair prices are made of.

(6) Ben answered that the bookmaker's profit comes from the settlement structure rather than from any fact about Beauty's beliefs: being invited to stake twice precisely when heads is false is a feature of the offer, and a halfer who remembers the protocol can decline the series, since she knows from Sunday that the offer will recur and can price the whole menu rather than each awakening in isolation. Change the ground rules, he continued, so that the bookmaker may accept one bet per experiment, placed at an interview chosen by lot: even odds on heads are then exactly fair by his lights, while the thirder, who wants odds of two-to-one against heads before she will bet on it at all, declines and forgoes a fair bet. The original book, he concluded, shows only that per-interview and per-experiment accounting come apart; it does not show whose credence is wrong.

(7) Finally, Ayesha proposed what she called a consistency test, the Mirror protocol: heads now buys interviews on both Monday and Tuesday, while tails buys a single interview, on Monday or Tuesday as a second fair coin decides. By her method, Beauty should now say two thirds for heads — and, notably, the same whether she is told it is Monday or Tuesday. By Ben's, she says one half on either day. Ben retorted that this is a reductio of her method rather than a test of his: a fair coin about which nothing whatsoever has been observed comes to be believed at two-thirds confidence purely because one of its outcomes generates more interviews, which is not epistemology but a preference for crowded outcomes. Ayesha finished with the claim that the bias is not toward crowded outcomes but toward worlds containing more of Beauty-now: she should reason as if sampled at random from the interviews the protocol generates, whatever their distribution.

Please adjudicate as follows:

(a) Go through moves (1) to (7) in order, saying who is right about each specific point and why, rather than merely declaring an overall winner.

(b) Is Ben's claim that Beauty receives no new information correct, and — whether or not it is — does it actually entail a credence of one half? Or is Ayesha right that something is lost on waking even if nothing is gained?

(c) In the betting exchange, which settlement structure, if either, is the proper test of credence, and what exactly does the halfer's exploitability under the first structure show?

(d) In the Mirror protocol, what credence in heads should Beauty actually give, and does that answer embarrass Ayesha's method, Ben's, both, or neither?

(e) Who comes out more right overall, and what, if anything, have both of them missed?
