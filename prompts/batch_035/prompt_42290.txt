so heres a question thats been bugging me and i want you to actually work through it. i sit on my university's IRB — the committee that reviews and approves psychology studies involving human subjects — and i've noticed something about how we handle deception. maybe you're familiar with the setup: after the Tuskegee scandal and the Milgram experiments, review boards became a big deal, and the current rulebook (in the US it's the "Common Rule") says studies need informed consent. except psychology basically can't function without deceiving people, so there's a carve-out. if the study needs to lie to subjects — fake feedback about their personality, a confederate pretending to be another subject, secretly measuring something else — the board can approve it anyway, PROVIDED the researchers debrief everyone afterwards: sit them down, explain what was actually going on, what the deception was for, and let them withdraw their data if they're upset. some boards even require that the subject be given the option to have their data destroyed post-hoc.

heres the thing. i got into a conversation with a collegue about whether this makes sense, and we realized neither of us could say what theory of lying the whole practice is built on. it's clearly not augustine's — he thought every lie is wrong, full stop, even to save a life or to protect someone from bad news, because a lie corrupts your own soul and the debriefing obviously doesn't un-lie the lie. the modern practice seems to assume instead something like: a lie is wrong insofar as it harms the person or violates their autonomy, and both of those can be fixed retroactively. the debrief "cures" the lie the way an antidote cures poison. and the retroactive data-withdrawal option seems to assume consent can be manufactured backwards — you consent to having been deceived, after the fact.

but that can't be the whole story, can it? because the rest of the consent framework insists that consent has to be informed and given BEFORE the thing happens. we treat prior-uninformed consent as worthless everywhere else (you can't consent in advance to a surgery without knowing what it is, and you can't be enrolled in a study without knowing you're in one), but somehow "you already agreed to something you'd have agreed to, probably" is fine here. and notice the debrief itself is delivered by the same person who just lied to your face — what exactly guarantees they're telling the truth NOW? also, and this is the part that really gets me: boards barely blink at deception-by-omission and technically-true misdirection. a researcher can't tell you a fake story about your test results, but can let you form a totally false impression from what they carefully don't say, no debriefing required. that's backwards from some views — it's arguably the more manipulative move.
