A good friend of mine died last year and left an unfinished novel, and I'm the one stuck editing it. There's a scene where a character burns a letter and I genuinely cannot tell if it's meant as defiance or grief or self-destruction, and she's not around to ask anymore. So I did what you do and went down the rabbit hole on authorial intention, and now I understand even less.

Here's the thing I noticed: the debate has been running in basically the same shape since the mid-twentieth century. You've got the anti-intentionalists, the "intentional fallacy" crowd, saying the author's purpose is neither available nor desirable as a standard, because the words on the page carry whatever they carry. Then you've got the intentionalists replying that a text is a communicative act and reading it like a message in a bottle with no sender is just weird -- meaning isn't a property texts have, it's something someone means. And then there's a middle position I keep running into, hypothetical intentionalism, which says the meaning is what an ideal reader, knowing everything about the author and context that's publicly available, would take the author to have meant -- so intention matters but only as it would show up to a competent audience, not the private version in the author's head.

And as far as I can tell this has been at a standoff for decades. Everyone keeps rehearsing the same moves. My working hypothesis is that it's not really one debate at all: "what does the text mean" bundles together (a) a historical question about a dead woman's mind, (b) a question about what constrains a good reading now, and (c) maybe just a normative choice about how we want to read, and the sides are answering different ones while using the same word. But I'm not sure, because the intentionalists do seem to think something substantive hangs on getting the historical question right, and the anti-intentionalists seem to think something substantive hangs on it NOT mattering, which doesn't feel merely verbal.

So: diagnose this for me. Is it verbal, or different questions under one heading, or a real disagreement about what interpretation is even for? Use what each side actually claims, not a survey. And be concrete about what would count as progress -- is there a case, or a distinction, or something, that could actually move it? Because right now I have a manuscript on my desk and I'd like to know whether the fact that I found her notebooks in a drawer last month should change one word of the text's meaning, and it turns out the whole tradition just shrugs at that question.
