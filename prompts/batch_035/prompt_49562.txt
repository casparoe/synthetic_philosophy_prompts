At a systems biology conference last month I sat through a keynote in which the speaker used the phrase the whole is greater than the sum of its parts at least four times, and always in the same way: as a license to dismiss molecular biology's reductionist program as doomed and to argue that his lab's organism-level, holistic methods were not merely practically convenient but the only scientifically respectable approach. When someone in the audience asked whether he meant that molecules interact, which nobody denies, he waved the question off as question-begging, and nobody pressed him, myself included. The phrase clearly did real argumentative work for him, but I have not been able to pin down what exactly it committed him to, and I suspect he had not either. In the reading I did afterward, I found that the Gestalt psychologist usually credited with the line, Kurt Koffka, complained about exactly this in his 1935 book: he wrote that it is more correct to say that the whole is something else than the sum of his parts, because summing is a meaningless procedure, whereas the whole-part relationship is meaningful. So even the originator thought the popular version was the wrong popular version, which has made me wonder how many distinct precise claims are hiding inside that one sentence.

To make this concrete, here are cases I would want any account to sort. Water's wetness is not a property any individual H2O molecule has, but I take it that this is old news to a reductionist and does not embarrass them at all. A melody is more than its notes, in some sense, but the sense seems to be about arrangement, and again, nobody thinks parts have to be heaped in a pile rather than organized. Traffic jams and ant colonies exhibit patterns that no component carries, yet some people model these successfully with purely bottom-up simulations, which makes me unsure whether this kind of thing is supposed to be a problem for reduction or a triumph of it. And then there is the strong reading, where wholes have causal powers or properties that are not even in principle fixed by the arrangement and behavior of the parts, which I gather most philosophers of science reject but some defend. Perhaps the speaker was also gesturing at an epistemic point, that even if wholes are fixed by their parts, prediction from the parts is so intractable that higher-level descriptions earn their keep independently.

What I want is a dissection of the slogan into its candidate readings, laid out as a menu: for each one, whether it is a triviality that even the most militant reductionist accepts, a substantive and defensible thesis, or something plainly false, and, crucially, which of those readings, if any, would actually entitle the speaker to his conclusion that reductionist molecular biology is a dead end. I would also like to know what to make of Koffka's emendation, whether the "other than" version is a genuinely different claim or just a grumpier way of stating one of the readings, and whether there is a principled line between those two versions that maps onto anything in the literature on reduction and emergence. Please answer in continuous prose only, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write a letter: I want to be able to read this straight through and come away knowing exactly which sentence I should have made the keynote speaker commit to.
