I do claims policy at a mid-sized home insurer, and I need your help with something that turns out to be weirder than it sounds. Our policies cap payouts per "article" — so whether the destroyed thing counts as one article or several can swing a claim from fully covered to mostly out of pocket, and adjusters argue about it constantly with no framework. Recent examples: a sectional sofa ruined by smoke (one article or five interlocking pieces?), a modular garage shelving system, a fence blown down in a storm (one fence or forty panels?), a custom kayak with an aftermarket rudder and fishfinder, and a set of china that's only valuable as a set.

Here's the thing — I gather there's an actual live debate in metaphysics about when a bunch of parts make up one thing. Like, one camp apparently says any old parts whatever always compose a further thing, another camp says strictly speaking there are no composite objects at all, a third says only special things (living organisms?) get to count as one, and some people think there's no informative rule and it's just a brute fact. This sounds exactly like our problem, except our adjusters have fifteen minutes per call and can't be discussing ontology with a customer whose stuff just burned.

So: write me a decision procedure an adjuster could actually run during a claims call, in plain language. I want the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and says "pay out as one article," where it says "several articles," and where it has to punt to a human because it's a values/policy call rather than a fact. Ground it in what those metaphysical positions actually imply — but be honest about where the theory just doesn't answer the adjuster's question and we're improvising. If the procedure only works because we quietly adopted one of those camps as policy, say that explicitly, because I'd rather know when we're choosing universalism for bookkeeping reasons than pretend the metaphysics settled it.

Then run it on three cases: (1) the sectional sofa, (2) the fence, and (3) a homeowner whose built-in floor-to-ceiling bookshelves were destroyed, where the question is whether those count as part of the house structure (different sublimit) or as furniture. Also worth handling somewhere: over the years before the storm, about half the fence's panels had been individually replaced after smaller incidents — does that change what "the fence" even is, or is that a different question than the composition one?

Constraints: adjusters have ~15 minutes, no jargon they'd have to look up, anything that changes typical payouts needs my sign-off, and if some step only works if we rewrite the policy language, flag that instead of burying it. Include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer I'm going to hate defending to the customer, because I want to know where those are before we roll this out.
