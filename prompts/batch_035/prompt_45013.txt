Two metaphysicians — one a mereological universalist, holding that composition is unrestricted and that any class of things whatever composes a further thing; the other a van Inwagen-style organicist, holding that some things compose something only when their joint activity constitutes a life — have spent a year in an adversarial collaboration on the special composition question. Draft the joint statement they should produce at the end of it.

The document should contain, in this order:

1. **Agreed claims.** A numbered list of propositions both parties can sign, including at minimum: a shared statement of what the special composition question asks and how it differs from the general question of composition; what the argument from vagueness for universalism can and cannot establish, given that both parties accept that classical mereology is not logically compulsory; an acknowledgment of the costs each view takes on board (the organicist's world without tables and ships; the universalist's world containing nearly every fusion short of absurdity); and a statement of what would count as each side having been wrong, phrased by that side itself.

2. **Disagreements.** A table in which each residual disagreement is stated twice — once in wording the universalist would accept as fair to universalism, once in wording the organicist would accept as fair to organicism — with a column marking whether the disagreement is genuine or merely verbal. I would expect candidates to include: whether composition can be vague; whether 'constitutes a life' is a joint-carving restriction or an ad hoc one; and what to say about artifacts and about the reference of ordinary singular terms like 'the table'.

3. **Cruxes with predictions.** For each genuine disagreement, identify the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one party, and record a concrete case both agree bears on it — for instance, a sorites-style sequence running from scattered gravel to an intact statue, or a case in which two people gradually interlock their parts — along with each party's prediction about the case, written down in advance.

Two further requirements. First, the pair must somewhere address the problem of the many (the one cloud surrounded by many equally good candidate clouds) and state what their views jointly commit them to there, since it is the one point at which both may be forced toward the same answer. Second, close with a short section that only a collaboration could produce: their verdict on which parts of their remaining dispute are verbal and which are not, with each party allowed one paragraph of reply to the other.

One touch of self-reference: include a single footnote in which each party states, consistently with their own view, how many composite objects came into existence in the course of drafting the joint statement itself. The footnote should be funny only insofar as the numbers are ones the views actually imply.

Aim for roughly 1,000–1,400 words. The agreed claims must not be vacuous, and each side's wording of its own view must not be slanted toward the other's; if a proposed agreed claim cannot be formulated without favoring one side, note that and drop it to the disagreements table.
