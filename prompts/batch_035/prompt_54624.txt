I've been going through the units of selection debate trying to figure out what I actually believe, because my gut verdicts on cases don't obviously add up to a single principle. You're the expert here — professional philosopher of biology — so please think carefully, maybe brainstorm a couple of candidate principles before settling on one, and be honest about where my verdicts just can't all be saved.

Here's my verdicts on whether selection is really operating at the gene, individual, or group level in each case:

1. Cheetahs getting faster over evolutionary time: individual selection, obviously.
2. Peacock tails: still individual selection, even though females are doing the selecting and it hurts survival. Sexual selection is individual selection, I feel strongly about that.
3. Meiotic drive, like the t-locus in mice that sabotages sperm carrying the other allele and thereby lowers the male's total fertility: this is genuine gene-level selection, and it's selection against the individual. If someone told me this is just individual selection to produce cheaters I'd say no way.
4. Worker sterility in honeybees: gene-level too (workers passing on copies of their genes through the queen), and I get annoyed when people call it group selection for the good of the hive.
5. Alarm calls in Belding's ground squirrels, where females with kin nearby call more: also kin selection, so gene-level. Not group selection, even though the whole local group benefits from the warning.
6. Ok here's my problem case. There were these experiments with water striders where groups kept crashing because aggressive males harassed females so much that fewer offspring got produced. Less-aggressive males did worse within every single group, but the population got less aggressive over generations anyway, because of which groups survived and how many new groups they founded. I want to say THIS is real group selection. Not merely-possible-on-paper group selection, real group selection. But it has exactly the same shape as the squirrel case — within-group cost, between-group benefit — and in the squirrel case I insisted on gene-level. My current defense is that the squirrel case involves kin and the strider case doesn't, but I can't state why that matters without hand-waving about how there isn't a real group in the squirrel case, which sounds like it's assuming the answer.
7. Lion infanticide: individual selection.
8. Aging: I go back and forth. Part of me says it's a gene-level phenomenon (selection is weaker on genes whose effects show up late in life), part of me says it's individual-level (tradeoffs between early reproduction and late survival within one body), and part of me says the question doesn't even make sense for aging. I genuinely don't know what I think.
9. One more: somewhere I read about a population, fish or clams or something, where within-group selection favored maturing faster, but groups seeded from later-maturing individuals lasted longer and produced more founders, so the later-maturing type spread — a whole Simpson's paradox, with the population-level trait winning. Verdict: real group selection. And I want to be able to say this is different from the meiotic drive case, where a gene wins by sabotaging the individual. But in both cases a lower-level entity does better within its container and the container pays for it?? No wait, that's not right either. Help.

I also have a tentative principle I want you to test and then repair or replace: selection is really at the level where the competitive interaction happens. Cheetah vs. gazelle = individual, sperm vs. sperm = gene, group vs. group = group. The problem is the squirrel case: the competition between the calling and non-calling variants seems to be among kin within a group, so it should come out gene-level, fine. But then the strider case should also be individual-level, males competing with males, and I don't want that.
