For years I've argued about supertasks in comment threads, and I've finally written down where I actually stand. I'd like a consistency audit: which of these commitments are in real tension, which only look like they are, and what the cheapest fix would be in each case. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than flagging everything equally.

1. Zeno is settled the standard way: Achilles really completes infinitely many sub-runs in finite time, and his position at the finish is the limit of his positions over the run. Supertasks are legitimate idealizations, and I have no patience for "you can't do infinitely many things" as an objection.
2. The bouncing-ball version — fixed fractional energy loss per bounce, halving bounce times, so the ball comes to rest exactly at the two-minute mark — ends with the ball at rest on the floor. The story settles it; nothing mysterious there.
3. Thomson's lamp is different. The switch toggles at the usual halving intervals, the sequence of states has no limit, and at the two-minute mark there is no fact of the matter whether the lamp is on. Not unknown — indeterminate. I take this at face value: a possible process with an indeterminate outcome, not an impossible or incompletely described one. And I'd generalize: the endpoint of a supertask is the limit of the states before it, when that limit exists, and when it doesn't exist there is no fact of the matter.
4. The standard lamp description — switch position at every instant before the mark, ordinary mechanics, nothing else — is complete. There is no extra physical fact about the mechanism that the story leaves out and that could settle the switch at the mark.
5. A "Goldbach machine" would work: by minute 2 − 2^-n it has checked the first n even numbers for counterexamples, and it is wired so that a lamp turns on at the two-minute mark iff some check failed. If we ran it, looking at the lamp would settle Goldbach's conjecture by observation — an experimental answer to a mathematical question, no worse in kind than a proof.
6. Outside quantum mechanics I'm an epistemicist about everything: vague predicates have sharp boundaries, and "no fact of the matter" verdicts about the concrete mid-sized world are nearly always map–territory confusions. When a story seems to leave a physical fact unsettled, that's a fact about the story — it's incomplete, or the scenario it describes is impossible — never a fact about the world.

My own suspicion is that (1) and (3) can't stand together, but I've never managed to make that argument work, and I half suspect the feeling is wrong. Where does the real problem lie, which commitment has to give first, and what does each possible fix cost?
