My seminar has been stuck for three weeks on whether Frankfurt-style cases actually refute the principle of alternative possibilities, and since we're grading on written argumentation rather than class participation, I want to force the disagreement into numbers. The setup, so we're on the same page: Frankfurt's original 1969 paper asks us to imagine someone (Black) who would intervene to ensure Jones does what he does — but never has to, because Jones does it on his own. The intuitive pull is that Jones is responsible even though he couldn't have done otherwise, so alternative possibilities aren't necessary for moral responsibility. My own credence that Frankfurt cases succeed in refuting the principle is about 0.75; the instructor's is, I'd guess, around 0.35, and what bothers him most is a variant we've been calling the "predictive Black" case: Black doesn't monitor Jones's current decision but instead runs a highly accurate prediction of what Jones will choose, and arms the intervention only in the tiny fraction of runs where the prediction says Jones would choose otherwise. It seems to matter to him whether the predictor is 95% accurate or 99.999% accurate, and none of us can say why that should change the modal facts about what Jones could have done.

So here is what I want from you. First, your own overall credence that Frankfurt-style cases refute the principle of alternative possibilities — a single number, with a brief statement of how you're interpreting the principle so the number has a determinate target. Then decompose that credence into the sub-questions it should ride on, each with a rough probability and a line of justification. At minimum I want you to take a position on: (i) whether the cases are even coherent — whether a counterfactual intervener's mere presence can make it true that Jones could not have done otherwise, or whether the intervention's absence leaves a "flicker" of alternative possibility that defenders of the principle can dig in on; (ii) whether the so-called dilemma defense succeeds (the one that says either the intervener's presence removes the agent's causal contribution, in which case the agent isn't responsible and the case proves nothing, or it doesn't, in which case the alternatives remain); (iii) whether moral responsibility ultimately tracks sourcehood rather than alternatives, so that the whole dispute is misdescribed; and (iv) whether my instructor's accuracy-sensitivity intuition about the predictive variant picks out something real about the modal status of the case or is just a red herring imported from thinking about predictors in Newcomb-style problems. Show how your sub-credences combine to your final number, tell me which sub-question the number is most sensitive to, and say what argument or kind of case would move that sub-question most. Finally, given your decomposition, tell me whether the gap between me at 0.75 and him at 0.35 most likely lives in one of those four sub-questions or somewhere else entirely — and what question I should ask him to find out.
