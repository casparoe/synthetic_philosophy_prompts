so i'm a retired geophysicist who spent forty years fitting models to noisy data, and i've gotten pretty deep into decision theory since hanging up my coat. the St. Petersburg stuff i can live with, but recently I hit its nastier cousin, the Pasadena game, and it's been bugging me more than the infinite one. quick setup so we're on the same page: flip a fair coin until the first heads; if that lands on toss n, the payoff has magnitude 2^n/n but the sign alternates, win on odd n, pay out on even n. since heads-first-on-toss-n has probability 2^-n, each term of the expected value is just ±1/n — the alternating harmonic series, which sums to ln 2 in the natural order but, by the Riemann rearrangement theorem, can be reordered to give literally any value, or diverge. same game, no reordering changes anything about it, yet the expectation doesn't exist and the payoff table's row order does all the work.

my question: what are the most important implications of this for a practically minded person? specifically, does the mere possibility of undefined expectations show that expected-value reasoning was always a convenience that only works because real lists are finite and utilities are (sort of) bounded — or is Pasadena a pure curiosity with no analogue in actual decisions, since nobody in real life gets to reorder your payoff table? part of me suspects there is a real-life version of the rearrangement trick, where whether a risky choice looks worth it depends on the order you happen to tally its ups and downs in, but i might be reaching. be honest if you think i am.
