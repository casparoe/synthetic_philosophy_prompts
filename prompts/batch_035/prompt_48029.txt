A friend and I spent basically an entire evening arguing about the Lucas-Penrose argument, the one where human mathematicians can "see" the truth of a Gödel sentence for any consistent formal system that models their own reasoning, so no such system can be a complete model of human mathematical understanding. He thinks it's basically a parlor trick that trades on confusing the system we happen to be with the system we can prove things about, and honestly he landed some punches. But I still lean toward thinking there's something real there, at least in the Penrose version where the claim isn't that we're literally sound machines-plus, but that our grasp of arithmetic outruns any formal system we could be shown to be equivalent to. He also brought up Gödel's own disjunction -- that either the mind surpasses machines or there are arithmetic problems absolutely undecidable by humans -- and said that even if the anti-mechanist wins, that second horn is where we'd actually end up, and it's way less romantic than I want it to be.

Here's what I want: take my position seriously (some weakened version of Lucas-Penrose is right: human mathematical understanding isn't capturable by any single formal system, at least not one we could identify) and tell me what would count against it. Not the standard textbook objections -- I've already conceded that humans make mistakes and that the unweakened Lucas claim about human soundness is a non-starter. I want the stuff that should actually worry someone holding the weakened version. Like: would it hurt if it turned out that for any given human, there are Gödel-type sentences they can't in fact settle? Does the "we can't know which machine we are" move from the mechanist side leave the argument intact or gut it? What if idealized-but-finite agents provably can't determine their own consistency, so the human's "seeing" is just another instance of the same blindness? Where exactly does the weakened view risk being qualified into unfalsifiability, and is that fatal for a view of this kind or just the normal cost of doing metaphysics of mind?

Sort the defeaters by how decisive they'd be, from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me which ones a committed but honest anti-mechanist should concede and which they're entitled to wave off. Also please flag where your own reasoning in all this is most likely to go wrong -- I gave you my leanings and I don't want you to just lawyer for my side, but I do want to know which single argument or consideration, if it worked, would flip you to a different verdict.
