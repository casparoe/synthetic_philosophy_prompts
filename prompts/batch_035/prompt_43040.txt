I'm writing a term paper for a graduate seminar on theories of causation, and rather than recycling the stock examples I want to build the centerpiece case myself. The paper's guiding question is whether the interventionist requirement of manipulability marks a genuine metaphysical border or only reflects our practical interests, so what I need is a case where the two classic frameworks simply disagree about one and the same causal claim. On one side, David Lewis's counterfactual analysis, in its simplest form: c causes e when e depends on c — had c not occurred, e would not have. On the other, James Woodward's interventionist account, roughly: X causes Y when there is a possible surgical intervention on X, holding fixed everything off the causal path, that changes Y. My advisor's standing objection to drafts so far has been that my cases secretly make the two theories answer different questions, so the disagreement must be about a single claim, stated once, and the verdicts have to fall out of each theory's own machinery — Lewis's events, their descriptions, and the closeness of the closest worlds; Woodward's variables, ideal interventions, and off-path holding-fixed — never stipulated by me.

The usual sources of divergence are off-limits because both camps have patched them to death: no preemption and no symmetric overdetermination (so nothing structurally like the two rocks thrown at a window), and no common causes. Instead, I want the disagreement generated by some structural non-independence between cause and effect, something manipulability is sensitive to while bare counterfactual dependence is not. Some leads I've been mulling, any of which you may take, combine, or replace: the candidate cause and effect stand in a constitutive or realization relation, so that any intervention on the cause is what philosophers of causation call fat-handed — it drags the effect along through additional channels, and no surgical wiggle of the cause considered in itself is even available; or the effect is an epiphenomenal or definitional byproduct of the cause, tied to it by convention or conceptual necessity the way a wake is tied to a boat or a scoreboard figure to the underlying events; or the closest worlds in which the cause fails to occur are not intervention worlds at all, so that the cause's absence travels together with something else that produces the effect anyway. The scenario itself must be everyday and ordinary enough that untutored judgment has a firm view about it — one paragraph, no demons, no perfect predictors, no quantum machinery, no science-fictional devices — and the disagreement must not be a mere artifact of variable choice or of how the events are described, since that would just restate my advisor's objection.

After the scenario, please do the following, in order. State the causal claim at issue precisely. Give the verdict each theory delivers, showing which parts of that theory's machinery do the work. Identify the single structural feature that generates the disagreement, and tell me what happens to the case when that feature is stripped out. Then give each partisan's best repair — for the Lewisian, whatever can be said about event individuation, alternative descriptions, or how close the closest worlds are; for the Woodwardian, whatever latitude the theory has about which interventions count as possible, or about interventions that are less than ideally surgical. Finally, be blunt about whether your case defeats the full versions of these theories or only their simplified textbook forms; if it's the latter, name the simplification it exploits, since that admission is the difference between a genuine counterexample and a party trick. And given the firm ordinary judgment your case is supposed to elicit, close by telling me which theory you would bet on here, and what that suggests about whether the manipulability requirement carves nature at a joint or carves us.
