I'm presenting at a population ethics workshop a paper defending a narrow procreation asymmetry: it is wrong to create a person whose life would foreseeably contain more suffering than flourishing, but merely permissible, and not required, to create a person whose life would foreseeably be worth living. My ground is a risk-imposition analogy: creating someone is relevantly like imposing unconsented-to risks on them, and we rightly demand justification for exposing people to harm that we do not demand for forgone benefits — so the asymmetry in duties doesn't need an existing subject on the benefit side. The audience is mostly totalist welfare economists who accept aggregation and have already bitten the bullet on the repugnant conclusion, a couple of person-affecting theorists looking for an ally, and one person who works on Arrhenius-style impossibility theorems and treats every population axiology as a proof pending its counterexample. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they'd do, phrased in their vocabulary, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is soft. It must include the symmetry challenge (why treat absent pain as counting in favor of the empty world while absent pleasure counts for nothing — aren't those precisely parallel?), the escalation worry that my narrow asymmetry is just Benatar's first premise with the antinatalist conclusion only one further step away, and a question about whether my view avoids the sadistic conclusion or merely relocates it. Also tell me what I should concede up front, and include the objection I am hoping nobody asks: whether, if the asymmetry is right, creating digital minds we cannot yet confirm will have lives worth living falls on the wrongful side of the line.
