In my seminar on political philosophy we spend two weeks on famine, and every year the same framing surfaces in discussion and in the students' written work: famine is a natural disaster, of a piece with earthquakes and hurricanes — the blight strikes, the rains fail, and people die, so what follows is a humanitarian question about relief and charity rather than a political question about culpability. The analogy does real work in the literature as well as in the seminar room: it is used to absorb famine into the category of events nobody is responsible for, to treat deaths as misfortune rather than homicide by policy, and to preempt any discussion of justice, restitution, or accountability. I would like to interrogate it properly rather than merely gesturing at Amartya Sen's entitlement approach.

Please give me an explicit correspondence table for the analogy as its users deploy it: what plays the role of the geological fault or the storm system (the blight, the drought, the shortfall), of the collapse (the deaths), of the warning signs, and of the emergency services that arrive afterwards (relief efforts). Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and — this is the part I care about most — separate the disanalogies that matter for the argumentative work the analogy is doing from the ones that don't. I take it the crucial question is whether the physical trigger to mass mortality link is as direct as the strain to earthquake link, and I gather the historical record complicates this at exactly the three cases we teach: Bengal in 1943, where Sen argues there was no exceptional shortfall in food availability at all while laborers' exchange entitlements collapsed; the Irish famine of the 1840s, where food continued to leave the country while a million people died under a doctrine of non-interference; and the Great Leap Forward, where grain was procured on the basis of inflated production figures from a countryside that was simultaneously barred from complaining about it. For each case, say what the natural-disaster picture gets right and what it quietly deletes — I'm especially interested in the Malthusian population pressure it imports, the assumption that the relevant causal chain runs through food rather than through who can command food, and the way it converts questions about institutions (publication of output figures, freedom of the press, anti-hoarding policy, relief works, the persistence of exports) into questions about weather.

Finally, assess whether the conclusion the analogy was carrying — famine relief as charity, famine as nobody's crime — survives once the disanalogies are on the table; note that this cuts both ways, since a partisan of the political-crime view (say, the Irish nationalist claim that the blight was providential but the famine was manufactured) owes us an account of what distinguishes a crime from a negligent failure to rescue, and the natural-disaster proponent may have a defensible weaker view that doesn't collapse into the strong one. And tell me whether there is a better analogy available: some of my colleagues suggest famine is less like an earthquake and more like a bank run or a siege, where the mechanism of death operates through the food's price and its ownership rather than through its absence. What would each candidate analogy have to get right, and which, if any, deserves a place in my lectures?
