I've been asked to give a guest lecture on the frame problem to an undergraduate cognitive science class, and I would rather open with a story than a definition. Give me a reading of Conan Doyle's "The Adventure of Silver Blaze" — the stolen racehorse, the trainer found dead on the moor, the guard dog that famously did nothing in the night-time — through the frame problem: the original technical problem from McCarthy and Hayes about how a reasoning system updating its beliefs after an action is supposed to know which of its indefinitely many beliefs are affected and which stay fixed without enumerating them all, and the broader relevance problem it became in the hands of Dennett and Dreyfus. The story seems made for it: the decisive clue is an absence, and its evidential force depends entirely on a background frame of expectations about dogs and intruders; knowing which non-events are informative is precisely the problem of relevance. But I want the reading to face the suspicion that detective fiction systematically hides the frame problem rather than solving it. Watson narrates only what turns out to matter; in the story-world every noticed detail eventually pays off, as though the world were pre-filtered by an author who has already done the relevance work Holmes only seems to do; Holmes's celebrated distinction between merely seeing and genuinely observing may just be the same problem one level up. Where does the story genuinely engage the frame problem, where does it cheat, and does it know the difference? Take both the story and the debate seriously, don't reduce one to an illustration of the other, and close by saying what the genre adds to the philosophy that the arguments alone do not — in particular whether detective fiction is the fantasy of a world without a frame problem, and what that fantasy teaches about the real one. Begin the essay directly, with no preamble and no restatement of my question, and let its opening sentence state the central claim of the reading.
