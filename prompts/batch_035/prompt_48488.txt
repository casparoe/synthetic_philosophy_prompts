I've been keeping a list of what my conscience actually says about killing household pests and insects, and I want to know whether the verdicts add up to a principle or just to habit. The cases, with my gut rulings:

- Swatting a mosquito that has landed on my arm: fine, obviously.
- A snap trap for the mouse that's been in my pantry: fine.
- A glue trap for that same mouse: not fine — the thought of it dying slowly, stuck to the board, bothers me more than the thought of killing it at all.
- Drowning a mouse I'd caught alive because I couldn't be bothered to drive it somewhere and release it: very much not fine.
- Sugar-and-borax bait that the foraging ants carry back, so the whole colony dies over the course of a week: fine.
- Spraying contact killer on the six ants wandering on my kitchen counter: also fine, though it oddly feels a touch worse than the bait, even though the bait kills thousands and the spray kills six.
- Destroying the wasp nest under the porch rail when my child is allergic to stings: fine. But I had the same kind of nest in the same spot two years earlier, when nobody in the family was allergic, and I still think destroying it was fine — just slightly less obviously so.
- Tenting and fumigating the whole house for termites: fine, no hesitation at all.
- Spraying the aphids on my roses because the buds come out deformed: I've done it, and I feel mildly bad about it, but not properly guilty.
- Pouring boiling water on the ant colony in the driveway crack that never comes inside: not fine. This is the one that keeps nagging at me, and I'm not sure why it feels categorically different from the counter spray.

My tentative principle is something like: killing pests is permissible when they threaten health or property, and the method should be no crueler than the threat warrants. But I can't get it to work. The aphids threatened nothing but the appearance of my roses, and I still think killing them was more or less permissible. The driveway colony threatened literally nothing, so my principle rightly condemns it — but the colony in the kitchen wall also threatened nothing but crumbs, and the slow-poison bait against it strikes me as completely fine. The cruelty clause should condemn the bait (a week of poisoning) more than the snap trap, yet the bait feels fine while the glue trap, whose death is arguably less gruesome, doesn't. And the termites threatened real property, got a fumigation that's plausibly nastier than a glue trap, and I feel nothing there either.

So: is there one principle, or a small set, that captures these verdicts? Please state it as precisely as you can, show me which clause each of my cases motivates, and then tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which one you would tell me to give up, and why. Then apply it to four cases I haven't listed: a larvicide dunk in my rain barrel that kills all the mosquito larvae; a quarterly preventive perimeter spray around the foundation even though there's no current infestation; live-trapping the pantry mouse and releasing it at the edge of the yard instead of using the snap trap; and paying a service to chemically pacify and relocate the wasp nest rather than destroy it. I'd like the entire answer in at most 600 words.
