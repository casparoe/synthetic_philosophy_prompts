I teach aesthetics, and every year the same thing: I'm marking essays where a student is halfway through a genuinely careful comparison of two poems, doing good work, and then suddenly writes 'but of course beauty is in the eye of the beholder' and just... stops arguing, as though they'd cited a theorem and the matter was closed. This week it happened in a seminar too. We were arguing about whether a fairly cack-handed parody is as good as the thing it parodies, and a student shut the whole discussion down with 'there's no accounting for taste', and the rest of the room nodded along, and I lost them. I've had my fill of nodding.

Help me build the response I never get time for in class. Lay out, as a list I can basically turn into a handout, the distinct precise claims that line could be making. There's the merely descriptive one — people's preferences just do vary — which is a platitude and surely can't be what anyone's leaning on. There's the semantic one, that aesthetic judgements aren't truth-apt in the first place. There's the epistemic one, that they're truth-apt alright but there's no shared standard for settling disputes about them, so arguments never terminate. Maybe there's a pragmatic reading too, about it being pointless or simply rude to argue over taste, and I suspect that's the one my students actually mean, though they'd never seperate it from the others. Add any others you can find hiding in there.

For each reading: is it plausible, and — this is the crucial bit — does it actually support what my students do with it, which is abandoning a comparison they were halfway through? Because it seems to me you can accept that preferences vary, even that there's no knocking-down argument available, and still owe me an answer about the parody. Then bring in Hume's essay on the standard of taste: on your best reading of him, what does the disagreement between the student who prefers the parody and the one who doesn't even consist in, and does he give them their nodding back or not? I don't want a survey of Hume scholarship, I want to know if his standard does any actual work here.

One more thing, and be honest about it: I praise and blame student work constantly, and so do they, and we all think some paragraphs are just better than others — so which reading of the slogan is doing the most work in ordinary aesthetic practice, given that none of us behave as if we believe it? Flag anything the readings genuinely can't settle, where I'd just have to take a side on values. Please write it all in British English — and keep 'eye of the beholder' out of your answer if you can, I've had my fill of eyes and beholding this week. Thirty essays to go.
