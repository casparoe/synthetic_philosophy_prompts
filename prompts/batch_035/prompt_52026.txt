I serve on the historical-memory advisory council of a mid-sized country; our job is to recommend whether the state should issue formal apologies for past wrongs, and though the government has occasionally overridden us, it has followed our recommendations in every case of this kind. Our record so far, in brief: we recommended a full apology, delivered by the head of government at a ceremony, for the 1960s eviction of fourteen hundred families to build the hydroelectric reservoir that still supplies much of our electricity, along with a regional development fund but no individual compensation — the history is settled and survivors are alive, though public opinion remains divided out of pride in the dam. We recommended a formal parliamentary apology for the 1940s deportation of our border minority to wartime labor settlements: a few hundred survivors remain, the confiscated property was long since dispersed, but the land under our national university's science campus was taken then, and the history is uncontested. We advised against a formal apology for the 1860s southern famine, in which the administration's continued grain exports may or may not have aggravated a drought — historians are genuinely split, no one is alive, and the state retains nothing; the head of government settled for expressing regret for the suffering at a museum opening. And after decades of refusal, we eventually supported the formal apology, delivered at the site three years ago, for the 1920s massacre of striking dockworkers by troops acting on provincial orders — no survivors, but an active domestic association of descendants, an army unit still occupying the barracks from which the order was issued, settled history, and a public that had by then shifted in favor.

A new petition has arrived and the council is split. Two centuries ago the eastern lowlands were a plantation economy worked by enslaved people, and on abolition the enslaved population was not emancipated into this country but deported across the border, so their descendants live almost entirely in the neighboring republic, whose civic organizations have now petitioned us; none reside here. The history is uncontested among our historians, and the domestic public is largely indifferent. But our public university's founding endowment traces to plantation revenues, and the National Archive still occupies a warehouse complex built on that wealth. What should we recommend if we want to be consistent with our own record — a full apology, the regret formula, or refusal? I want the candidate rules that fit our past verdicts, what each predicts here, and since they seem to diverge on this case, an explicit statement of what principle we would be adopting with each available choice. Please identify the closest analog among our precedents and say whether it is genuinely the closest or merely superficially so, and given that our record has not been perfectly stable — we refused the massacre for decades before reversing — tell me how much weight consistency should carry. Finally, describe the further petition, of the sort our country could plausibly receive, that would best discriminate between the candidate rules. Please reply in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — something I could paste directly into a council memorandum.
