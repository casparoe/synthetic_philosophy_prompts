i'm a novelist deep in revisions on a book where the antagonist never lies — not once, and it's kind of the whole engine of the thing. she only ever tells people true things, but somehow everyone she talks to ends up doing exactly what she wants, and half of them end up wrecked by it. my problem is i want her to read as a genuine manipulator, not just as persuasive and sharp, and i keep circling the question of what the difference actually is. every definition i've come across boils down to something like "bypassing the target's rational faculties" or "subverting their will," but she doesn't do either, near as i can tell. she gives people better reasons than they had, all true, all consistent with values they already hold.

what she does instead, i think, is arrange what people are aware of. she makes someone notice their own inconsistency at the worst possible moment, or mentions a true fact at exactly the point where it will do the most damage, and then lets their own rationality do the rest of the work for her. the manipulation seems to live in the timing and the selection, not in any false or irrational move.

so here's my ask: give me an example — a worked-through case, the way you'd construct one for a thought experiment — of an influence attempt that is perfectly truthful, non-deceptive, and reason-respecting on both of those definitions, and yet is still intuitively manipulation. ideally make the manipulator's only technique choosing which truths to deploy, to whom, and when. then tell me where in the case the wrong-making feature actually lives — is it in the intent, the asymmetry of control, the exploitation of something about the target even though nothing false was said, or somewhere else entirely? i want it sharp enough that i can hand the scene to readers who've never heard of this debate and still have them feel she's done something dirty, and know exactly why they feel it.
