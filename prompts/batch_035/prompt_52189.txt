I have followed, for more than a decade, the debate over the moral structure of classical Buddhist ethics, and it appears to me to be genuinely stalled. The positions are well mapped. One camp reads the tradition as a virtue ethics: the Dhamma is described as a path, the arhat as a person of perfected character, and the analysis of action in terms of intention looks like a doctrine about the agent rather than about states of affairs, so the natural comparison is with Aristotle. A second camp reads it as consequentialist: the criterion of the good is the diminution of suffering for all sentient beings, compassion supplies the motive, meditation the technique, and the bodhisattva ideal — in which one being's progress is deferred for the sake of others — looks like an extreme commitment to impartial maximization. A third camp holds the whole question is malformed: Buddhist teaching is a soteriological discipline, not a normative theory, and the attempt to classify it imports a taxonomy it never had; this camp sometimes leans on the distinction between conventional and ultimate truth, claiming that what looks like ethics operates only at the conventional level, while the ultimate level contains no obligations at all. What strikes me is that the disputants agree on nearly everything textual: all sides concede the texts contain both the language of cultivation and the language of the relief of suffering, and all concede the bodhisattva places others before self. And yet in thirty years nobody has moved an inch.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not about ethics at all but about the status of suffering itself, and I would like a diagnosis on this point. If suffering is a bad-making feature of experience — something that happens to a person and is bad for them — then the tradition's focus on its elimination is naturally read as a theory of the good, and the consequentialist interpretation is at least on the table. But if suffering is better modeled as a kind of cognitive artifact — a product of craving and mistaken views about the self, something to be seen through rather than reduced — then the tradition is closer to a theory of error correction than a theory of value, and the consequentialist framing presupposes exactly the value theory the tradition lacks. On that reading, the dispute between the three camps is a proxy fight: they are not disagreeing about how to classify a shared moral theory but about which of two different subjects — a theory of welfare or a theory of delusion — the word ethics is being used to point at.

Is this diagnosis correct, or is the impasse something else — a verbal dispute about the word consequentialism, a substantive disagreement about the texts that a better reading could settle, or simply a hard question being answered from within incommensurable frameworks? I want a diagnosis supported by what each camp actually claims, not a survey of positions I already know. And given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, a test case that forces one reading to pay a price the others do not, a reformulation of the question — or an argument that no progress should be expected here, and why.
