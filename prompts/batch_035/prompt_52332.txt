My friend and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about epiphenomenalism, and the conversation reached a point neither of us could resolve, so I'd like you to write a dialogue that continues it.

The argument went as follows. My friend holds that qualia are real but causally inert: the physical world is causally closed, and phenomenal properties ride along without pushing anything. I pressed the standard objection — the paradox of phenomenal judgment — that his view seems to undermine his own reports about his experience. When he sincerely says that the sunset looks a particular shade of orange, the utterance is a physical event with a physical causal history, and by his own lights the phenomenal orange makes no causal contribution to it. So what, exactly, does his report report? He pointed out that an epiphenomenalist's zombie duplicate would utter the same sentence with the same conviction, which he claimed is a feature his view can live with rather than a refutation. I found that reply evasive, and the argument turned on it for a while.

The most interesting twist came near the end. My friend conceded that his position faces a structural problem similar to the one Benacerraf raised about mathematics — roughly, the difficulty of explaining how causally inert things could be known, given that our knowledge seems to require causal contact with what is known — and he argued this is simply the price of taking both physics and experience seriously. I replied that paying that price in mathematics is arguably acceptable because mathematical entities aren't supposed to be parts of our own mental lives, whereas qualia are, which makes the inertness harder to swallow.

Please write a dialogue between the two of us continuing this argument, treating each side as fully competent — neither a strawman. The dialogue should contain genuine back-and-forth in which each interlocutor responds to the other's actual points, not adjacent ones. I'd like it to cover at least these threads: whether the zombie-duplicate reply really does dissolve the paradox of phenomenal judgment or merely relocates it; whether the parallelism option — non-causal psychophysical laws that happen to be reliable — is a principled position or an ad hoc rescue; and whether the comparison to mathematical knowledge makes epiphenomenalism more or less defensible. You are welcome to add considerations we didn't reach, provided they arise naturally from the exchange rather than being delivered as a lecture.

Two further requirements. First, at some point each character should state, in their own voice, what argument or consideration would most plausibly move them toward the other position, or where they suspect their own reasoning is weakest — not as a concession to end the scene, but as a genuine moment of self-assessment that the other character then probes. Second, after the dialogue, append a short note identifying where the piece leans on contested readings or takes liberties — for instance, any point where the case made for one side is stronger or weaker than it is usually considered to be, or where the dialogue papers over a live dispute within the literature.

Write the dialogue in a relatively formal register — two philosophers in earnest, not a theatrical set piece. Keep it to a length at which each exchange can do real argumentative work; I would rather have six sharp turns than twenty shallow ones.
