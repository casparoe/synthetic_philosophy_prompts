I've been commissioned to write an essay for a film-and-philosophy collection, and my assigned pairing is Capra's It's a Wonderful Life with modal metaphysics — specifically counterpart theory versus transworld identity. The more I sit with the Clarence sequence, the more it looks like a staged seminar example: George is shown the world that would have obtained had he never been born, and he watches it as a spectator whom nobody in it recognizes — Mary stares through him, his brother's grave stands in the cemetery where his own house would be, Zuzu's petals are gone from his pocket. So my central question: does the film's staging quietly take sides between Lewis and the friends of transworld identity? The Lewisian reading is that Capra got it accidentally right — the viewer-George is emphatically not in the world he is watching; Pottersville contains counterparts of Mary, Harry, Mr. Gower, and the rest, but no George, which is exactly why his absence there can only register as what everyone else's life lacks. The opposing reading is that the vision is unintelligible unless it is the very same George who is absent from that world — his non-existence there is de re, a fact about him, and Clarence's whole consolation (this is what the world would have been without you) presupposes that the displayed world is about George himself rather than about whichever individuals in it happen to resemble his friends and family. And there is Kripke's old complaint about Humphrey to reckon with: if counterpart theory is right, the grief George feels at Harry's grave is grief at what happens to someone other than his brother, and the redemption runs through resemblance rather than identity. I want a reading that takes that worry seriously rather than waving it off — does it show that counterpart theory cannot carry the moral weight the film hangs on the vision, or does it show that the weight was never carried by identity in the first place?

A second thread I'd like the reading to address: the film presents Pottersville as the output of a single subtraction — remove George, hold everything else fixed — which is the naive picture of possible worlds as edited copies of the actual world. Lewis's closeness ordering raises a real question about whether the nearest worlds without George are ones where the whole town collapses into Potter's grip, or whether they are worlds where Uncle Billy, Harry's comrades, Ernie and Martini and the Building and Loan's other guarantors pick up enough of the slack that Bedford Falls muddles through. The film's moral — that one modest life is load-bearing for an entire community — then becomes a substantive, contestable claim within counterfactual semantics, not merely a sentimental one, and I'd like the essay to say whether the film's vision is a plausible verdict about closeness or a dramatic license the philosophy would never grant. At the same time, I want to know where the film resists the reading: this is an angelic revelation, not a box on a modal operator, and Capra's theology might make the vision neither a possible world nor a representation of one — a dream sent for a purpose, answerable to pastoral rather than semantic standards.

I'm trained in metaphysics but not in film studies, so I'd also like guidance on which scenes can bear this weight — the graveyard, Mary's failure to recognize him, the vanished petals — and which I'd be overworking. I'm tempted to bring in haecceitism as well, on the thought that the film's vision might turn on there being a fact about which world is George's beyond all qualitative fact, but I genuinely don't know whether that thread earns its place or is just showing off. Tell me plainly if you think the whole exercise is overreading — if the sequence trades on something more like a nightmare or divine vision and cannot adjudicate anything in the Lewis-Kripke dispute, say so rather than manufacturing a deep reading, because I would rather cut the section than strain it.
