I've been following the control vs alignment arguments since they started heating up on the forum, and the whole thing feels stuck, so I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict on who's right. The control side says: assume the model is misaligned, and build monitoring, tripwires, and limits that keep things safe anyway. The skeptics inside safety say control (1) gives labs an excuse to defer actual alignment work, (2) only handles the sudden schemer, not the slow value drift case where nothing ever trips a wire and everything just gets subtly worse over years, and (3) is wierdly self-undermining, since you're mostly using models to catch models. Here's the thing though: both sides agree on the facts about current models, agree deception is the main thing to worry about, and haven't moved an inch in like two years. So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? My tentative guess is it's a hidden empirical bet about which failure mode dominates — fast scheming vs. slow drift — but it could just as easily be a research-portfolio fight dressed up as a factual one, or two camps meaning different things by "safe": the system wants what we want, versus the system stays within bounds whether or not it wants anything. Given whichever diagnosis you land on, tell me what would count as progress here — a worked-out threat model taxonomy, a demo of a control method against a deliberately misaligned model, or is there a structural reason this one just stays stuck?
