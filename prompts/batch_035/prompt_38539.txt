I'm a backend engineer and, thanks to being the only person who didn't pretend to look at their phone in the planning meeting, I'm now running the 75-minute session at our company's junior dev onboarding week. About 20 new hires, a meeting room with whiteboards, and the loose theme my manager handed me is "metrics and why they betray you." I have zero philosophy background, but a while back I read some blog post about Goodhart's law — the idea that once a measure becomes a target, it stops tracking the thing you actually cared about — and it stuck with me mostly because we lived it: our team once had a code review SLA (everything reviewed within 4 hours) and a bunch of us quietly turned into rubber-stampers to hit the number, which I only realized in hindsight was the exact pathology everyone warns about.

So: design me an activity where they run into Goodhart on their own, before I ever name it. My instinct is the right shape is a game with a visible score that teams optimize and a hidden real goal that only I, as the game master, know — so that somewhere around round two or three, somebody discovers an input that maxes the scoreboard while being garbage for the real thing. That wait, it's giving me full marks for THIS? moment is what I want the whole room to feel, not have described to them. Give me rules, materials (nothing exotic, stickynotes and printouts are fine), a scoring scheme that works without real money, and a round structure that fits 75 minutes including the debrief. The debrief should map what actually happened in the game onto the parts of the idea — and if there are genuinely different flavors of Goodhart (I vaguely gather there's a difference between, like, statistical regression-type cases and adversarial gaming cases?), only bring in whichever ones the game itself actually demonstrated. I don't want a lecture bolted onto the end.

Two more things I really want. First, a frank list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I've never facilatated anything, I will forget some step, some team will find a loophole that breaks the game entirely, somebody will be checked out on their laptop — and what to do about each. Second, be honest about which part of Goodhart the game can't reproduce no matter how well it runs. My guess is the slow-motion, months-long, nobody-is-individually-evil version that shows up in real orgs is exactly what gets lost when you compress it into an hour with a visible game master, but tell me if I'm wrong, and tell me what I should say out loud during the debrief to patch that hole.
