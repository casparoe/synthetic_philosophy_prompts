Last night my friend and I had a long argument about what Harsanyi's work actually establishes, and I left it unable to tell how much of what he said to believe. My friend is a graduate student in economics, very confident, and at one point he laid out the whole case in a paragraph I more or less memorized because it sounded so tidy. As I recall it, he said: Harsanyi showed back in the fifties that utilitarianism is not just a moral intuition but a theorem — if every individual has von Neumann–Morgenstern preferences, if society's preferences satisfy those same axioms, and if social preferences never contradict a unanimous individual preference, then social welfare must be a sum of individual vNM utilities; and since vNM utilities are cardinal rather than ordinal, the ancient objection that you cannot add one person's utility to another's dissolves, because the theorem supplies precisely the common scale that utilitarians were always accused of lacking. He then added that the same argument shows that a rational agent behind a veil of ignorance, not knowing which person in society it will be, must choose to maximize average utility, which means Rawls, starting from his own original position, simply draws the wrong conclusion from his own setup — maximin would be rational only for someone with essentially infinite risk aversion, which no agent satisfying the vNM axioms could have.

I argued back for a while but mostly on moral grounds, and I could tell he thought I was changing the subject. Two things nag at me, though. First, I have a vague memory that cardinal utility in the vNM sense is only unique up to a positive affine transformation for each person separately, which sounds like it might matter for the "common scale" claim, but he brushed this off. Second, I also have a vague sense that Harsanyi gave two different arguments — one about aggregating individual preferences, and a separate one about a single impartial observer choosing under uncertainty — and I don't know whether it's legitimate to run them together the way my friend did. I don't know where the real errors are, if there are any; the summary sounded professionally competent, and I'm inclined to believe at least the mathematical part.

Could you go through his summary claim by claim and tell me what a welfare economist or a Harsanyi scholar would accept, what they would wince at, and what they would reject outright? In particular, I'd like to know whether the interpersonal-comparability step is as innocent as he made it sound, whether the veil-of-ignorance point is genuinely part of the same theorem or a distinct argument with different assumptions, and whether the conclusion he drew — that utilitarianism has a mathematical proof and that Rawls's maximin is thereby refuted on Rawls's own terms — actually follows from whichever parts of the summary turn out to be accurate. If some of it needs repairing, I'd also appreciate a corrected version of the paragraph that a careful defender of Harsanyi would be willing to sign.
