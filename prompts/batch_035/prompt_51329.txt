You are a philosopher specializing in population axiology and the measurement of well-being. A classmate presented the following reductio of lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism in seminar, and I cannot locate the flaw, though the conclusion is one even the theory's defenders should refuse. Please work through it step by step.

(1) Devastating sufferings — those above the threshold — cannot be compensated by any amount of mild pleasure: no quantity of mild pleasures, however vast, can outweigh a single one. (2) If a pleasure cannot compensate a suffering, then the suffering counts against a world more than the pleasure counts for it. (3) So any world containing one devastating suffering is worse than any world containing none, no matter how the mild pleasures are distributed between them. (4) Compare W1, containing a trillion flourishing lives plus one devastating suffering, with W2, containing a single mildly pleasant afternoon and nothing else. By (3), W2 is better. (5) And by (3) again, any world containing a devastating suffering is worse than W3, a barren world with no experiences at all — so painlessly preventing all future life would be an improvement if it forestalled even one devastating suffering. (6) W2 being better than W1 is absurd, and even friends of the theory balk at (5). (7) So lexical threshold negative utilitarianism is false.

Which premise or inference should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I suspect (2) — perhaps failing to be outweighed by something is not the same as outweighing it — but I cannot see how the theory denies (2) without denying (1), and if it denies (1), it collapses back into plain hedonic commensurism, which the threshold was introduced precisely to avoid. If (2) can be denied, explain exactly what relation between the two worlds replaces "worse than," and what the theory pays for it — in particular, whether transitivity lets the conclusion sneak back in through chains of intermediate worlds. Please brainstorm the possible diagnoses briefly before committing to one, then give your answer: name the faulty step, say why it looked innocuous, and price each alternative way out.
