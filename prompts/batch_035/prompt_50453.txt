I'm on the historical legacies review panel at my university, and we're stuck on something that turns out to be less administrative than it sounded. Quick background: our institution's founding endowment, back in the 1820s, came substantially from a family whose money came from plantation slavery in the Caribbean, and after a couple years of archival work the university has agreed to issue a formal public statement plus fund some scholarships. Everyone's fine with the scholarships. The fight is entirely over the wording of the statement, and we now have three drafts on the table. Draft A says "we apologise" — the university of today apologising in its own name for what the founders did. Draft B says "we deeply regret what was done in the name of this institution" — regret, with the actors implied to be the dead. Draft C acknowledges the facts and calls them "a grave injustice" but pointedly contains no apology or regret verb at all, on the theory that an institution can state facts about wrongs but not have attitudes about them.

I got assigned to write the short opinion paper we'll circulate before the vote, and I'd like you to draft it. The disagreement has become weirdly philosophical. The Draft A camp keeps invoking collective guilt — the idea that a nation or institution can itself be guilty, not just its past members — and someone circulated a summary of Jaspers' old book on German guilt where he splits guilt into criminal, political, moral and metaphysical types, with only political guilt (liability, which is inherited through the continuing polity) actually attaching to successors who personally did nothing wrong. The Draft C camp retorts that guilt in the strong sense requires a subject who did the deed and who can now feel remorse, and a corporate body 200 years on is neither, so "we apologise" is at best a metaphor and at worst a kind of moral costume. The Draft B people think they're splitting the difference but arguably they've just made it vaguer. For what it's worth, I suspect the real crux is whether an apology performed by the present institution presupposes collective *guilt* at all, or only some inherited responsibility that falls short of it — but I'm genuinely unsure how Jaspers' own distinction would cut on that, since his political guilt was about a defeated polity paying consequences, not about apologising.

So: draft the memo I can circulate. It should recommend one of the three drafts (or a fourth option if you think there's a better construction, but be concrete about its wording), give the reasoning in terms a mixed panel of historians, lawyers and one philosopher can follow, and engage honestly with the strongest argument on the losing side rather than caricaturing it — the C camp in particular have a real point about who exactly is apologising to whom. I'd also like the memo to end with a candid section on what would change your recommendation: which argument, or which consideration, would flip it to a different draft, and where you think the reasoning is most likely to fail. And please write it in British English throughout. It should be maybe 800-1200 words, formal enough to circulate but not stiff — and ignore my typo-ridden rant above, the memo itself obviously needs to be clean.
