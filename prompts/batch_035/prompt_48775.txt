I spent thirty-five years as an experimental condensed-matter physicist, most of it concerned with mesoscopic devices and the physics of electrical contacts, and since retiring I have joined a reading group devoted to Zeno's paradoxes and the modern literature on supertasks. In our last meeting we discussed Thomson's lamp: a lamp that is switched on at one minute before noon, off again at half a minute before noon, on again at a quarter minute before noon, and so on through an infinite sequence of switchings packed into the minute before noon. The question, of course, is whether the lamp is on or off at noon. My group settled on what I understand to be the standard verdict: the story simply does not specify the state of the lamp at noon, so that state is somehow "underdetermined," and this is supposed to reveal something deep about the limits of what supertask descriptions can achieve. I left the meeting dissatisfied, and I would like to see the case for my alternative made properly. As I see it, "on" and "off" are not fundamental properties of any lamp; they are coarse-grained descriptions of continuous state variables — switch position, current, contact separation, and so on. Any physically coherent realization of the switching schedule will fix how those continuous variables evolve as noon approaches. For example, if the lever's travel between on and off positions shrinks suitably at each step, the contact gap converges to a definite limit; if that limit is zero, the lamp is on at noon, and if it is some positive separation, the lamp is off — and at the scales I worked with, whether current flows across a sub-nanometer gap is not even a binary matter in the first place, since tunneling makes "on" and "off" a matter of degree. So my suspicion is that the "no fact of the matter" verdict is an artifact of modeling the lamp with a single binary state variable — a modeling shortcut no engineer would be permitted — and that the genuine lesson is about our coarse-grained vocabulary, not about supertasks as such.

Please construct the argument for this dissolution as rigorously as you can: argue that Thomson's lamp demonstrates, at most, that a certain idealized description fails to determine a certain coarse-grained predicate, and not that supertasks have intrinsically indeterminate outcomes. In the course of making the argument, please confront what strikes me as the strongest objection — namely, that physical realizability is beside the point, since the lamp is an idealization and the puzzle is designed to probe the very coherence of such idealizations, so that appealing to contact mechanics and tunneling merely changes the subject. I would also like you to say whether the dissolution extends to neighboring constructions such as the Ross–Littlewood urn, where balls are added and removed in a supertask and the contents at noon can seem to depend on the order of removal, or whether the lamp is special in virtue of its binary coarse-graining. I do not need any scholarship or references — just the argument itself, set out carefully enough that I can bring it back to my reading group and defend it against the philosopher who started all this.
