So, full disclosure up front: I write historical fiction — mostly Bronze Age Aegean stuff — and im now starting a novel where the Trojan War is the backdrop. I'm not after a bibliography or a reading list here, I can find books myself; what I want is concept clarification.

Here's the pattern I keep hitting while doing research, and its starting to drive me a little crazy. Whenever I read a popular history book or watch a documentary about Troy, King Arthur, or the Exodus, the same move shows up. Nobody can point to a single finding that establishes the legend's core claim — the ten-year war, the once and future king, the mass departure from Egypt. But there are always several weak, partial findings, each perfectly real on its own: a mound in Turkey with a name tradition going back through a nearby ancient town, plus a destruction layer from roughly the right period; a hillfort in Somerset that the locals were already calling Camelot by the time the antiquarians got there; Egyptian records of Semitic-sounding names doing forced labor. None of these is the legend. But put them side by side and everyone visibly relaxes and says well, there's no smoke without fire, so there's clearly a kernel of truth — and once that sentence has been uttered, the war / the kingdom / the departure gets treated as basically established, and the debate quietly shifts to details (which layer was Homer's Troy? was Arthur Riothamus? which pharaoh was the one?). The weak evidence gets compounded into the strong claim, and I can't for the life of me see what does the compounding, because each item taken alone supports a much weaker claim — that there was a city, that there were warlords in Britain, that there were Semitic-speakers in Egypt.

Is there an actual name for this in historiography or philosophy of history? I know euhemerism, but that seems to be the general belief that legends grow from real people and events, not this specific aggregation move. I also know it's adjacent to the Texas sharpshooter fallacy and plain confirmation bias, but in this pattern every individual finding is genuine, public, and independently checked — the problem lives entirely in how they're summed. And it's not quite motte-and-bailey either, because nobody seems to be consciously retreating; the slide from there was a city to there was a war just... happens, over and over, in seperate authors who've never met.

So: take the role of a philosopher of history who has also waded into the biblical archaeology debates, think about it properly before answering — maybe brainstorm the nearest candidate concepts first and explain why each one doesn't quite fit — and then carve the exact phenomenon: what it is, what it isn't, and where the edges are. If there's no established term, coin one and give me boundary cases. For instance: does confirming the setting transfer any credibility at all to the events, and is there a principled line between evidence that the world of the story existed and evidence that the story happened? Don't just hand me a label and move on, please. I'm going to have to write an author's note that says either inspired by real events or set in a mythical past, and I'd like to actually know what I'm claiming when I pick one.
