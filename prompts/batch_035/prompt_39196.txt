For the past two semesters I've been in a graduate seminar that has become a ritual reenactment of the same fight about heritability, and I've started to suspect we're not actually disagreeing about anything empirical anymore. The setup, so you can see the terrain: one faction, which I'll call the statisticians, holds that a heritability coefficient is a perfectly respectable descriptive statistic — the fraction of trait variance in a specific population, in a specific range of environments, associated with genetic differences in that population — and that it makes no claims about individuals, no claims about other populations, and no claims about malleability. The other faction, the skeptics, holds that the number is worse than uninformative: it smuggles in causal commitments (that genetic and environmental contributions decompose additively, that gene–environment correlations don't distort the partition), it varies with the population and even with the measuring instrument, and when it reaches the public it reliably gets misread as a fact about individuals and about immutability. The maddening thing is how much common ground we've established and how little it matters. Everyone in the room grants the rectangle analogy — that an individual trait is, like a rectangle's area, fully a product of genes and fully a product of environment, so that asking how much of *this person's* intelligence is genetic is a category mistake. Everyone grants the PKU case, where a trait is essentially fully heritable and yet a dietary intervention eliminates the outcome. Everyone grants that a coefficient computed in one environment need not transfer to another. And still, every meeting, we end with the statisticians saying the skeptics are blaming a tool for its misuse, and the skeptics saying a tool that is misused by virtually everyone who touches it has a design flaw.

I have a hypothesis about what's going on, offered only as a hypothesis: the fight looks like a methodological dispute but is actually at least two disputes wearing one lab coat. One is a live empirical question — how prevalent crossover gene–environment interactions actually are, since if reaction norms rarely cross, the variance decomposition is robust in a way that matters, and if they commonly cross, the whole additive framework is fragile — and this is, in principle, settled by data. The other, I suspect, is not empirical at all: it's a disagreement about the pragmatics of science communication, about whether a number that is valid conditionally and misleadingly simple-looking should be published prominently, kept in the technical appendix, or abandoned — which is really a debate about audiences and responsibilities, not about heritability. And there may be a third element, harder to name, about whether explaining variance in a population is even a species of explaining anything, which feels more like a standoff over what explanation means.

So here is what I want from you, and it is a diagnosis rather than a verdict on who is right. Using what each side actually says, tell me: how much of this disagreement is substantive (and if so, where the substance lies — the empirical question about interactions, or somewhere I haven't located), how much is verbal or a matter of the two camps answering different questions under one heading, and how much is a genuine but non-empirical dispute about the duties of researchers toward a public that will misread them. For each component you identify, tell me what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, a class of studies, a formal result about when variance decompositions are and aren't robust, a change in what gets reported and how. And if some component of the dispute is such that no progress should be expected — because it tracks a value difference that no evidence bears on — say so plainly and explain why, rather than pretending another meta-analysis will fix it.
