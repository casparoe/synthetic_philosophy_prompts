Journalist here -- I mostly cover science and ideas, and I'm kicking around a piece about how people handle unknown odds versus known ones. I ran Ellsberg's two-urn thing on colleagues at the pub: one urn with a known 50/50 split of red and black balls, one with an unknown ratio, and you win by drawing your chosen color. Sure enough, everyone -- including me, which annoyed me -- preferred drawing from the known urn, and they preferred it for bets on red and for bets on black alike. Then a friend of a friend who studies decision theory walked me into this argument and I haven't found the exit since. (1) Having rational degrees of belief just means they obey the probability axioms -- probabilities of mutually exclusive and exhaustive outcomes sum to one, that kind of thing. (2) If you strictly prefer one bet to another at equal stakes, you're thereby revealing that you assign a higher probability to the first bet's winning event. (3) The typical person, in the Ellsberg setup, prefers the known urn both when betting on red and when betting on black. (4) So by (2), the typical person assigns less than one half to red-from-the-unknown-urn and less than one half to black-from-the-unknown-urn, and those sum to less than one. (5) By (1) that's incoherence, and the standard penalty for incoherent probabilities is the Dutch book: a bookie can sell you a set of bets, each one you'd individually take, that loses you money in every outcome. (6) So the ordinary Ellsberg preference isn't a charming quirk, it's exploitable irrationality, and a rational person should be exactly as happy betting on the mystery urn.

Here's what bugs me: the argument seems to prove too much. Change the setup slightly -- say the unknown urn is filled by a croupier who gets to see my bet before setting the ratio, or by a guy I have decent reason to think wants to take my money. Preferring the transparent 50/50 urn in that version looks not just permissible but obviously right, yet premise (2) reads that preference as an incoherent probability assignment all the same. And if I try to wriggle out by saying people are allowed to just... not have any probability for the mystery urn's red count, the machinery of (2) plus (5) convicts them anyway: whatever they do with bets on that urn, it either reveals probabilities or it reveals incoherence, and those seem to be the only options on the table. For what it's worth, I'd like to keep the Dutch book idea doing real work somewhere, and it would be nice to also keep the thought that my pub companions (and me) are being at least somewhat unreasonable about the plain version. But I can't tell which premise is the rotton one, or whether the flaw lives in a step rather than a premise -- something felt like it slid between (2) and (4) but I can't name it. Tell me which step to reject, why it looked fine in the first place, and what it costs to reject it.
