I teach a small seminar on decision-making (biases, sunk costs, that sort of thing) and for the last session of term we do a film night. This time I picked The Bridge on the River Kwai, and I want to frame the discussion around escalation of commitment, but I'm honestly not sure the standard frame fits the whole film, so I'd like your help thinking it through.

Here's the set-up as I see it. The standoff between Nicholson and Saito over whether the officers do manual labor looks like a clean entrapment contest — two parties who have each already paid too much to quit, staring at each other, and Saito blinks first only because he's on a deadline. Fine, that part maps onto the literature nicely. But then something weird happens: Nicholson doesn't pocket his winnings and enjoy the concession, he starts building the bridge better than the Japanese engineers could, and by the end he's the one defending it against his own side's sabotage team — it takes a mortar shell and him stumbling onto the detonator for the bridge to finally go up, with the train. My hypothesis is that the film is showing a substitution mid-game: Nicholson stops playing the auction he was actually in (officers' rights, the Geneva convention stuff) and starts playing a different one where the prize is the bridge itself, or maybe his own identity as an officer and engineer — and the horror is that nobody around him notices the prize changed, partly because escalating looks, from the inside and the outside, exactly like virtue: leadership, competence, espirit de corps. The audience gets seduced too, since the bridge-building stretch of the film is basically played as a triumph and its a great watch.

So what I'd like from you: read the film through the escalation of commitment literature — Shubik's dollar auction, the sunk-cost experiments, the distinction between escalation driven by face-saving or self-justification and what only looks like escalation but is really rational persistence under uncertainty. Does that literature actually predict a Nicholson, or does he need something else — identity fusion, self-deception, or the peculiar fact that the good money he's throwing after bad is other men's labor and lives rather than his own? Is there a real difference between the dollar auction, where the loser's motive is transparently that quitting now means eating the whole loss, and a case where the committed party has half-convinced himself the project is intrinsically worth finishing? And does the film show something the experiments can't — e.g. that escalation can be distributed across a whole camp, each person's small concession normalizing the next one's? Two asks on the honesty front: first, if you think the escalation frame is just the wrong lens for the second half and I'd be forcing it on my students, say that plainly — I'd much rather be told the film resists the frame than get a tidy reading that isn't there. Second, flag any point where my reading leans on a contested interpretation, whether of the experiments or of what the film intends, since I'll be repeating this stuff in front of a room.
