I teach an evening class on earth history for adults with zero science background, and next month's session is on the so-called age of humans — the Anthropocene. Now that the official stratigraphers voted the whole thing down in 2024 and the IUGS confirmed the rejection, I want the class to see that "when did the Anthropocene start, and who gets to decide that" is still a live fight with actual positions in it, not just a vocabulary footnote.

What I need is a decision tree that takes a reader from one starting commitment — roughly "humans have left a fingerprint on the earth system, and the question is when that became epoch-worthy" — to a named position on a start date AND on who's allowed to set it. Each branch node should be a question someone can answer without knowing any of the position names: for instance, does the marker have to be something a geologist 500,000 years from now could find in rock with no historical records at hand; does pre-industrial stuff count (rice paddies and forest clearance bending the atmosphere thousands of years ago); does the marker have to be one sharp global signal (a plutonium layer, that strange CO2 dip after the New World epidemics) or can it be a change that spreads gradually from region to region; is the culprit humanity as such or a particular economic system; and is pinning a date even a job for stratigraphers working under golden-spike rules, or is it a cultural question those rules were never built to settle.

Leaves I'd expect, roughly seven: Ruddiman's early-anthropogenic camp (start thousands of years back, with farming); the 1610 Orbis-spike people (Lewis and Maslin); the Industrial Revolution crowd (Crutzen, around 1784, steam engines); the Great Acceleration / Crawford Lake 1950 camp with its bomb plutonium; the 2024 winners who say there's no formal epoch at all and the Holocene just continues, keeping "Anthropocene" only as an informal descriptor; the Capitalocene folks who think the term indicts the wrong culprit; plus any genuinely occupied combination you come across. Each leaf should carry one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and please explicitly note the combinations the logical space allows that nobody actually occupies — is a given spot empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored?

Also, definately flag where the tree fails. My suspicion is it can't cleanly separate the keep-the-Holocene people from the Crawford Lake 1950 people, since they can agree on all the facts and differ only about procedure, but tell me where else it draws a line that doesn't really hold up.

Audience is curious adults with no geology, so every technical term you need (GSSP, diachronous, whatever comes up) should get half a line of plain-language explanation right there inline. Im guessing around eight to ten branch questions total is right, but use your judgment.

At the end, tell me how confident you are in the tree overall, where you'd bet a working stratigrapher or an ecomodernist would redraw a branch, and which single leaf assignment you're least sure of. That one specific weak point is what I want to stress-test in class, so no generic disclaimer please — point at the actual thing.
