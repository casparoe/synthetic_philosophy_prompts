I work in software supply-chain security — package managers, signed releases, SBOMs, transitive dependency trees, trust-on-first-use, reproducible builds. Lately I've been reading about the debate in the epistemology of testimony between reductionism and anti-reductionism, and I want it translated into my vocabulary, honestly. The reductionist picture (Hume's, roughly): testimony isn't a basic source of knowledge — every belief you get from someone's say-so needs backing from independent, non-testimonial evidence that testimony of that kind tracks the truth, the way an ideal world would validate every package in your tree against something that isn't itself just another package. The anti-reductionist picture (Reid's, and the modern revival): trust in what you're told is a default entitlement — you can't bootstrap it from non-testimonial evidence because the correction data itself arrives via testimony, so you can't pin every dependency without downloading from somewhere. Also do the middle position, local reductionism, which says you only need spot-checks against track records, not a global proof that testimony in general is reliable.

Then the residue report, which is what I actually care about: what translates cleanly (trust-on-first-use, track records as reputation scores, what a key rotation is an analog of), what translates only with distortion, and what has no counterpart in my world at all. My suspicion is that the thing that doesn't map is the testifier as an agent — someone who can be sincere or insincere, who can be addressed, challenged, held responsible for what they said — and I want to know whether that residue means my vocabulary is missing something real, or whether the philosophers are smuggling in a notion that doesn't do any work once you're honest about it. Don't be charitable to either side for my sake; if something in the debate only looks deep because of how the philosophy vocabulary is built, say so.
