I am a novelist, and I am planning a book set in Memphis during the sanitation workers' strike of 1968 — the one that began on February 12 after two workers, Echol Cole and Robert Walker, were crushed to death by the compactor of a malfunctioning garbage truck, and that ended weeks later, after Martin Luther King Jr. was assassinated in the city while supporting it, with union recognition and a raise for a workforce that was overwhelmingly Black. I want the novel's conceptual spine to be Hegel's master--slave dialectic, and I would like your help stress-testing that choice before I commit to it.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher with deep expertise in post-Kantian German idealism and in the philosophy of labor and race, and think carefully before answering; start with a short brainstorming phase in which you consider several possible mappings before settling on the most defensible one. Then analyze the strike through the lens of the dialectic, running the analysis in both directions, as I will use it in the book.

In the theory-to-case direction, be disciplined about the mapping: who or what plays the master, who or what plays the slave, what corresponds to the struggle unto death, to the fear of absolute negativity, and to work as the shaping of a world through which the laborer gains a self-certainty the master never achieves. Here the strikers' iconic signs demanding recognition as men, and their withdrawal of labor as such a withdrawal is the very thing that made them indispensable -- these seem to me the richest joints, but I may be pressing them into the wrong holes. Tell me where the fit is genuine and where it is strained.

In the case-to-theory direction, tell me what the strike exposes that the dialectic does not anticipate: that the struggle here was not for the master's status but against it; that recognition was being demanded of an employer, a city government, and a watching nation rather than a single opposed consciousness; that the demand was collective and juridical rather than dyadic and existential. And tell me plainly whether the dialectic, imported into this setting as my structure, risks exactly what the workers were refusing -- the reduction of a political fight to a drama between two consciousnesses. If you think a different Hegelian lens, or the lens of a later critic of recognition, would serve the novel better, say so and say why, but do not spare my idea just to be agreeable. A conclusion that the dialectic should appear only in the mouths of characters who are wrong about it is one I would accept.
