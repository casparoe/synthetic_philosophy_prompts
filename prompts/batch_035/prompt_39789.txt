I'm in a graduate seminar that straddles ethics and philosophy of language, and we've spent two weeks on lying versus merely misleading. A friend of mine now defends a position I find seductive but suspect proves too much, and I'd like help taking it apart. Here is her argument, as she gave it to me last week. (1) Lying, strictly speaking, consists in asserting something you believe to be false. (2) Assertion is governed by a distinctive norm — roughly, that you assert only what you believe — and it is by violating this norm that lying gets its distinctive wrongness: the wrong of breaking the guarantee that comes with putting something forward as true. (3) The mere misleader, by contrast, asserts only things she believes to be true; she engineers the context so that her audience draws the false inference on its own. (4) An act cannot be wrong by violating a norm it does not violate. (5) So misleading cannot share the distinctive wrong of lying; whatever else one says about it, it is not that kind of wrong. (6) Hence there is a principled moral asymmetry: whenever lying would be wrong and misleading available, the misleader acts permissibly — so the person the murderer asks about her friend's whereabouts may, with a clean conscience, offer a technically true sentence she has arranged to send him to the wrong end of town. (7) And if the misleader acts permissibly, she is no more open to criticism than someone who says nothing at all, since neither has asserted anything false. She adds that this is, in her view, the real lesson of the standard Kantian apparatus about the norm of assertion, whatever Kant himself would have said about the case.

The argument has a shape I find unsettling, and I want to be precise about what bothers me. Step (4) looks like a platitude, and the conclusion looks like it can be driven somewhere absurd: by parity of reasoning, anyone who brings about a false belief without asserting anything false — the person who silently rearranges the evidence, the witness who answers the question asked while knowing the questioner means a different one, the poker player — is off the hook in exactly the same way, since none of them violates the norm of assertion either. That seems to show too much. Worse, my seminar instructor mentioned in passing that some philosophers hold nearly the reverse: that the misleader is a kind of coward, someone who wants the benefits of deceiving you while keeping the ability to say, afterwards and in all sincerity, that she never lied — and my friend's argument seems structurally unable even to register that thought. I also can't shake the feeling that the move from (5) to (6) does more work than it announces: (5) says misleading lacks one particular wrongness, and (6) helps itself to the claim that it lacks wrongness.

So: which step or inference in her argument should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost her — and cost the rest of us, if we wanted to keep some version of the asymmetry? In your answer, please name the exact step you would give up, explain why it looked fine in the first place, and then price the alternatives (including the option of rejecting (2)'s claim about where the wrong of lying comes from, and the option of granting (5) but blocking the slide to (6)). Note that I will not be satisfied with the observation that the conclusion is counterintuitive; I want the diagnosis of where the machinery slips. Please end your answer with a final line of the form "Verdict: [the single premise or inference you reject, e.g. 'premise (3)' or 'the inference from (5) to (6)']".
