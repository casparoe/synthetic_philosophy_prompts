I'm the hiring manager at a 40-person logistics software company, and I've been tasked with redesigning how we screen applicants for mid-level roles (analysts, junior engineers, ops people). We get a few hundred applications per posting, I have maybe 20 minutes per batch, and we can't fly everyone in for structured interviews. Our board member — a big pop-econ book reader — keeps pushing the idea that we should make candidates jump through genuinely expensive hoops: a substantial unpaid take-home project, a portfolio built to our spec, maybe even requiring them to obtain some certification on their own dime before we'll even look at them. His argument is basically that "real commitment shows when it costs something," and half of me finds that convincing while the other half suspects it just filters for applicants with rich parents and free evenings.

I half-remember from a biology course the idea of the peacock's tail: it's honest advertising precisely because it's expensive, and only a genuinely healthy male can afford to carry it around. Someone on a podcast claimed the same logic explains why people do unpaid internships and gruelling degrees — the cost is the point. But it seems to me the logic only works if the expense hurts the candidates we'd want to reject more than the ones we'd want to hire, and I have no idea how to check that in practice for any given hoop. The cost of a 6-hour take-home is clearly not the same for a single parent working two jobs as for a 22-year-old living with roommates, and that difference has nothing to do with how good either of them would be at the job.

What I'd like is a procedure my screening team (two juniors plus me, no stats background between us) can actually run on any proposed screening requirement before we adopt it: an ordered list of questions to ask about the requirement, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where an answer kills the requirement dead, and where the procedure has to hand the decision back to me because it's a values call rather then a factual one. Then run it on three concrete cases: (1) the 6-hour unpaid take-home project; (2) requiring an industry certification that costs around $600 and three months to obtain; and (3) treating a caregiver gap on a CV as a negative signal, which one of my screeners keeps doing and which bugs me in a way I can't quite articulate. Please also include at least one place where the procedure gives an answer I won't like — I'll admit upfront that I find heartfelt cover letters persuasive, so if the procedure says they're worth nearly nothing as evidence, tell me that anyway. And be honest about where the whole framework misfires — for instance whether it matters that other employers in our sector are using the same hoops, and anything the procedure flatly cannot settle.
