I'm a science and ideas journalist, so I read a lot of pop-adjacent coverage of decision theory and cosmology, and last week a piece in a general-interest magazine I won't name tried to explain anthropic reasoning to lay readers. The gist, as I scribbled it down, was something like: "There are two ways to reason about your own existence. The first, called the self-sampling assumption, says you should treat yourself as a random sample from all the humans who will ever live -- so if the future held trillions of people, finding yourself alive this early would be wildly improbable, which is the doomsday argument. The second, the self-indication assumption, says your existence is evidence for hypotheses on which more observers exist, because there are more chances for you to be one of them. The philosopher Nick Bostrom dramatized this with the 'presumptuous philosopher': two theories of the universe, identical in every observable respect, except one has vastly more observers. SIA tells you to be near-certain of the crowded one, which is why Bostrom argued that SIA is the right assumption and anthropic reasoning is mostly harmless." I want to use some version of this in an article I'm drafting, but before I do I'd like to know how much of it survives contact with someone who actually knows this literature.

I'm inclined to think the basic two-views framing is right, since I've seen it elsewhere, but a few things nag at me. My recollection is that the presumptuous philosopher is meant to be uncomfortable for SIA, not an advertisement for it -- but maybe I misremember? Also the piece seemed to slide between "observers" and "humans" and "people in your reference class" as if those were interchangeable, and somewhere toward the end it claimed that once you adopt SIA, the doomsday argument simply cancels out and anthropic reasoning stops mattering for any practical question, which sounds too tidy.

So, sentence by sentence where possible: what would an anthropic-reasoning person accept, what would they wince at, what's just wrong? In particular I'd like to know (a) whether that first gloss of the self-sampling assumption leaves out anything load-bearing, (b) whether the description of SIA's logic is fair, (c) whether the article's take on what the presumptuous philosopher is supposed to show is a real misrepresentation, and (d) whether the "cancels out and never matters again" conclusion follows from the accurate parts. I'd also apprecaite a quick note on what the actual open dispute is, so I don't accidentally present one side's position as the settled reading. If it helps you calibrate: I don't need citations or a reading list, just the substance, since I'll be doing my own reporting on sources later.
