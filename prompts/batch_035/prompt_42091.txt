I am a novelist with fifteen years' experience, and I have come to hold a strong view about translation, shaped in part by my reading of Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot: that a literary work's formal constraints -- its meter, rhyme scheme, syllable count, stanzaic architecture -- are not a decorative container into which "the meaning" is poured, but are themselves part of what the work means and does. On this view, a translation that renders the semantic content faithfully while abandoning the form has not translated the work at all; it has produced an essay about the work. The implications are practical for me: I want my novels' wordplay, chapter-length constraints, and prose rhythms to be treated by my translators as binding commitments, not as unfortunate losses to be footnoted.

You are a philosopher of language who has also worked professionally as a literary translator. Think carefully and, before answering, briefly brainstorm the strongest objections you can imagine to this position. Then tell me, honestly and rigorously, what would count against it: which translations, cases, intuitions, or arguments should move me, and how decisive each would be. Consider, for instance, whether some formal properties are simply artifacts of the source language with no translatable function; whether the view conflates the causal history of a form with its contribution to meaning; and whether it leads to absurd conclusions when applied to traditions whose forms are radically untranslatable, say tonal patterning or logographic wordplay. Distinguish what a sophisticated defender of the view would concede as a genuine defeater from what they would dismiss, and rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Finally, tell me frankly whether the position can be qualified into unfalsifiability -- and whether, for a claim of this kind about art, that would even be a defect.
