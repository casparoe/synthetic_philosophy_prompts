I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I've been noticing a pattern in workplace communication — in standups, incident reviews, vendor negotiations — that I'd like a name for, if one exists. The configuration is this: a person makes only statements that are literally true, but selects and orders them so that the listener comes away believing something false, and the false belief is clearly the point, not an accident. A couple of instances. At a stakeholder review last quarter, someone asked whether we had fixed a particular flaky test, and the engineer on it answered that the test hadn't failed since Tuesday. True — because the test had been disabled and skipped since Tuesday. When I asked our release manager whether we were on track for the deadline, she said all critical-path tickets were closed. Also true — the remaining ones had been reclassified as non-critical the previous afternoon. In both cases the answer was engineered: had the speaker said the false thing outright, it would have been a lie; had they said nothing, it would have been evasion. Instead they said something true, chosen precisely for the false thing a reasonable listener would infer from it in context.

I want to be clear that this isn't the same as the more familiar moves around it. It's not ordinary vagueness or spin, because every sentence uttered is true, on-point, and informative at face value; the listener isn't left to guess from a non-answer. And I don't think it's plain deception by omission, because the speaker isn't just staying silent about the crucial fact — they make positive assertions, and those assertions do the deceptive work; the omitted fact just makes the true assertions mean the opposite of what they appear to mean. There's also a distinctive aftermath: when challenged, the speaker retreats to "everything I said was true," and it's oddly hard to pin down exactly what they did wrong in that moment, even though everyone in the room feels that something was.

So my questions are these. Does this configuration have an established name, either in philosophy or in ordinary language that I've simply never encountered? Is there any settled position on whether it's morally the same as lying, worse, or meaningfully different — and where exactly does the wrongness live: in the original utterance, in the expectation the speaker exploits, or only in the later refusal to own the inferred conclusion ("I never said the test was passing")? I'm also curious about the edges: does it still count when the speaker didn't specifically intend the false reading but obviously should have known how it would land, and does it matter whether the victim asked a direct question versus the statement being volunteered? Please don't just give me a label — I'd like the concept properly carved out, with the boundary cases, and if no established term covers it, tell me that honestly and propose one.
