Been reading the rationalist blogs for over a decade now (yes I did the whole sequences in college, no I never got around to meditating on the zombie thing) and I finally wrote out an argument I've seen floating around in pieces, and now that I see it all in one place it looks like it proves way too much. Tell me where it goes wrong.

(1) The coherence theorems: if an agent's preferences over lotteries violate the standard axioms — completeness, transitivity, independence — then a trader who knows those preferences can build a sequence of trades, each one the agent happily accepts, that leaves it strictly worse off with certainty. Transitivity violations give the classic pump: if you prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, I sell you C, buy A off you, buy B, sell you C again, skimming a fee every lap.

(2) Being money-pumped is the closest thing decision theory has to an actual proof of irrationality. Predictably paying for nothing, by your own lights, is just about the definition of bad agency. This is the part I trust most — it's math, not vibes.

(3) So a rational agent has a complete, transitive, independent preference ordering, i.e. it acts as an expected utility maximizer over some fixed utility function.

(4) But the pump also works across time. If I know your preferences today AND know they'll be different tomorrow in a specific direction, I can sell you the round trip: trade you into something today that you'll be desperate to trade out of tomorrow, taking a cut both ways. So a predictable preference change is an exploitable preference change, same as an intransitive one.

(5) Any agent still figuring out its values has predictable preference changes — that's just what figuring out means. A kid learning that other kids' pain matters like their own, a society halfway through a moral reform, an AI whose whole design goal is to learn what to care about from the humans around it.

(6) So all of those are irrational, and the only rational agents are ones with frozen values from day one. Value learning, moral progress, deliberating your way into caring about new things — all incoherent. Real agents get one utility function at t=0 and deviations from it are bugs.

I refuse to believe (6), but every earlier step looks like homework straight out of the blog canon. My suspicion is that something sleazy hides in the jump from (3) to (4), or maybe in what (1) quietly assumes about being able to trade with you at every moment, but I can't pin it down. Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it actually cost me? I'd really like to keep (1) and (2) — they're the only load-bearing planks in the whole neighborhood — so if I have to give something up elsewhere, I want to know exactly what I'm paying. Bonus points if you can tell me whether the escape hatch also saves the people using these theorems to argue that sufficiently smart agents must have stable goals, or whether those two projects come apart.

Also, answer in American English please.
