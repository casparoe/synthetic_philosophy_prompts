I'm writing a term paper for a graduate seminar on personal identity, and my advisor has walked me into an argument about the Buddhist no-self doctrine that I cannot find my way out of. Some background for the setup: the anatta doctrine denies that there is a persisting self; what exists, on this view, is a causally connected stream of psycho-physical events, the five aggregates, with nothing extra that owns them. The same tradition, of course, affirms rebirth and karmic inheritance, and when pressed on how the one who is reborn relates to the one who died, its stock answers are analogies — a flame passed from one candle to another, milk turning into curd, Nāgasena's chariot in the Milindapañha — together with the insistence that the one reborn is neither the same being as the one who died nor a different one. My advisor's argument tries to show that this refusal of both alternatives is not profundity but a contradiction.

Here is the argument. (1) If anatta is true, no persisting self exists; there are only causally connected streams of aggregates. (2) Rebirth with karmic inheritance is not meant as a metaphor: it is meant to be the case that the one who now bears the consequences of deeds done in an earlier life is the one who did them, in a sense strong enough to make those consequences deserved rather than a random affliction of an unconnected bystander. (3) For the consequences to be deserved, there must be a determinate answer to the question whether the present bearer is the same agent as the original doer, or a different one — these exhaust the possibilities for any pair of things, since identity is bivalent: x and y are identical or they are not. (4) The tradition denies sameness, on the ground that no self transmigrates, and it denies difference, on the ground that if the one reborn were simply a different being, karmic inheritance would be the punishment of the innocent and rebirth the replacement of one person by another. (5) So the tradition is committed to a third relation between the earlier stream and the later one that is neither identity nor distinctness. (6) But there is no third relation between things; identity and distinctness jointly exhaust the field. (7) Therefore the doctrine of rebirth, as the tradition itself understands it, is incoherent, and no-self and rebirth cannot both be affirmed.

The reason I suspect the conclusion proves too much is that the same reasoning seems to condemn views nobody wants to condemn. Applied by parity, it would show that Parfit's psychological continuity is incoherent too, since his claim that what matters is neither simple identity nor simple distinctness would, by steps (3) through (6), be a commitment to an impossible third relation. Worse, it would dissolve my ordinary concern for the person who will wake up in my bed tomorrow, since that later stream is, on the no-self view, also neither me nor another, and my advisor's argument would leave me with no reason to care about his toothache rather than a stranger's. That seems wrong even from within the tradition, whose whole practice is meant to benefit later links in the stream. My own preferences, if they matter: I would most like to keep (1) and (3), and I have no attachment to (4). So: tell me which step to reject, explain why that step looked so innocent, and say what rejecting it costs — including, if you think the Buddhist has a principled way to block the parity argument that would not equally rescue Parfit, what that way is and whether it is available without appeal to doctrine I have no way to verify. Finally, please say how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the point in your answer you are least sure of.
