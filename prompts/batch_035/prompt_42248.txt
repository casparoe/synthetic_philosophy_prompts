My rationalist meetup has this running bit: we treat our friend Dana's judgments about democratic backsliding like a weather service. She's followed this stuff obsessively since the 2010s, and she's annoyingly consistant, but if you ask her for her actual criteria she just waves you off — "I know it when I see it," which offends all of us on principle. So we've started a prediction registry: whenever a new case comes up (some country in the news, or a hypothetical one of us cooks up), we each write down what Dana will say and why, and the person whose model of her is closest buys the next round. I'm currently leading and intend to stay ahead.

Her track record, compressed: the Hungary-type case, where an elected government gradually stacked the media regulator and the constitutional court while keeping the opposition on a ballot it could never win — backsliding, yes. Turkey — yes, obviously. Poland — yes, though she took her time and only committed after the second round of fighting over the courts. The military coup that ran the country for two years, held elections, and went back to barracks — firm no, "that's a coup, different box." The US-shaped case — also a no: attempts to contest results and mess with courts that all failed and left nothing stuck, she called it constitutional hardball, a stress test the system passed. And a case where gerrymandering gave one party a structural edge but the press was free, the courts were fine, and the opposition did occasionally win anyway — no on that one too; her gloss was "tilt without capture, and it doesn't change who can win."

The new case, which is hypothetical, comes from our group's resident GM: a mid-sized country where a populist party wins a legal supermajority, rewrites the constitution, and uses it to entrench. Core policies get locked behind supermajority amendment rules, and a stack of new agencies gets nine-year staggered boards that the current majority staffs, so the next government can't touch them. Courts stay fully independent, the press stays fully free, elections stay genuinely competitive — in fact the incumbent loses the capital and several provinces mid-stream and hands them over without a fuss. But whoever wins the next national election inherits a state where most of the levers are held by the other side's appointees and half the agenda is constitutionally off the table. Dana's verdict is due Tuesday.

What should I predict? Honestly I care less about the yes/no than about which rule she's running under the hood. I can see at least three that fit her whole track record — referee capture, playing-field tilt, and something like entrenchment-so-that-losing-doesn't-cost-the-incumbents-anything — and as far as I can tell they come apart on this one. Give me your best guess with your reasons, lay out which candidate rules survive and which die if she rules either way, and, since the registry also pays out on discriminating cases, tell me what further case would most cleanly split the surviving rules so I can pitch it to the GM for next session.
