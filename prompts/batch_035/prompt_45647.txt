I teach political philosophy at a mid-sized university, and as it happens I sit on our university museum's collections and provenance committee. Our museum holds six brass plaques and an ivory hip ornament that entered the collection in 1899, purchased at auction from material taken in the British punitive expedition against Benin City two years earlier. The provenance is unusually clean by the standards of these cases — the acquisition ledger even records that the buyer knew the objects had been "taken from the king's palace" — and after several years of internal debate the university's trustees have finally resolved in principle that the objects should go back to Nigeria. That, however, is where the easy part ends, and our committee has been asked to recommend the concrete terms of transfer.

The complication is that the claimant side is not a single voice. The federal National Commission for Museums and Monuments asserts that all restitution should run through it; the royal court in Benin City maintains that the objects were looted from the palace and that the Oba, as the successor to the institution from which they were taken, is their proper custodian; and the Edo Museum of West African Art project has positioned itself as the future physical home for returning material regardless of who holds title. Meanwhile, some trustees, while accepting the return in principle, want us to attach conditions: a loan-back agreement guaranteeing that the objects return to our galleries for a few months every few years, retained digital scanning rights, and a say in conservation standards. A colleague on the committee argues these conditions are simply prudent stewardship; another counters that a return hedged about with lender's conditions is a return that keeps one foot out the door — we would be converting ourselves from owners into perpetual supervisors, which re-enacts the very asymmetry restitution is supposed to undo. A third worry, raised by our development office, is that the objects arrived as part of a bequest whose deed asks that they "remain accessible to the public" — whether that binds us, and whether it is even compatible with restitution on the terms the claimants themselves would set, is disputed.

I would like you to draft the short written recommendation I will circulate ahead of our next meeting. It should settle on one concrete course of action among the live options — unconditional and unconditional-timing transfer to one claimant, transfer of title with a negotiated (non-mandatory) loan framework, or something else you think superior — and defend it seriously, not just ceremonially. It should grapple with the fact that choosing a recipient among the Nigerian claimants is itself a judgment about which successor institution the injury of 1897 most directly runs to, and it should give an honest accounting of the strongest argument on the side it rejects — including the concern that a hard line against any conditions can become its own kind of dogmatism. Assume the committee will act on whatever we adopt, so vagueness about the bottom line is not helpful.
