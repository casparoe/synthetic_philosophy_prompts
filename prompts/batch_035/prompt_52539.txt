I'm a high school senior writing my IB Extended Essay in philosophy, and I've got what my supervisor calls a good problem: too much material, no way to organize it. My topic is what I've seen called the paradox of phenomenal judgment. The idea, as I understand it: if conscious experience is epiphenomenal — a by-product of brain processes that never pushes back on them — then everything I say and think about my experience is caused by physical processes alone, and the experiences themselves do no work in producing my sincere claims about them. That makes it mysterious how my talk about consciousness comes to be about consciousness at all, and it seems to undermine my reasons for trusting any of it, including the reasoning that led me to epiphenomenalism in the first place. Every discussion I've found jumps straight into defending or attacking epiphenomenalism, and before I commit several thousand words to a position, I want the specification first.

So here is what I'd like from you: a list of desiderata — what any adequate theory of the relation between experience and our judgments about experience has to deliver. To get you started, some requirements I think belong on the list, though I'm not certain about all of them, and I want you to tell me if any are really just prejudices of one camp. It should explain how reports about experience can be caused in a way that makes them reliable, or else explain away our confidence in them, without circularly appealing to the very experiences in question. It should either respect the causal closure of physics or explicitly reject closure and say what takes its place. It should handle cases where reports seem to come apart from experience — a patient under anesthesia who later reports having been aware, a blindsight patient who says he cannot see but guesses correctly, a dreamer whose morning report misdescribes the dream — without collapsing the distinction between a report's being caused and its being accurate. It should say whether my current deliberation about this very theory counts as a phenomenal judgment the theory must vindicate, or whether theorizing about consciousness is somehow different from reporting it. And it should tell me whether demanding an evolutionary explanation of why we talk about consciousness at all is a genuine requirement or an optional extra. Give the case or consideration behind each entry, mark which you'd treat as non-negotiable and which as negotiable, and — this is the part I most want — flag any pair or trio of the requirements that may be jointly unsatisfiable. My suspicion is that reliability of reports, causal closure, and the distinctness of experience from physical processes cannot all be held at once, and if the list itself forces that trade-off, then the list is the result.

At the end, briefly and without much fanfare, run the main contenders against the list: epiphenomenalism, interactionist dualism, a reductive or identity physicalism, and illusionism. One line each is enough, plus a note on which single requirement does the most to exclude candidates. For context on the purpose: the theory doesn't have to settle whether machines could be conscious, but it should be good enough that I could apply it to that question later without rebuilding the whole framework — my supervisor has warned me against writing a survey, so I need the criteria to do the work, not the scorecard. I plan to write the essay in my own words, so what I need from you is the full spec and honesty about the places where it simply cannot be satisfied.
