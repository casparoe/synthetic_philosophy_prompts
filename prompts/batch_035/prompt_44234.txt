I'm a high school junior writing an extended essay for my Theory of Knowledge class on whether people have stable character traits. My teacher lent me a popular psychology book with a chapter titled "The Death of Character" (I'd rather not name the book, since I'm reconstructing the argument from my notes and may get details wrong). As I understand it, the chapter's argument goes roughly like this:

"In Milgram's famous obedience experiments, about two-thirds of ordinary, decent people were willing to administer what they believed were dangerous electric shocks to a stranger, simply because a calm man in a lab coat told them to — and by and large they went on doing it without visible distress, as though the situation had simply taken them over. In the Good Samaritan study, theology students who had just been preparing a talk on the parable of the Good Samaritan stepped around a man slumped in a doorway because they were told they were running late; how much of a hurry they were in predicted whether they stopped to help, while the personality measures the researchers had taken predicted nothing. Findings like these show that character traits as we ordinarily understand them — stable dispositions such as honesty and courage that carry across situations — do not exist. What looks like a person's character is really a set of situational pressures that we don't even notice. Virtue ethics, which tells us that the moral life consists in acquiring exactly these stable traits, is therefore refuted by the experimental record. The practical lesson is that we should stop trying to cultivate character and start redesigning situations: don't teach people to be honest, build environments in which it is hard to be anything else."
