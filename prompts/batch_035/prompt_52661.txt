For most of my working life I was a physical chemist, and since retiring I have been reading widely in philosophy of science and the rationality literature, where I keep encountering appeals to Aumann's agreement theorem. A popular book on decision-making that I recently finished summarizes it like this: "In 1976, Robert Aumann proved that rational agents cannot agree to disagree. If two people begin with the same prior beliefs and each updates on private information, then as soon as each learns the other's posterior probability, they must end up with identical beliefs. The upshot is that persistent disagreement between two honest, intelligent people is evidence of irrationality, and that much public debate is theater: since rational agents converge on the truth the moment they exchange conclusions, a protracted argument between experts signals that at least one of them is motivated or incompetent." In my own career, disagreement with colleagues was usually productive rather than pathological, so I suspect something is off in this summary, but I don't yet have the background to say exactly where.

Could you audit it for me, claim by claim? Please tell me which parts a Bayesian game theorist would accept as stated, which would make them wince because a technical condition has been quietly dropped or a term has slid into its everyday sense, and which are simply wrong. I have a few suspicions of my own, which you should feel free to correct where they are misplaced: (i) the phrase "as soon as each learns the other's posterior" sounds like ordinary two-way knowledge, whereas my understanding is that the theorem requires their posteriors to be common knowledge — the infinitely nested condition of my knowing that you know that I know, and so on — which is far stronger, and that the theorem can genuinely fail when the agents merely know each other's posteriors; (ii) "identical beliefs" seems stronger than what the theorem licenses, since it concerns the posterior probability of a particular event rather than the totality of the two agents' beliefs; and (iii) "converge on the truth" — does the theorem say anything about truth at all, or only about the two agents converging with each other, possibly onto a shared error?

Finally, the book goes on to draw a practical moral from all this: "once you have heard your opponent's considered opinion and still disagree, further discussion is a waste of time, and you are entitled to conclude that your opponent is either dishonest or irrational." Does this practical moral follow from whatever parts of the summary survive your audit, or does it depend essentially on the misrepresentations — in particular, on treating a single exchange of conclusions as sufficient for the common-knowledge condition, and on treating any residual disagreement as ipso facto evidence of irrationality?
