So me and my friend Raj have this thing going — we've been reading about the Churchlands and Dennett and Stich on our own for maybe two years now, mostly evenings after work. Neither of us studied philosophy at uni (I did electrician training, he does accounting) so it's all been kitchen-table arguing. We decided to formalize it: once a month one of us writes an "exam" for the other, one hour, closed book, written out, and then we mark each other's answers as honestly as we can. It's my turn to set the paper and I want it on eliminative materialism — the idea that beliefs and desires might just not exist, the way phlogiston turned out not to.

Here's my problem. When we discuss this we keep falling back on the same two moves: he says "but if beliefs don't exist, how can you believe that they don't," and I say "well, chemists dumped phlogiston and doctors dumped evil spirits as causes of disease, nobody thinks that was self-refuting" — and then we just trade those two back and forth all evening like a seesaw. I want an essay question that forces whoever answers it off that seesaw. In particular I want it to make the answerer notice that my phlogiston comeback actually cuts both ways: the chemists who denied phlogiston still had beliefs, so the analogy only works if belief is not in the same boat as phlogiston — but if the eliminativist is right that belief IS in that boat, then the analogy itself was presumably just some believing happening, which is exactly his point. Unless you take something like Dennett's line that belief-talk picks out real patterns, so the position ends up being revision rather than elimination, in which case the self-refutation worry maybe never got a grip. So the question should make the person take a stance on whether BOTH the "you're refuting yourself" objection and the "we've abandoned posits before, calmly" reply can be answered at the same time, or whether you have to pick one, and what you pay for it on the other side.

What I want from you: the question itself (answerable in an hour), a model answer of around 600 words, and a marking scheme. Don't offer me three alternative questions to pick between — just commit to the single best one you can write, and in the model answer commit to a definite position even if you think the issue is contested (that's the whole point of the exercise for us, we want to see where someone actually plants their feet). For the marking scheme, the thing I most care about is being able to tell apart an answer that just recieves "folk psychology is a bad theory and neuroscience will replace it" from one that actually sees where the pressure points are — so name the specific move that separates a top answer from a merely decent one, and name the specific thing that should lose marks (like, what does the self-refutation objection actually have to claim about the eliminativist's own utterance for it to bite, and what does a good answer have to say about that). Total marks out of 20 if that helps you structure it.
