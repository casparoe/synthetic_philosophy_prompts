I write a science column for a mid-size paper, and last month I did a piece on a new antidepressant trial where I wrote that there's a 95% chance the drug works. A retired stats professor wrote in — quite friendly about it — saying that's not just wrong but meaningless, and laid out why. I've been chewing on it for weeks because it seems to prove way too much. His argument, as best I can reconstruct it: (1) Probability means long-run frequency — the probability of a die showing six is just how often it does across indefinite repetition. (2) Frequencies only attach to things you can repeat, so probability only applies to repeatable events. (3) "This drug works" is not a repeatable event — either it does or it doesn't, once and for all, so there's no long run in which it comes up true 95 times out of 100. (4) So no hypothesis of that kind has a probability at all — not "the coin is biased," not "the defendant is guilty," not "climate sensitivity is above 3 degrees." (5) The 95% really belongs to the procedure: rerun the whole experiment many times and 95% of the intervals it produces will capture the true value, whatever it happens to be. (6) So I should stop printing probabilities of hypotheses and start printing probabilities of methods, or at least stop confusing the two. The thing is, if (4) is right then every weather forecast I've ever relayed ("70% chance of rain Tuesday") is gibberish too, and he cheerfully accepts that — he says the forecaster's number is either a subjective degree of belief smuggled in where it doesn't belong, or at best a frequency across "similar days," which he notes no one ever defines. That seems like a whole lot to give up, and I also can't quite see how his own move to (5) escapes, since "rerun the experiment" is a hypothetical I'm not going to execute either. Where does this go wrong, if it does? I suspect something slides between (3) and (4), maybe between an event and a claim about the event, but I can't pin it down. And if the argument is actually sound, what exactly am I allowed to say to my readers when the trial comes out?
