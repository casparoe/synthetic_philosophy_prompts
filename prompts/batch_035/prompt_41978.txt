I'd like your help extracting a decision principle from my gut reactions to variants of the two-envelope problem. The setup throughout: two envelopes, one holding some amount of money and the other twice that amount; I'm handed one of them and may either keep it or swap for the other. What varies across the cases is what I'm told about how the amounts and the assignment were determined, and whether I may look inside my envelope first. Here are my verdicts, stated as firmly as I actually hold them.

1. Classic case. I know only the doubling relation, nothing about how the amounts were chosen or which envelope I got. My envelope is sealed. Verdict: swapping is worthless; I wouldn't pay a penny to swap, and I'd be embarrassed to accept a penny to swap back.

2. The same case, but I'm told in advance that I'll get exactly one opportunity to swap, sealed, before either envelope is opened. Verdict: still indifferent. The thought that keeps me honest is that a standing policy of swapping must lose to a policy of keeping, since the two policies end up holding opposite envelopes that were assigned by the same procedure.

3. I'm told the envelopes contain $100 and $200, but not which one I'm holding; my envelope is sealed. Verdict: indifferent. Knowing the actual amounts changes nothing, which already makes me suspicious of any formula that says "the expected gain from switching is a quarter of what's in my envelope."

4. Same as (3), but I open my envelope and see $100. Verdict: now I lean toward swapping, and I catch myself running the argument that the other envelope is equally likely to hold $50 or $200. Then I notice the same argument would apply verbatim after I swapped, and I wobble. I'm honestly about 60/40 for swapping here, which irritates me, because it conflicts with (3).

5. I'm told the smaller amount was drawn uniformly from between $10 and $5,000, and I open my envelope. If I see $300, swap; if I see $4,000, keep. These verdicts feel solid and quantitative, not just hunches.

6. I'm told the smaller amount is exactly $100. I open my envelope and see $100. Verdict: obviously keep — the other envelope certainly holds $200... wait, no: it certainly holds the double, but mine is certainly the smaller one, so swapping gains me exactly $100. What I mean is: if I instead see $200, keep, because the other certainly holds $100. Sorry — the case I care about is this one, and my verdict on it is that once I've seen an amount and know the distribution, the right answer is just to condition on what I've seen and take the better expected value. This verdict I hold firmly.

7. I'm told the pair was chosen as follows: for some whole number n, the pair is ($2^n, $2^(n+1)) with the smaller amount being $2^n, and the pair for n was selected with probability proportional to (1/3)^n, normalized over n = 0, 1, 2, ... . I open my envelope and see some specific amount. However much I see, an expected-value calculation conditioned on that amount says swapping gains. Verdict: given what I see, swap. I've checked the arithmetic and I trust it. But I refuse to conclude, ahead of opening anything, that I should pay to swap sight unseen — which is what (1) and (2) tell me not to do, and this is where my set of verdicts stops cohering. I either endorse conditional swapping here and give up the symmetry argument in (1) and (2), or I keep symmetry and have to say something about why conditioning on the seen amount doesn't govern, even though in (5) and (6) I insisted it does.

8. A degenerate case I want on the table. I'm told one envelope holds triple the other, not double. The argument with the shape of the one I caught myself running in (4) now says the other envelope is worth, in expectation, about 1.67 times mine. Run it with "a thousand times" and it says the other envelope is worth about 500 times mine, which is absurd on its face given symmetry. Verdict: whatever that argument is, it proves too much, and this seems to me to bear on whether the reasoning in (4) and (7) is of the same species.

What I want from you: a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely enough to compute with, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible. For each clause of the principle, tell me which case motivates it. Be explicit about which of my verdicts your preferred principle cannot accommodate, and make the case for overturning exactly those — in particular, tell me whether my firm commitment in (7) to conditional swapping is compatible with my firm commitment in (1) and (2) to unconditional indifference, or whether one must be a mistake and which one. I'd also like the principle's verdict on the case I keep going back and forth on but haven't dared to rank: the setup of (7), except that after opening my envelope I've forgotten the exact amount and remember only that it was between $80 and $120. Finally, state a prediction of the principle for a case type I haven't mentioned — say, a three-envelope variant or a version where the amount in my envelope is revealed to a friend who then advises me — so I can test it against my own reactions.
