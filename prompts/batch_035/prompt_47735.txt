I'm in a decision theory course this semester and we just covered the St Petersburg paradox, but the thing that's actually eating me is the Pasadena game from that Nover and Hájek paper. Quick setup in case it matters: flip a fair coin until it lands heads, and if heads comes up on toss n you get paid 2^n/n dollars with the sign alternating — +2 if heads comes on the first toss, -2 on the second, +8/3 on the third, -4 on the fourth, +32/5 on the fifth, and so on forever. If you write out the expected value as the sum of (probability × payoff), the magnitudes cancel out and you get something that looks like the alternating harmonic series, which converges... but only conditionally. So by the Riemann rearrangement theorem, if you group or reorder the terms differently you can make the "expected value" of the exact same game come out to anything you want, or diverge to infinity. Compared to St Petersburg, where the problem is at least a definite value (infinity), this feels way worse — there's no number there at all, just an artifact of how you happen to write the series down.

Our professor kind of waved it off as a technical curiosity, but a friend of mine who's really into effective altruism pointed out that EAs run expected value arguments about preventing existential risk, where you multiply tiny probabilities by astronomically large payoffs (trillions of future lives etc.), and literally nobody checks whether those infinite sums even converge. That made me wonder if Pasadena is less of a curiosity than it looks. And then someone in section mentioned an idea called "weak expectations" (Easwaran, I think?) where instead of the series you look at whether the average over many plays converges almost surely — but I don't get why playing it repeatedly should be relevant to whether I should pay to play it once, so if you bring that in you have to say why that move is legitimate.

So here's what I want: an essay on what the Pasadena game actually shows about expected value reasoning. Is EV maximization just broken for these cases, or is there a principled way to assign the game a value — some canonical ordering of the terms, some constraint on how you're allowed to aggregate, maybe treating the series as undefined and refusing to choose? And does any of this matter practically — are the EA-style calculations with tiny probabilities of huge payoffs genuinely Pasadena-like, or is there a relevant structural difference that saves them? Reach a verdict and defend it: either EV theory survives with a fix (say which one and why it's not ad hoc), or Pasadena is a real counterexample to decision theory and something has to give (say what), or it's idle because nothing in real decision-making has that structure (say why the EA worry fails). Don't just survey the options — I need to commit to one in my paper and I want to see the argument done properly.
