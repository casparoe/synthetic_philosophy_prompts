My dad's hospital had him fill out one of those advance directive forms — the paperwork where you say now, while you're of sound mind, what care you'd want later if you can't speak for yourself anymore, like with advanced dementia. It got me thinking about my grandpa: by the end he seemed content most days and probably would have said yes to more time, but a form he signed twenty years earlier said no heroic measures, and the doctors followed the form. So the system basically ruled that the man who filled out the form matters more than the man in the bed. What theory of the person, or of whose life it even is, does that practice presuppose? My hunch is it assumes the earlier self is the "real" one and the later self is just something that happened to him. But some experiences change what you want in ways the earlier self couldn't have imagined before going through them — how could he possibly consent on behalf of a person he couldn't predict? Or maybe the practice rests on something else entirely, like the idea that a promise binds you even once you'd rather it didn't. Please take the role of a professional philosopher who knows the personal identity literature, think carefully and maybe brainstorm a couple of candidate theories before committing, and then tell me: what exactly does the practice presuppose, do the presuppositions hang together, is it underdetermined (i.e., several different theories would get you to the same result), and what would have to change if we decided the current patient's wishes should count for more? Keep it honest — if the practice only makes sense on one story, say so, and if several would work, say that too.
