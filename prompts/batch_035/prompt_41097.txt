My friend Dara and I have been arguing about Searle's Chinese Room on and off since undergrad, and we finally got sick of talking past each other. She's basically a Searlean: she thinks the argument shows that running the right program is never enough for genuine understanding, that syntax can't get you semantics, period. I'm a systems-reply type: I think the argument at most shows the *man* in the room doesn't understand Chinese, and that concluding anything about the whole setup is a level mistake. We want to run a proper adversarial collaboration, and I need you to draft the joint statement we could both sign.

What it needs:

1. A numbered section of claims we both accept. Real ones, not filler — e.g. we both agree the man himself doesn't understand Chinese, that memorizing a rulebook isn't what a native speaker's fluency is, and that behaving as if you understand doesn't settle the question by itself. But push past the obvious where you can.

2. Our disagreements, each phrased so that its own side would sign the wording. There are at least three: (a) whether understanding should be attributed to the whole room-system or whether only systems with the right causal powers can have it; (b) whether syntax sufficing for semantics is a contingent open question or a barrier in principle; and (c) whether the argument applies to modern LLMs at all — Dara says they're just faster rooms, I say the thought experiment was built around a lookup-table process and never contemplated a system trained end-to-end, so its an open question, not a settled one.

3. The cruxes: sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of us. For each, give a concrete case, test, or line of argument we'd both agree bears on it, with each of our predictions written down in advance. Be specific — not "a mature theory of intentionality" but something like: if an AI learned a brand-new invented symbol system purely from worked examples, with no paraphrases in our language anywhere in its training, what could and couldn't it do afterwards, and what would each of us predict about that.

4. If part of our dispute is just verbal — say, whether we're using understanding as an everyday success term or as a technical one — say which part and why. But don't use that to dissolve the whole thing; neither of us thinks it's all verbal.

Format: numbered agreed-claims section, then a table for the disagreements with columns for the crux, Dara's version, mine, and what would count as the crux being resolved. Keep the whole document under 800 words — we want something we'd actually circulate to people, not a treatise. No citations or reading lists either, it should stand on its own.
