I'm on the team at a mid-sized AI lab drafting our assistant's refusal and pushback policies, and I want the specification written before we fight about cases. Write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of when an AI assistant may refuse, deflect, or water down a user's request on purely paternalistic grounds — for the user's own good, not legal risk or third-party harm — has to get right. Some considerations I want treated as requirements with the case behind each:

- It must say what, if anything, can substitute for informed consent when the user can't cross-examine the assistant in real time and may not even be told a refusal is paternalistic rather than required.
- It must handle leakiness: a refusal is typically circumventable — the user retries with a paraphrase, asks a jailbreak-tolerant model, or gets worse information from a forum. If a policy is self-undermining in this way, the theory has to say so rather than treating the refusal as the end of the story.
- It must confront that the assistant bears none of the costs of the user's choice, has no ongoing relationship with the user, and is not a fiduciary — precisely the features that justify paternalism in the doctor or parent cases it gets analogized to.
- It must work for the fact that one model serves hundreds of millions of users of wildly differing competence, so any threshold of "this user can decide for themselves" is set blind; and it must not collapse in the other direction into sycophancy, where the assistant's judgment never counts against the user's present wishes.
- It must respect the distinction between overriding what the user wants now and overriding what the user has said they want overall — refusing a gambling addict's midnight request the same user asked to have refused a month ago is a different animal from refusing a stranger's same request.
- It must treat reversibility as mattering, and it must say whether the same standard can cover both one-shot refusals and subtler moves: disclaimers, loaded framing, burying the answer, answering a question the user didn't ask.

Say which of these are non-negotiable and which are preferences of one camp (the user-autonomy camp, the "assistants are not vending machines" camp), flag any pair of requirements you suspect can't be jointly satisfied, and identify the single requirement that does the most to exclude the main approaches labs actually use — blanket topic refusals, tiered model access, constitution-style lists. Then, unlike a spec, I want a committed verdict from you: if the desiderata are jointly unsatisfiable in practice, tell me which one you would drop and why, rather than presenting the tension as something to be balanced case by case. A short scorecard of the live policy families against your final list is welcome, but the criteria are the deliverable.
