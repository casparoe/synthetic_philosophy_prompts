I'm TAing a big intro-level philosophy of language course this semester (first year grad student, inherited the syllabus from the professor), and I'm staring down a stack of midterm short-answer responses on Russell. The question I wrote for the exam reads:

"Russell argued that his theory of descriptions dissolves several puzzles raised by the sentence 'The present King of France is bald.' (1) Explain the analysis the theory gives of this sentence — what exactly does it assert, according to Russell, and why does the analysis help with the puzzle of how a sentence about a non-existent entity can be meaningful (and the related puzzle about the law of excluded middle)? (2) Explain the scope ambiguity that arises when negation is added, and say what the theory predicts for 'The present King of France is not bald.' (3) Does the theory, as Russell presented it, extend to empty proper names like 'Vulcan'? Briefly justify. (4) How did Strawson criticize the theory, and what did he propose instead?"

One of my students, who's usually solid, wrote this:

"Russell's theory of descriptions says that a sentence with a definite description isn't really about an object at all — it's shorthand for a conjunction of claims. 'The present King of France is bald' says three things: that there is at least one King of France, that there's at most one, and that whatever is King of France is bald. So the sentence comes out false, because the first claim fails. This solves the meaningfulness puzzle because we don't need to posit a non-existent King as the subject; there's no commitment to weird entities like Meinong's round square. It also solves the excluded middle problem: since 'The King of France is bald' is false and so is 'The King of France is not bald', Russell has to admit that the law of excluded middle fails for empty descriptions, which he was upfront about — it only holds for genuine subjects. For the negation sentence, the theory predicts it comes out plainly true, since there is no King of France, and hence he can't be bald, so he's not bald — this is just how negation works on absent subjects, like in the Meinong case. The theory does extend to empty proper names: Russell explicitly treats 'Vulcan' as a disguised description (something like 'the planet causing such-and-such perturbations in Mercury's orbit), so the same three-part analysis applies and 'Vulcan is a planet' comes out false rather than meaningless. Strawson disagreed: he argued the sentence is simply false, but for presuppositional reasons — the existence and uniqueness claims are presupposed rather than asserted, so the presupposition fails. So Strawson and Russell agree on the truth value, they just disagree about why."
