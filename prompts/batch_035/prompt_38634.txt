In 1889, Poincaré was awarded the prize that King Oscar II of Sweden had offered for work on the three-body problem, even though a serious error was found in his memoir just after the award was announced; he corrected it at his own expense, with the revised memoir appearing in 1890. In it he showed that for certain configurations of three bodies the series expansions on which celestial mechanics relied need not converge, and that initially nearby trajectories could thereafter behave very differently — a result now routinely described as the first discovery of deterministic chaos. Analyze this episode through the lens of chaos theory as it was later developed. Map the elements carefully: what in Poincaré's result corresponds to sensitive dependence on initial conditions, and what does not. Then be honest about where the mapping is anachronistic: Poincaré had no notion of an attractor, a Lyapunov exponent, or a prediction horizon, and his theorem was about the failure of one representation (convergent series solutions) rather than a direct result about the practical limits of forecasting — so say plainly how much of "chaos theory" is genuinely present in 1890 and how much is retrofitted. Finally, consider what the episode shows about determinism: does it establish, decades before quantum mechanics, that Laplace's ideal of deriving the future from the present fails even in a strictly classical world, and if so, does the failure concern predictability rather than determinism itself — a distinction the later theory trades on but that Poincaré never had occasion to draw?
