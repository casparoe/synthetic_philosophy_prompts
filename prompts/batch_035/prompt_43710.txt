After a family dinner spent listening to my brother's enthusiasm for the Navy pilot sightings and the Pentagon's UAP reports, my uncle, a retired physicist, closed the subject with the line that extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence, and everyone nodded as though something had been demonstrated. I am half-persuaded by him, but when I tried to say exactly what that sentence claims, I found I could not. Please lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing, and give a verdict on each: the Bayesian reading on which it is close to tautological (and arguably misphrased, since what must be extraordinary is not the evidence but the likelihood ratio it carries); the Humean reading on which a sufficiently low prior swamps any realistic weight of testimony, however credible the witnesses; the reading about kinds of evidence, on which extraordinary claims demand physical traces and instrument records rather than merely more observers; and the deflationary reading on which 'extraordinary' just means 'a claim to which I have already assigned a low prior,' so that the slogan becomes a device for ending inquiry rather than guiding it. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible principle, or outright false, and say which of them, if any, would actually entitle my uncle to refuse to examine the footage and testimony at all, as opposed to merely expecting it to add up to little. Finally, address whether the relevant prior is even secure enough to carry that weight: 'life exists somewhere' seems less like a miracle claim than most, given that life demonstrably exists here, and I suspect a good part of my uncle's confidence rests on Fermi-paradox reasoning — that the galaxy's silence licenses a very low prior for visitors. Is that reasoning firm enough to do the work his slogan needs it to do?
