Watched Arrival for the first time a couple weeks ago and its been living in my head ever since, because of some stuff going on in my life. Quick context: my dad got diagnosed with early-onset dementia last month, and the neurologist more or less laid out the whole course for us -- roughly how it'll go, when things get bad, what the end looks like. So when the film got to the part where Louise has already seen her daughter's whole life, including the death, and goes ahead and has her anyway, it hit me pretty hard. My partner thinks I'm overthinking a movie. Maybe. But I keep circling one thing and I want an actual reading, not vibes.

Here it is. Aristotle has this puzzle about a sea battle: if it's already true today that there will be a battle tomorrow, rather than the denial being true, then the future looks settled all the way back, and fate sneaks in through the side door -- so the usual move is to say future-tense statements aren't true or false yet, and the future stays open. Arrival seems to take the other side of that and mean it: Louise's scenes with her daughter are shot exactly like her memories, and the film's whole emotional bet is that she chooses that life while knowing how it turns out. What I want to know is whether a film like that even makes sense if the future is open. If "Hannah will die young" was not already true before Louise ever met Ian -- true the way the sea battle would have to be -- then what exactly is she seeing when she "remembers" it?

So read the film as taking a position on the sea battle problem, and tell me what the position is. Does the film need those future statements to already be true (settled, fixed, whatever you want to call it) for Louise's choice to count as the choice the film says it is? Or can it get by on something weaker, and if so, what? And do you think the film keeps apart knowing what will happen from its being fixed that it will, or does it get its effect by sliding between the two? Please don't hand me a menu of positions with pros and cons -- commit to one reading and argue it, and if a scene pushes back against your reading, say so instead of quietly dropping it.

Last thing, one paragraph on what I'm actually circling under all this: if you know the general shape of how something will go -- not because the future is metaphysically closed, just because experts have told you, the way I now know how my dad's next years probably go -- does that put you in the sea battle situation anyway, or is that exactly when the puzzle starts looking like a technicality? Its fine if you think thats a stretch. Just tell me which way you come down.
