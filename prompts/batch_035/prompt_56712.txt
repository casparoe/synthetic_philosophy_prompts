I'm a graduate TA running discussion sections for a second-year course on rational choice theory. Next week's fifty-minute section covers Newcomb's problem. The students have already worked through expected utility maximization and dominance reasoning, but they have not formally encountered causal versus evidential decision theory, and I want to keep it that way for now — the goal is for them to feel the genuine pull of both the one-boxing and two-boxing arguments before they have labels that let them file the disagreement away. Please don't explain the problem to me; plan how I should teach it.

Specifically, I'd like the following:

1. The order the pieces must go in. Which features of the setup — the predictor's accuracy, the timing of the prediction, the fact that box contents are already fixed at choice time, the fact that the students know all of this — have to be nailed down before the dilemma actually bites, and which parts of the standard presentation are load-bearing rather than decoration? Where should I put the moment where the students are asked to commit to what "the predictor is almost never wrong about people like you" obliges them to accept about their own choice?

2. The carrying example. I'm torn between building the session around the standard black-box version and a variant in which the predictor is explicitly described as something the students might actually face — a copy of the agent that has already deliberated, or a process that observes the agent's earlier behaviour. What must the carrying case have, and lack, for students to be unable to dismiss the dilemma as an artifact of an impossible omniscient predictor? Which variant best serves a section whose real aim is the tension between dominance and expected-payoff reasoning, and why?

3. The misconception map. My predictions are: (a) that students will reason as if their choice causally determines the already-fixed contents of box B, (b) that students who one-box will treat the payoff structure as a rigged intuition pump and the two-boxers as simply correct, and (c) that many will conclude the case has no bearing on any real decision and stop caring. In roughly what order will these arise, where in the sequence should each be headed off rather than left to fester, and which misconceptions am I missing?

4. One question I can pose in the final five minutes whose answer would show me whether a student has understood the dilemma rather than merely picked a team — ideally something that separates 'got it' from 'memorized which answer the course prefers'.

5. What to openly refuse to cover at this level — I'm inclined to keep free will, and the question of what an agent should do upon learning it has been predicted, out of the room entirely — and how to fence those off gracefully without seeming to dodge.

Constraint: no readings, and please treat the section as self-contained; the students will not have looked at anything beforehand.
