I am a professor of philosophy, and in a fortnight I present to our departmental colloquium a paper defending what I call two-track conciliationism. The view is this: when I discover that an epistemic peer disagrees with me about some proposition p, the immediate requirement of conciliation falls on my practical and public commitments rather than on my credence itself. I should hedge in action, qualify my assertions, and bracket p as a premise in shared reasoning; my credence in p, however, is permitted to remain where it stood until we have deliberated the matter through. Conciliation, on this picture, is first a demand of assertion and action, and only afterwards, if at all, a demand of belief.

I want a preparation sheet for the question period. My audience: a formal epistemologist who will insist that credence and betting disposition cannot come apart in the way my view requires; two convinced equal-weighters, one of whom will say that this is merely steadfastness with extra bookkeeping; a Kantian colleague who will ask whether a credence one declines to act or assert upon is a coherent epistemic state at all, or instead a piece of self-deception about one's own fallibility; and a cohort of graduate students who will press me on whether bracketing p in joint inquiry is not itself a move that should be subject to the very disagreement it brackets.

Please give me the objections in roughly the order they will arrive, each phrased as the colleague in question would phrase it, with a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
