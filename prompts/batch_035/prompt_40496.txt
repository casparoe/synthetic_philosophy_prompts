I am a novelist about a third of the way into drafting a historical novel that begins in Sarajevo in June 1914 and follows three families — one in Vienna, one in Belgrade, one in a Galician village — through the July Crisis and the opening months of the Great War. As I plan the middle section, I have found myself stuck on a structural question that turns out, I think, to be a genuinely philosophical one, and I would like your help thinking it through.

When philosophers of history debate what counts as an explanation of a past event, one old position holds that explaining an event means showing that it was to be expected: you identify initial conditions and a general law, and the event then follows the way a prediction would. On this covering-law picture, we understand the outbreak of war in 1914 roughly the way we understand a solar eclipse. The opposing tradition insists that this badly misdescribes what historians actually do. Historians explain by constructing narratives: they trace intentions, deliberations, blunders, and contingencies; they show how one thing led to another; and the explanation consists in the shape of the story itself, not in the subsumption of the event under any law. Even the best historical generalisations seem riddled with ceteris paribus clauses and vague hedges, and no one has produced the crisp laws the covering-law picture demands.

Here is my structural idea. I want each chapter of the middle section to open with an epigraph stating a candidate generalisation — for instance, something of the form "when rivals are locked into rigid alliance blocs and mobilisation timetables outrun diplomacy, crises escalate into general war" — and then to narrate the particular day or week of the crisis in a way that lets readers feel all the ways it might have gone otherwise. My hope is that the epigraphs will embody the covering-law ambition and the chapters will embody the narrative one, so the book stages the philosophical dispute rather than taking sides.

Please describe the most important implications of the covering-law-versus-narrative debate for this project. In particular, I want to understand: whether the two conceptions really compete, or whether narratives might be a covert and unacknowledged vehicle for covering-law explanation (as the defenders of the law model sometimes claimed, holding that narrative works only by silently relying on generalisations about how people act); what the debate implies about narrative craft itself — about point of view, foreshadowing, and the presentation of contingency, and specifically about whether telling a story in a way that foregrounds "it could have happened otherwise" is doing explanatory work or merely rhetorical work; and whether my chapter-epigraph device is fair to both sides of the dispute, or whether it stacks the deck — say, by making the covering-law position look stronger or more brittle than its best defenders would allow. I would also welcome your judgement on whether a novelist committed to narrative explanation owes her readers anything like the sense of inevitability that covering-law thinking promises, or whether the whole drama of historical fiction lives in refusing it.

Please say how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you are least sure about. Please write in British English.
