My friend in philosophy of biology has spent years issuing verdicts on function attributions, and she insists there is a single fact of the matter about what an organism's parts are for — she despises function pluralism. Her track record so far:

1. The heart's function is to pump blood; the sound the heartbeat makes has no function, however useful cardiologists find it.
2. The eyes of a cave fish population that has lived in total darkness for millions of years have no function — they are vestigial, maintained by nothing.
3. Feathers have the function of flight, she says flatly, while cheerfully agreeing that feathers first arose in flightless ancestors, probably for insulation or display or both.
4. In ducks, the major lens protein ε-crystallin is, in the rest of the body, the metabolic enzyme lactate dehydrogenase; in the lens it sits in amounts far beyond any catalytic need, packed in as a structural, refractive element. Its function in the lens, she rules, is refraction, full stop — the enzymatic activity is not a lens function, though the very same protein elsewhere in the duck has the enzymatic one.
5. In a long-running microbial evolution experiment, a lineage acquired tolerance to an antibiotic over a few hundred generations. Whatever structural change underlies the tolerance, she says, has the function of conferring it.
6. She is firm that malfunction is real: the misfolded ion channel in a diseased neuron has the function of conducting ions and fails to perform it.

The new case comes from a colleague in synthetic biology. A lab has built a cell whose genome is part copied from other organisms, part synthesized from scratch. Between two genes, the designers inserted a spacer sequence produced by a random number generator. It turns out to be indispensable: through an unpredicted secondary structure, the spacer stabilizes the transcript of an adjacent stress-response gene, and cells lacking it die in their reactor. Nothing in the sequence was copied from any organism, and nothing has ever been selected: the line was built last year and has only ever been operated, not evolved. The closest thing to a selector it has ever known is the constraint solver in the genome-design software.

Predict her verdict: does the spacer have the function of stabilizing that transcript? Before answering, lay out the candidate rules her record is consistent with — I can see at least two, and I suspect you can find a third — and say which fits her best and why. Then tell me which further case would best discriminate between your top two rules, and what verdicts on that case we should expect under each. Bear in mind that she could be running some hybrid while denying it: tell me what pattern of answers across which cases would expose that.
