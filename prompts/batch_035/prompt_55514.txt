Here's a thought experiment that keeps derailing my seminar discussions, so I want to reframe it as a question about what a theory owes us rather than a duel of intuitions. The case: a single innocent person is tortured for fifty years, versus a barely-noticeable dust speck, once, in the eye of each of 3^^^3 people (a tower of iterated exponentials so large that even the number of digits in the number of digits is beyond physical reality). The people pushing the torture side insist that any harm, however small, must be able to sum; the specks side insists that some harms are so trivial no number of them can reach a different order of magnitude. I'm a public policy student, so this isn't idle for me: every cost-benefit analysis that totals a minute of delay for ten million commuters against one death, or a small cancer risk spread across a population against a visible harm to a few, is a scaled-down version of the same question, and I'd like to know what standard I should hold a view to before anyone starts defending one.

Write me the desiderata list for an adequate theory of interpersonal aggregation of harms. I have some candidate requirements I'd like you to include, plus whatever else you think belongs. First, the anchoring cases: it has to get the trivial cases right (one speck in one eye is a negligible harm; fifty years of torture is among the worst things that can happen to a person) — a theory that can't say both is a non-starter, but say whether you think these are genuine desiderata or just the intuitions the whole debate is supposed to be testing. Second, symmetry: if 3^^^3 specks were all delivered sequentially to one person's eye, most people would say that is torture by another name, so intra-personal aggregation of trivial harms clearly does cross thresholds; a theory owes us an account of why or whether inter-personal aggregation differs, and "because the specks land on different people" is doing enormous work that needs to be spelled out. Third, flip disclosure: a theory should say where, if anywhere, its verdict changes as the population grows — between a thousand speck-sufferers and a trillion and 3^^^3 — and if it flips at some point, it must own the arbitrariness or justify the threshold. Fourth, the lottery test: a tiny probability of torture imposed on each of many people is mathematically very close to the specks case (small harms distributed over persons), so must a theory treat ex ante risk aggregation and ex post harm aggregation alike? Fifth, workability at human scale: the theory should deliver verdicts for realistic numbers, not just astronomically large ones, since that's where I'd actually use it. And I suspect some of my candidates are partisan demands smuggled in as neutral requirements — the "separateness of persons" constraint looks like it may just be a premise of one camp — so flag which items on the list are genuine desiderata and which are prejudices of a side.

Finally, two things I most want to see: identify any subset of the desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied (if symmetry, flip disclosure, and the anchoring cases conflict, that conflict is itself the result, and I want it located precisely), and tell me which single requirement does the most to eliminate contenders. Then, briefly, run three familiar positions against the list — strict lexical thresholds where trivial harms never sum, straightforward linear summation, and the Parfitian middle path where large benefits to many can outweigh harms to a few without any summing of the small harms themselves — and say where each fails rather than which wins. No readings, no literature review; the criteria and the tensions are what I'm after.
