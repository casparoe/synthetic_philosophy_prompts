A graduate student in my applied ethics seminar, writing a term paper on effective altruism, opened our meeting with a summary of the importance–tractability–neglectedness framework that he attributes to a popular economics blog he read over the break. As he relayed it to me, the framework holds that the expected value of working on a problem is found by scoring each of three factors from zero to ten and multiplying them together, so that any cause scoring zero on one factor drops out entirely. In his telling, importance is essentially the number of people affected (so malaria beats the long-term future of humanity, since more actual people are currently sick), tractability means that we already know how to solve the problem outright (which is why, he says, scientific research into fundamental questions scores near zero), and neglectedness is simply the number of people currently working on the cause, with no attention to how much money rather than labor has already gone into it, or to whether the marginal returns to additional effort are flat or steep. He concluded that the framework is therefore rigged: because it rewards causes with few workers and allows speculative long-run benefits to be assigned arbitrarily large importance scores, it can only ever output the conclusion that whoever is doing the scoring should abandon concrete, verifiable humanitarian work in favor of whatever they were already predisposed to believe in — and that this circularity has been widely recognized as fatal to the whole approach.

I suspect this summary is a mixture of defensible simplification and genuine distortion — the bit about multiplication sounding like a formal decision procedure rather than a heuristic rubric strikes me as wrong in an interesting way — but I want to be careful before I tell him so, because parts of his critique resemble objections I have seen raised in earnest. Please give me an audit I can take back to him. Distinguish carefully among: (a) what a thoughtful proponent of the framework would accept as a fair statement of it, (b) claims that are crude simplifications of it — where the simplification matters to the conclusion he draws, not merely to its elegance — and (c) claims that misrepresent it outright. I am particularly interested in whether his "rigged" conclusion actually follows from the strongest correct version of his premises, or whether it depends on the distortions; and in whether the worries he raises at the end (arbitrariness of importance estimates, neglecting diminishing returns, the pull toward exotic causes) are better understood as internal objections a sophisticated defender has answers to, external criticisms of the enterprise as a whole, or considerations the framework's designers themselves would insist are built into its proper use. Since I will be sending him written comments rather than discussing this aloud, please organize the audit so that each element of his summary is addressed on its own terms, with his final conclusion treated last.
