Here is my question: when a social norm disappears, when does that count as genuine moral progress, and when is it just fashion or self-interest changing clothes? My teacher had us read about how norms actually die, and one thing stuck with me: the arguments against dueling in England were centuries old, but dueling only collapsed in the 1840s, apparently around the time it stopped making duelists look noble — partly because people who weren't aristocrats started doing it, so it no longer marked anyone out as high-born. That unsettled me, because it suggests norms can die of embarrassment rather than persuasion.

Here are my verdicts on cases, and I've tried to be honest rather than consistent:

1. The collapse of dueling in England: genuine moral progress, even though the arguments hadn't changed and what changed was that dueling stopped conferring honor.
2. The abolition of slavery: genuine moral progress, and I think of it as argument-driven in a way dueling wasn't.
3. The end of footbinding in China: genuine moral progress, even though from what I understand families abandoned it largely through public pledges arranged so no one's daughter was left unmarriageable — which looks like coordinated self-interest more than a change of heart.
4. Prohibition in America: not moral progress, even though the temperance movement had moral arguments, passionate advocates, and elite supporters, just like abolition did. When it was repealed, that seemed like the right call.
5. The disappearance of men tipping their hats to women: not moral progress, just fashion, even though it also died by prestige changing.
6. Indoor smoking bans: moral progress, though I'm bothered that I believe this partly because smoking lost prestige among the people I respect before the laws came.
7. The death penalty being abolished in countries like France, where I've read the parliament abolished it while a majority of the public still supported it: I want to call this moral progress, but notice it was elite-driven against majority opinion, which is closer to the dueling mechanism than the abolition-of-slavery mechanism.

My tentative principle is: a change is moral progress when the abandoned norm was actually wrong and people abandoned it because they came to see that it was wrong. But this principle seems to fail immediately — I count the dueling case as progress even though the mechanism wasn't persuasion at all, and I refuse to count Prohibition even though millions of people genuinely came to see drinking as wrong. Meanwhile, a purely outcome-based principle ("progress = a genuinely wrong norm died, however it died") would count cases 1 and 7 easily but makes me uneasy, because it seems to imply that whether something was moral progress depends on nothing anyone did or believed, just on whether the norm was wrong all along.
