I'm a high school junior working on an extended essay for a philosophy elective, and I've gotten myself stuck on the question of what an emotion actually is. The background reading I've done says that philosophers divide roughly into three camps: some think emotions are judgments (evaluations of how things are going for you), some think they are perceptions (of, say, value or danger), and some think they are just feelings (bodily states that happen to matter to us). Going in, my own tentative principle was that an emotion is a judgment that something good or bad is happening, felt in the body — anger is judging you've been wronged, felt as heat; fear is judging there's danger, felt in the stomach. But when I test that principle against my honest gut verdicts on particular cases, it keeps failing, and I can't tell whether the principle is wrong or my verdicts are.

Here are the verdicts. On a flight last spring I was genuinely terrified during turbulence, even though I believe, and would argue in class, that commercial flying is safer than driving — that fear was an emotion, and a full-strength one. When a car backfires behind me and I jump, I don't think that's an emotion at all; it's a reflex, closer to blinking than to being afraid. I have been moved to tears by an instrumental piece of music with no lyrics and nothing I believe about the notes — no judgment I can identify — and I count that as an emotion. Watching a classmate freeze through a class presentation, I felt strong embarrassment on his behalf, even though I don't think he did anything embarrassing (I thought he was brave to push through), and I'd still call that an emotion. Feeling betrayed when a friend shared something I'd told her in confidence is my clearest case of an emotion, and there the judgment seems essential: the feeling is inseparable from thinking a wrong was done. Yet I also felt real anger at my coffee table after stubbing my toe on it, while believing perfectly well that tables can't be at fault, and I don't want to be told that wasn't anger. Finally, if I'm irritable all afternoon for no reason I can name, I'd call that a mood rather than an emotion, though I can't articulate the difference. The pair I keep grinding on: the turbulence fear and the backfire startle both involve no judgment of real danger, and both are bodily, sudden, and unwilled — yet one goes in the emotion column and one doesn't, and I don't know what does the sorting.

What I'd like from you is a principle, stated precisely, that captures these verdicts or as many of them as can honestly be saved — and I'd like to know which of the three camps (judgment, perception, or feeling) it belongs to, or whether it cuts across them. Please tell me what the principle predicts for cases I haven't listed: is nostalgia an emotion? Is jealousy about a partner's affair that I know for a fact never happened? Is the chill at a horror movie I know is fake? And rather than adding one saving clause per case, tell me plainly which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate and which one you'd overturn first, so I can see the trade-off between a complex principle that saves everything and a simple one that doesn't. One more request: since my essay will be graded on accuracy, please keep a clear line between what is actually settled in this area and what is genuinely contested among people who study it. If the judgment theory I started with is a live option, say so; if it's widely thought to fail on cases like mine, I'd much rather learn that than be handed it back as if it were the consensus.
