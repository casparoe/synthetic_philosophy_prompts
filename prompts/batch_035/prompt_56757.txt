I'm a reliability engineer at a large industrial firm, and last month I sat on the review panel for a serious near-miss at one of our plants. Our draft report ended up fingering a single "root cause" — a maintenance error — even though several of us felt that four or five other things had to go wrong, in the right order, for anything bad to have been possible at all. A colleague who did graduate work in philosophy of history told me that what was bothering me has names: overdetermination, joint causation, Mackie's INUS conditions, the NESS test that legal philosophers developed for exactly this problem of picking out causes among a crowd of necessary-but-insufficient contributors. I understand those ideas in the abstract now, but I want to see them actually do work on a hard case, and I've settled on one I know well enough to follow the details: the Challenger disaster of January 1986.

Take the standard facts as given — the overnight cold snap at the Cape well below any previous launch temperature, the Thiokol engineers' recommendation the night before that the field joints were unsafe in the cold, the management decision to proceed anyway under pressure from NASA, the schedule pressure itself (a launch that had already slipped repeatedly, the Teacher in Space payloads, the manifest backlog), and the background fact that O-ring erosion and blow-by had been observed on earlier flights, including a cold launch the previous winter. Now run the machinery. Which of these are causes in the philosophers' sense and which are mere background conditions, and does Mackie's or the NESS framework actually sort them differently than the ordinary but-for test? Is this a case of overdetermination in the strict sense at all — of multiple independently sufficient causes — or is it joint causation where nothing short of the whole package suffices, and does that distinction matter for what we say? And here's the part I most want done carefully: given that earlier flights had shown erosion and gotten away with it, there's a tempting thought that the disaster was somehow "waiting to happen" across many possible launches, so that no single decision on the night of the 27th made the difference. Is that a legitimate extension of the overdetermination idea (some kind of probabilistic or cross-world overdetermination), or is it a confusion that the framework should refuse to tolerate?

Finally, turn the analysis back on the tools. Where does the multi-causal machinery go silent about the Challenger case — what does it not even try to explain — and does it misdescribe anything, for instance by treating NASA's schedule pressure as a cause on all fours with the cold morning? I'd also like you to address the normative bite directly: the Rogers Commission's report, and Feynman's famous ice-water demonstration with it, was in the business of singling out a cause, and everyone understood that where you point the causal finger determines who gets blamed and what gets fixed. If overdetermination-style reasoning concludes that no single factor was the cause, does that reason toward no one being responsible, or does it just relocate the question? And as someone who writes root-cause reports for a living, I want to know whether you think these frameworks should change the practice — is "root cause analysis" a category error for systems like this, or is INUS-style thinking something an incident review panel could actually adopt? Please be honest about where the philosophy is doing real work and where it's just relabeling what engineers already do.
