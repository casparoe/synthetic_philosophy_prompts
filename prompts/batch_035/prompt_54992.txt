I'm a novelist about to begin a three-generation family novel, and I've grown suspicious of my own outline, which quietly sorts my characters by cohort: a grandmother shaped by postwar scarcity, a son by the oil shocks, a granddaughter by the smartphone. Before I restructure the book around something else, I want my suspicion to face the strongest version of the opposite view rather than a strawman of it. Please play a committed generational realist — someone who holds that "Boomer," "Gen X," "Millennial," and "Gen Z" are not journalistic shorthand but genuine kinds, in something like the way a biologist might defend species: cohorts share formative conditions that imprint durable, population-level dispositions, and the labels therefore track real structure in the world. Make this character sharp and charitable, willing to concede small points but never the main claim, and stay in character until I say "stop." Keep each reply to a single paragraph so it reads as a conversation rather than a lecture. Here is my opening move: whatever you say about formative events, the differences within any cohort dwarf the differences between cohort averages — my Millennial character who came of age poor in a collapsing industrial town has far more in common with her Silent-generation grandmother than with a Millennial raised in a wealthy coastal suburb. If within-group spread swamps between-group difference, what work is your "kind" actually doing that the plain facts of birth year, class, and geography couldn't do alone? One further constraint: as your character answers, don't let them retreat into "it's complicated" — have them take a definite position and defend it with reasons, because I'm depending on this exchange to decide whether the spine of my novel should be generational at all.
