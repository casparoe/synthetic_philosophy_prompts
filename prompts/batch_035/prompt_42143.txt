I'm in a seminar on collective action and we've been going back and forth for weeks about the inefficacy objection -- you know, the thing where one person's contribution to a huge collective harm seems to make no difference (no chicken is saved, no election is tipped, one molecule of carbon), so what's the complaint exactly? I've given up on deriving my view from first principles and I'm just going to write down my gut verdicts on cases and see if anything coherent is behind them. Here they are. Eating chicken even though my one purchase doesn't change the number of birds raised: wrong, though I couldn't tell you how wrong. Voting in a national election, where my vote is nearly certain to change nothing: I should do it, and people who don't are doing something wrong-ish. Pouring one bucket of water into a stream when 4,999 other people are doing the same thing and the stream floods a farmer's field, with the flooding being threshold-based so that no single bucket made the difference between flooding and not flooding: everyone who poured a bucket did something wrong, including the people whose buckets were causally idle. Donating $10 as the 3,400th donor to a crowdfunding campaign that will only fund a neo-Nazi militia if it hits 10,000 donors, and it goes on to hit 10,000: I feel like the early donors did something wrong even though their individual $10 caused literally nothing, and I feel the same about the late ones. Leaving my porch light on all night every night for a year, which releases about as much carbon as some fraction of my chicken consumption but, like the chicken, changes nothing on its own: not wrong, barely even worth mentioning. Riding the bus in a city that runs the route whether I'm on it or not, where my individual ride does nothing but annual ridership totals determine whether the route exists next year: I think I should ride, and I'd criticize someone who drove out of pure laziness.

So there's the mess. The chicken verdict and the porch light verdict seem to be in tension (same causal inefficacy, same rough scale of harm, opposite verdicts), the bucket verdict and the early-militia-donor verdict seem to be in tension (both causally idle participants in a threshold outcome, but in the bucket case the harm arrives without any threshold being special? no, there is a threshold, the stream overtops at some point), and the voting verdict plus the bus verdict together seem to imply that I care about something other than my actual causal effect, which then makes me wonder why the porch light gets a pass. My tentative principle, which I don't love, is something like: a contribution to a collective harm is wrong when it's part of a practice whose aggregate effect is foreseeable and bad, AND the practice wouldn't function without participants like me (market demand, election turnout), but not when the contribution is genuinely independent of the mechanism (the porch light changes no aggregate anyone responds to, the bucket brigade was happening regardless of me). But I can already see this doesn't cleanly handle the militia crowdfunding, because the 3,400th donor seems like they're "part of the practice" in exactly the porch-light way until the threshold is suddenly hit, and it makes the wrongness of the early donor depend entirely on what 6,600 strangers do later, which feels off.

Then be honest about which of my verdicts your best principle just cannot accommodate, and make the case for which one I should give up. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence -- name the principle or say no single one works -- and then give the reasoning, no preamble and no restating my cases back at me.
