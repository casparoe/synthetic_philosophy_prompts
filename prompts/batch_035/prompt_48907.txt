Next week my upper-level seminar on formal epistemology reaches logical uncertainty, and I want the students to feel the problem in their wallets rather than read it off the board. The setting: fifteen students, one 90-minute session, play money only. My plan is an in-class prediction market over a slate of closed arithmetical and combinatorial statements — some cheap to verify with pencil and paper (e.g., whether 2^40 exceeds 10^12), some verifiable only by a computation we run at the end of the session or overnight (e.g., the parity of the number of primes below 100,000, or whether the digit 7 appears more often than the digit 3 in the first ten thousand digits of the decimal expansion of π) — alongside a control set of contracts on ordinary empirical chancy outcomes like dice and shuffled cards. The students are comfortable with Dutch book arguments for the probability axioms from earlier in the term, and a colleague has suggested I also work in a lesson from the literature on computationally bounded reasoners: that a rational agent's posted prices should be such that no trader running an efficiently computable strategy can buy and sell against them over time at a guaranteed profit. I like the idea of having the facilitator act as a market maker whose initial prices deliberately violate an arithmetic identity — say, pricing three contracts so that a student who notices the logical relationship among them can lock in a sure gain without knowing which statements are true — but I'm open to other ways of staging that.

Please design the full activity: the exact contract slate (a dozen or so statements, chosen so that difficulty of pricing and cost of verification come apart), the rules of trade, the payoff scheme and how the overnight-resolvable contracts get settled, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the session onto the formal problem — why classical Bayesianism presupposes that the agent knows all logical consequences of its evidence, why credences strictly between 0 and 1 on an a priori settled question look like a violation of the probability axioms under one reading and like ordinary epistemic humility under another, and how the market itself illustrates the idea that consistency of prices, rather than omniscience about truth, might be the achievable standard for a bounded reasoner.

Also tell me, frankly, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I expect students will race to compute the cheap statements instead of trading, that liquidity will dry up, that the first shouted price will anchor everyone — and how I should handle each failure mode. Finally, be explicit about which features of the philosophical problem the activity cannot reproduce no matter how I run it: the students get a fixed, finite slate, while the real issue concerns a reasoner facing unbounded deduction and an indefinite future of revisable prices, and I want to know what I will have to supply by lecture that the game simply cannot show.
