I'm taking an undergraduate seminar on historical method, and we've just finished a stretch on source criticism from Herodotus and Thucydides down to modern philology, so I've been trying to write out my actual working intuitions about which testimony about the distant past to believe. I'd like to know whether they add up to a principle, and I'd be grateful if you could extract one for me, test it, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it forces me to give up. Here they are, as concretely as I can state them.

1. The infant set adrift in a basket or left on a hillside, who grows up to found a dynasty: I don't believe this story in any of its instances, across any culture, no matter how old the source or how many versions there are.

2. A battle reported in the victor's triumphal inscription and, in much duller terms with less detail, in a defeated neighbor's chronicle: I believe the neighbor's version, including where they disagree about numbers.

3. A monastery's household accounts noting in passing that fewer pilgrims came after the bridge washed out: I believe this, even though it survives in one manuscript with no corroboration.

4. A vivid account of court intrigue by a chronicler writing two centuries after the events, our only source for that stretch of decades: I don't believe it, even though we have nothing else.

5. A speech reported word for word by a historian writing decades later: I don't believe the words were spoken, but I do believe roughly what was decided.

6. Casualty figures carved on a public monument: I believe the figures more than the narrative that surrounds them in the same historians, and I can't fully explain why.

7. Atrocity stories told about a society exclusively by its enemies: I dismiss them as propaganda — except that when physical evidence later surfaces (say, a burial ground consistent with the accused practice), I believe the practice actually occurred, and I'm troubled that I reversed myself on testimony I'd already rejected just because a dig came out one way.

8. A fire dated by a contemporary annal and by tree-ring or radiocarbon evidence that disagree: I trust the scientific dating over the contemporary source, though I notice that contemporaneity is usually the thing I prize most.

9. The hard one: I dismiss the exposed-infant stories wholesale as motif, but if archaeology corroborated one incidental detail of such a story — an earthquake, a sacking, a founding date within a few years — I feel a real pull toward taking the next-most-spectacular claim in the same source seriously, and I don't know whether that pull is justified or is just my principle leaking.

So: is there one account of testimonial credibility that covers all nine — something involving, perhaps, whether the witness had a reason to shape the passage, whether it was written to be a story at all, and where material evidence sits in the hierarchy — or do I need several principles? If one principle fits, state it precisely, say which verdict motivates each clause, and tell me what it predicts for cases I haven't listed: Icelandic sagas written down long after the events they describe; a forged charter that may nonetheless record a genuine grant; an oral tradition transmitted with formulaic aids over centuries. If it can't fit all nine, tell me which verdicts to overturn and why those rather than the others, and tell me straight whether I'm applying a double standard anywhere — the enemy-atrocity reversal in point 7 seems like the strongest candidate, but I'd like your view rather than mine.
