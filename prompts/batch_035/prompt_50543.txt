A friend of mine has gone full geometric-rationality guy lately — Kelly criterion, Ole Peters and ergodicity economics, "time averages not ensemble averages," all of it — and he's been running an argument on me that I can't beat but also can't believe. It goes like this:

(1) To evaluate a gamble, what matters is what happens along the single trajectory of wealth you actually live through, not the average over counterfactual copies of you who got different coin flips. Averaging over copies of yourself is averaging over people who don't exist.

(2) There's a real theorem backing this up (Breiman, early 60s, on optimal gambling systems): if you face a long sequence of favorable betting opportunities and can choose what fraction of your bankroll to stake on each, the Kelly sizing — maximize the expected log of your wealth — beats every other fixed sizing with probability approaching 1 as the sequence gets long. Not just on average. Along nearly every trajectory.

(3) And nothing in that reasoning cares that it's the *same* bet repeated. Your life is just a long sequence of sizing decisions, one after another, all compounding. So the rule generalizes cleanly: at every risky choice, maximize expected log wealth.

(4) But log of zero is minus infinity. So any act with a nonzero chance of taking you to zero wealth has expected log wealth of minus infinity, and no upside, however large or likely, can cancel that. Flip side: eliminating a ruin risk is worth any finite price.

(5) Therefore: never accept any gamble that risks your entire bankroll, however favorable. And pay anything up to (but, funny enough, not including) everything you own to eliminate a chance of total ruin. Concretely, if someone offers you double-or-nothing on your whole net worth at 99% in your favor, you must refuse — even if refusing means the hospital won't do the surgery and you're dead in a month. Because, my friend says, being dead with $100k in the bank isn't log(0), it's just the end of the ledger, whereas the flip has a 1% chance of actual minus infinity.

(6) Also, hand basically everything to any bandit: to dodge a 1-in-a-trillion chance of losing everything, pay any premium that leaves you one dollar — 99.99999% of your wealth to avoid a risk you'd take for a nickel.

His conclusion is that a truly rational person turns down the 99% rescue flip and pays the trillion-to-one premium. Which seems insane to me — the theory that's supposed to save you from the expected-value gambler's ruin ends up recommending voluntary certain death and giving your life savings to trivial risks. But I've gone thru it and each step sounds right? (1) is the whole ergodicity sales pitch and honestly I find it pretty compelling. (2) I looked into and it's true as far as I can tell. My stomach says the trick is in (3) — the theorem is about staking fractions of a bankroll across many repetitions, and it's getting used on a one-shot, indivisible, all-or-nothing choice — but I can't say exactly which property of the theorem fails to transfer, and I also can't shake the feeling that (4) is where the infinities start doing illegitimate work, since the bandit case doesn't look like a repetition issue at all. So: which step do I reject, what exactly goes wrong in it, and what do I pay for rejecting it? And if geometric rationality survives only as a rule for repeated, divisible bets, why did (3) sound so natural in the first place — is there any principled way to let it back in for genuinely one-shot decisions, or does it stay broken?
