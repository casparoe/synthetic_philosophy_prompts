My friend and I got into a three hour argument last night about whether the US is in decline — you know the kind, it started as a conversation about something else entirely. At some point I noticed something weird about how the whole thing went. Both of us kept appealing to history as evidence, but we each had our own stash of past cases. He'd say every great power that gets financially overstretched and hollows out its industrial base eventually goes down (he had Rome, Habsburg Spain, Britain lined up like dominoes), and I'd say every power that loses an empire or a war but keeps its institutions and its universities stays rich and relevant (postwar Britain, Japan after the 90s, take your pick). And the thing I keep chewing on since: the individual facts we each cited were propably accurate. The real fight was about which past cases count as "the relevant ones," and neither of us ever actually argued for our selection — we just each picked the bucket of history that produced the conclusion we came in with. It's not exactly cherry-picking, because the examples themselves weren't wrong; it's more that the choice of comparison set was doing all the work and never got defended. I gather there's something called the reference class problem in statistics, but that seems to be about there being no uniquely correct class, not about people choosing one motivatedly. So: does this move have an actual name? And if not, can you define it properly — what it is, what it isn't (vs. ordinary cherry-picking of evidence, vs. a legitimate disagreement about which similarities matter), and where the edges are. Because sometimes picking the comparison set IS the substance of a good argument, right? Historians do that for a living and we don't call them dishonest. So the boundary has to be somewhere and I'd like to know where.
