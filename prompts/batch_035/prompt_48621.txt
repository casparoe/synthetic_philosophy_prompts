Bit of background so this makes sense: I'm a retired physicist. Thirty-six years in condensed matter, mostly neutron scattering, so basically my whole career was back-of-envelope estimates that a proper mathematician would've laughed at. Now that I'm retired I've been sitting in on a philosophy of mathematics seminar at the university here, and I've somehow talked my way into a guest slot: 90 minutes, about 15 students, mixed math and philosophy backgrounds, and the instructor says I can run whatever I want.

Here's the thing I want them to feel in their bones, because it carried my entire working life and I've noticed the math students don't really have it. When you're faced with a pile of quantities you can't rigorously handle, you treat them as independent unless you can actually point to the mechanism that would correlate them. That's how you get the density-of-primes estimate, the birthday paradox, half of statistical mechanics. It's not a proof and it's quietly doing an enormous amount of work in physics. I read an essay by an AI researcher of all people that calls this the "presumption of independence", which is a much better name than anything we ever had for it, so that's the phrase I want to use with the students.

So design me the session. My rough idea is two rounds. Round one, the presumption works: give them something they could never compute exactly, let the independence heuristic nail it fast, and have the class check it against a simulation so they feel the magic. Round two, same kind of setup, but I've hidden a channel correlating things — some innocuous shared cause, like all the "random" numbers quietly coming from the same seed, or one variable feeding into two of the quantities they treated as seperate. Now the heuristic gives an answer that's confidently wrong, and the only way out is to hunt for the mechanism, not to grind through the computation. Maybe have some of them play referees deciding whether to accept an estimate, with reputation points or something as the payoff — I dont want to actually pay philosophy students.

Give me the actual rules and materials (I can print handouts and bring a laptop, no projector problems), how to split the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the idea — especially the difference between "I see no reason these would be correlated" and "these are uncorrelated", which I suspect is where the philosophy students will have the most to say. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. And be honest about which part of the real thing the activity can't reproduce — my guess is the hard part is that in actual research nobody ever hands you the rigorous answer to check against, so either build in a way to handle that or tell me I just have to live with it.
