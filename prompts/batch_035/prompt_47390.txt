My friend Dana has been reading Margalit's The Ethics of Memory and Rieff's In Praise of Forgetting, and after months of arguing about it over dinner I've realised I can actually predict her answers — mostly. Just so you have her track record: she's adamant that Holocaust education should be compulsory in every country, not just the ones that did it, but she gets uncomfortable when a curriculum seems designed to make twelve-year-olds feel personally guilty rather than just knowledgeable. She forgave and basically stopped thinking about an old friend who betrayed her, and thinks that was the right thing to do. She was all for the South African-style trade of amnesty for full testimony when that was on the table, even though it made her furious at the time. When her cousin wanted to burn their late grandfather's abusive letters, she refused to let it happen, but she keeps them in a box in the attic and won't have them displayed or read aloud. She thinks a politician's dodgy tax affair from thirty years ago should stay findable online forever, but that a private person's should be deletable. And when a nearby town wanted annual ceremonies for a mine disaster whose last survivor had just died, she said no — fund a proper history book and a plaque instead.

Now three new things have come up and I want to be prepared for Sunday, when she's coming round. First: her late mother's diaries reveal that her mother informed on the neighbours under the old regime, which contributed to the father of that family being imprisoned; the neighbours' daughter is still alive, lives two streets away, and Dana knows her well. Second: there's a campaign for a national day of mourning for something our country did to a minority in the 1760s — expulsion, basically, with a lot of deaths. Third, more personally: I've found letters from a dead friend of mine confessing to a wrong he did me before he died, and Dana and I already disagree about whether I should keep rereading them or put them away for good.

Predict what she'll say on each of the three, but what I really want is her underlying rule, because I can read her track record at least two ways. One reading is pretty Margalit-ish: duties of memory come from thick relations and from how radical the evil was, while purely private wrongs are supposed to fade into forgiveness. Another is more like a living-memory rule: once the participants and victims are all dead, you get records and plaques but no rituals, and while they're alive the duties are at their strongest. And maybe there are other rules that fit her just as well — something like an archive-always rule, where the duty is only ever about preserving the record somewhere and never about what anyone does with their feelings. So: which rule does she seem to be running, what does each candidate rule predict for the three cases, which fits best and why, and — since we're clearly going to keep arguing — what further case would genuinely divide these rules from each other, because everything I've come up with so far her old answers already settle. Please answer in British English, and definately take the diary one seriously, that's the one keeping me up at night.
