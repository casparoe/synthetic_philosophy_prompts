I lead a platform-infrastructure team of about twenty engineers, and a dispute has broken out between two of my senior colleagues over how we should triage our CI failures. I'll reproduce the exchange as faithfully as I can, then say what I'd like from you.

The context: whenever a pipeline fails, someone has to classify it as either a genuine regression or an environmental flake before it enters the weekly report. This is currently done by Dmitri, our most experienced engineer, who reviews every failure himself. Maya, who runs our onboarding program, has proposed replacing him with a rotating panel of three junior engineers who each inspect the failure independently and vote by simple majority. I'm the one who has to decide whether to approve the change, and the argument that ensued is above my pay grade, since it turned mathematical.

The exchange, numbered for your convenience:

(1) Maya said there's a classical result about juries — something like: if each voter is even slightly better than a coin flip at reaching the right answer, the majority vote is more accurate than any individual voter, and the advantage grows as you add voters. Since even our newest engineers are clearly better than chance at telling a broken build from a flaky one, three independent judgments should beat one.

(2) Dmitri replied that the effect is asymptotic, and we have three voters, not three thousand. He did the arithmetic: three voters each right 55% of the time give you a majority that's right about 57.5% of the time — barely better than the individuals, nowhere near his own track record, which he puts around 85% on this exact task. A panel of mediocrity doesn't sum into an expert.

(3) Maya said fine — grow the panel. Five juniors, seven, whatever the theorem needs. The whole point is that accuracy is bought with headcount, and junior review time is far cheaper than senior.

(4) Dmitri said the theorem only works if the voters' errors are independent, and ours aren't even close: the juniors read the same dashboards, follow the same triage checklist, and were all trained by him. If they share a blind spot, the panel votes wrong in unison, and three correlated voters are effectively fewer than one careful one.

(5) Maya answered that a single expert is the ultimate correlated error source: one set of heuristics, one set of blind spots, no mechanism of self-correction, and no visible record of when he was unsure — his 85% is unaudited self-report. A panel at least averages out idiosyncrasies and leaves a traceable vote whenever the members disagree.

(6) Dmitri closed with what he called the killer: the theorem also assumes each voter is better than 50%, and if the juniors are slightly *worse* than chance — which he claims is plausible for flake-vs-regression calls, since the telltale signs are counterintuitive and their training labels were produced by him — then adding voters drives accuracy *down*, and the bigger the panel the worse it gets. And since we have no ground truth on this task, we can't even measure which side of 50% they're on. The panel isn't a theorem-powered improvement; it's a gamble on an unmeasured parameter.

Neither of them will budge, and I'm not qualified to referee the probability claims. Could you go through the exchange move by move — rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating — and then tell me who is right about what, whether there's anything both of them are missing, and whether the proposal should be approved as stated? Please end your reply with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing exactly one of: approve, reject, or modify, reflecting your overall recommendation for the panel proposal as stated.
