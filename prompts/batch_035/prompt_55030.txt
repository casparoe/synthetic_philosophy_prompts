I teach a survey course that touches on historiography, and I've been keeping a little list of my gut reactions to how historians explain things, because I suspect there's a principle buried in there somewhere but I can't dig it out myself. Here's the list. Some of these reactions probably don't hang together and I'd like to know which.

- A book that explains the fall of the Roman Republic as more or less baked in once the legions became loyal to their generals rather than to the state: I find this persuasive, and I don't feel it's cheating, even though hardly any Roman at the time thought the Republic was doomed.
- A book that explains the outbreak of the First World War as a chain of blunders and accidents, everybody sleepwalking into it: I find this persuasive too, and I actually like it more than the structural rival.
- A colleague who explains the outcome of the Second World War purely by productive capacity — once the Americans were in, Axis defeat was just arithmetic: I nod along.
- But when a different colleague explains the Soviet collapse as overdetermined by economic stagnation and imperial overstretch, I bristle. That feels like hindsight dressed up as analysis, since plenty of very smart people in 1985 thought the USSR would last decades. And yet I can't for the life of me say why this is different from the productive-capacity story I nod at.
- A psychohistorical account tracing a dictator's policies back to his upbringing and his relationship with his father: feels tainted to me, almost embarrassing.
- A counterfactual chapter arguing Germany wins in 1914 if the right wing of the invasion isn't weakened to shore up the east: feels perfectly fine, even fun.
- Any account of a financial crash that leans on the phrase everyone could see the bubble: feels cheap. That phrase almost never survives contact with the primary sources.
- An explanation of a military victory that credits one leader's singular vision against the doubts of everyone around him: feels tainted, like the author wanted a hero.

So that's the pile. The tension I already see is that I want contingency for 1914 but deep structure for Rome and for 1945, and my reaction to the USSR case cuts against the structural ones without me being able to say why. Is there a single principle that captures all or most of these verdicts, or a couple of rival principles that each capture a different subset? If there are rivals, please state them precisely, say which of my verdicts each one has to overturn, and tell me what they'd each predict about a case I haven't listed — say, a planned book arguing the Reformation's success was basically guaranteed once the printing press arrived, or one arguing the Berlin Wall fell because of Gorbachev's personality rather than structural pressures. And if one of my verdicts simply has to go, be honest about which and why. Please write your answer in British English.
