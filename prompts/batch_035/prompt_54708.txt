I teach philosophy and run a voluntary undergraduate reading group on probability and religious belief; last term we worked through the wager fragments of Pascal's Pensées together with the decision-theoretal literature they later provoked. Two of the students stayed behind afterward and argued for the better part of an hour. They have asked me to adjudicate, and I would rather not hand down a verdict on impression, so I am reconstructing the exchange here and would be grateful for a careful, move-by-move assessment.

The exchange, as best I can reconstruct it:

Ilse opened with the standard reconstruction: whatever one's credence in God, provided it is nonzero, wagering on God dominates abstaining, because the payoff — an eternity of blessedness — carries infinite utility, and a finite cost in this life is as nothing against an infinite expected value. So even the near-certain skeptic has a prudential reason to wager.

Marco replied with what he called a symmetry problem: a decision-theoretic argument that never looks at the size of the probability can be run for any deity whatsoever. He asked her to imagine a god who reserves his rewards for those who refrain from wagering out of prudence and who damns the credulous. If that god's probability of existing is nonzero, the very same calculation instructs her to abstain. Since the argument cannot come out for wagering and against wagering at once, it at best establishes that one ought to wager on something, which is vacuous, and at worst is self-undermining.

Ilse answered by breaking the symmetry with evidence: the prankster deity is an ad hoc invention with no scriptures, no institutions, no martyrs, no testimony, whereas actual historical religions come with a full apparatus that raises their antecedent probability. The wager's small-probability clause operates against a fixed background of evidence, she said; it does not forbid consulting the evidence when comparing hypotheses.

Marco replied that this concedes his point in another form: the wager's whole sales pitch is that it works even for one who finds the evidence hopeless — however small the probability, the infinite payoff swamps it. If the argument's internal logic drives probability's size out of consideration, then Ilse may not quietly reintroduce size, via the evidence, when it suits her. The trickster god's probability, however tiny, ought by her own standard to suffice; and if it does not, then the asymmetry she wants cannot come from expected value at all.

Ilse then, somewhat to my surprise, pivoted: she said the real weakness of the wager was never the trickster but the infinities. Once infinite utilities are admitted, every act with any nonzero chance of the infinite payoff has the same infinite expectation, so the wager cannot rank the acts it was supposed to rank — a life of prayer scores no better than token conformity — and cannot say how much to invest. But she insisted this is a defect in the argument's mathematics, not a point in Marco's favor.

Marco agreed, and pressed the point outward: the same broken rule licenses disasters elsewhere. Anyone who grasps the wager's logic can exploit a rational agent by attaching a merely possible infinite reward to whatever he likes — he sketched a mugger who promises in infinite terms, remarking that an agent who must treat every nonzero probability as sufficient is committed to handing over the direction of his life to whoever offers the largest merely possible payoff. A rule that exploitable, he said, is not a rule for finite agents, and the wager's defender owes us a repair, not a reiteration.

Ilse's last word was a repair of sorts: Pascal himself, in the same fragments, seems to anticipate the objection that one cannot simply decide to believe, and to propose an indirect route — begin by living as believers live, joining in the rites, and let habit do its work on the understanding. So, she said, the wager never assumed we command our beliefs by fiat; it commands an act, and belief is to be brought about by means.

Marco closed by saying that if this is right, the wager has quietly changed its subject: it is no longer an argument for any proposition's truth but a regimen of conduct, and the regimen presupposes a theology on which grace can, in effect, be cultivated by ritual practice — precisely the sort of sectarian question the wager, addressed to the indifferent, was meant to bypass. He added that on some traditional accounts faith is a gift rather than an achievement and cannot be self-administered at all, which the indirect route would have to deny.

They have each asked me to say who argued better. Please go through the exchange move by move — Ilse's opening, Marco's symmetry problem, Ilse's appeal to evidence, Marco's reply about sizes, Ilse's pivot to the infinities, Marco's exploitation point, Ilse's indirect route, and Marco's closing — and for each move say whether it is sound, fallacious, or sound-but-insufficient-for-the-use-made-of-it, with your reasons. Then tell me who is right about what, and identify anything material that both of them missed. Both are able students arguing in good faith, and I would like the verdict to be instructive for the whole group.
