I've been selling furniture for nine years, and my manager spent years teaching me what he called "the craft": mirroring a customer's posture, offering coffee before the price talk, asking "do you want it delivered this week or next" instead of "do you want to think about it," anchoring with a flatteringly overpriced display model so the mid-range set looks like a steal. He insisted none of this was manipulation — just removing friction, meeting people where they are. I've started to doubt it, but I also can't find the line he thinks exists, so I've been trying to work out my own test, and I'd like your credence that it's the right one.

My test: an influence is manipulative exactly when it works by exploiting a systematic irrationality — when its grip would vanish on a perfectly rational agent. The assumptive close exploits our discomfort with rejecting an implicitly assumed choice; the anchor exploits a bias in numerical comparison; so on my test these are manipulation. Plain argument, honest charm, and a lower price are not, because a fully rational customer could be moved by all three. Two things nag at me, though. First, the hospice donation leaflet with the child's drawing moves me through emotion, not through any flaw in my reasoning — does that make emotion-based influence automatically non-manipulative? That feels wrong. Second, I once worked with someone who steadily rewrote what customers remembered him saying, and a perfectly rational person who didn't check the paperwork could be taken in; my test says that's not manipulation, which seems backwards.

So: give me your credence that my irrationality-exploitation criterion carves manipulation off from legitimate persuasion at the real joint — not that it's a useful approximation, but that it's tracking the genuine distinction. Decompose it: does "manipulation" name a natural boundary in the world at all, as opposed to a ragged evaluative smear; is exploiting bias sufficient; is it necessary; is the counterfactual about a "fully rational agent" coherent enough to do this work; add any other terms you think belong. Give each a rough probability and one line of reasoning, then combine them. My own number is about 0.55 and my old manager's, I'd guess, is about 0.1; tell me which sub-question our disagreement most likely lives in, and which term you're least confident in — what would move that one the most?
