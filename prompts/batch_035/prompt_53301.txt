Our reading group has spent two semesters on pleasure and I've come out more confused than when we started, mostly because of the heterogeneity problem. The setup is ancient: the pleasure of eating something good, of listening to a piece of music, of finally cracking a proof, of a warm bath — none of these seem to share a felt quality, they just share the word. That's basically Butler's old objection, and it's supposed to be lethal for hedonism: if pleasures have nothing in common, then a theory that says well-being is the sum of pleasures isn't summing anything, there's no common currency.

Here's the weird part. The responses have been on the table for a long time and nobody convices anyone. Roughly: the phenomenologists (Crisp's line) say there IS a distinctive felt quality, that 'pleasant' names something you can detect in a current episode from the inside, and the bath/proof variation is variation in everything else; the attitudinalists (Feldman's line) say stop looking for a quality entirely — pleasure is an attitude, enjoyment-of, and what differs between the bath and the proof is the object of the attitude, not the attitude itself; and then there's a newer-ish move where pleasure is a kind of seeming, things seeming good to you, so the common factor is quasi-perceptual rather than qualitative. Mill's quality-vs-quantity thing cuts across all of these. And then pain asymbolia — patients who report the sensation of pain but say it doesn't bother them — gets recruited as evidence by every single side, which should probably tell us something.

My working theory is that this is a proxy war about hedonism, not really a debate about pleasure. People who want hedonism true go deflationary (attitudes, seemings), because that hands them their common currency back at a bargain price; people who want it false lean hard on the heterogeneity intuitions; and the actual question — what the pleasantness of an episode consists in — gets nobody's undivided attention. If that's right, the disagreement is strategic rather than substantive, which would explain why it never moves. But I can also see it as a plain verbal dispute: one camp using 'pleasure' for a class of sensations, another for a property of experiences, and 'heterogeneity' being just the noise of them talking past each other. Or maybe they're two different questions under one name — a phenomenological question about what pleasures share, and a value-theoretic question about whether the sharing (or lack of it) matters for whether they can be aggregated.

So that's what I want from you: a diagnosis, not a survey. Substantive, verbal, or two-questions-under-one-heading? Ground it in what each side actually does with the hard cases — the bath versus the proof, pain asymbolia, false pleasures where you enjoy something you merely believe is happening — rather than in how they advertise themselves. And then tell me honestly what progress would even look like here. Is there a thought experiment, a distinction, some formal move, that could break the stalemate, or is this one of those debates that's stuck because the question itself is badly posed? If it's the last one, say why, because I'd genuinely rather know that than keep reading another round of 'but surely pleasure just IS...'
