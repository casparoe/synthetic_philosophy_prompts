I've got a pile of verdicts about when someone is dead, collected from arguing with myself about organ donation policy, and I want to know whether there's an actual principle underneath them or whether I'm just patching things case by case. You're the right audience for this: please think like a philosopher of medicine who also knows the clinical details cold, and don't just hand me back the standard three-views survey (whole-brain, higher-brain, circulatory) and tell me to pick one — commit to something.

My verdicts:

1. A patient who meets whole-brain criteria — no brainstem reflexes, failed apnea test, flat line on the exam — on a ventilator, heart still beating, kidneys still making urine: dead. Confident about this one.
2. Same patient, but it turns out her posterior pituitary still works, so she keeps making antidiuretic hormone and the docs have to manage her sodium. Still dead. It bugs me that "death of the whole brain" is the slogan when clearly not the whole brain died, but the verdict doesn't move.
3. A pregnant woman declared brain-dead at 22 weeks, kept on a ventilator for three months so the fetus could be delivered: dead the entire time, even though the body gestated a baby. This is the case that made me doubt "organism as an integrated whole" language — carrying a fetus to viability strikes me as pretty integrated behavior.
4. Donation after circulatory death: the heart stops, everyone waits two minutes, the surgeons go in. Dead — even though autoresuscitation (the heart restarting on its own, the Lazarus phenomenon) has been reported well past two minutes. I notice I'm leaning on "permanent" rather than "irreversable" here and I don't love that I do it, but there it is.
5. An anencephalic newborn: brainstem works, breathes sometimes, no cortex, never going to be conscious. Not dead.
6. A patient in a permanent vegetative state — eyes open, sleep-wake cycles, but no consciousness ever again: not dead. And I know that this one plus #5 puts me at odds with any higher-brain principle, but I can't make myself call a PVS patient dead; that feels like it gets the ethics backwards. The pair I keep going back and forth on is actually #5 against #6: with the baby, consciousness never existed, with the PVS patient it existed and was lost, and I feel differently about the two, more than I can justify.
7. Locked-in patient, fully conscious, can move one eyelid: alive, obviously. Sanity check, that's all.
8. Far-future case: someone is conscious, but every organ function besides the brain is done by machines — circulation, filtration, everything, no integrative biology left at all. Alive, obviously. Which I think wreaps any account where death is the loss of the organism's integrated functioning, since the only integration happening belongs to the machines.
9. Reverse case: cortex and brainstem completely destroyed, machines keep the heart going, and suppose (this part isn't real medicine yet) they can keep the body's homeostasis running indefinitely with no brain involvement. Dead, same as #1, and I'd say it even if they kept the cells going for decades.

So: is there one principle that gets all or nearly all of these? The candidates I'd half-offer myself are "death is the permanent loss of the capacity for consciousness" (fits 1, 2, 7, 8, 9, but forces me to call 5 and 6 dead, which I refuse) and "death is the loss of the organism's ability to function as a whole" (dies on #3 and #8). What I want from you is a real answer, not a menu: pick a principle, tell me plainly which of my verdicts it cannot save, and tell me which verdicts you'd order me to give up and in what order of reluctance. Then say what the principle predicts on cases I haven't listed — e.g. a patient where only the cerebellum survives, or a conscious person whose labs show zero residual hypothalamic function, or where exactly you'd put the DCD waiting time and why that number and not another one. Take a position and defend it. If the honest conclusion is that my verdicts are a mess and at least one has to go, say that, and say which one goes first. And please no reading list and no bibliography of everyone who's ever written on this — I want your committed reconstruction of what I'm tracking, or a straight verdict that I'm tracking nothing.
