I read a popular blog post on voting theory that summarizes the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem as follows:

"In any election with three or more candidates, every voting system that isn't a dictatorship can be manipulated: some voter, in some situation, does strictly better by casting a ballot that misrepresents her true preferences. So there is no such thing as a strategy-proof voting method — tactical voting isn't a defect of plurality voting or of ranked-choice ballots specifically; it's a mathematical feature of voting itself. The only escapes are to hand the outcome to a single voter or to narrow the field to two candidates. And since real voters obviously do vote tactically, the theorem shows that sincere voting is never rational, and that no election outcome can be trusted as a picture of what the electorate actually wants."

The author clearly knows some economics, and I'm inclined to accept it, but a friend who studies social choice said the post "smuggles in hypotheses and then forgets them." Take the summary apart claim by claim: state which parts a social choice theorist would accept as written, which parts are conflations or overstatements of a more modest thesis, and which parts are simply false. Pay particular attention to (a) what the theorem's actual hypotheses are, and which ones the post never mentions; (b) what "manipulable" means in the theorem — the quantifiers over voters and preference profiles — and whether that notion supports the post's final inference; and (c) whether the cases the post waves at (two-candidate elections, ranked-choice ballots) really behave as it claims.
