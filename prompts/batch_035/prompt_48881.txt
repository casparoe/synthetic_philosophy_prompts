I'm a junior in a semester-long ethics elective, and our teacher has spent the last three weeks running what he calls an "adversarial collaboration boot camp" on whether individual acts that contribute to collective harms can actually be wrong. The class has split into two camps that have been arguing past each other. Camp A ("the Difference Makers") holds that my eating a chicken sandwich, taking a transatlantic flight, or casting a single vote can each be wrong in itself, whether because there's a tiny probability my act is the one that pushes a production run or an election over a threshold, or because participating in a collectively harmful practice is itself a moral problem even when my individual causal contribution is effectively zero. Camp B ("the Causalists") holds that an act can't be wrong because of a harm it doesn't actually cause: if the slaughterhouse processes chickens in batches of thousands with a buffer against demand fluctuations, my sandwich changes nothing that happens to any animal, and a vote that isn't pivotal is a non-event morally, so whatever is wrong here must be wrong at the level of policy, systems, and collective action, not individual choice. Our teacher's test cases are: the school cafeteria's Meatless Monday (does one student's tray matter?), a class election decided by a margin of one, and a scenario he invented called the "10,000 Diners Problem," where each of 10,000 people independently orders a meal from a kitchen that only buys an extra crate of meat per full 500 orders, so almost nobody's individual order triggers a purchase.

The assignment is to produce the document both camps could actually put their names to — not a debate transcript and not a verdict on who wins. I need the claims both sides can sincerely sign (about, for example, what thresholds and buffers do, what expected value does and doesn't show, whether the wrongness of an act and the wrongness of a practice come apart, and whether collective action problems are real even when individual acts are inefficacious); the precise statements of where they still differ, with each formulation worded so that its own side would accept it rather than feel strawmanned; the cruxes — the sub-questions that, if settled, would actually move at least one camp; and, for each crux, a concrete case, argument, or piece of evidence both camps would agree bears on it, with each camp's prediction about that case written down in advance. If part of the dispute turns out to be merely verbal — for instance, about whether "makes a difference" means "changes the outcome" or "is part of what explains it" — please flag which part and why, since our teacher keeps hinting that at least one of our disagreements dissolves once we fix our words.

Please write the joint statement itself in clean prose, organized as a numbered section of agreed claims followed by a table of the residual disagreements (columns: topic, Camp A's position in Camp A's own acceptable wording, Camp B's position in Camp B's own acceptable wording, and whether the disagreement is substantive or verbal). Then, after the document, append a machine-readable summary as a JSON object with exactly these fields: "agreed_claims" (array of strings), "disagreements" (array of objects, each with "topic", "difference_makers_position", "causalists_position", and "disagreement_type" set to either "substantive" or "verbal"), and "cruxes" (array of objects, each with "crux_question", "agreed_test_case", "difference_makers_prediction", and "causalists_prediction"). The two camps should come across as equally competent and intellectually honest — my classmates will refuse to sign anything that caricatures them.
