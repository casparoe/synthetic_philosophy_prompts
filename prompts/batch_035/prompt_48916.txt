I teach an evening civics class for adults at a neighborhood community center — about fifteen students, mostly new citizens and people who left school early, one two-hour session. I have no academic background of my own, but I've spent years chewing on a puzzle about voting, and I'd rather my students run into it themselves than hear me describe it.

My puzzle, as best I can put it: in any large election, the chance that my single ballot changes the outcome is vanishingly small, so if I weigh the small cost of voting against what it buys me, the honest arithmetic says don't bother. Yet I've never missed an election, and I'd feel diminished if I did. I suspect the arithmetic is answering the wrong question. There seems to me to be a real difference between being the one vote that tips a majority and being one of a hundred thousand votes that together constitute a majority — a difference between mattering the way a lever matters and mattering the way a voice in a chorus matters. Perhaps voting is less an instrument for producing outcomes than an act of taking part in something. But I can also see how that thought could be a soothing story I tell myself to justify doing something pointless, and I don't want to hand my students a comfortable excuse either way.

Design the two-hour session as a game or structured activity, not a lecture. The students should first feel the arithmetic — the pull toward not bothering — through the rules and payoffs themselves, whatever those are, using tokens rather than money. Then something in the game should put pressure on the pure cost-benefit way of seeing it, so they experience the tension between the two views of what a vote is instead of being told about it. I'd like the whole thing staged so that neither the arithmetic nor my hunch gets named until the debrief, and even then I want them to have lived both sides of it first.

Please give me: the setup, rules, and materials; a timeline for the two hours; the payoff scheme and how it's settled; and a debrief script that maps specific moments in the game onto the parts of the idea — the tiny chance of being pivotal, the cost side, and the difference between tipping an outcome and joining one.

Two further constraints. About half my students didn't vote in the last election and some are defensive about it; the activity must not be built so that abstainers end up shamed or played for fools — the point is that a sensible person can land on either side. And be candid with me about what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, including how I should handle it, plus an honest note on which part of my hunch no game with tokens can actually reproduce, and which I will have to supply myself at the end.
