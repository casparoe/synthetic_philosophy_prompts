I teach an upper-level seminar in the metaphysics of science (12 students, 90 minutes) and we're about to take up Humeanism about laws of nature. I don't want to lecture the Mill–Ramsey–Lewis "best system" view at them and then have Armstrong-style governing laws presented as the only alternative on the page; I want them to feel what it costs to say that a law is just an axiom of the system that best balances simplicity and strength against the totality of particular facts. Design a staged activity around a toy universe. Give the students a complete "Humean mosaic": a handout listing, in tedious particularity, the entire history of a small artificial world — say, a finite grid of cells that change state over a finite number of ticks, with every state of every cell at every tick recorded. The students, in competing groups, are to act as rival codifiers who must produce the system of rules that best summarizes that history, judged by the group's own declared standards of simplicity, strength, and balance, with a winner chosen by class vote. Crucially, bake into the handout a single anomalous event — one cell, at one tick, does something inconsistent with the otherwise perfect pattern — so that the groups are forced into the trade-off between (a) stating a simple, strong rule that their own handout falsifies, (b) complicating the rule to accommodate the anomaly, or (c) adding a brute exception clause, and must argue about which system wins and why the criterion isn't just whatever gets their own entry elected.

Then stage the anti-Humean moment inside the activity rather than after it. My idea — feel free to improve on it — is to introduce, partway through, a character or role-card: before the anomalous tick, an inhabitant of the toy world (or a bookmaker inside it) placed a bet that the next tick would follow the pattern, and lost. The students must adjudicate whether the bettor was unlucky or wrong — whether the rule that had held until then was something the world had to obey, such that the single deviation deserves the name of a miracle or violation, or whether nothing was violated because there was never a rule doing any enforcing, only a regularity that happened to hold and then, once, didn't. Require them to settle this before they're allowed to use the words "law" or "violation" on the scoreboard. Close with a debrief that maps specific moments onto the dialectic: which features of the activity reconstruct the best-system analysis genuinely, which quietly cheat (e.g., I suspect the class will want to count "simplicity" as whatever is easiest to say in English, and the facilitator will have to supply a fixed notation to prevent that), and which feature of the real debate — the modal force, the sense in which laws govern rather than summarize — no finite handout can reproduce, so that the instructor must supply it and say so honestly. Also flag what will predictably go wrong on the first run: students treating the class vote as the arbiter of truth rather than as a stand-in for the super-human intelligence Lewis invokes, or getting lost in the notation. No lecture beforehand; the students should not see the name "Lewis" or "Humeanism" until the debrief.
