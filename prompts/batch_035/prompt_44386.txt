I teach an upper-level seminar in formal epistemology for philosophy graduate students — twelve of them, one eighty-minute session — and this week's topic is the common prior assumption: the Harsanyi-style idea that differences in rational agents' beliefs should trace back entirely to differences in their information, so that disagreement is a symptom of asymmetric evidence rather than of genuinely different starting points. My students can recite the formal statements, but they don't feel why anyone would care whether the assumption holds. Design an in-class trading game that makes them feel it.

Here is the shape I have in mind. Participants trade a simple security that pays off in points depending on the outcome of some staged event. Each participant draws a signal card at the start; some cards carry genuine information about the outcome, others are pure noise, and — the crucial wrinkle — a subset of participants receive "house priors" on their cards, framed as the background odds their home office has always used, which differ from everyone else's. Participants then trade over several rounds, first bilaterally and then in a simple posted-offer market, and at the end the event is resolved and portfolios are scored. The question the game should force on them, without my naming it in advance: once trading has run its course, is the remaining trade between participants driven by information asymmetries that simply haven't leaked out yet, or by priors that never had to be common in the first place? Ideally I'd also run a second configuration in which every participant shares the same prior and the information structure is unchanged, so they can watch speculative trade evaporate — or fail to, which would be just as instructive.

Please provide: complete rules and materials preparable with index cards, play money, and a whiteboard; a payoff scheme that generates real incentives without real stakes; the full sequence of rounds and what I should do between them; a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the formal machinery — the common prior itself, private information as a partition, the agreement result that says agents who share a prior and know each other's posteriors cannot agree to disagree, and the no-trade result that says common-prior agents should not engage in purely speculative trade at all; a candid note on which features of the idea the game cannot reproduce (genuically Bayesian agents, the iterated public announcements the agreement theorem presupposes, and so on) and that I must supply in the debrief; and a frank list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — participants trading for entertainment rather than expectation, misunderstanding their cards, refusing to trade at all — with concrete fixes for each. I want the participants to run into the assumption rather than hear about it. Please write your answer in American English.
