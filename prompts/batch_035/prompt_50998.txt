I've come around to a pretty deflationary take on the cogito: all it establishes is that thinking is going on at that moment, not that there's a thinker, a self, a substance, or anything that persists even a second past the thought. The "I" in I think is basically a grammatical illusion -- the Lichtenberg point that we'd do better saying it thinks, like we say it storms, and Russell's worry that "I am" only smuggles in a demonstrative to this bit of thinking, never a referent for "I". So the strongest thing Descartes gets, on my view, is something quantificational: there is thinking, now, and that's it.

What would count against this? I want the actual defeaters, not just Descartes fans insisting it's obvious. Is there something about the first-person form of the thought -- the fact that it can only be *expressed* by someone, that avowing it seems to involve self-reference that you can't paraphrase away -- that commits me to more than an episode of thought? Do considerations about immunity to error through misidentification, or about what makes the second cogito (the one remembering the first) count as the same subject, sink the minimal reading? And is there some result or consideration under which the view collapses back into something close to Descartes? Tell me which of these a committed minimalist should concede and which they can safely dig in on, rank the objections from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest about whether my view has been qualified into something unfalsifiable -- and whether that's just what you'd expect for a claim this thin, or a real problem with it.
