I have recently become the parent of a child with problems, and in the past few months I have found myself arguing about a pattern of reasoning that I now encounter constantly and cannot name. My seven-year-old has been diagnosed with significant short-sightedness, and my elder daughter is being assessed for reading difficulties; both conditions, I keep being told, are "largely genetic". The inference that follows is always the same, and it is the inference I want a label for, or a refutation of.

The move works like this. Someone cites a figure — a leaflet at the optometrist's stating that myopia is around eighty per cent heritable; a relative remarking that dyslexia runs in families and so my daughter "is simply wired that way" — and then treats that figure as settling a question about changeability. Because the trait is highly heritable, I am told, more time outdoors cannot help my son's eyes, and intensive structured phonics teaching for my daughter is money thrown away. Yet the same people who say this wear spectacles without embarrassment, and they accept that a child's reading can be transformed by the right tuition. So the move cannot be a general belief that genetic means fixed; it seems to be something more specific.

I have since read just enough to suspect where the trouble lies, but no more. As far as I understand it, a heritability figure is a statistic about how much of the variation in a trait, across some population living under some range of conditions, goes along with genetic variation — it is not a percentage of any one child's trait attributable to genes, and it is not a statement about what would happen under conditions the population never experienced. What I have also read, and what I find quietly devastating for the family consensus, is that the prevalence of short-sightedness among school leavers in parts of East Asia rose from something like a tenth or a fifth to the great majority within two generations, which is far too fast for the gene pool to have changed. A trait, it seems, can be highly heritable within a population and yet almost entirely a matter of conditions at the level of the whole population.

So here is my question. Does the illicit inference I keep meeting — from a population-level heritability estimate to a conclusion about the immutability or unresponsiveness of the trait in a particular individual — have an established name in genetics, philosophy of biology, or statistics? I half remember phrases such as "genetic determinism" and suspect the nearest neighbours include that, but I am not sure whether the thing I am describing is a doctrine, a fallacy, or a confusion between two different questions, and I would rather not attack my relatives with a mislabelled weapon. What I want from you is not a bare term but a careful carving: if there is an established name, tell me its precise definition and who uses it, and distinguish it from the neighbouring things it is not — the doctrine that genes fix traits outright, objections to twin-study methodology, and simple misuse of a technical word. If there is no settled name, say so plainly, propose one, and define its boundary cases. Along the way, please tell me which conclusions actually do follow from a high heritability estimate and which do not, because as a parent I need to know whether the school's fatalism, my relatives' reassurances, and my own hopes are each warranted. Please reply in British English.
