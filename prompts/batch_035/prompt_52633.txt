Here's a thing I keep bumping into and I want it done properly: a case analysis of the Libet experiments through the lens of Dennett's Cartesian theatre / multiple drafts material, running in both directions -- the model applied to the episode, and the episode turned back on the model.

The episode, in brief, so we're working from the same facts: in the 1980s Benjamin Libet had subjects make trivial wrist movements whenever they felt like it while he recorded their EEG. A readiness potential builds up well before the movement -- starting around half a second ahead -- but when he asked subjects to report when they first consciously felt the urge, using the position of a spot on a fast-moving clock, they placed it only about 200 ms before moving. So the brain's motor preparation seems to begin before the person is aware of anything. Libet drew a lot out of this: conscious will arrives too late to cause the act, but retains a veto in the last stretch before the movement; and later imaging work in the 2000s, which claimed to predict choices seconds in advance from pre-motor activity, sharpened the moral further.

Now the mapping, and I want it disciplined, not vibes:

- Which parts of the setup presuppose the Cartesian theatre? My suspicion is the timing-report method does the heavy lifting -- asking subjects to catch the moment the urge became conscious seems to assume there is such a moment, a point at which the content crosses into an audience chamber in front of which the whole person sits. Spell out whether the entire edifice (the 200 ms figure, the veto window) inherits its apparent precision from that presupposition.
- Then the multiple drafts side: what does it say happened instead? If there is no fact of the matter about when the urge became conscious, what happens to Libet's numbers -- and is the veto still a well-defined thing, or does the model threaten to dissolve it as well? And here I most want honesty about silence over forcing the model to speak: multiple drafts was built for perception and report, and it's a fair question whether it was ever designed to handle action initiation. If it genuinely has nothing to say about whether the veto is real, say that plainly.
- And the reverse direction: does the episode test the model at all? Is there anything in the data -- the consistency of subjects' reports across trials, the predictive success of the readiness potential, whatever -- that is awkward for multiple drafts, or that it handles better than the theatre picture does? Or does the whole thing collapse into the model replying that Libet asked an ill-posed question, which is cheaper than it sounds, and I'd like you to say so if that's the situation.

Throughout, flag how confident you are in the main claims and single out the point you're least sure of. I'd much rather get a marked-up confidence picture than a smooth story -- the smooth version of this is everywhere and it's mostly rehearsed.
