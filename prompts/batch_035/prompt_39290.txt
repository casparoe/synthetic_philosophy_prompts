I'm a retired physicist who's fallen down a philosophy-of-history rabbit hole since I stopped teaching, so bear with me if this is naive. Something keeps nagging me about the way history gets chopped into named periods. Historians use "antiquity", "the middle ages", "early modern", people talk about a "short twentieth century" running 1914 to 1991, and the whole calendar pivots on a single year that even secular historians kept when they swapped BC/AD for BCE/CE — they changed the labels but not the hinge. That all seems to treat history as one line with privilaged breakpoints. Yet the very same books casually narrate in circles: empires rise and fall, dynasties harden and collapse within a few generations (Ibn Khaldun's line), whole civilisations get discussed as though they had natural lifespans, Spengler-style. So my question: what view of historical time does the practice of periodisation actually presuppose? Does slicing history into named epochs require a linear, directional picture, or is it neutral between that and a cyclical one, where epochs are just phases of a repeating loop? And is the practice even coherent when the same historian periodises linearly in one chapter and narrates cyclically in the next — or does the linear ladder quietly rule out the circles? I'd also like to know what periodisation would have to look like if we rebuilt it on a cyclical view instead: concretely, what changes in the textbooks?

Please answer as a professional philosopher of history, think carefully, and do a bit of brainstorming before settling on your answer. Start your reply with the bottom line in one sentence, then give the reasoning — no preamble, and no restating my question.
