I have published three novels and am deep in the revisions of a fourth, and it is possibly the least glamorous fact about my working life that I first came to understand a philosophical argument about moral responsibility by sitting at my desk making people do things. I refer to Galen Strawson's Basic Argument: roughly, that you act as you do because of the way you are; that to be truly responsible for what you do you would have to be responsible for the way you are; that being responsible for the way you are would require having made yourself that way through earlier conduct, but you conducted yourself then because of the way you already were; and that the regress terminates only in something like self-creation, which is impossible for a finite being. When I read the formal versions of this, I confess they feel almost tedious in their obviousness, and I suspect the reason is that I occupy the position the argument describes from the inside. Every character I have ever written does exactly what she does because of what I made her: her temperament, her backstory, the scenes I constructed to shape her, the sentences I put in her mouth. From where I sit, none of my characters is responsible for anything, and I have never once felt the urge to hold one of them guilty in the way Strawson says we cannot be held.

What I would like from you is a careful translation of the Basic Argument into the working vocabulary of my craft, and then an honest accounting of what the translation damages. The vocabulary available includes things like backstory, character arc, motivation, voice, revision, and the outline; it also includes the phenomenon that my fellow novelists will recognize immediately, in which a character goes off-script — refuses an action the outline demanded, starts speaking in a register I did not plan, so that I find myself discovering rather than deciding what she does. There is a further wrinkle I want explicitly translated: Strawson insists the argument does not depend on determinism, since adding a random element to one's nature would not make it one's own doing. The closest analogue on my side is that I could roll dice at key drafting moments and the character would gain no authorship from it — the surprise would still be mine to manage, hers only to instantiate. And there is the question of how to render the notion of a self-created being in this vocabulary at all: a character who wrote her own backstory, who was somehow the author of the outline that produced her. I can gesture at that with the image of a novel that writes its own earlier chapters, but I want to know whether the gesture is doing any real work or is just picturesqueness.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Which of Strawson's claims translate cleanly into author-and-character terms, which only survive the translation with distortion, and which have no expression at all in the vocabulary of novel-writing? In particular: is my recurring sense that characters acquire a life of their own — the voice that dictates sentences, the refusal of the planned ending — a genuine counterweight to the Basic Argument, or is it an artifact of my limited access to my own compositional processes, in which case it might map onto the compatibilist replies Strawson dispatches? Does the fact that no one inside my novels can ever occupy my position, the author's chair, say anything about whether the God's-eye standpoint from which the Basic Argument looks obvious is a standpoint anyone in the actual world could coherently take? And at the end, tell me whether the residue points to something the argument has failed to see or merely to something my craft vocabulary cannot say — because if it is the latter, I would like to know what concept I am missing before I finish this revision and decide, as its author, what my protagonist finally deserves.
