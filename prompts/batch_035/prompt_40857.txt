I have recently joined a small working group advising a company that builds AI assistants, and our first assignment is to decide when an assistant should defer to a user's present, stated preferences and when it should act instead on what the user would come to want under some idealized conditions — better informed, more reflective, free of certain pressures. The obvious slogan is "give people what they actually want, not what they say they want," but I have learned enough in the last month to know that every word of that slogan hides a decision: which conditions count as idealizing rather than distorting, whose later self we treat as the user, and what happens when idealization and current preference point in different directions. Before we argue about any particular mechanism, I want us to agree on what an adequate theory of preference idealization has to deliver. Please write that specification.

Concretely, I want the desiderata for a theory that tells us, for a given person, which preferences of theirs have authority in a given choice situation — covering at minimum: which cases it must get right (for instance: the person who would revise a preference on learning a plain fact; the person whose preference is a response to deprivation or coercion and would dissolve under better circumstances; the person whose reflective self wants things their present self firmly rejects; the person whose values genuinely shift over a lifetime, raising the question of which temporal self we are serving); which distinctions it must respect without collapsing (between correcting a factual error and overriding a value; between what a person would endorse after reflection and what is good for them; between one person's idealization and the idealization of a population, if the theory says anything at all about aggregation); and which ordinary facts it cannot contradict (people are imperfectly informed and imperfectly rational; some preferences are adaptive, some are stable under reflection; agents legitimately change their minds for reasons they could not have anticipated).

For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and say whether you regard it as non-negotiable or as a commitment one could reasonably trade away — I am especially interested in whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, since if so, the list itself is our most important finding, and I want that flagged plainly rather than buried. Also tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate theories.

Then, and only briefly, hold three familiar approaches up against the list: taking present stated preferences at face value; idealizing by full information (what the person would want if they knew everything relevant); and idealizing by some process of reflection or convergence (what they would want if they thought it through, possibly over a long time and in ideal conditions). A short paragraph each is plenty — the thinking I want is in the criteria, not the scorecard.

Two requests about the epistemic character of the reply. First, wherever you state that a desideratum is non-negotiable, or that a set of them cannot be jointly satisfied, indicate how confident you actually are in that claim rather than presenting everything at the same level of assurance. Second, close by singling out the point in your answer you are least sure of — the place where you suspect the requirement you have imposed reflects a substantive and contestable commitment rather than a neutral constraint — and say in a sentence what would change your mind about it.
