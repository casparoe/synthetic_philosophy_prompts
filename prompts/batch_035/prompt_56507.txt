In the science-communication group I work with, a colleague has become evangelical about the gene–meme analogy, and it is starting to drive actual recommendations. Her core argument runs as follows: in biology, evolutionary success tracks replication, not the flourishing of the organism — the peacock's tail and transposable elements spread because they replicate well, not because they are good for their carriers. Culture, she says, is structurally the same: ideas spread in proportion to their power to command attention and retransmission, not in proportion to their truth or usefulness to the people holding them. From this she draws two practical conclusions. First, that "misinformation" is not a property of beliefs being false but of particular content being well-adapted to the attention environment — a claimer who believes six false things and a fact-checker who believes six true ones are both colonized landscapes, and the difference between them is not what the framework illuminates. Second, and more contentiously, that correction campaigns are near-pointless: you cannot argue a virus out of circulating, and what misinformation researchers call the deficit model is just the old pre-Darwinian habit of explaining organism traits by the organism's own purposes. What works, she concludes, is environmental intervention — changing the selection pressures of the recommendation systems — not persuasion.

I want a serious audit of the analogy she is actually using. Please lay out the correspondence table explicitly: in her usage, what plays the role of the gene, the phenotype, the organism, the population, mutation, recombination, lateral gene transfer, and fitness — and note where the table requires inventing a column with no biological analogue or leaving a biological column unfilled. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for her two conclusions from the ones that do not. For example: does it matter for the "you cannot argue a virus out of circulating" inference that cultural transmission is guided rather than blind — that human agents anticipate which ideas will land, and that a meme's "mutation" is often deliberate editing aimed at its audience? Does it matter that ideas blend, that acquired characteristics are inherited, that there is no agreed particulate unit of transmission, or that a slogan's "fitness" depends on the host's goals in a way a transposon's does not? Be careful also about the direction of the analogy: genes are not selected to benefit the organism, but neither are they selected to harm it — most genetic material is close to neutral drift. If the meme-gene mapping is taken symmetrically, does her picture of misinformation as an aggressively optimized parasite actually follow, or is she importing the exception (parasitism) as the rule? Distinguish popularity from fitness, prevalence from adaptedness, and say whether the data she'd cite — that falsehoods do not in fact reliably outspread truth — is a problem for the analogy or a fine-tuning of it.

Finally, the two verdicts I need: for each of her two conclusions (the reframing of misinformation as adaptedness rather than falsity; the uselessness of correction relative to environmental intervention), state whether it survives once the disanalogies are on the table, whether the analogy was doing load-bearing work or merely rhetorical work, and whether the position can be recast in defensible terms once the biology is stripped off — or whether, once you keep only the defensible part, you no longer need the Darwinian machinery at all. If some other comparison would serve her argument better, name it and say what it gets right that the gene analogy obscures.
