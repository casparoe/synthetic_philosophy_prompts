I'm teaching a section of a contemporary ethics course built around suffering-focused ethics — Popper's asymmetry between relieving suffering and promoting happiness, weak versus strong negative utilitarianism, lexical views on which no amount of happiness can outweigh certain depths of suffering, and the benevolent world-exploder objection that strong views seem obligated to endorse. For the fiction pairing I've settled on Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go, but I've discovered that my own reading keeps sliding between two incompatible verdicts and I'd like a careful philosophical reading to bring to class.

Here's the tension I want the essay to work through. Miss Emily's eventual defense of the donation program, offered when Kathy and Tommy finally confront her and Madame, is basically an aggregate utilitarian one: the clones have given the rest of the world enormous benefit, and Hailsham was a reform project meant to soften the margins, not to challenge the arithmetic. So a naive thought says negative utilitarianism simply condemns the program flat-out — it is a machine for producing intense suffering, and no offsetting benefits count under any strong version. But the novel keeps complicating that. The Hailsham childhood is genuinely happy, and the students' art, the guardians' tenderness, and Kathy's retrospective narration all suggest lives that contain a great deal that NU axiologies apparently don't count. Does the novel dramatize the internal difficulty of deciding whether a life organized around eventual harvesting is one a suffering-focused axiology should say shouldn't have existed at all? Then there's the ending: the famous quietism — no rebellion, the deferral rumor revealed as baseless hope, Kathy standing by the Norfolk fence imagining Tommy among the fluttering rubbish. Critics complain that the passivity is unearned, but from a negative utilitarian standpoint one could argue the characters are quietly right: rebellion and hope might add suffering to lives already dominated by it. Or is their acceptance just adaptive preference formation dressed up as wisdom, which would be exactly the kind of thing a suffering-focused ethic most needs to detect and can't?

I'd like you to say where the lens illuminates and where the novel resists it — be honest about scenes that fit better under an ordinary hedonistic accounting than a suffering-focused one, and about whether the deferral and Madame threads have more to do with moral status and recognition than with axiology at all. End with whether you think the novel contributes something to the debate between weak and strong negative utilitarianism that the arguments alone don't: I have a suspicion it's something about the difference between knowing your life is priced into an aggregate and living inside that fact, but I'd like that either developed properly or debunked. Refer to scenes by description rather than quotation.
