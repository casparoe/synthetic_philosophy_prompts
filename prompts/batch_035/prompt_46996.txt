For the past year I have chaired the publications committee of a small independent forecasting institute, and a decision has just arrived that I find harder than our usual business. In 1974 the institute published a projection of a global food-and-energy crunch by the year 2000; it was widely mocked, and the crunch never came. The modelling programme survives, and a staff team now proposes to publish a fresh run — same structure, revised parameters — dating a crunch around 2075. Runs of the same model in 1995 and 2012 each placed a crunch roughly fifty years ahead of their publication date; each was wrong on timing, and each revision pushed the date outward. The team concedes that every dated prediction has failed, but insists the model is 'right but early': the mechanism is sound — demand growth pressing on a finite constraint, with technical change entering as a rescuing term that the model does not itself explain — and only the time constants have been mis-set. The committee must choose among publishing it as a forecast (with the model's full record stated), publishing it as one scenario within a fan of alternatives, or declining to publish until the team can offer something that could fail.

Please draft the decision memo I shall circulate ahead of Thursday's meeting. It must recommend one course outright, or a variant with explicit conditions and a deadline, and it should address the epistemological heart of the matter rather than manage it as a reputational risk. Is 'right but early' a coherent defence of a theory, or an immunising strategy? In particular, the memo should say what the constant-fifty-year-horizon pattern is and is not evidence of; whether the mechanism-claim and the date-claim can be separated, so that publication puts only one of them at risk; what advance commitments — dated checks whose failure would count against the model class, fixed before publication and not revisable afterwards — would make the next report more than a rescue; and whether the team's covering note, which leans on the analogy of Malthus (mechanism vindicated, dates wrong), earns its place or merely flatters the model. The losing side deserves its due: the historical fact that long-run warnings dismissed as premature have often been vindicated decades late, and the argument that demanding short-run falsifiability from a theory of a slow variable is a category error. One disclosure the memo need not carry but you should weigh: I was the junior modeller on the 1974 report, and some colleagues will suspect me either of settling old scores or of protecting my life's work. Write the memo so that its reasoning would look the same whichever motive I were secretly governed by.
