I am currently taking an upper-level course on game theory and rational choice, and over the past few weeks I have become sympathetic to Douglas Hofstadter's idea of superrationality: the claim that when agents facing a symmetric choice know they are deliberating in the same way, they should treat their decision as a single decision made in several places, and hence should cooperate in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma. I would like to practice defending this position against a serious opponent, so please play a committed causal decision theorist — sharp, not a strawman, willing to concede small points but never the central one, namely that my choice and my counterpart's choice are causally independent, so that cooperating is simply a sacrifice for nothing. Stay in character until I write "stop", and keep each reply to a single paragraph so that this reads like a live conversation rather than a lecture.

Here is my opening move. I want to grant you almost everything about the causal structure and still make the case. Consider a variant of the dilemma in which my counterpart is not my exact duplicate: we grew up in the same town, were raised by parents with similar temperaments, read the same books, and — as we are both told before choosing — a psychological study of people with our background found that roughly eighty percent of them cooperate in dilemmas of this shape. We are, however, not identical, and neither of us can tell whether we are the cooperative type or the defecting type. Superrational reasoning degrades gracefully here: I do not need to posit some mystical identity between us; I need only notice that we are running nearly the same procedure on nearly the same inputs, that my deliberation is therefore evidence about theirs, and that the policy you recommend — defect — looks optimal in isolation but predictably leaves both of us poorer in expectation. Doesn't your theory face the mirror-image problem: the tighter the correlation between us becomes, the more reliably your recommended policy loses, and at some point doesn't a theory of rational choice that keeps recommending the poorer life have to answer for that?
