I'm a PhD student in financial engineering (thesis on drawdown-optimal portfolio construction, so basically all I think about is when positive expected value isn't enough), and I keep getting into a long-running argument with a friend in philosophy about what they call fanaticism: the view that an option with a genuinely tiny probability of an astronomically huge payoff beats an option with a certain merely-very-good payoff. So roughly: donate to some speculative extinction-risk thing with a one-in-a-trillion chance of saving an unfathomable number of future lives, rather than fund something that reliably saves a thousand people now. Expected value theory seems to just say yes, and the philosophers have a whole fight about whether that's a bug — bounded utility functions, impossibility results showing you can't cleanly mix fanatical and modest conclusions, Pascalian muggings as a reductio, Wilkinson defending the fanatic, MacAskill wanting to discount tiny chances of huge payoffs. My gut reaction every time is that my field already owns machinery for exactly this dispute, it just goes by different names, and I'd like this actually checked rather than vibes.

So here's the ask: restate the fanaticism/moderation debate for me in my working vocabulary — arithmetic vs geometric growth rates, Kelly betting and the log-utility answer to positive-EV longshots, time averages vs ensemble averages (Ole Peters' ergodicity stuff), risk of ruin as a category distinct from ordinary drawdown, diversifiability, lottery-like assets, convexity and tail hedges that are negative EV but pay off when everything else burns. Translate each of the main philosophical positions into the closest statement in that language — e.g. what does a bounded utility function look like from a Kelly standpoint, is "rejecting the fanatical option" the same kind of move as sizing down a bet whose geometric mean is negative, does the Pascalian mugging have a counterpart in anything (the classic scammy penny-stock pitch?), and so on. Then give me the residue report: which parts of the philosophical debate map cleanly, which only fit with distortion, and which have no expression at all. I suspect the residue is big — my entire toolkit assumes one agent making repeated multiplicative bets in time, while the fanatic's choice is supposedly one-shot, with the payoffs being other people's lives rather than my wealth — but I want to know whether the residue points at something risk management genuinely can't say, or whether the philosophical debate is less substantive than it looks because the two sides aren't even using "expected value" the same way.

Don't be gentle about it either — if some of my analogies are just wrong (my friend claims Kelly reasoning has literally nothing to do with any of this and I'm pattern-matching), say so and explain which ones break. But if there's a real translation to be had, I want it done honestly, without smuggling in portfolio-theory assumptions and calling them philosophy, and with the untranslatable stuff treated as the interesting part rather than swept aside.
