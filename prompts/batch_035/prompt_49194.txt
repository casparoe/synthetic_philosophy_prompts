I'm on the naming and legacies committee at a mid-sized state university, and we're deadlocked, so I want you to draft the written recommendation I'll circulate before the final vote in three weeks. The concrete case: our oldest residence hall is named for a donor whose fortune came substantially from enslaved labor in the 1830s–50s. The university recieved the original endowment, and through mergers and reinvestment we are, by our own archivists' account, still drawing income from it. The board will only realistically consider three options: rename outright and endow a scholarship from the remaining funds, keep the name but add a permanent unflinching exhibit in the lobby, or rename the building while keeping a research chair that carries the name. But the real fight on the committee is about the accompanying statement. One faction — including me, I'll admit — wants the university to formally accept guilt, actual guilt, not the word "regret," which our student government has already rejected as a dodge. The other faction says an institution can't be guilty of anything: corporations don't have consciences, everyone with hands on the original decisions is 170 years dead, and current students are being asked to feel bad about money they never took. They also suspect the whole thing is institutional performance for the rankings.

One of my colleagues keeps bringing up Jaspers' The Question of German Guilt, the little book from 1946, where he sorts guilt into criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical. I half understand it. I don't want the memo to just cite him as an authority — what I'd like is for the reasoning to actually use that four-way structure, or something like it, to sort out which kinds of guilt (if any) can attach to a corporate body that persists across generations, and which only ever attach to persons. If the categories genuinely don't transfer from a nation after a war to a university after a bequest, the memo should say that plainly and do the work some other way.

So: draft the memo. It needs a clear recommendation on both the building and the wording of any statement (with actual language, or at least the sentence that does the heavy lifting), a timeline the board could act on this semester, an honest treatment of the strongest version of the "institutions can't be guilty" side rather than a strawman, and a fair account of why our side isn't just guilt theater — including where the pro-guilt position is weakest. One more thing: as you go, or at the end, flag how confident you are in each major move and single out the point you're least sure of. I'd rather know where the memo is on thin ice than have it sound bulletproof in front of the board. And please write it in American English — one of the committee members is British and I refuse to give her ammunition about my spelling.
