Title (working): "Neither Gives Way: Epistemic Zoning and the Memory/Record Conflict"

Thesis: The standard framing of the collective memory vs. history problem — one of them must "give way" when they conflict — is a mistake. It presupposes that memory and historical record are competing answers to the same question. They're not. They serve different functions, and the right response to conflict is not adjudication but zoning: separating the domains in which each has authority.

Key moves:

1. The standard debate, quickly: a community remembers its past in a certain way (say, a founding that was more heroic and less violent than the archival record suggests, or an origin story where the community was purely victim and never perpetrator). Historians dig up documents that contradict the memory. Memory partisans say collective memory does something the record can't — it sustains identity, solidarity, practical orientation toward the future. Historians say that's exactly why it's dangerous: memory that's immune to correction becomes a machine for laundering interests into "truth."

2. My first move, which I think is underexplored: the "record" is not the antithesis of collective memory — it IS collective memory, just on a longer loop. Archives are produced by institutions, which are communities, which remember selectively. What we call "the historical record" is the sediment of earlier collective memories that happened to get written down, and the current historians' consensus is the memory-loop of the professional community of historians. So the conflict isn't memory vs. record; it's short-loop memory (living, embodied, revised on the scale of generations) against long-loop memory (sedimented, revised on the scale of centuries). This reframing matters because it blocks the picture where one side is "social" and one side is "purely evidential." Both are social. The long loop just has different error-correcting norms.

3. But — and this is where the essay turns — I don't want to conclude that therefore anything goes (the strong social-constructivist slide). The long loop's error-correcting norms are genuinely better at tracking what happened, because its disputes are adversarial across communities in a way short-loop memory's aren't. So when it comes to the question "what happened?", the long loop wins. Full stop.

4. The zoning idea: this is where the "neither gives way" comes in. Think of it like floodplain zoning in urban planning. You don't resolve the conflict between building on the land and the flood risk by picking a winner everywhere. You designate zones. Commemorative contexts (rituals, monuments, holidays, origin-story telling inside the community) are zones where short-loop memory operates with its own norms — coherence with identity, capacity to orient action, transmissibility across generations. Archival/educational contexts (schools, museums' factual plaques, courts, scholarship) are zones governed by long-loop norms. The sin on each side: letting the commemorative norms colonize the archival zone (that's propaganda, or at least bad education) or letting archival norms tyrannize the commemorative zone (that's a certain tone-deaf rationalism that flattens meaning into information).

5. Anticipated objection I want to take seriously: doesn't zoning just enshrine falsehood? If the community commemorates something the record shows didn't happen, I've apparently licensed the ritual affirmation of a falsehood. My reply: commemorative speech is not assertion. When a community tells its origin story at a festival, the speech act is closer to a vow or a renewal of commitment than to a truth-claim. Its sincerity conditions are different — you can "truly" commemorate in a way that's false as description but true as commitment. I want to say the vow is answerable to what the community does next, not to what happened before. That's why commemoration can survive correction of its descriptive content: the descriptive content was never its load-bearing part.

6. Where I get nervous: section 5 seems to give away too much to the memory partisans when the community's identity itself depends on the falsehood. If the point of the ritual is, at bottom, "we were the innocent ones," then correcting the record but preserving the ritual feels like keeping the roof while removing the foundation. My hunch is that there's a further distinction between commemorations whose falsity is incidental and commemorations whose falsity is constitutive of what's being committed to — and that zoning is fine for the first but not the second. But I haven't worked this out and I suspect it's the crux. Any thoughts on how to develop it, or whether it sinks the whole zoning picture?

One more small thing: I've been using "short-loop" and "long-loop" as my own terms. Do they collide with existing terminology in a confusing way, or are they okay as stipulated labels? Happy to hear any suggestions for cleaner ones.
