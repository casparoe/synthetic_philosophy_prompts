I am a high school junior writing a term paper for my social studies course on technological determinism. We read Langdon Winner's 1980 essay "Do Artifacts Have Politics?", and my assignment is to take a position on the question in the title. Before drafting my own paper, I would like to compare my reasoning against a carefully argued model essay. Please write one for me.

The essay must reach a definite conclusion about whether artifacts can have politics "in themselves" — that is, whether the political character of an arrangement can be located in the physical design of a thing, as Winner claimed, rather than only in the intentions of designers or in the surrounding institutions. To keep the essay from recycling the standard examples (the low bridges over the Long Island parkways, the tomato harvester, nuclear power plants, and so on), please build the argument around the following three cases. Two of them are hypothetical scenarios of my own invention, so please do not treat them as reports of real events or try to identify them with any:

1. A mid-sized city, purely for budgetary reasons, removes fare inspectors from its trams and installs automatic gates that accept only electronic payment cards. Nobody in the city government ever mentions the poor or undocumented residents who pay in cash; the stated rationale is entirely financial. Fare evasion becomes impossible, and homeless people lose a warm place to ride on winter nights.

2. A school rebuilds its crumbling courtyard and, at the architect's unprompted suggestion, replaces the four informal paths students had worn across the grass with a single central staircase that everyone must use between classes. Discipline incidents drop noticeably, and the principal takes credit.

3. A rural county must choose between two designs for a new junction: a roundabout, which forces every driver — including the politically well-connected owner of the gravel quarry — to yield to whoever is already circulating, or a traffic signal whose timing the county engineer can adjust at will.

For each case, the essay should say where, exactly, the politics resides: in the artifact's physical form, in the designer's intention, in the pattern of use, or in the institutional context. It should notice that these locations come apart across the three cases — in the first, the designers' intentions appear clean while the effect is not; in the second, the intention is innocent but the artifact reshapes social relations; in the third, the artifact either enforces a kind of equality or delegates power depending on which option is chosen. You may draw on Winner's own distinction between artifacts that require a particular social order and artifacts that are merely strongly compatible with one.

The essay should then use these cases to adjudicate between the strong reading of Winner — an artifact's political character can inhere in its design so robustly that it persists even when the intentions and institutions change — and the deflationary reading, on which the politics always lies with the people and institutions, and the artifact is at most a tool they pick up and put down. Please do not conclude with some version of "it depends" or "both sides have merit." Commit to one reading, defend it, and state what would count as evidence against your conclusion. Finally, please do not include a bibliography, footnotes, or citations of specific empirical studies; my teacher wants our own argumentation, and I can track down sources separately if I need them.
