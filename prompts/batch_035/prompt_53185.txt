I'm a retired HVAC technician with no training in philosophy, and I want to say up front that everything below I worked out on my own at the kitchen table, so I may be rediscovering things everybody knows or making errors a student would catch. I've deliberately avoided reading up on how the experts handle this, because I wanted to see how far I could get first. I've now gotten somewhere I can't accept, and I'd like a step-by-step diagnosis.

The puzzle I started with: on the front of an index card I write, "The sentence on the front of this card is not true." Call that sentence L. My reasoning went like this.

(1) The matching rule: a sentence that says something is true exactly when things are the way it says they are. "Snow is white" is true if and only if snow is white. This seems to me to be just what the word "true" means.

(2) Suppose L is true. Then by the matching rule things are as L says they are, so L is not true. Contradiction.

(3) Suppose L is false. Then things are not as L says they are, so it is not the case that L is not true, so L is true. Contradiction again.

(4) So L is neither true nor false, and the only escape I could find is that L never manages to say anything at all. On this view it's marks on a card that never got into the business of being true or false -- a sentence has to say something before it can be true or false, and L never gets off the ground.

That felt like a solution, and it might have ended there, except that I then turned the card over and wrote on the back: "The sentence on the front of this card is not true." Call that B. This is where I got into trouble.

(5) B is an ordinary sentence about a different sentence, the same kind of thing as "The forecast was wrong." And what B says about L -- that L is not true -- is exactly what L says about itself.

(6) If L says nothing, as step (4) claims, then L is not true. A sentence that says nothing is surely not true.

(7) B says that L is not true, and L is not true, so by the matching rule B is true.

(8) But B and L say the same thing. If B manages to say it, then L manages to say it too, and step (4) was wrong: L does say something. And the moment L says something, steps (2) and (3) apply and L is both true and not true. The escape I found at step (4) is closed by the very sentence I wrote to test it.

Here is why I think the argument proves too much. B is not about itself in any way -- it's about L, the way "The forecast was wrong" is about the forecast. If L's condition is contagious, and B caught it just by calling L untrue, then a third sentence that calls B untrue should catch it from B, and so on down the line. Every sentence that says of anything that it is not true would be one link away from a liar sentence, and there would be no telling, just by looking at an ordinary sentence like "The forecast was wrong," whether it's safe. My own conclusion appears to be that I can never truly call any sentence untrue -- that the word "true" can't survive contact with sentences that talk about sentences -- and I don't believe that. I tell people the forecast was wrong all the time.

So either the matching rule fails even for perfectly ordinary sentences, in which case I no longer know what "true" means, or I've slipped somewhere I can't see. Please tell me which step I should reject, why that step looked fine to me when it isn't, and what giving it up costs me. If the argument is actually sound as far as it goes, then tell me which premise is the least costly to abandon and what the price is. For what it's worth, I'd most like to keep step (1); it's the only piece of this I feel I genuinely understand.
