The two of them: Marcus, who's very much a classical frequentist, and Dana, who is Bayesian about everything and does all her models in Stan. It went like this:

1. Marcus said our analysis is basically invalid as it stands: if you check the data repeatedly and can stop whenever p < .05, the long-run false-positive rate isn't 5% anymore, it's way higher — under the null, a procedure like that will eventually cross the threshold nearly every time if you let it run. The p-value is only meaningful relative to the sampling plan, and we broke the sampling plan.

2. Dana answered that his complaint is about the long-run behavior of a procedure across hypothetical replications, but the question on the table is what *this* data says. The likelihood of the observed data is identical whether we had fixed n at 200 or stopped when it looked good — the stopping rule doesn't enter the likelihood, so her posterior over the effect is exactly the same either way. That's the likelihood principle, and she said his whole objection lives on the wrong side of it.

3. Marcus conceded the principle has that consequence but said it cuts both ways: when she reports something like a 92% probability the effect is real, she's mixing the data with a prior, and priors aren't the neutral default they look like. A flat prior on the effect size quietly piles prior mass on big effects — which, he said, is exactly the disease behind the replication crisis, published effects that are real-but-way-smaller-than-advertised or not real at all.

4. Dana replied that at least her prior is out in the open where people can argue about it, whereas his p-value also depends on a pile of choices — one- vs two-sided, which exclusions, which covariates — that just get buried in a methods section nobody reads. And anyway, she said, all the frequentist fixes for the crisis are priors under another name: preregistration is literally writing down your beliefs and intended analyses before seeing the data.

5. Marcus said no, preregistration is about disclosure, not beliefs — it's there to mark out which analyses were the planned ones, so that the garden-of-forking-paths stuff can't operate in the dark, and it constrains him rather than biasing him. And he pointed out that every actual disaster in the replication crisis — p-hacking, outcome switching, trying analyses until something works — is something her framework has no built-in defense against: a motivated Bayesian with a free choice of stopping rule and a convenient prior can confirm anything at least as fast, because nobody is auditing any error rate at all.

If his theory of inference says two researchers looking at identical data must reach different conclusions because one of them had been planning another look, that's a cost of his theory, not a feature of the world.
