I'm advising a funder who can support a small group — four researchers over three years — on conceptual questions in AI safety via debate, where the funder wants the entire portfolio organized around the problem of obfuscated arguments. To fix terms: an obfuscated argument is one in which a misleading or false conclusion is defended by a very large set of individually verifiable but jointly unrepresentative considerations — every step checks out, yet the overall picture is wrong because the sheer bulk of the argument conceals the few considerations that would decide the matter. A standard illustration: a debater defends the claim that some chess move is a blunder by citing dozens of plausible refuting lines, each a valid sequence on inspection, while the one line that saves the move is buried in a search tree the judge will never have the budget to examine. The worry is that debate-based oversight — two systems arguing before a human judge — degrades precisely when arguments are obfuscated, because the judge's evaluation capacity is small relative to the size of the argument. Theoretical responses ask, roughly, for protocols in which an honest debater can refute an obfuscated argument with an effort that grows only logarithmically in the argument's length, but it is far from obvious that this ideal survives contact with human judges who have realistic limits on attention, time, and background knowledge.

Given that setting, which open questions in this area matter most, and in what order should the team attack them? Please give a dependency-aware prioritization rather than a survey: for each question you rank highly, explain why it sits upstream of the others, whether it is tractable for theorists with backgrounds in complexity theory and epistemology rather than empirical machine learning, and what evidence over the next few years — new impossibility results, stronger protocol constructions, or empirical studies of how human judges actually fare against obfuscated argumentation — would move it up or down the list. I am equally interested in what you would deprioritize: for example, further game-theoretic analysis of ideally rational debaters, additional protocol constructions whose assumptions about judge competence are never tested, or work on judge-side heuristics that presupposes the very guarantees the protocols were meant to supply. Finally, tell me candidly whether you think the framing itself rests on a mistake — for instance, that obfuscated arguments are just ordinary epistemic opacity relabeled, or that the logarithmic-effort ideal is an artifact of idealized judges with no analogue among humans — since an answer of that kind would change where the money should go more than any ranking of the questions within it.
