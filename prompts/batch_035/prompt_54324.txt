I'm a PhD student in cultural anthropology finishing my dissertation fieldwork, and I keep running into a piece of conversational machinery I want to take apart properly. At a recent departmental workshop on my fieldwork, the discussion turned to whether a community's patrilineal land-inheritance rules — which work to the systematic disadvantage of daughters who have migrated to the city for paid work — can be criticized as unjust, and by whom. A senior colleague waved the whole question off with who are we to judge, and the room treated that as decisive. Our discipline has historical reasons for this reflex, but I want to know whether the one-liner actually earns the work it was made to do.

Please lay out, as a numbered list I can circulate before the next session, the distinct precise claims that who are we to judge could be expressing in that exchange. I'd like them ordered from the least to the most revisionary. At minimum, I want to see the following separated out: the sociological observation that the community's norms differ from ours; the epistemic point that we lack local knowledge of how the practice functions; the epistemic point that we are not positioned to know the moral truths even if such truths exist; the metaethical claim that there is no framework-independent fact of the matter about whether the practice is unjust; the metaethical claim that there is such a fact but it is indexed to a framework, so that the judgment is true or false only relative to a standard; and the non-cognitivist reading on which such judgments merely express a stance and so cannot be assessed for truth at all. Where I've missed a natural reading, add it.

For each reading, tell me three things. First, is it plausible — and is it genuinely distinct from a position a thoroughgoing moral universalist could also accept, or does it collapse into such a position under pressure? Second, is it true or false, or too underspecified to evaluate without further commitment? Third, and most importantly: does it actually support the use my colleague put it to — namely, suspending criticism of this specific practice altogether — as opposed to merely demanding that criticism be better informed, more cautious, or offered with acknowledgment of our own record of bad judgments?

Two further things I want the answer to address. Some of the community's own spokespeople are perfectly willing to judge us — Western labor markets, our family forms, our treatment of the elderly — and the daughters affected by the inheritance rules have themselves begun invoking national law and human-rights vocabulary against it. So please flag which readings become self-undermining or lose their force once the people we declined to judge start judging back, or once the allegedly closed culture turns out to contain internal dissenters who are doing the normative work for us. And finally, close by saying which reading my colleague most plausibly meant, given that he invoked the phrase to end rather than open a discussion, and sketch what a fair, non-caricatured response to that specific reading would look like when I raise it. I don't need a defense of moral universalism; I need a careful map of the ground the slogan is covering.
