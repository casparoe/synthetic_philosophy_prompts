I'm a science and ideas journalist working on a feature about how disagreement persists in an age when everyone has the same information at their fingertips. The spine of the piece is Aumann's agreement theorem: agents with common priors who know each other to be rational cannot agree to disagree, provided their posteriors are common knowledge. I've been invited to run a hands-on session at a monthly ideas salon I sometimes cover — about 15 attendees, mixed backgrounds (a couple of statisticians, some humanities people, several stubborn generalists), one 60-minute slot. Design a game that makes them feel the force of the theorem and, just as importantly, its fine print.

The core mechanic I have in mind: private, slightly different evidence about some factual question, then forced public exchange of opinions round by round, with points paid out for accuracy at the end — but I'm open to your improvements. What I want is for participants to run into two things themselves: first, the strange experience of watching honest announcements cascade toward consensus; second, the moment the cascade stalls because someone's announcement reveals less than it seems to — she says "60%" but is she sincere, is she strategic, is she even the rational agent the theorem requires? If you can build in a betting element so they hit the no-trade corollary — that agents meeting the theorem's conditions can't find a bet both would accept — without lecturing about it first, so much the better.

Please specify: rules and materials (nothing exotic — cards, tokens, a whiteboard), the payoff scheme (points, not money), a round-by-round timeline that fits the hour, and a debrief that maps what actually happened onto the theorem's moving parts: common priors, common knowledge of posteriors versus common knowledge of the evidence itself, and the distinction between exchanging conclusions and exchanging reasons. Tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — skepticism from the humanities people, gaming from the statisticians, the saliency of people anchoring on the first number spoken — and which part of the theorem no parlor game can reproduce, so I know what I have to supply in the debrief rather than let them discover. My deadline for the session plan is three weeks out, and I'd like to quote the game's outcome in the feature, so design it so its results are quotable: something crisp happened, or crisply failed to.
