I'm a graduate student in public policy taking a course on regulatory cost-benefit analysis, and something about the standard methodology has been bothering me for weeks. In nearly every jurisdiction, agencies evaluating a regulation or an infrastructure project convert all future costs and benefits into present value by discounting them at a constant annual rate: a benefit arriving in a hundred years counts for a small fraction of the same benefit today, and a benefit arriving in five hundred years counts for essentially nothing at all. The rate is usually justified by appeal to some version of the Ramsey formula, which decomposes it into a term for pure time preference (counting a unit of welfare for less simply because it arrives earlier), a term for the probability that humanity won't be around to enjoy it, and a term reflecting how much richer future people are expected to be. Where the level of the rate is contested, the argument is over whether to infer it from observed market interest rates or derive it from first principles — the standoff between economists who hold that a near-zero pure rate of time preference is ethically mandatory and those who reply that anything far from market rates is paternalistic, incoherent, or both.

What I want to understand is what this practice presupposes philosophically. Does discounting future welfare in this way presuppose some substantive moral view about the standing of future people — that their welfare counts for less, that obligations weaken with temporal distance, or that, while we would never discount the welfare of contemporaries merely because they live on another continent, futurity is somehow different? The growth term, at least, looks like it can be given a straightforwardly utilitarian justification through the diminishing marginal utility of consumption, whereas the pure time preference term looks as though it cannot be given any justification at all without smuggling in something like temporal egoism applied collectively. So is the practice implicitly an unstable mixture — utilitarianism for the growth component plus an ethically indefensible discount for futurity — or is it more coherent than it first appears? Could, say, a contractarian who denies that future people can be parties to any bargain, or a sufficientarian who thinks marginal benefits to the very wealthy barely register, accommodate the practice just as well? Put differently: I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory, then tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several moral theories would license roughly the same calculations.

There's a final wrinkle that puzzles me most, and I'd appreciate your thoughts on it specifically. The same governments that discount future benefits in cost-benefit analysis also make binding climate commitments, write environmental protections into constitutions, and design nuclear waste repositories whose entire purpose is the welfare of people thousands of years out — and in some jurisdictions regulators quietly switch to lower, declining discount rates when the time horizon gets long enough, which suggests they already sense a problem. Does the practice of constant-rate discounting presuppose a view that these other practices contradict, or can the inconsistency be resolved — say, by distinguishing what cost-benefit analysis is strictly presupposing from what it merely fits comfortably with? And concretely: if a government accepted the claim that pure time preference is ethically indefensible and set that term to zero, what would actually have to change in regulatory methodology — just the number in the spreadsheet, or the entire framework for deciding whether a project is worth doing?
