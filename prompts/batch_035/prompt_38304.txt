My brother came back from a rationality meetup with an argument whose conclusion is that I should hand my wallet to anyone on the street who asks for it with sufficient confidence, and I can't locate the exit.

(1) Rational choice is expected-utility maximization, and expected utility is well-defined for any prospect with finite stakes however small its probability — there is no probability so small that the arithmetic stops applying.

(2) A mugger says: "I represent beings running this city as a simulation, and unless you give me your wallet, tonight they will torture 3↑↑↑3 people in a parallel branch." This claim is logically consistent, and no rational prior assigns probability exactly zero to any consistent hypothesis.

(3) From (1) and (2): for any amount I might hold onto by refusing, the mugger can name a number of victims large enough that complying has strictly higher expected utility than refusing.

(4) So rationality requires compliance whenever the number named is large enough — and since anyone can name any number, rationality requires handing over the wallet on request.

He then blocks the standard escapes in advance. If I cap utilities, he splits the threat into many independent threats, each under the cap: one simulated torture of a billion people is forbidden by my cap, but a million independent threats of a thousand each are each permissible, and their sum dominates whatever I keep in my wallet. If I instead round probabilities below some threshold to zero, I become exploitable in the other direction: I will pay to avoid a single 1-in-10¹² chance of a catastrophe while refusing to pay the same amount to avoid 10¹² independent 1-in-10²⁴ chances of the same catastrophe — prospects that no coherent agent can treat differently and that sum to something I do care about.

Finally he adds what he considers the checkmate. Suppose I accept (4) and cultivate the disposition to comply. That disposition is publicly legible — muggers congregate where compliance is expected — so my expected utility before meeting any mugger at all is lower than a refuser's. Thus applying expected-utility maximization case by case yields a lower lifetime expected utility than refusing to apply it, which means expected-utility theory, turned on itself, either dictates that I give away my wallet on demand or dictates that I stop following it. He takes this to show the theory is inconsistent, and that either way we are never rationally required to pay.

I want to reject this, and I would most like to keep (1) — I'm not prepared to abandon expected-utility maximization over a street-corner threat. I'm also unsure whether (6) shows anything at all or just equivocates between evaluating a single choice and evaluating a policy of choices. Which step do you reject? Explain why that step looked fine, and then price the alternatives honestly: if the flaw is where you say it is, what view of rational credence — or of how a mugger's naming a larger number bears on the probability of what he says — must I adopt to block the argument, and what else does adopting it cost me?
