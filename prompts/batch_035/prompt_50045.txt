My roommate and I have been keeping a spreadsheet for a couple months rating our food choices by gut feel, and I've noticed my own verdicts are all over the place, so I want to figure out what principle (if any) is actually behind them. Roughly where I land: I think someone switching from chicken to beef is making a genuine improvement — the cow suffers more as an individual, I guess, but you're trading hundreds of meals-with-a-victim for a couple dozen. Eating store-bought eggs feels nearly as bad as eating chicken meat, but eggs from my neighbor's genuinely pampered backyard hens feel fine to me. Eating a hunted deer is clearly better than the same weight of any farmed meat, since the deer actually got a real life. Eating shrimp bothers me way less than eating chicken, even though the numbers per meal are way bigger for shrimp — they're tiny and dumb, and I can't make myself care the same way. Oysters are basically fine, like eating plants. And bread/pasta feels like a zero issue: I know combines flatten field mice and pesticides kill enormous numbers of insects, and I've seen estimates that per-calorie collateral harm from grain is nontrivial, but it just doesn't register for me the way slaughter does — which my roommate says is motivated reasoning, since I like bread.

So: is there one principle that captures all these verdicts? My best attempt so far is something like "sentience-weighted suffering, but only for deaths I'm paying someone to cause on purpose" — deliberate slaughter counts, collateral damage in fields doesn't. But that seems to have consequences I'm not sure I can live with, and I want you to dig into them rather than just cheer for my formula. My uncle raises a dozen hens that honestly live great lives and kills them painlessly when they're old; by a life-quality version of the principle, his eating his own birds should come out better than my buying oysters, and my gut is embarassed to say that out loud. And if crop deaths genuinely don't count, should I feel fine about conventional beef, given how much grain goes into fattening a cow anyway — or is that the place my bread verdict finally has to go?

What I want from you: state the principle (or a couple of candidate principles) precisely, tie each clause to a specific verdict of mine, tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot save, and say what it predicts on a case I haven't listed — for example, someone who eats only farmed insects, or a lacto-vegetarian whose cheese comes from cows whose calves all go to veal. If the simplest principle forces me to drop one of my verdicts, tell me which one and why, because right now I honestly can't tell whether I have moral views here or just food preferences with moral paint on.
