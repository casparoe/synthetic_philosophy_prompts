I am a psychiatrist who sits on the standing workgroup that revises our country's diagnostic manual. Every cycle we receive a few dozen proposals to add, rename, or delete categories, and between plenary sessions a subcommittee of three must dispose of each proposal in about half an hour, producing a one-page rationale that the full plenary — which includes several people who consider Szasz a crank — will have to sign off on. The stakes are what they always are here: what we call a disorder determines what insurers reimburse, what courts treat as mitigating, what schools accommodate, and what ordinary people come to believe is wrong with them.

The working distinction the subcommittee actually reaches for — does this category name a disease or a disapproved way of living? — comes, at second hand, from Thomas Szasz, who argued in The Myth of Mental Illness (1961) that medicine deals in literal, bodily disease, where a lesion or measurable pathophysiology could in principle be found, whereas psychiatric categories typically label patterns of conduct and feeling that an audience judges: what he called problems in living, clothed in the language of illness. I find Szasz more persuasive than my colleagues do, but I want his distinctions operationalized rather than endlessly relitigated.

Please write a decision procedure the subcommittee can actually run on any proposal that crosses our desk. It should pose its questions in a fixed order, for instance: What finding, sign, or test would show the condition in a body, and what follows when the honest answer is none? Is the category defined by conduct some audience disapproves of, so that calling it a disease converts a moral verdict into a medical fact? Can the diagnosis be applied without invoking a norm, and if not, whose norm is being enforced and by what authority? Would the diagnosis still be issued if the person consented to the conduct — which, in Szasz's terms, is the difference between having a sickness and being assigned the sick role? For each question, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a no (or with a yes that means: this is not a medical judgment, and the proposal must be reframed as an open question of policy or criminal law for someone else to decide), and where it hands the decision to a person because what remains is a question of values that no procedure can settle. Keep the distinctions that matter — literal versus metaphorical disease, sickness versus sick role, diagnosis as a signed social act with legal consequences — while cutting whatever else in Szasz is unnecessary for people who have never read him. And be honest about what the procedure cannot do, including the limits I should expect if I deploy it on our messier proposals.

Then run it on three: a proposal to add gambling disorder as a full diagnosis; a proposal to add internet gaming disorder, defined by impaired control over play and continuation despite evident harm; and a proposal to leave major depressive disorder exactly where it is. I expect the third to be the hard one. I am not a full Szaszian: I believe depression is treatable, that calling it an illness does work I do not want undone, and that patients are better off inside the sick role than outside it. So if the procedure, applied consistently, yields a verdict on depression that I will not like, do not soften it to spare me — show precisely where it does or does not come out differently from the gaming case, and tell me at which step any difference has to be manufactured by judgment rather than delivered by the procedure.
