I teach an upper-level seminar on moral responsibility — fourteen students, one 80-minute session, scheduled early enough in the term that we have not yet reached the unit on moral luck; the students will have met the two drivers in passing, but nothing systematic, and I want the position named only in the debrief. Design an in-class game in which they generate instances of the problem themselves and commit to judgments about those instances in writing, so that the debrief can confront them with their own records rather than with my assertions. The game must run on paper, index cards, a timer, and a single die — no software, no real money.

My working design, which I invite you to improve or replace if you see a better shape: students are paired as Agent and Auditor. Each Agent allocates a fixed endowment across three named projects under time pressure, on the basis of a written brief. But each Agent also draws a hidden circumstance card from me that silently changes what the allocation does — one card makes a project live, one makes it dead, one doubles the stakes of any misallocation — so two Agents can make numerically identical allocations and produce very different worlds. A few Agents are additionally dealt a handicap at random (half the decision time, or a brief with a page missing), and the outcome of every allocation is finally passed through a die I roll behind the desk. The Auditor, who sees none of the cards, the handicap, or the die, grades the Agent's conduct on a numbered Judgment Ledger at three checkpoints: before outcomes are known, after outcomes are known, and after the hidden factors are revealed. The ledger is the point. I want the debrief to show the class the drift in their own numbers — whether and how identical conduct gets regraded once outcome and circumstance are in — rather than argue about that drift in the abstract. If you can build in a closing turn in which the Auditors' own grading is then graded by the rest of the class as if it were a pure exercise of judgment, while I reveal that their scoring rubrics were themselves randomly corrupted at the start, so much the better; I want that regress felt, not described.

What I need from you, then: complete rules (dealing order, timing, the exact text of the cards, the grading scale), a scoring scheme that keeps Auditors engaged and honest without cash, a minute-by-minute run sheet, and a debrief that maps each mechanic onto the relevant kind of luck — resultant, circumstantial, constitutive, causal — treating the collected ledgers as data to be read aloud or projected. Tell me plainly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be equally plain about which feature of moral luck no classroom game can reproduce and that I will have to supply by lecture.

On format: a short prose rationale for the design may come first, but keep it under a page. Then give me the run sheet as a table with columns [minutes, segment, what participants do, what the facilitator does, what can go wrong here]. Close with a single JSON object having two fields: "mapping", an array of objects each with the string fields "mechanic" (the game element), "luck_type" (which of the four kinds it stages), and "ledger_evidence" (what pattern in the ledgers would demonstrate the drift); and "unreproducible_feature", a string stating what the game cannot do and how I should cover it in the lecture that follows.
