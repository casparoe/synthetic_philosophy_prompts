I am a novelist working on a collection of long stories, each anchored to a different scientific or philosophical dispute, and my current piece concerns the question of how consciousness might be distributed across the animal kingdom — whether it comes in degrees, whether it has sharp boundaries, and what follows for our obligations when we cannot tell where the boundaries fall. Rather than dramatizing this through debate, I want to stage it inside a character's working life, and I would like your help drafting the story itself.

Here is the setting I have in mind. A mid-sized agricultural research institute has been ordered by its accrediting body to implement a graduated welfare protocol: species are assigned to tiers based on evidence of sentience, and the tiers determine everything from required anesthesia to permissible killing methods. My protagonist is the institute's newly hired welfare compliance officer, an ex-veterinary technician who is conscientious to a fault and initially relieved to find that the tier assignments feel obvious for most animals. The trouble begins when she has to apply the protocol to species in the ambiguous middle of the vertebrate–invertebrate divide — fish whose nociception is well documented but whose pain is contested, crabs and lobsters recently granted some recognition in law, cephalopods whose distributed nervous systems seem to scramble the mammalian assumptions built into the assessment checklists, and a swarming colony of bees that does not fit the protocol's unit-of-consciousness framing at all. Each tier decision she makes quietly reallocates suffering, and she starts to suspect that the tidy tier list is less a discovery about animals' minds than a bureaucratic artifact — a machine for converting an unresolved question into a form that can be signed off on.

Please write the story (roughly 1,500–2,500 words is fine, though length is your call) with the following emphases. First, keep the philosophical stakes concrete and procedural: show her reasoning through actual assessment decisions rather than delivering lectures. Second, at some point have her confront the possibility that the tier system's precision is spurious — that it functions more to relieve human discomfort than to track the truth about animal minds — and let this raise, without resolving, the question of whether consciousness is a single underlying capacity admitting of degrees or a cluster of loosely related capacities between which the very question of "how much" may be ill-formed. Third, I would like an ending that resists both easy despair and easy reassurance — perhaps a decision she makes that is defensible but that she knows rests on a guess. Avoid making the scientists villains; I want the story to implicate the reader gently, through the accumulating smallness of the choices. You may add scenes and secondary characters (a pragmatic lab director, a philosopher consultant hired to defend the tier scheme, a trainee who asks the naïve but piercing questions) as you see fit.
