I'm a junior taking an elective called Ethics and Public Life, and for our unit on disagreement our teacher introduced an idea I can't stop thinking about: she said that when two people or two groups have deeply different moral values, the situation can be like trade between countries with different resources — each side gives up something it values less in exchange for the other side giving up something the first side values more, and the resulting compromise can be better by the lights of everyone involved, not just a grudging second-best. She called these "gains from trade through moral compromise," and the example that stuck with me was a dispute between a town that wants to protect an old forest for its own sake and a logging company that wants the timber, where the final deal (protecting most of the valley in exchange for selective harvesting rights elsewhere) was something no one originally wanted but arguably everyone had reason to prefer over fighting it out.

I've been trying to write up where I stand for my final paper, and I've ended up with a set of five commitments that I thought were all fine when I wrote them down separately, but now I'm not so sure they fit together: (1) I believe there are objective moral truths — some things really are wrong, full stop, whatever anyone thinks; (2) I believe that in deep moral disagreements, the morally best outcome is very often a genuine compromise rather than either side winning outright, precisely because of those gains from trade; (3) I believe you only owe a compromise to people who might, for all you know, be right — you don't owe one to someone you're certain is simply wrong; (4) I believe that splitting the difference with someone who is simply wrong is itself a moral failure, since if only one of two positions is correct, the midpoint isn't automatically better than the correct one — it's just partially wrong; and (5) I believe that the clearest cases of moral progress in history, like the abolitionists, were people who refused to compromise and were right to refuse.

My question is which of these actually hang together. Which pairs are in genuine tension, which only look like they conflict but come apart under inspection, and what is the cheapest revision that would restore consistency — plus, what following through on that fix would cost me elsewhere in the set? For instance, I can imagine someone saying (2) and (4) flatly contradict each other, but I can also imagine a way of reading (2) on which compromise is only best when both sides are partly right, and I don't know whether that reading saves me or quietly sabotages (3) and (5). I'd rather not smuggle in a hidden assumption, so please go pair by pair, rank the tensions from most to least severe, and be specific about which commitment has to bend and what I lose if it does — don't just tell me they're all "in dialectical tension." I have to present this in class, so I need to know where the weakest joint is before my classmates find it for me.
