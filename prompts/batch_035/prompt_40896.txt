I'm a PhD student in epistemology, and I'd like you to take on the role of an expert epistemologist and adjudicate a dispute that broke out in my reading group last week. The topic was pragmatic encroachment: whether how much is at stake, practically, can affect whether someone knows.

The case we were discussing involves a radiologist who reads a head CT and believes, on a fixed body of evidence, that there is a small hemorrhage. In the emergency room, where acting on that belief means administering a drug that can cause fatal bleeding if the diagnosis is wrong, she reports: I don't know whether it's a bleed. In a routine follow-up read weeks later, with no treatment hanging on it, she says she knew it was a bleed all along. Same evidence, same belief, same person.

Dana said the stakes plainly changed what she knows: when more is at risk, more evidence is needed for knowledge, so the emergency-room report is true and the follow-up report is true too — knowledge itself shifted. Marcus replied that this confuses knowledge with decision-worthiness: her epistemic position is fixed by her evidence and the fact; what the stakes change is the standard of care for action, which is a practical standard, not an epistemic standing. Dana countered that if knowledge is the norm of practical reason — you may act on p only if you know p — there is no room for that split: the very same evidence licenses the treatment in the routine setting but not in the emergency, so knowledge itself must have moved; otherwise the norm cannot do its work. Marcus offered a fallback: even granting the knowledge norm, he could locate the stakes-sensitivity in the justificatory threshold for action — act on p only if one's justified credence clears a line that rises with the stakes — leaving knowledge itself untouched. Dana answered that this makes knowledge idle in practical life, and that Marcus still owes an account of why "I don't know" sounds true in the emergency and false in the follow-up; she claimed contextualism — the standards for "knows" vary with the stakes of the conversational context — explains that datum in one step. Marcus closed by arguing that contextualism overgenerates: the stakes of a decision can be high while the conversation remains casual, and the malpractice lawyer evaluating the case a year later, under low conversational stakes, seems to be assessing the very same knowledge claim — something a story about the semantics of "knows" can't easily accommodate.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move, rating each of the steps from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying each rating. Then address two further questions: does the dispute ultimately turn on the knowledge norm of action, and what, if anything, are both parties missing? Think the moves through carefully before writing, and feel free to begin with some brief brainstorming. Answer in American English.
