I'm a final-year PPE student, currently taking a module on twentieth-century debates in political economy, and I sit as the student representative on my faculty's Research Computing Committee. Our faculty runs a shared GPU cluster that is chronically oversubscribed. At present a subcommittee allocates compute quarterly on the basis of written proposals, in which researchers state what they need and why; the subcommittee can rarely check the claims, everyone has learned to pad their requests, urgent jobs sit behind the whole quarter's plan, and reserved capacity goes idle when projects slip. A working group has now proposed replacing this with an internal credit system: each research group receives a termly budget of compute credits, jobs are debited at a posted rate per GPU-hour, and the rate is adjusted each term until the queue clears — with an open question about whether groups should be allowed to trade credits with one another. Before the Michaelmas round of allocations, the committee must choose between the credit system, the status quo committee allocation, and a hybrid, under which credits would operate alongside a committee-held reserve for exploratory work by junior researchers.

I have been asked to draft the decision memo we will circulate ahead of the vote, and I want it to be informed by the socialist calculation debate we have just covered in my module: Mises's argument that rational allocation is impossible without genuine market prices, Hayek's knowledge problem (the dispersed, tacit knowledge a central body cannot collect — which sounds a lot like our padded proposals), and Lange's reply that a planning board can find prices by trial and error, adjusting posted rates until demand meets supply. The memo should say whether and how these arguments apply at our scale — a faculty cluster rather than a national economy — and should not assume they transfer unchanged. One of our members insists that, because researchers can always compare our internal rate with commercial cloud prices, the whole problem dissolves; the memo should address that objection rather than pass over it. It should also confront the distributive question the classic debate mostly brackets: credit budgets would presumably reflect group size or past usage rather than scientific merit, and I would like an honest discussion of whether the market mechanism imports unfairnesses of its own that the proposal-based system, whatever its failures, at least pretended to correct.

Please draft the memo — roughly 800 to 1,000 words — with a clear recommendation the committee could actually vote on, the reasons for it, and a serious treatment of the strongest argument for the losing option. Begin your response with the memo itself, with no preamble or commentary before it, and open the memo with its recommendation in the first sentence. Please write in British English throughout.
