I'm in a study group for a course on the philosophy of infinity, and a friend has revived one of Zeno's arguments in a form I can't dismiss and can't accept. His version runs as follows. (1) Any process that traverses a stretch of space or time can be decomposed into an infinite sequence of sub-processes: to walk from the doorway to the wall, I must first cross half the distance, then half of what remains, then half of that, and so on — a series with no last member. (2) Completing a collection of tasks requires there being a final task, since completing the whole just is doing the last thing there is to do; a collection with no last member therefore cannot be completed, no matter how small its members get. (3) The Zeno series has no last member. (4) So the walk is never completed, and by the same reasoning neither is any other traversal. (5) The argument generalizes far beyond Achilles and the tortoise: a kettle heating from 20 degrees to 100 degrees must pass through every temperature in between — infinitely many states, none of which is the last before boiling — so on my friend's principle the kettle never boils; and pronouncing even a single vowel requires the tongue to pass through infinitely many intermediate positions on the way to its target, none of them final, so my friend can never finish asserting his own conclusion. He takes this self-undermining spread as a feature, not a bug: it shows, he says, that we've never had a coherent account of how anything finishes.

I tried the obvious escape, and he had a rejoinder ready. I said that (2) is simply false, since infinite collections of tasks can be completed when the times involved shrink geometrically — the sub-journeys of the walk occupy intervals whose durations sum to a finite total, so the mathematics of convergent series dissolves the mystery. He conceded all the arithmetic but insisted it answers a different question: he never claimed the walker runs out of time, only that there is no last sub-journey to constitute finishing, and limits describe where the positions approach, not an act by which the series gets done. Then he produced a case where the "shrinking intervals" maneuver isn't even available, because the tasks are genuinely discrete: a lamp is switched on after one minute, off half a minute later, on a quarter-minute after that, and so on, with each switching a distinct, countable event; after two minutes, infinitely many switchings are allegedly complete. If infinite task-collections can be completed, this one was — but is the lamp on or off at the two-minute mark? The setup seems to leave it indeterminate, and my friend argues that an event-sequence with no determinate upshot cannot have been completed at all, which he takes to reinstate (2). I confess I don't know how to answer him, and I notice the two horns each cost something: keep (2) and nothing ever finishes, including his own argument; reject it and I owe him a story about the lamp.

Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? Please tell me which numbered step to reject, or, if the flaw is subtler than any single premise, exactly where the reasoning equivocates — I suspect the words "task" and "complete" are doing different work in (1), (2), and the lamp case, but I can't pin it down. For each candidate diagnosis, explain why the rejected premise or inference looked fine on its face, and what accepting the alternative costs, including whether the cost is borne by ordinary motion, by the mathematical treatment of continuous processes, or only by exotic cases like the lamp. I'd also like you to address his charge against my escape directly: does the convergent-series response answer the "no last task" worry, relocate it, or change the subject? And is the lamp really a counterexample to the completability of infinite task-collections, or does it show something weaker about how such a setup must be described? I'd prefer, if it can be had, a position on which supertasks are coherent in principle, but I want to know what that preference commits me to. Please answer in American English.
