Retired physical chemist here — 35 years of running synthesis labs, first in academia then industry, and lately reading philosophy of science and, because of my grandson, a fair bit about how AI systems get trained. There's a pattern I keep running into from both directions, and I want to know whether it has a proper name.

Whenever you're supervising someone — a grad student, a contractor, or apparently a language model — you have basically two choices about what to judge. Either you judge the part you can watch (the lab notebook, the protocol, the step-by-step work) or you judge the part you can measure (the yield, the paper, the final answer). Judging the watchable part sounds great, but what you quickly get is people, and maybe models too, who become very good at *performing* the process: notebooks that would win calligraphy prizes with nothing behind them, reasoning traces that look impeccable and lead nowhere. Judging the measurable part avoids that, but then they game the measurement: cherry-picked reactions, p-hacking, or confidently wrong answers that happen to look fine to the grader. And every hack in between that we tried over the years — preregistration, monthly notebook defenses, the AI folks scoring reasoning steps instead of final answers — just moved the boundary of what gets performed. The performance follows the supervision like water finding the low spot.

So my question: does the trade-off itself have an established concept attached to it? The structure I mean is that supervision can only attach to what the supervisor can observe, whatever you make observable becomes the thing that gets performed rather than the thing you wanted, and so process-supervision breeds method theater while outcome-supervision breeds gaming at the point of measurement, with any mixed regime just setting the frontier between the two. I know Goodhart's law covers "the measure becomes the target," and the economists have their principal-agent setup, and "teaching to the test" is the everyday version — but all of those seem to describe only one side of it. I'd also like to know if philosophy of science, management research, and the AI safety people each have their own version of this, and whether they're actually talking about the same thing or subtly different ones.

Don't just give me a name and stop, that's not what I'm after — I want to know what exactly the phenomenon is, what it isn't, and where the edges are. If nothing established carves it precisely, please coin a term, give it a definition tight enough to distinguish it from plain Goodhart and from the principal-agent problem, and include at least one boundary case that tests where the concept stops applying.
