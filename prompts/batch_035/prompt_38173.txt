My closest friend is a set theorist, and we spent most of last night arguing about actual infinities. She is a thoroughgoing Cantorian; I keep balking at the physical cases; neither of us moved the other. I came away with a page of my own verdicts and no principle holding them together, so let me lay out the verdicts and ask what principle, if any, captures them, what it predicts on cases I have not considered, and which of the verdicts has to go.

First, Hilbert's hotel: fine. Infinitely many rooms, all occupied; a new guest arrives, everyone shifts down one room, and everyone is housed. Strange, but nothing in the story struck me as incoherent. Second, the checkouts: also fine, but it poisons the word "full." If the guests in the even-numbered rooms all check out, infinitely many remain; if the rest then check out, none remain. The same subtraction yields any answer you like, which leaves me doubting that "the hotel was full" ever meant anything. Third, Thomson's lamp: not fine. A lamp switched on and off at times converging on one minute from now, and the question whether it is on or off at the limit; I want to say the question has no answer, and I take that to show that infinitely many discrete acts cannot be completed within a finite interval. Fourth, the infinite past: fine. The universe could have existed forever, every event finitely distant and no first event. She pressed the Tristram Shandy case on me — a historian who has been writing for eternity, one page per day about one earlier day, matched so that by now every past day has been described — and I said fine, though I cannot say why it does not sting the way the lamp does. Fifth, the fair lottery over the natural numbers: not fine. Infinitely many tickets, each equally likely to win; I cannot make sense of that, whatever the hotel says. Sixth, the reals: fine, as mathematics. I accept Cantor's diagonal argument that they are uncountable, and I accept that they form an actually infinite totality.

Two of these I cannot reconcile, and they are the ones we argued about longest. Turn the lamp around in time: it has been switching at times converging on the present from the past, and I am asked whether it is on right now. She says that if I accept the infinite past, I must accept the setup, and that its present state is simply undefined. I want to say the lamp is possible, since I accepted the eternal past, and I refuse to say that a lamp sitting in front of me has no determinate state. One of those verdicts has to go, and I keep going back and forth on which.

My tentative principle, offered for testing and repair, is this: actual infinities are admissible in mathematics and in unbounded processes, but infinitely many discrete acts may not be compressed into a finite interval. It handles the lamp and the eternal past, but I can already see two problems. It says nothing at all about the lottery, and she observed that if compression is impossible, no one could ever finish a footrace, since finishing is completing infinitely many sub-distances in a finite time — a result I do not accept. So, please: is there a principle, or a small set of candidates, that captures these verdicts? State it precisely, with the case that motivates each clause. What does it predict for the lamp turned around, for the lottery, and for a case I have not listed — say, an infinite stack of turtles, each half the height of the one below it, reaching exactly two meters? And which of my verdicts does the best principle have to overturn? I would accept being told that my position amounts to Cantorianism about mathematics and finitism about the physical world, but only if you can also tell me whether that combination is stable or merely the hotel verdict trying to keep a foot in each camp.
