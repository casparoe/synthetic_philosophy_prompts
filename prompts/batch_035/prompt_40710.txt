I run behavior evaluations for LLMs at a mid-sized AI company -- not cutting-edge research, more like QA-for-behavior: every week or two someone files a ticket because the model said something like "I'm refusing because that request could enable self-harm" or "I double-checked my arithmetic and the answer is right," and they want to know whether that's what actually happened inside the model or whether it just said a plausible thing. Recently I got re-reading Dennett (leftover interest from a college course ages ago) and his idea of free-floating rationales stuck with me: the reason tree leaves are broad is light capture, but no tree represents that reason anywhere; the reason exists at the level of design, not in anybody's head. It made me realize my tickets actually have three possible verdicts and I can't currently distinguish them: (a) the stated reason genuinely caused the behavior, (b) the stated reason is confabulation -- a story generated after the fact about behavior whose real causes are elsewhere, and (c) the weird middle case where the explanation is actually true about why the model works, but true in the free-floating sense, an account of why the behavior got selected rather than of any computation happening at inference time -- like when a model says it avoids giving medical dosages because errors there are unusually costly, and that's a correct story about its training without being something the model consults or represents.

What I need is a procedure my team (three people, decent at running experiments, no interpretability background, roughly one GPU-day of budget per ticket) can actually run: an ordered list of tests on a transcript, where each test has a clear pass/fail condition and the results branch toward one of the three verdicts or toward "can't tell, hand it to someone who knows the training process." Our safety lead has to sign off on findings and is openly skeptical of anything that smells like philosophy, so the procedure has to look like engineering even when it's secretly epistemology. I can definately imagine some candidate tests -- corrupt the reasoning text and see whether the answer changes, paraphrase the stated reason and see whether behavior shifts, re-ask in a context where the stated reason wouldn't apply -- but I don't know what order to run them in, which results dominate which, or how to handle the fact that all of these tests are themselves model calls whose outputs need interpreting, which seems like it might regress forever. Please also be blunt about where the procedure simply can't deliver, especially for the (c) verdict, since my suspicion is that free-floating rationales are exactly the kind of thing black-box tests can never confirm, and I'd rather know that up front than discover it mid-meeting.

Then run it on three tickets we actually got: a model that says it "rechecked the work" and then confirms the user's incorrect algebra, where the recheck text looks like genuine verification; a model that gives a correct step-by-step solution to a competition problem where every step is separately checkable; and a model that explains its own limitation with what sounds like real insight into its architecture (something like: it drifts in long documents because attention to early context decays) -- product wants to put that explanation in our user-facing docs, and they're going to be annoyed if we can't certify it. I suspect the procedure's verdict on that third one is exactly where it has to hand back an answer nobody wants, and I want to see how it does that gracefully. For each verdict, tell me what test result would have flipped it to a different one, and point at the step in the overall procedure where you think we're most likely to fool ourselves.
