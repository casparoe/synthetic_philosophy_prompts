I write about science and technology for a general-interest magazine, and I'm preparing a long essay on what automation does to the meaning of work. Before I send it to my editor, I want to make sure the views I'm about to defend actually form a coherent position rather than a stack of individually plausible sentiments. Here is the position, as I would state it in print:

(1) The empirical core: paid work does more for people than pay does. The harm of unemployment runs well beyond the lost wages — what the unemployed lose is time structure, regular activity, social contact, status, and the sense of being collectively useful, roughly the latent functions of employment that Marie Jahoda identified. On this basis I treat employment as the principal carrier of meaning and psychological order in most people's lives, and I'm skeptical of any proposal premised on work being dispensable.

(2) The fallacy: the lump of labor is a fallacy, and every generation's automation panic has been wrong so far. Machines displace tasks, not demand for human effort, and the economy has always found new work for the displaced. So policy should neither slow automation nor tax it in order to preserve particular jobs, and I have no patience for automation-limiting protectionism dressed up as a jobs program.

(3) The hedge: nonetheless, I think general-purpose machine labor is different in kind from mechanizing a list of tasks, and I endorse introducing an unconditional basic income before the disruption arrives rather than after — as insurance, not as a response to a crisis already in progress.

(4) The no-make-work rule: I oppose job guarantees and subsidies for human labor in any domain where machines could deliver the same output more cheaply. A job whose output no one would miss is worthless however busy it keeps its holder, because the meaning of work is partly constituted by the work being genuinely demanded by someone.

(5) The migration thesis: if paid work does shrink, the meaning it carried can migrate — into caregiving, craft, civic participation, and art. The coming problem, on my account, is institutional (how to fund, recognize, and confer status on unpaid activity) rather than anthropological (some fixed human need for employment that nothing else can serve).

(6) The distributional addendum: access to whatever meaningful work remains should be treated as a matter of justice and shared out — through shorter working weeks, career sharing, and deliberate spreading of the demanding, recognized positions — rather than left to market allocation, while income is handled by the basic income.

Before I commit all this to print, I'd like a rigorous consistency audit. Which of these commitments are genuinely in tension with each other, and which only appear to be? Please rank the real tensions by severity rather than presenting them all as equally damning, say for each one what exactly would have to give and what I'd lose by giving it, and tell me which single revision would restore the most coherence to the package. I'm also happy to be told that a tension I've been quietly worrying about dissolves on inspection — I just want to know which worries are real before the fact-checkers and readers find them for me.
