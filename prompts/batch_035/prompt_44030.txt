I am a second-year PhD student in neuroscience with a confession I have so far made only to my notebook: over the past year I have drifted into panpsychism. It began with my migraine auras — watching a scotoma devour half my visual field while the intact half carried on, serenely convinced it was seeing everything, convinced me that experience comes in degrees and that unity is something to be explained rather than assumed. I now hold, roughly, that consciousness is a fundamental feature of matter, and that my apparently single self is in some way a composite of micro-experiences. In three weeks I must defend this position at a departmental seminar, and the opponent will be my own supervisor, a functionalist who considers the combination problem decisive: if fundamental particles carry proto-experience, nothing in our physics explains how trillions of them sum into one unified headache, and a view that cannot even say how many minds are in the seminar room is not a view.

Before I face her, I need to know whether my position survives contact with the best version of her objection. Please write a dialogue, around 1,200 words, between us over coffee after a practice talk. The panpsychist (me) should not merely assert that combination is a mystery but accept that physics is closed; she should deploy the serious replies available — that experiential unity may be a matter of structural matching between micro-experiences rather than bare summation, that the alternative theories face parallel explanatory debts with respect to how neural dynamics generate experience, and that the so-called palette problem (explaining how simple experiential qualities yield the rich palette of my headache) may be no harder for her than the zombie-like conceivability of absent experience is for her opponent. The functionalist should press hard: that "structural matching" borrows all its structure from physics while leaving experience's contribution opaque, that positing subjects everywhere does nothing until one says what binds them, and that a fundamental property with no dynamics to constrain it starts to look epiphenomenal in the wrong way. Both characters should be equally competent, each conceding a genuine point when the other lands one, and the dialogue should end unresolved but with the exact fault line between them made precise, so that I know what I actually have to defend in three weeks. Do not have either of them strawman the other, and do not let the panpsychist retreat into vagueness about "emergence" — she should know that word is what got her into this.
