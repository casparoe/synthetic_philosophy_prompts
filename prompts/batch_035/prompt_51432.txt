So I've ended up, after a few years of reading mostly 19th and 20th century history, holding a pretty strong position: that historical explanation is constitutively corrupted by hindsight, and the narrative fallacy isn't a minor bug careful historians can wash out but built into the genre itself. Once you know how things turned out, you can't help selecting the causes that "lead" there — the assassination at Sarajevo, the structural rot of the late Soviet system — and quietly discarding all the ones that looked just as salient at the time but happened to fizzle. What I've started to suspect, and this is the bit I'm least sure about, is that the professional correctives make it worse rather than better. The counterfactualists who ask what if Napoleon had won at Waterloo seem to me to just run the same teleology in reverse, and the "contingency" school — the people who insist everything could have gone otherwise — are still narrating from the endpoint, only with the sign flipped. I'd even extend this to biography: explaining a person's life by their later achievements is just hindsight about a single individual, and every "the young Churchill already showed" chapter is doing the same thing as "the Treaty of Versailles made WWII inevitable."

What I want to know is what actually counts against this. Not the standard reply of "historians are aware of hindsight bias, see also the narrative fallacy literature" — awareness of a bias doesn't remove it, that's the whole point of the bias. I want the defeaters, ranked from most to least decisive. For instance: if a historian writing in real time, before the outcome, identified the causes that later accounts settled on, does that show the post hoc narrating is tracking something real rather than projecting? What about the forecasting tournaments people like Tetlock ran — if historical-style analysis does better than chance at predicting political outcomes in advance, is that evidence against my view, or is prediction and explanation seperable enough that it cuts no ice either way? Is there any observation that could occur that would make me abandon this, or has the position been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if it has, is that fatal for it, or is it the kind of claim (like maybe "all observation is theory-laden") where unfalsifiability is survivable? I'd also like an honest take on the pedagogical tail of my view: if narrative is constitutively corrupt, is there anything left for historians to do, or does the view just rebrand history as literature with footnotes, and would that rebranding itself be a consequence that counts against it? Please write the answer in American English, and be direct about which defeaters a reasonable person holding my view could just absorb versus which ones should genuinely move them.
