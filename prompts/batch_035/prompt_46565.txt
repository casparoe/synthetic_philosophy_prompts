A friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about whether equality is valuable for its own sake. She took the strong egalitarian line — that a society in which some are much worse off than others is flawed in itself, regardless of how the inequality came about — while I kept returning to Parfit's leveling-down objection: if equality is intrinsically good, then a change that brings the better-off down to the level of the worse-off, without improving anyone's life at all, would have something to be said for it, and I find that impossible to accept. That objection is what pushed me toward the priority view, on which benefits to the worse-off carry greater moral weight, but leveling down has no value whatsoever.

I'd like a poem of roughly five quatrains in which the form enacts the dispute rather than merely describing it. The first stanza should have lines of conspicuously unequal length, standing for the unequal distribution; each subsequent stanza should even the lines out a bit more, so that the final stanza is perfectly level — and hollow, expressing that nothing has been gained by the leveling. Somewhere in the middle I want a turn that gives the egalitarian her best reply, the thought that what she values is people standing in relations of equality to one another rather than any pattern in the distribution itself, so that the ending does not simply award me the victory. Someone who knows this debate should recognize both positions and feel the force of each; the level closing stanza should read at once as a defeat and, briefly, as a kind of peace.
