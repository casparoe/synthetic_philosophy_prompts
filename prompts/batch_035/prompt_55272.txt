I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas for a general audience, and I want to build an interactive quiz for our website: "which theory of perception do you actually hold?" The hook is all the noise lately around predictive processing and the free energy principle, and my suspision is that most readers (and honestly most science writers, me included) hold views about perception that don't hang together, and a decent quiz could expose that.

Here's what I want. Build the quiz as a decision tree, not a list of labels. Start from something almost everybody accepts -- that expectations shape perception somehow -- and each node should be a concrete case a reader can answer without having a clue which theory it's nudging them toward. For cases I'm imagining things like: you suddenly hear a garbled song lyric clearly the moment you read the actual words; the hollow-face thing where a concave mask stubbornly looks like a normal face; a radiologist who says she just sees the tumor in the noise; whether a frog snapping at a drifting dot has anything worth calling perception; the rubber hand illusion. Use those or better ones, your call, but each one has to genuinely split the positions, not just be fun. Aim for roughly eight branch questions total.

The leaves should be real positions: old-school unconscious inference in the Helmholtz tradition, garden-variety predictive processing, the stronger view where perception is basically the brain's best guess and we only ever encounter reality indirectly (what some people describe as a controlled hallucination), active inference where perception and action get folded into one thing, enactivist or sensorimotor views where perceiving is a skill of the whole organism, and the deflationary option where all the prediction talk is a helpful metaphor but not a literal description of the brain. If some combination of answers has no resident, tell me, and tell me whether that's because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just a vacancy nobody ever got around to filling.

One thing that really matters: I don't want to embarrass myself in print. Please keep straight what's settled versus what's genuinely fought over among people who actually know this stuff -- for instance whether the free energy principle makes falsifiable claims or is closer to a framework, and whether prediction is meant literally or loosely. Anywhere one of my node questions would smuggle a contested view in as if it were consensus, flag it, and tell me which pairs of leaves my tree honestly can't pull apart. Do this as a short closing note that the readers wouldn't see but I need before publishing.

On formatting, and I know this sounds fussy: give me continuous prose only, the way you'd write an email or a letter. No headings, no numbered lists, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. Just describe the tree, the questions, the scoring key, and the caveats in flowing paragraphs, and our developers will translate it into the interactive thing.

And since our style desk is pedantic about it, write the whole response in British English. Ta.
