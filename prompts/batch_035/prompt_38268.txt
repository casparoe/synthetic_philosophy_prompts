A woman I have known since graduate school spent fifteen years drafting the personal-liberty sections of country reports for a human-rights organization, and since retiring she has turned the habit into a dinner-table game: describe a person in a situation, and within seconds she will say whether that person is free — not free in this respect or that, just free or not free. Her record so far: a nurse in a country where nothing she might say, read, join, or pursue is restricted and no official has any power to restrict it — free; a dissident who wants to publish an essay and is stopped by a censor who seizes the manuscript and threatens prosecution — not free; a monk under lifelong vows of silence and enclosure who wants nothing the rule forbids and calls the rule his own — free, her ground being that the gate is unlocked; a woman whose letters are opened and copied by the secret police without her knowledge, though she cares deeply that her correspondence stay private — not free, her ground being that they can read whatever they like, whether or not they happen to bother; a wife whose husband has confiscated her passport to stop her visiting her mother — not free, and she counts a husband's power exactly as she counts a state's; a man who dreams of flying unaided, with no law and no person standing in his way — free, since the obstacle is his body, not unfreedom; and a voter whose party always loses in honestly counted elections — free, merely disappointed.

She is coming to dinner on Friday, and I have three cases written on index cards. First, a librarian with no contract protections: her employer may dismiss her at any time for any reason or none, but in eleven years has never done so or even hinted at it; she speaks her mind at staff meetings, takes leave whenever she likes, and says the possibility of dismissal never crosses her mind. Second, a citizen of a country that bars all its citizens from visiting one particular country and enforces the ban at the border; he has never wanted to go there, never speaks of it, and would decline a free ticket if offered one. Third, a woman raised in a closed order that assigns her work, dress, and company, where the elders can reassign or expel her at whim; she has never once wanted anything other than what she is assigned, says the life chose her, and has never needed to be corrected.

What I want from you is less the three answers than the reasoning around them. Will she call each of the three free or not free? Which rule is she implicitly running — non-interference, on which only actual prevention by other people counts; non-domination, on which what matters is that someone could prevent you, whether or not they ever do; doing as one pleases, on which what matters is that nothing one wants is being prevented by anyone; self-mastery; or something else? Several of these, as usually stated, will not fit her record, so part of your task is to say which candidates die on her verdicts and to state the survivors precisely enough that each yields a definite verdict on all three of my cases. Then give your best guess about what she will say, with your reasons for preferring it, and finally tell me the single further case — a fourth index card, if you like — that would best split the surviving candidates apart, along with what each of them predicts for it.
