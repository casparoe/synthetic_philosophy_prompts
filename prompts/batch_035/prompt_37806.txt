I've been reading a popular book on metaphysics for non-philosophers, and its chapter on objects and parts sums up the debate like this: 'The special composition question asks under what conditions several objects add up to a further object. There are two extreme answers. Nihilism says composition never occurs, so the only things that exist are fundamental particles. Universalism says composition always occurs, so there is an object scattered across your left shoe and a distant quasar. Nihilism is refuted by perception: we literally see tables, so it cannot be true that none exist. Universalism, by contrast, is unfalsifiable, and since no observation could ever settle whether the shoe-and-quasar object exists, believing in it is a leap of faith rather than science. The sensible middle position, defended by Peter van Inwagen, is organicism: composition occurs only within living things, so strictly speaking there are no tables or chairs — they are useful fictions, like the average taxpayer.'

I'm inclined to accept the middle position because it sounds like common sense, but the chapter's tone is suspiciously tidy, as if each side gets dispatched in a sentence. Could you go through the summary claim by claim and tell me which parts a metaphysician working on mereology would accept, which are simplifications a defender of the relevant view would accept with a wince, and which are just wrong? In particular, I'd like to know whether the appeal to perception really refutes nihilism, whether the unfalsifiability complaint is a fair way to assess universalism, and whether the chapter's characterisation of van Inwagen's position and his attitude towards ordinary talk about tables is accurate. Please answer in British English.
