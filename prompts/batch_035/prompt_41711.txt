I've spent the last fifteen-odd years as an environmental consultant, mostly writing pieces of environmental impact statements under NEPA and various state analogues, so the vocabulary I actually think in is stuff like no-action alternatives, baseline conditions, scoping, cumulative impacts, mitigation banking, significance thresholds. Lately I've been talking with a friend at an AI lab who works on what they call low-impact agents — roughly, reinforcement learning systems whose reward function gets an extra penalty term for changing the world too much, so the agent tries to get its reward while leaving everything else as close to "how it would've been anyway" as possible. She tells me there's a whole running debate about how to formalize that "how it would've been": whether you reset the counter factual before every single action or just once at the start, and whether you measure distance from it in terms of some hand-picked set of features, or in terms of how well the agent could still accomplish a big grab-bag of unrelated goals after the fact. And there's a lurking worry that you can't actually say what counts as an impact without smuggling in a full account of everything anyone could ever care about — which is the part I keep wanting to butt in on.

So here's the ask: translate that whole debate into my vocabulary, the EIA vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. What is their counterfactual baseline doing if not choosing a no-action alternative, and which of their options corresponds to which way of fixing one? What plays the role of scoping — who decides which variables even count as "the environment" to be left unchanged, and is that decision ever neutral? Is their penalty anything like our significance thresholds, and is there anything like mitigation banking or offsets, where an agent (or a developer) could "pay down" an impact rather than avoid it? What about cumulative effects — lots of individually small changes that nobody flagged because each fell below threshold?

Then give me the residue report, which honestly is the part I care about most. Which parts of the RL debate have no counterpart at all in how we do EIA, and when something does translate, what got distorted in the translation? And the big philosophical one — whether "side effect" can be defined without a full specification of value — restated in my terms: is there such a thing as a value-neutral baseline, and if there isn't, what exactly have we environmental folks been pretending all these years every time we write "existing conditions" into a document? I want your honest read on whether the residue points at something EIA has solved institutionally (by committees and lawsuits rather than math) that they're trying to solve with a loss function, or whether their problem is genuinely different from ours in a way my vocabulary can't express — and if it's the latter, tell me what my vocabulary is missing.

For calibration purposes: treat me as completely fluent in EIA practice and only passably literate in RL — you can use the concepts, just define them as you go like you would for a smart colleague from another agency.
