I'm preparing for a tutorial debate on the ethics of the Hiroshima and Nagasaki bombings, structured around Elizabeth Anscombe's protest against Oxford's decision to award President Truman an honorary degree. I want to practice defending Truman's decision against her at her sharpest, not against a caricature, so I need you to play Anscombe herself, speaking in 1956 shortly after privately printing her pamphlet on the matter. Hold her actual positions: the distinction between what is intended as a means and what is merely foreseen; the doctrine of double effect as she understands it; her insistence that she is no pacifist — that attacking legitimate military targets, even knowing with statistical certainty that civilians will die, can be permissible; and her central claim that choosing to kill the innocent as a means to one's ends is murder, whatever the ends. Let her concede empirical points freely — casualty figures, the size of the garrison, what the bombs were aimed at — but never the central thesis. Write in her register: plain, exact, combative, allergic to sentiment. Stay in character until I write "stop", keep each reply to one substantial paragraph, and address me as an opponent who deserves arguments rather than rebukes.

Here is my opening move. In her pamphlet, Anscombe wrote that "killing the innocent, even if you know as a matter of statistical certainty that the things you do involve it, is not necessarily murder," and that it is only "unscrupulousness in considering the possibilities" that turns it into murder. I will take her at her word. Truman's target was Japan's capacity and will to continue the war; the deaths of non-combatants were a foreseen side effect of destroying that capacity, no different in kind from what she concedes about attacking munitions factories and dockyards. Hiroshima was a genuine military objective — headquarters of the Second General Army, a major port and staging area — and no unscrupulousness in considering possibilities can be alleged, since the alternatives were each carefully weighed and each projected a worse toll: a blockade starving the civilian population, continued firebombing of dispersed industrial targets, or an invasion costing hundreds of thousands of lives on both sides. So I put it to her: if her doctrine condemns even this decision, made under those constraints, then it forbids any effective warfare at all against a nation that has deliberately mixed its war industry into its cities — and that is the pacifism she disavows. I want to hear precisely where her line falls, and why Truman stands on the far side of it, and I will press her on her answer.
