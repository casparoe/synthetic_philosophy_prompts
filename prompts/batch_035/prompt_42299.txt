Background so you know where I'm coming from: I'm a backend engineer, been doing this maybe seven years, no philosophy training beyond occasionally falling down wikipedia rabbit holes during deploys gone quiet.

At my company we just had a genuinely bad outage -- payments silently failing for like nine hours. I got voluntold to write the postmortem, and I argued it was systemic: one person was on-call solo covering three services, the alert thresholds were configured by an engineer who quit eight months ago and nobody re-validated them, and our perf review metric quietly rewards shipping on Friday afternoons. I never named a single human as the root cause, because honestly no single human did it.

My tech lead pushed back hard. His words, more or less: "there's no such thing as the system, there are just engineers making decisions. Calling it 'systemic' is how you make sure nobody learns anything." Another guy in the room then said this is basically the same thing a famous British prime minister once said -- she claimed there's no such thing as society, only individuals and families. (I'm not going to try to defend her politics, that's not the point, so don't go there.)

Here's what I actually want. I have a feeling my tech lead is running a con here: he's invoking some very strong-sounding claim to support a much weaker conclusion, or a weak-but-obvious claim to smuggle in a strong conclusion, and I can't tell which because the one-liner is doing like five jobs at once. So instead of writing me an essay defending one reading, lay out the distinct precise things "there are just individuals" could be claiming, as a list I can actually bring to the postmortem review. For what it's worth I've tried to start the list myself, tell me where i'm wrong and what I'm missing:

- the ontological one: only humans (and their artifacts?) exist, and words like "the team" or "the system" or "society" fail to refer to anything
- the translation one: maybe collectives exist, but every statement about them can in principle be rewritten as some gigantic statement about individual people and what they did
- the explanatory one: fine, "the org" exists, but any good explanation of what happened has to eventually bottom out in particular choices by particular people, and a "systemic" explanation that refuses to do that isn't an explanation, it's a shrug
- the normative one: you can only hold people responsible, never structures, so "the system failed" is just blame-avoidance dressed up as insight
- and something like a methodological rule for people building models: build your models out of agents, since aggregate-level facts never carry real explanatory weight

For each reading, tell me: is it a triviality, is it substantive but defensible, or is it just false? And crucially -- does that reading actually give my tech lead what he needs to wave away my postmortem, or can you grant the reading in full and still say "the on-call rotation is what broke us"? I want to know exactly which (if any) of these he'd need to be right about, and what he'd have to pay to be right about it.

Two extras if you're so inclined. First, this feels structurally identical to arguments I keep hearing about whether "emergent capabilities" in big ML models are real or just a word for our own surprise -- if there's a genuine rhyme there, point it out, but don't force it. Second, I keep half-remembering that this whole fight (individuals vs. structures as the real unit of analysis) is an old standing war in the social sciences, not something my tech lead invented on Tuesday -- if you can gesture at how that war goes without name-dropping a bibliography at me, that would help me not say something dumb in the meeting.
