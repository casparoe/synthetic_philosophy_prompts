In the small but long-running debate over functional decision theory's handling of the classic dilemmas, a strange asymmetry has hardened into opposing camps that no longer seem to move each other. FDT's proponents point out, with some pride, that their theory pays the stranger in a counterfactual mugging (where a coin flip you cannot influence determined whether you're asked to pay), yet refuses to pay in XOR blackmail (where the blackmailer sends the letter only if predicted you wouldn't succumb — or so I recall the setup; the details don't matter beyond the following). Critics, notably Will MacAskill, press a symmetric objection: the two cases are structurally near-identical from the agent's point of view — in both, the crucial event happened before your choice and is evidentially or functionally linked to it, and in both there is some possible predictor whose behavior depends on your dispositions — so any theory that pays in the one and refuses in the other needs a difference-maker, and neither the cases nor the theory seems to supply one. The FDT side replies that there is a difference: in XOR blackmail the predictor is tracking your decision procedure specifically in order to exploit it, whereas in the mugging the coin is not tracking anything about you — but this smells to critics like an ad hoc appeal to the evaluator's intentions rather than a principled distinction, and the exchange then repeats. My hypothesis is that the real division is not about cases at all but about what a normative decision theory is supposed to be: a theory of what a single deliberating agent should do in the moment (which naturally favors causal reasoning about the now-fixed past), or a theory of which decision procedures an agent should want to be running, evaluated across the whole class of situations the agent will face (which is what FDT's 'policy' perspective amounts to). If that's right, the two camps are answering different questions under one heading, and the case-based war is a proxy fight. Diagnose the disagreement: is it verbal, a difference about the standard of evaluation, a genuine formal dispute about whether a principled difference-maker exists, or bedrock intuitions about whether being the sort of agent who pays in a mugging is rational or merely useful? And given your diagnosis, say what would actually count as progress — a formal criterion distinguishing the cases, a clarified bifurcation of the question into a theory of choice and a theory of agent-design, or an argument that no resolution should be expected — rather than another tour of the examples.
