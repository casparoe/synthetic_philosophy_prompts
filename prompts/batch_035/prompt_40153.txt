I teach an upper-level elective on the philosophy of probability — fourteen students, one seventy-five-minute session — and we've just finished a unit on the principle of indifference, including the classical definition of probability and the trouble with applying it when a problem can be parametrized in more than one natural way. For the next session I want an in-class activity built around Bertrand's chord paradox. The idea is that students should physically generate "random chords" of a circle and estimate the probability that a chord is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle — without being told in advance that the answer depends on how "at random" is operationalized. My plan is to split them into small groups, hand each group a different set of innocuous-looking instructions for producing a random chord (one group spinning twice for two points on the circumference, one group choosing a distance along a diameter and constructing the perpendicular chord, one group choosing a random point in the disk's interior as the chord's midpoint), and have each group pool maybe forty chords, count how many beat the triangle, and report a number. The groups should arrive at visibly different estimates, and the room should have to work out why before I say anything. I tried a version of this two years ago with dice and it fell flat — the mechanics swallowed the point — so I'd like the physical procedure to be fast enough that the counting, not the construction, dominates the session.

Please design the full activity: exact instructions per group (improvised materials only — paper circles, rulers, spinners, a pin to drop, whatever works and can be run by teaching assistants), a timing plan, a clean way to display the three estimates side by side, and a debrief that maps each physical procedure onto the corresponding way of applying the principle of indifference, so students see precisely which quantity each method is treating as uniformly distributed. Include a structured moment where the groups are asked to defend their own procedure as "the" natural one before they see the argument for the others — I want them to feel the pull of their own parametrization first. Also give me your honest predictions of what will go wrong on first run: the obvious candidates are small-sample noise making the estimates look compatible, and students suspecting the decks are stacked because I gave different instructions — tell me how to handle both.

Two further requests. First, do not treat the three methods as symmetric in the debrief: I want you to take a definite position on which sampling procedure (if any) corresponds to genuine randomness in the chord problem, and defend it — whether by appeal to invariance under the symmetries of the problem, by rejecting all three as ill-posed, or some other line — rather than presenting the options and leaving the class to pick. Flag clearly if your verdict rests on a contested move, since I'll be teaching your position as a live option rather than a consensus. Second, tell me plainly which feature of the paradox the activity cannot reproduce physically and I'll have to supply verbally — my guess is it's whatever your favored resolution is, since the physical devices always smuggle in one metric, but I'd like your diagnosis rather than mine. Please write everything in American English.
