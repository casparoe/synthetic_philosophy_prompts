My friend Devi has very firm opinions about when someone has lied vs when they've just been sleazy, and I've been keeping a list of her verdicts because I'm trying to figure out what rule she's actually running before our next argument about it. So far she's ruled on these:

1. Someone says to a friend, sarcastically, that they just LOVE waiting three hours at the DMV — the friend obviously gets the sarcasm. Not a lie, she says, don't be ridiculous.
2. A used-car buyer asks "has it ever been in an accident?" and the seller replies "one careful owner, full service history, garage kept" — every single word true, but he knows exactly what conclusion the buyer will draw, and the car was totaled and rebuilt. Devi says that's a lie. Straight up.
3. Kant's murderer at the door asks where your friend is hiding, and instead of answering you say, truthfully, "why would he ever come here?" Devi: not a lie, and it'd be fine even if it were.
4. You tell a friend her talk was really interesting when it was a slog. Devi: lie, though she wobbles on how bad that is.
5. You tell a coworker the meeting starts at 7 when you genuinely believe that, but it's at 6. Not a lie, just a mistake.

Next week's cases. A salesman type who just doesn't care whether what he says is true — he tells you the engine is bulletproof, never checked, doesn't know, it's false (bullshit in Frankfurt's sense, indifference to truth rather than intent to deceive). You notice your roommate believes you already paid your half of the rent, and you let her keep believing it, saying absolutely nothing. And a poker player who announces they have the flush when they don't — bluffing at a table where everyone understands bluffing is part of the game.

Predict her verdict on each of the three. Then the real question: what rules actually fit her five earlier verdicts? I can see the naive "a lie is just asserting what you believe false" one dying on the car salesman alone, so it has to be something else — my hunch is there are at least two rules that fit everything so far, something like deception-through-assertion (where misleading implicatures count but omissions and pure questions don't) versus something about intending a false belief in someone who's owed the truth. Do those two fit all five? Which of the new cases do they split on? Give me your best guess at which one she's running and why, and then tell me what single case I should put to her to settle it — because I want to win this argument, not just understand it.
