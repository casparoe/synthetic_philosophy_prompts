I'm assembling materials for a satirical-writing workshop and would like a piece that requires genuine philosophical literacy from whoever attempts it. Write a publisher's marketing package for a new self-help bestseller by a tranquilist guru: a back-cover blurb, three or four advance-praise quotes from invented figures, and a short reader FAQ. Tranquilism, as this author holds it, is the view that the only thing good in itself is the absence of craving and suffering — satisfied desires contribute nothing positive that never having wanted at all wouldn't have contributed, pleasures are valuable at most as preventions or reliefs of worse states, and a quiet life of absence beats any life of striving, however rich. The target of the satire is the machinery of the self-help genre, but the joke must run through the doctrine itself, not around it: the collision between an industry powered by manufactured dissatisfaction (you deserve more; unlock your best life; don't get left behind) and a doctrine on which wanting anything — including wanting the book's promised result — is the very disease on sale.

Every element has to be written so the guru could sign it without hypocrisy. The blurb cannot promise happiness, only the end of needing it. The testimonials cannot come from people who loved the book, since loving a book is a preference with an object. The FAQ must confront, deadpan, the reader who asks what to do about wanting tranquility — the one craving the book is supposed to remove, and the only one that will get anyone to do the exercises — and should handle the money-back guarantee (claiming a refund requires wanting money, which reintroduces the disease) and the sales problem that fully cured customers have no reason to buy the sequel. Somewhere in the package the publisher's commercial desperation should be faintly audible, since marketing is itself an engine of craving and the editor knows it.

Keep the voice straight-faced and internally consistent throughout; no winking. A reader who knows nothing of the view should come away able to reconstruct it — the asymmetry between positive and negative states, the thought that fulfillment is only the removal of a lack, the awkward fact that the author kept writing and the audience kept buying — while a reader who knows the utilitarian literature on the asymmetry between pleasure and the absence of pain should find the satire fair rather than a caricature. Around 600–700 words, formatted with the labeled sections an actual marketing packet would have.
