My philosophy of religion seminar has turned into a year-long argument between two theists about how to answer the evidential problem of evil (the Rowe-style one, with the fawn burning to death in a forest fire and no good ever coming of it). One guy is a theodicist: he thinks God's reasons for permitting evil can actually be articulated -- free will, soul-making, laws of nature needing stability to be learnable, that family of moves. The other is a skeptical theist: she thinks the whole theodicy project is a mistake, that our cognitive position is such that we should expect NOT to see God's reasons even when they exist, and that trying to name the reasons is both epistemically overreaching and kind of morally gross (imagine telling the parents of a kid with bone cancer that it builds character). They've been talking past each other for months: he thinks she's just dodging the question, she thinks he's doing PR for God. I want them to do a proper adversarial collaboration and produce the joint statement.

Write that document. It should include:

1. A numbered section of claims both can sign -- and I want these to be substantive, not "evil exists" filler. Things like: both reject the claim that a positively-evil event is logically incompatible with God's existence (they're both on the same side of the logical problem); both agree Rowe's fawn case is the strongest version of the argument; both agree that "gratileous" evil means something like "evil not integrated into any outweighing good," not "evil that looks pointless to us" -- or if they can't agree on that, that itself is one of the disagreements, and it should go in the table instead.

2. A disagreements table. Each row should state one point of residual disagreement, and crucially it should be worded so the side that holds the view would accept the wording. So the theodicist's position on whether theodicies are morally repugnant has to be phrased in a way he'd actually endorse (maybe something like: articulating possible reasons is different from asserting them, the way a defense attorney can offer an account of innocence without claiming to know what happened), and the skeptical theist's position on noseeum-style inferences has to be phrased so she'd sign it. If some of their fight turns out to be verbal -- e.g., about what "gratuitous" means or about whether "a possible reason" and "the actual reason" are being run together -- flag that explicitly and say which part is verbal.

3. Cruxes. I suspect at least one crux is whether theists should expect, a priori, that God's reasons would be accessible to creatures (she says no in general, he says yes at least for the big categories of evil), and another is whether the skeptical-theist move, if it works against Rowe, also undermines too much else -- like whether she has to give up saying God is good in any determinate sense, or whether it kneecaps other parts of theology or even everyday moral inference ("I can't see a reason for my friend's suffering, therefore..."). For each crux, give the case, argument, or thought experiment both would agree bears on it, and write down each side's prediction about it in advance, so it could actually come back to bite one of them.

Keep it in the register of a real joint statement, not a lecture -- first-person-plural where they agree, then "the theodicist holds / the skeptical theist holds" where they don't. And don't have them converge at the end; the point is to make the remaining disagreement exact, not to paper over it. Also if there's a spot where each side should concede something to the other (e.g., maybe he should grant that some of the popular theodicies really are terrible, or she should grant that pure skepticism about divine reasons leaves the believer with very little to say pastorally), include those as separate agreed concessions -- I want the document to show each of them giving up one thing, not just entrenching.
