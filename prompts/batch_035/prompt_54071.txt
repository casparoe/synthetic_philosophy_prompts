So some context: I'm a backend engineer at a mid-size company, and I've somehow ended up on an internal working group that's supposed to figure out what "honesty" even means for the LLM features we ship. I don't have a philosophy background at all (BS in CS, closest thing was an ethics requirement I mostly slept through), and I keep noticing that everyone in these meetings uses "the model lied" and "the model hallucinated" and "the model was deceptive" as if they're obviously different things, but nobody can actually say where the line is. Like, when the model confidently makes up a library function that doesn't exist because that pattern was rewarded in training, that feels different from a case where a model says something false because saying the true thing would get a worse reward. But I genuinely can't articulate why, and I've started to suspect the difference between "deception" and "error" is doing a lot of unexamined work in how people talk about AI safety.

I've been reading around in my spare time and it seems like there are a bunch of tangled open questions here that nobody has really sorted out, and I want to figure out which ones are worth my limited attention. Off the top of my head: (1) does deception require intent, and can we even make sense of intent for something that's "just predicting tokens"? (2) is there a meaningful notion of what the model "believes" as distinct from what it says, and if so can we detect gaps between the two? (3) does omission or selective framing count as deception for an AI assistant, the same way a human advisor can mislead you with only true statements? (4) is self-deception (the model generating claims it "knows" are false by its own lights, e.g. when it role-plays or sandbags on evals) a special category or just the normal case wearing a costume? And probably (5) whether "honesty" as a target is even coherent, or whether it collapses into "say what your training objective rewards," which would be depressing.

Given that I've got maybe 5 hours a week and the patience to read papers but not the background to evaluate fancy philosophy arguments, which of these questions (or ones I haven't listed) would you actually tell me to dig into first? I care less about a survey and more about a defended ordering: which question is upstream of the others, which ones are genuinely tractable for a motivated engineer rather than a professional philosopher, and which ones I should just accept I'll never resolve and should set aside. Also happy to hear that my framing of the questions themselves is confused, if it is.
