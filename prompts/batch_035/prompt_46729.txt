I've been reading up on the units of selection fight -- genes vs. organisms vs. groups, the thing that started with Wynne-Edwards and got heated when Dawkins and Williams pushed the gene's-eye view, and never really ended (multilevel selection people are still publishing). My armchair diagnosis is that after Sober and Wilson formalized things with the Price equation, the two camps stopped disagreeing about biology and started disagreeing about what "a unit of selection" is even supposed to mean: one side treats it as a bookkeeping choice (where you partition covariance doesn't change the biology), the other treats it as a claim about causation (which entities actually have the causal profile -- variation, heritability, differential fitness -- at that level). Is that right? Or is there a real empirical disagreement hiding underneath, like about how common group-structured selection actually is in nature, or whether kin selection and group selection accounts are genuinely interchangeable or just mathematically translatable? I'd love a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is -- verbal, empirical, or two research programs answering different questions under one name -- and an honest take on whether anything (a formal result, data on population structure, a conceptual distinction) could actually settle it, or whether we should just expect this one to grind on forever. Answer in American English please, and keep it grounded in what the different camps actually argue, not a survey.
