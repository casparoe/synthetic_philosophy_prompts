Suppose you are a professional philosopher of language, with particular expertise in the tradition of ordinary-language philosophy and in the literature on speech act theory that grew out of it. I would like a careful, essay-length treatment of the following comparative question, and I ask you to think it through carefully before writing: begin with a short brainstorming phase in which you lay out the candidate readings of the texts and the key edge cases, then develop the comparison in a structured argument.

The question: compare Austin's suggestion that an illocutionary act requires the securing of uptake — the hearer's recognition that the act is being performed — with Searle's later handling of the same phenomenon, and then evaluate which of two reconstructions of the disagreement is more defensible:

(1) A constitutive reading, on which uptake is part of what makes the illocution the act it is: if no one recognizes the utterance as a promise, an order, or a christening, then, strictly speaking, nothing of that illocutionary kind has occurred.

(2) A causal-consequential reading, on which the illocution is fixed by the felicitous execution of the relevant conventional or intentional procedure, while uptake belongs to the act's effects rather than to its identity conditions.

To keep the comparison from collapsing into textbook labels, please work through the following edge cases explicitly. First, the promise made to a sleeping addressee — someone who cannot possibly recognize the promise, yet from whom third parties demand the promiser's performance. Second, a warning shouted across a noisy street: the hearer ducks, but interprets the utterance as a joke rather than a warning, so the beneficial perlocutionary effect occurs without the uptake it would ordinarily accompany. Third, ceremonial and multi-party settings, such as a courtroom or a marriage ceremony, where the relevant audience includes officials whose uptake may matter while bystanders' uptake does not — a case that pressures both readings, since it suggests uptake conditions are interest-relative in a way neither Austin nor Searle clearly licenses. Fourth, consider whether perlocutionary effects can loop back and become partly constitutive of later illocutions: for instance, whether a standing pattern of successful persuasion can change what counts as issuing a threat in a particular relationship.

Then introduce a third position into the comparison: a broadly Gricean intention-based account, on which the illocutionary force of an utterance is fixed by the speaker's reflexive communicative intentions, so that uptake is at most evidence about what was intended, never a condition of the act's occurrence. Assess whether this third view dissolves the Austin–Searle disagreement or merely relocates it — specifically, whether it can account for the intuition that certain acts, like marrying or sentencing, seem to depend on something socially external to the speaker's intentions in a way promising or warning does not.

Conclude with a considered verdict: does the constitutive reading, the causal-consequential reading, or the intention-based third view best explain the full range of cases? Your verdict should be argued for, not merely asserted, and you should be explicit about which cases force you to pay theoretical costs. Aim for roughly 700–900 words, written in a formal register suitable for an upper-level philosophy of language seminar. Do not confine yourself to exegesis of Austin and Searle; treat their positions as starting points for the conceptual question of what, if anything, the hearer contributes to the identity of an illocutionary act.
