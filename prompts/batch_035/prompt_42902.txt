Two guys on my team got into it after a standup about a bug in our weather-simulation side project, and it turned into a whole philosophy thing that neither of them would let go of. Can you go through their argument and tell me who was right at each step?

Marco's claim: the atmosphere is (basically) a deterministic system, so the weather is in principle fully predictable -- we just need better sensors and more compute. Chaos just means it's expensive, not impossible.

Dana's first reply: that's exactly backwards, chaos is the proof that it's impossible in principle, not just hard. If errors in your initial conditions grow exponentially, then no finite measurement gets you there, so there's a hard horizon on prediction no matter what you throw at it.

Marco's counter: "no finite measurement" is smuggled in. Determinism means the state at time t fully fixes the state at time t+1. If you somehow knew the exact state, the future would follow. The fact that we can't measure infinitely precisely is a fact about us, not about the system -- the unpredictability is epistemic, not physical.

Dana: but "if you knew the exact state" is doing magic-level work there. The state is specified by real numbers, and any physical measurement has finite precision, not because our instruments are bad but because that's what measurement is. So a "predictability" that requires impossible inputs isn't predictability at all, and he wants to say what the system actually does, not what a demon with infinite digits would see. Also, Marco keeps saying "deterministic" like it's obvious -- quantum mechanics, or so Dana has heard, puts randomness in at the bottom, and those tiny random deviations get amplified by the same chaos Marco's been talking about, so the long-range future may literally not be there to be predicted.

Marco's last word: whether QM randomness is "real" randomness or just our ignorance of hidden variables is an open question, and Dana is leaning on a specific interpretation like it's settled. And even granting it, amplification takes time, so it only ruins prediction past some horizon -- which was his point all along: deterministic-ish + chaotic means predictable up to a horizon that recedes as your instruments improve, not unpredictable full stop.

They both appealed to me as the "sciencey one" and I honestly could not referee this. Who's right about which specific claims, are they maybe both right and just using "in principle" to mean different things, and is there anything both of them are missing?
