A friend in my game theory course walked me through an argument about a small pricing game, and I can neither refute it nor accept where it ends. The game: two firms simultaneously submit a price of 1, 2, or 3. Both firms are paid the lower of the two submitted prices; the firm that submitted the lower price collects a bonus of 2 on top of that, while the firm that submitted the higher price is docked 2; identical submissions are simply paid the common price. Payoffs (row firm, column firm):

- Row posts 1: (1,1) against column 1; (3,−1) against column 2; (3,−1) against column 3.
- Row posts 2: (−1,3); (2,2); (4,0).
- Row posts 3: (−1,3); (0,4); (3,3).

His argument runs as follows:

1. If some strategy of yours — including a randomization over your own pure strategies — strictly beats strategy s against every strategy the opponent might play, then no beliefs about the opponent could ever make s a best response, so rationality forbids playing s outright.
2. In this game, price 3 is beaten exactly that way. No pure price strictly dominates it (price 1 merely ties it when the rival posts 3; price 2 merely ties it when the rival posts 1), but the fifty-fifty mixture of prices 1 and 2 strictly dominates it, whatever the rival posts. My friend adds that the equivalence between never-best-responses and dominated strategies, with mixtures allowed, is a standard result, which is what licenses passing from no-beliefs-make-it-optimal to delete-it.
3. So no rational firm posts 3; and each firm, knowing the other is rational, strikes 3 from the rival's menu.
4. Once 3 is struck, price 1 strictly dominates price 2; and since each firm knows the other knows it is rational, both strike 2 as well.
5. Therefore firms between whom rationality is common knowledge post price 1 and earn 1 apiece — even though both would have earned 3 apiece had both posted 3, and no one's belief was ever false at any point in the cascade.
6. Hence, he concludes, common knowledge of rationality is a collective liability: rationality itself recommends seeing to it that the knowledge is destroyed — for instance, by publicly and irrevocably committing to a dominated price, or by installing a manager incapable of the arithmetic — and firms that ignore the argument and post 3 come out ahead of firms that follow it, so the theory, taken seriously, instructs you to do worse than you would by ignoring it.

I have checked the arithmetic twice: the mixture really does strictly dominate 3, and 1 really does strictly dominate 2 once 3 is gone. I also vaguely recall being told that iterated deletion can depend on the order in which strategies are removed, which makes me distrust step 4, though I cannot see how to generate any order-dependence in so small a game. My real difficulty is that steps 1 through 4 each look unimpeachable, while 6 seems to prove both that we must be rational and that each of us does better not to be known as rational — and that a firm profits from disabling the very capacity the argument attributes to it. So tell me where the argument goes wrong: which numbered step, or which hidden assumption behind 3 and 4, and why the culprit looked innocent. And if the cascade itself is beyond reproach, tell me which premise it is least costly to give up and what rejecting it commits me to, since of all the steps I would far rather keep 1 than any of the rest.
