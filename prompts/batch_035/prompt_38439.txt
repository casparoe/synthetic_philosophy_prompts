Explain how these two claims fit together, what role the morality of custom and the long 'breeding' of the human animal play in the story, and what the figure of the sovereign individual represents in Nietzsche's argument."

Here's the student's answer, typed out from their bluebook:

"Nietzsche's big move in the Second Essay is to argue that guilt didn't start out as a moral feeling at all. The German word Schuld means both guilt and debt, and Nietzsche takes this etymology as a clue that guilt descends from the very concrete contractual relation between creditor and debtor. In the earliest communities, debts were secured by making the debtor suffer -- pain functioned as a kind of memory aid, since prehistoric humans were forgetful animals who needed to be trained to keep promises. So the feeling of being guilty originally just meant owing something, and the moralized version came later, once the concepts were spiritualized.

The second part of the story is bad conscience. Here the morality of custom does the real work: the priests of the community enforced conformity with relentless social pressure, and anyone whose instincts couldn't discharge themselves outward had to turn them inward instead. This inward-turning of cruelty is bad conscience -- the human being making itself suffer because it can no longer suffer others. The two threads meet in the sovereign individual, who is Nietzsche's ideal of the master moralist: the blond beast who has shaken off the morality of custom entirely and now keeps promises out of pure self-command rather than fear. Since guilt is just internalized debt, Nietzsche's practical point is that we should abolish creditor-debtor relations wherever we find them, and the guilt-feeling will dissolve along with them, freeing us from the last remnant of slave morality."
