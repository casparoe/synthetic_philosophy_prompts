My roommate is a philosophy major and she's been running a months-long campaign to convert me to dualism using Mary the color scientist. Fine, classic stuff. But last week she went further, and now I think the argument she ended up with proves something genuinely insane and she can't see it. Here it is as she gives it:

(1) Mary's case is supposed to show: if someone has complete physical knowledge of a domain but has never had the relevant experience, then when they finally have it, they learn something that wasn't in the physical facts.
(2) Nothing in that reasoning is special about color. Run the same setup for every experience type there is: a mini-Mary who knows all the physical facts about durian but has never tasted it, one who knows everything about the physiology of fevers but has never had one, one who knows everything about what heartbreak does to the brain but has never been dumped.
(3) Each mini-Mary learns something when she finally gets the experience. (I'm inclined to grant this. If you deny it, you're saying experiences never teach anyone anything, which seems like way too much to swallow.)
(4) So for every experience type, there are facts that outstrip the physical facts.
(5) Which means ordinary people are learning non-physical facts all day long. I learned one when I first tasted coffee at fourteen. My cat, arguably, when she first saw snow.
(6) So the physical facts don't fix all the facts -- physicalism is false, and not in some abstract metaphysical corner either, it's false about what my coffee tasted like in 2011.
(7) And here's the part where she lost me: she says the same principle applies to experiences of external things. A meteorologist who knew every physical fact about tornados but had never been in one would learn something when one finally hits -- so there are facts about tornados that outstrip the physical facts. Facts about weather that no completed physics contains.

I can't accept (7). A tornado is just air doing stuff, and the meteorologist is a guy standing in it. But I also can't find where the inference from (1) to (7) breaks, because (7) really does look like the same move that got us from (1) to (5). Is there a principled place to stop?

The obvious reply is that what the meteorologist learns is a fact about his own experience, not about tornados. But then doesn't the same move say what Mary learns is a fact about her experience -- and if physicalism is true, that experience is a physical thing, a brain state, so she learned a physical fact she somehow didn't already know despite having complete knowledge? Does that help the physicalist or just make everything worse? She's also ready for the "she only gains an ability, like learning to ride a bike" line, whatever that's called -- but I notice nobody says they learned an ability when they tasted coffee, they say they learned what it tastes like, and abilities seem like the wrong category anyway.

For what its worth, I'd like to keep (3), and I'd like to come out a physicalist if that's at all affordable. Name the exact step I should reject, explain why that step looked fine (it did look fine to me), and tell me what rejecting it costs. If the honest answer is that the price of keeping (7) out is letting something surprising in elsewhere, I want to know what and how much.
