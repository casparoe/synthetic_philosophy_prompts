A podcast I've been listening to on the history of mathematics devoted a segment to Zeno of Elea, and the host's account has stuck with me because it seemed to dismiss a rather famous puzzle very quickly. Summarizing the dichotomy paradox, the host said roughly the following: Zeno observed that before you can reach the door, you must first reach the halfway point; before you reach the halfway point, you must reach the quarter point; and so on without end. So any journey, however short, requires completing infinitely many sub-journeys, and since a series with infinitely many positive terms must sum to something infinitely large, the total distance can never be covered. Aristotle refuted this by replying that time divides infinitely just as space does, so the infinite sub-journeys also take infinitely many sub-times and everything balances out. Modern mathematics then settled the matter for good with the theory of convergent series, which shows that infinitely many positive quantities can have a finite sum — something Zeno simply didn't know. And since each of us walks to the door several times a day, the host concluded, the paradox is best understood as a straightforward confusion, cured by calculus, with no remaining philosophical content beyond its historical interest.
