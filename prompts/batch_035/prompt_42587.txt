Draft the joint statement that would conclude an adversarial collaboration between two philosophers of mind who have been arguing for several years about whether dreams are experiences. The first, in the tradition of Norman Malcolm, holds that dreams are not experiences had during sleep: a dream report made on waking is not a memory of a temporally extended episode of consciousness, and the ordinary concept of dreaming does not require that anything was experienced at the time; what we call "the dream" is, on this view, something expressed or composed at the moment of waking rather than recalled. The second, in the tradition of Daniel Dennett, holds that dreams are experiences — consciousness does occur during sleep — but that the narrative we report on waking is typically a construction written at or around the moment of awakening, so dream reports are systematically unreliable guides to what, and when, anything was experienced. The document should be one both parties could sign in good faith.

Structure it as follows. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: include at minimum what both accept about the physiology of sleep (REM and its cycles), about the demonstrated unreliability of dream reports, about the well-established laboratory finding that lucid dreamers can send pre-agreed signals — e.g., distinctive eye-movement patterns — that are recorded while they are asleep, and about what the disagreement is not about (it is not about whether dreams matter, nor about whether dream reports have narrative structure). Second, a table of the precise residual disagreements, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording — in particular, give a formulation each side would sign of the question whether "I dreamt such-and-such" is a memory claim with truth-conditions about a past experience or something closer to an avowal composed on waking.

Third, identify the cruxes. At least two should be considered: whether the lucid-dream signaling results show that experience can be attributed to a sleeper as it happens, or only that something in the sleeping brain can issue reports; and whether the distinction between misremembering an experience (the experience happened, the report is wrong) and fabricating a memory at awakening (the report is wrong about there being any experience at all) marks a substantive difference or a verbal one. For each crux, specify a concrete test both sides agree bears on it, and register each side's prediction in advance. For at least one crux, use a protocol of the following kind: subjects are woken at randomly chosen moments during REM and asked immediately to narrate; before any data are collected, each side states what pattern it expects in the relationship between reported dream length, position of awakening in the REM episode, and reported duration of the dream. Where a side cannot give a prediction without abandoning its position, say so, and note whether the immunizing move leaves that side anything at stake in the experiment.

Finally, address two matters of interpretation. The lucid-dream experiments postdate the original exchange; include a short passage both would sign on how a view should be treated when confronted with evidence its author never faced. And conclude with the collaborators' own verdict on which parts of their dispute were substantive and which were verbal, with reasons — noting that the two sides may disagree about exactly where to draw that line, and if so, say why in the closing paragraph, signed by each party separately.
