Quick backstory so this makes sense: I'm a novelist, mid-way through plotting the ending of a near-future trilogy about an engineered pandemic, and I've discovered that my gut reactions to my own candidate endings don't add up, and it's starting to bug me enough to actually ask someone about it. Ending one: the virus kills everyone on Earth within about a week, basically painless, no survivors. Ending two: same virus but it only takes out a third of humanity and the rest carry on, rebuild, whatever. Ending three: no mass death at all, just a slow fertility collapse over two centuries, everyone who lives gets a decent full life, and then there's nobody. My gut says ending three is melancholy but almost peaceful, while ending one is a different *category* of horror — which is strange, because ending one involves less total suffering than a lot of scenarios that feel less bad to me, and the same number of people die in the long run either way. It's like the instant version destroys something the slow version doesn't, and I can't name what.

More verdicts, since they might help: if I let a Mars colony of maybe two thousand people survive the virus in ending one, most of that special horror evaporates for me, even though the colonists are culturally nothing like Earth and the eight billion still die — two thousand strangers shouldn't do that much work if what I cared about was the people dying. Also I'm confident the eight billion deaths are themselves bad, but when I imagine the total badness of ending one it feels like *more* than those deaths, something extra on top. And here's one I keep chewing on: suppose the final generation, the people born last, know they're the last and live genuinely wonderful lives — I feel it's good that they existed, and yet their existence seems to be part of what makes the whole scenario so bleak, which feels like it can't both be true. A writer friend mentioned some philosopher (Scheffler? I may be butchering it) who apparently argues that much of what we care about in our own lives — finishing things, having kids, writing books that get read — only makes sense against the assumed background of humanity going on after us, and that this is supposed to explain why extinction is bad beyond the deaths involved. That resonates with my Mars-colony reaction but I don't see how it explains the rest.

So here's my actual ask: is there one principle that captures all my verdicts — instant extinction is a special horror over and above the death toll, the slow fade is oddly gentler, a small surviving colony dissolves most of the horror, the last generation is good to have existed even though it deepens the bleakness? If there isn't, tell me which verdict you'd overturn and why, and be concrete about it, I think in scenes not syllogisms. And then tell me what your best candidate principle says about the ending I haven't dared commit to yet: the virus spares everyone alive but permanently destroys human fertility, so there's no mass death, no last generation that knows it's last, no fade-out anyone experiences — everyone just lives out a full life and humanity ends quietly, off-stage. Do give me the principle stated precisely, and what each clause is doing for which verdict. At the very end, put a one-sentence statement of the principle in \answer{...} so I can pin it above my desk.
