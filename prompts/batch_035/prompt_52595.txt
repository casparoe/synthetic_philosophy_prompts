I've become convinced that if you watch what a person does over a long enough time, their behavior contains enough information to infer what they actually want — roughly, that machines could someday learn human values by studying how we act rather than by being told what we value. I like this idea because it treats people's choices as evidence about them, and it seems to explain everyday cases: we infer that a friend must care about her health from her habits, without ever asking her. But I've run into worries I can't resolve on my own. For one, people act against their own interests all the time — out of habit, weakness of will, fear of being judged, or just because everyone around them does the same thing — and I can't tell how much of this a careful observer could untangle, and how much makes the underlying wants permanently invisible. For another, the same behavior seems explainable by very different sets of wants: someone who never travels could love staying home or be terrified of planes, and I don't know what further observation, if any, would settle it. So my question is: what would have to be true for my view to be wrong? Which of these problems would a committed defender of my view admit counts against it, and which would they explain away — and at what point does that explaining-away turn the view into something that can no longer be refuted by anything? Please rank the counterconsiderations from fatal to merely awkward.
