I've been reading the rationalist blogs and forums for a long time now, and the habit that has worn the deepest groove in me is refusing to accept a noun as an explanation. "It was cultural" has always struck me as the respectability-preserving version of "stuff happened" — a residual category that grows every time the materialist explanations run out of steam. At the same time, I've read enough about Weber's Protestant ethic thesis to suspect that dismissing it outright isn't the clear-eyed move it feels like, since Weber's actual claim is more careful than the strawman: not that Protestantism caused capitalism, but that a specific constellation of values — innerworldly asceticism, the calling, salvation anxiety with no sacramental release — restructured everyday economic conduct in a way that made rational accumulation feel like a moral duty rather than avarice. The question I keep circling is whether "culture" can be a genuine cause at all, or whether invoking it is always either circularity, reverse causation, or a disguised description of the institutions we already know about.

The case I want worked through is the divergence of the two Koreas since 1945. Here we have a natural experiment that a cultural explanation should have to face: same language, same Confucian inheritance, same war, and then radically divergent institutions and outcomes. Complicating the picture, the very same Confucian inheritance was later invoked — in the post-Confucian hypothesis and the Asian values debates of the later twentieth century — to explain the rise of the East Asian tigers, South Korea emphatically included, while Weber himself, in his comparative writings on China, had treated the Confucian ethic as part of why that civilization had failed to generate capitalism on its own. A trait credited with explaining both a thing and its negation deserves suspicion, and I want that suspicion articulated precisely rather than just gestured at.

So please analyze the Korean divergence through the lens of Weber's thesis, but treat the theory as the thing on trial as much as the case. Concretely, I'd like you to sketch the causal graph the Weberian needs: is culture a confounder (a common cause of both institutions and growth), a mediator (values → institutions → outcomes), a moderator, or something that dissolves when you condition on the right variables? Does the northern regime screen culture off entirely, or is there residual work for values to do even holding institutions fixed — for instance in explaining how each society metabolized its institutions? Then turn it around: what does the episode do to the thesis? Is this a disconfirmation of Weber, a disconfirmation only of a lazy reading of Weber, or evidence that the thesis was never the kind of claim that natural experiments could test — and if the last, say plainly what would count as evidence for or against a claim like that. I'd also like your honest view on whether the counterfactual is even well-formed: what does it mean to intervene on a culture, as opposed to a policy or a regime? Please be disciplined about where the framework is silent rather than forcing it to speak, and assume I know the standard textbook moves, so spend your effort on the mapping and the failure points, not on rehearsing the thesis back to me.
