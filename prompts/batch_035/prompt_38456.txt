I'm a graduate student organizing a daylong workshop on the split-brain literature, and I've been asked to draft the joint statement that would conclude a supervised adversarial collaboration between two of the participants. One of them holds that complete callosotomy leaves a single conscious subject whose hemispheres merely lose direct access to each other's contents; the other holds that the operation leaves two subjects sharing one body, prevented from displaying their distinctness chiefly by the design of the testing apparatus rather than by any residual mental connection. They have been arguing past each other for years, and the point of the document is to make the residual disagreement exact rather than to resolve it.

Draft that document with the following structure.

First, a numbered section of claims both sides could sign. This should be restricted to empirical common ground: what the surgery severs and what it leaves intact, including the subcortical routes and the anterior commissure; the basic lateralization findings from the Pasadena patient series of the 1960s, including the confinement of language to the dominant hemisphere in most patients; the observation that patients in ordinary life do not spontaneously report a divided mental life and are typically unaware of interhemispheric conflicts; and the forced-lateralization findings, including the well-known configuration in which a patient, given information to one visual field only, sustains incompatible outputs at the same moment — a verbal answer through one channel while the other hand acts at cross purposes with it. Resist the temptation to let either side's favorite framing leak into this section; agreed claims should be statable without commitment to either camp's interpretation.

Second, a table of residual disagreements, each row phrased so that the side holding the view would accept the wording as accurate. I expect rows on at least: whether the disconnected minor hemisphere's processing constitutes a second stream of experience or is better described as a single subject's impaired access to it; whether verbal report understates or overstates what the minor hemisphere can do, given its limited output channels; whether the notion of unity at stake should be parsed into phenomenal unity and access unity, and if so which of the two the disagreement is really about; and what follows for the concept of a person — whether "one person with two partly dissociated systems" and "two persons in one body" are substantive rivals or, at the limit, a choice of vocabulary.

Third, the cruxes: for each, a concrete study, observation, or argument both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Candidate cruxes to consider: whether residual subcortical transfer carries genuine representational content or only arousal and affect; whether the minor hemisphere shows evidence of a self-concept and autobiographical memory under testing paradigms less dependent on language; and whether implicit cross-field priming survives the operation. If you see a crux I have missed, add it — and if any part of the dispute strikes you as verbal rather than substantive, say which part and why.
