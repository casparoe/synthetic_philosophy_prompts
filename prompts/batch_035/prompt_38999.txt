I teach an intro political theory course, and we do Machiavelli for two weeks — mostly The Prince, with the fortune/virtù material getting the most class time. Fortune as this destructive river that floods everything unless you build dikes, fortune as a woman who favors the bold and impetuous over the cautious (my students hate that one, which is productive), the idea that she governs maybe half our affairs and leaves the rest to us. This semester I also showed Forrest Gump as an end-of-unit fun night, and now I can't stop thinking about it as a Machiavelli text, so help me think it through.

Here's the reading I want to take seriously: Forrest is almost pure fortuna and almost zero virtù, and he wins everything. He drifts (literally, that feather) into football, Vietnam, ping-pong diplomacy, the shrimp business, and wealth, while everyone around him who plans, strategizes, and exercises agency — Jenny especially — gets wrecked. Lt. Dan is the most Machiavellian character in the film: he comes from a martial lineage, has a mapped-out destiny of dying with honor, rages at fate when it's denied him, then finally becomes a planner again (the shrimping charts, the investment in the computer company) and prospers — which reads like a little vindication of virtù buried inside fortune's victory lap. And the hurricane destroying every other shrimp boat while sparing theirs is fortune eliminating the competition, which is exactly the kind of arbitrary favoritism Machiavelli complains about in his Caesars and Borgias.

So my questions: does the film dramatize a real objection to Machiavelli, namely that fortune is not actually tamed by preparation and boldness, and that the whole virtù framework is survivorship bias — we call it virtù when the gambler wins? Or is Forrest's adaptability itself a kind of virtù (the flexibility Machiavelli praises, the man who can adjust as fortune shifts), dressed up as innocence? And where does Jenny fit — is the film quietly moralizing her suffering as the price of agency, which would make it not anti-Machiavellian at all but weirdly cruel? I want a reading that takes both the film and the actual Prince chapters seriously, points me to specific scenes I could show back-to-back with the text, and tells me honestly where the lens breaks — e.g. whether Forrest's simplicity is doing philosophical work or is just a sentimental device the Machiavellian frame can't digest. Also flag if you think I'm leaning on a contested reading of virtù (I know some scholars want to translate it as something closer to resourceful manliness or craft rather than virtue vs. luck simpliciter, and if my framing depends on the crude version, say so).

If you think the whole exercise is confused — say, because Machiavelli is describing princes in political time, not private lives, so mapping him onto a Hollywood fable about an innocent is a category error from the start — please just tell me that plainly rather than manufacturing a reading to make my movie night seem profound. I can take it. But if there's something real there, I'd like to build one class session on it, so concrete scene references and the strongest version of the case would genuinely help.
