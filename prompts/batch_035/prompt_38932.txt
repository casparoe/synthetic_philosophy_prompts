I've been studying international law, and for the past year something about the practice of recognizing governments has bothered me in a way I can't quite articulate. When a new regime takes power — by election, by coup, by victory in a civil war — other states eventually extend recognition, exchange ambassadors, and resume treaty relations once it exercises stable control over a territory and population, more or less regardless of how it came to power. There is lip service to legitimacy, and the occasional refusal to recognize a junta, but the working criterion in practice is effectiveness: control, population, territory, and a plausible willingness to honor international obligations. Yet we are taught that the entire point of law, domestic or international, is that it stands on something other than sheer power. On its face, then, the practice seems to codify at the level of states exactly the view of political legitimacy that Pascal skewered in the Pensées — his observation that justice without force is impotent, force without justice tyrannical, and that, since we could not make what is just strong, we ended up making what is strong just, so that the apparent justice of any established order is really just its power wearing respectable clothes. Everyone involved officially denies that might makes right, while the institutional machinery quietly operates as though it does.

I'm not convinced, though, that the practice really presupposes anything that strong. Maybe recognition is agnostic about legitimacy, a mere bookkeeping rule for identifying whom to address obligations to; maybe it rests on a Hobbesian thought that the sovereign is simply whoever is habitually obeyed; maybe it reflects the calculation that any criterion of legitimacy would itself be enforced by whoever has force, so we may as well be honest about it. What I want from you is a reconstruction of what the recognition practice actually presupposes about political legitimacy. Does it require anything as strong as Pascal's resigned identification of justice with force, or is it philosophically underdetermined, compatible with several rival theories — consent-based, effectiveness-based, purely pragmatic — and if so, which parts of the practice, if any, would have to change if it were rebuilt on a consent-based theory of legitimacy instead? I'd also like your view on whether Pascal's own distinction between the order of force and the order of justice gives us the vocabulary for saying what the practice gets wrong, or whether his position, taken seriously, in fact vindicates the practice as the most honest arrangement available to us.
