I'm on the board of a small regional museum built around an Iron Age burial site excavated in the 1970s. Our permanent exhibition presents confident reconstructions of the site's religion: grave goods are labelled as offerings to the ancestors, a bank and ditch is described as a processional way, and the person interred with the richest assemblage is called a priest. A curator has begun arguing that this entire presentation rests on a chain of inferences, each of which is underdetermined by the material record: the same assemblage of objects is compatible with very different practices; the same practice is compatible with very different beliefs; and — as ethnography of living communities repeatedly shows — people doing materially indistinguishable things can give radically different accounts of what they are doing and why. On her telling, our labels quietly assume a whole picture of institutions, offices, and doctrines that the objects by themselves cannot vouch for.

Please describe the most important implications of this underdetermination problem for a museum like ours. In particular: how should it change what we are prepared to assert on labels and in guided tours; whether and how we should present competing reconstructions of the same evidence; what work the vocabulary of ritual, cult, and priest is actually doing, and whether we can keep using it with appropriate hedging; and how we should weigh the educational value of a vivid, memorable story against the epistemic honesty of saying plainly what we do not know. I would also like you to distinguish the implications that follow from the underdetermination itself — from the nature of the inferential gap between objects, practices, and beliefs — from those that depend on further commitments, for instance about a museum's obligations to its visitors or to descendant communities, rather than treating them all as equally forced by the argument.
