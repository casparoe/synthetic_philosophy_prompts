I'm in a political theory reading group, second year, and we've been going round and round on the boundary problem of the demos -- who exactly is entitled to a say in a democratic decision -- for the better part of a year now, and I've started to suspect that nobody in the room is actually disagreeing about anything empirical. Briefly, the set-up as it plays out with us: everyone agrees "the people" entitled to rule isn't literally everyone on earth and also isn't just whoever currently holds citizenship, but when we try to say who's in, the same three positions keep coming back. One group pushes the all-affected view: everyone affected by a decision should get a vote, so for something like carbon regulation that's basically everyone alive and arguably future people too. A second group goes with all-subjected: it's whoever is under the coercive power of the law in question. And a third keeps insisting on something like a stakeholder view -- anyone whose interests the decision is supposed to take into account counts, whether or not they literally cast a ballot.

Here's why I think we're stuck. Whenever we test the views against hard cases, each side concedes the counterexample but then says the principle was never meant to be applied case-by-case like that. The subjected people agree that a tourist hit by a decision isn't thereby owed a vote (fine, tourists are "affected but not subject"), and the affected people agree that the same tourist passing through airport security is technically subject to the law but obviously shouldn't be enfranchised on that basis. So each principle gets quietly narrowed until, in practice, all three converge on roughly the same extension: permanent residents. At which point I genuinely can't tell whether we've discovered that the three views coincide, or whether we've each just smuggled in the same answer and dressed it up in diferent vocabulary -- and that distinction seems to matter a lot for whether this debate is going anywhere.

My working hypothesis is that it's mostly a verbal dispute about what "the demos" is supposed to pick out, but one thing keeps not fitting that diagnosis, and I want you to weigh it. The all-affected people in my group don't back off even when their principle gets restricted -- they say the narrowing is pragmatic, for a world with borders, and that the principle stays ideal, so future generations and (sometimes) non-resident foreigners are genuinely owed votes in principle, full stop. That doesn't look verbal to me; it looks like a real moral disagreement about how far democratic standing extends. So: diagnose this thing. Is it a verbal dispute, a substantive moral disagreement hiding behind a shared set of cases, or are the three camps actually answering three separate questions -- who should vote, whose interests must be counted, who may permissibly be coerced -- under a single heading? And given whatever diagnosis you land on, tell me honestly what would count as progress here: a distinction, a decision procedure, a particular kind of test case -- or whether I should just expect the debate to stay stuck, and why. I'd rather have an answer I don't like than a survey of the positions, we've done that already.
