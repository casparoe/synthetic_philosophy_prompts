I'm giving a guest lecture at a military academy next month defending the traditionalist view of combatant equality: soldiers on both sides of a war are moral equals, entitled to kill each other without committing murder, regardless of whose cause is just. My angle is mostly institutional rather than the old mutual-consent story — I want to argue that even if the revisionists are right in the abstract about who's liable to be killed, soldiers can't be the ones adjudicating their own war's justice, and that this isn't just pragmatics but a real point about what individual combatants can be responsible for. The audience is a few hundred cadets plus one philosophy instructor who's a committed revisionist and will come armed, and some officers who think the whole debate is academic hand-wringing. Prep my Q&A sheet: the objections in roughly the order they'll actually come, phrased the way a cadet would put them and not the way a journal article would, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Include the one I'm hoping nobody asks — I suspect it's about whether my argument covers volunteers in wars of aggression who sincerelly believed they were doing right, and I'd like to know if you think that's actually the weakest spot or whether something else is. One more thing: keep straight for me what's settled versus genuinely contested among people who work on this — I don't want to present the revisionist critique as fringe when it's arguably the field's majority view now, and I equally don't want cadets walking out thinking nothing about a war's justice filters down to individual conduct at all.
