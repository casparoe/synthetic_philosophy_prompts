I'm writing a final paper for a philosophy of mind course, and my supervisor keeps reaching for the same comparison: introspection is just perception turned inward. On this picture there is an inner sense that scans one's own mental states the way vision scans the environment, and she uses it to draw a specific conclusion — that introspective beliefs are like perceptual beliefs in being the output of a faculty that is generally reliable even though it occasionally misfires, so the experimental cases where people confabulate reasons for their own choices or report attitudes they don't act on are like visual illusions: interesting exceptions that don't discredit the faculty as a whole. My partner, who does psychology, thinks the comparison is doing all the work and doing it badly: perception has the checks and balances of a world that pushes back — other senses, other observers, instruments — while introspection faces no independent tribunal, since the only access to the mental state in question runs through the very faculty being checked. On her view, that means the analogy can't even make sense of introspective error in the way it needs to, let alone support my supervisor's cautious confidence in the faculty.

Help me audit the comparison properly before I build the paper on it. Lay out the mapping explicitly — what in introspection is supposed to play the role of the sense organ, the external object, attention, and the gap between how things look and how they are — then say which parts of the mapping are load-bearing for my supervisor's conclusion and where the analogy breaks down. Two places I especially want your judgment: first, whether introspective states allow anything like the appearance/reality gap that makes perceptual illusion possible — can one's own current headache, say, present as other than it is, or does the idea of an introspective illusion presuppose a picture of the mind that the perceptual model smuggles in? Second, whether the absence of an external check really makes reliability claims empty, or whether there are structural checks — prediction, later remembering, coherence across time and between verbally and behaviorally expressed attitudes — that could do some of the work the world does for perception, and whether they are enough. I'd also like your view on whether some better comparison is available (introspection as memory-like, as a kind of self-interpretation from evidence, as constitutive rather than observational), or whether the failure of the perceptual analogy tells us something about what introspection must be instead of just what it isn't.

Since the paper will be graded on care, one request about how you frame all of this: keep a clear line between what is settled among people who work on this and what is genuinely contested. As I understand it, that introspection sometimes errs is not in dispute, but what the confabulation findings actually show about reliability, and whether the inner sense model is viable at all, very much are. Don't let a contested reading of the analogy or of the empirical cases pass as the consensus; where the field splits, tell me roughly along which lines it splits and what would move someone from one side to the other.
