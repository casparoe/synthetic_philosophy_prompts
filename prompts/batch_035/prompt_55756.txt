Retired physicist here (materials science, semiconductors mostly, 34 years at a national lab). Since retiring I've gotten deep into philosophy of mind, and my reading group has now spent two whole meetings on the Lucas–Penrose thing: the idea that Gödel's incompleteness theorem shows human mathematicians can't be Turing machines. I want to make a handout that sorts people in the group by what they actually believe, and I've realized it has to be a decision tree, not a list of names. The debate doesn't really split along "mechanist vs anti-mechanist" like the pop books say. It splits on much smaller hinges, and people who agree on the big labels end up on opposite sides of the small ones.

The tree should start from a point everyone at the meeting could accept, something like: for any consistent formal system that's strong enough for arithmetic, there's a true arithmetical sentence it can't prove. From there each branch node should be a question a person can answer without knowing which position it's leading them to. I have a draft set of nodes but I'm not sure it's the right carving, so fix it if there's a better one:

1. To make the Gödel move against a machine, does the human have to see that the machine is consistent/sound -- and if yes, is that something a fallible, coffee-stained human can actually have, or only an idealized "perfect mathematician"? (Half the group swore Lucas meant real mathematicians, the other half said it only works for an idealization.)
2. Does the conclusion collapse if we can't know which machine we are? I.e., the argument kills "I am machine M" for each specific M we get to inspect, but maybe it leaves open that we're some machine we'll never pin down. One guy swears Putnam pressed something like this and calls it the "existential vs specific" escape.
3. Could we ever know our own consistency? A machine can't prove its own consistency, but neither can we -- we're a mess of heuristics and we make errors. If neither side gets to know it, maybe the symmetry saves mechanism. One member said Gödel himself was more careful than Lucas and thought the argument needs the extra assumption that the mind can see its own consistency.
4. If the anti-mechanist conclusion goes through, does it follow that some physical process in the brain is non-computable (which I take it is Penrose's move, since he then has to go hunting for it in physics), or does it only tell us something about "mathematical understanding" in the abstract?
5. Do our errors and self-corrections matter? The mechanist can say humans are just inconsistent systems that occasionally assert falsehoods, so Gödel doesn't bite. But then is the anti-mechanist allowed to stipulate that we're at least consistent enough for the argument to run?

Leaves I'd like to see, with the name the literature uses or "unnamed" if nobody defends it: the strong Lucas view (mechanism is flat false, we can out-Gödel any machine), the idealized-mathematician version, the Penrosean view (non-computable physics plus a story about consciousness), the "we're a machine but not a knowably sound one" mechanist, the "we're inconsistent and that's fine" mechanist, and the deflationary view that the whole argument just slides between what a theorem says and what would follow from a theorem. If your tree can't actually seperate two of these -- I suspect the last two mechanists -- flag it, don't paper over it with a question that doesn't really split them.

Two more asks. If there's a position my five nodes can't place anywhere (something about robots that respond to Gödel sentences, or about whether the target of the argument should be "formal systems" vs "Turing machines" vs "algorithms"), add the node for it. And at the end, tell me which single node does the most work. If the group could settle that one question, half the debate collapses, and I want to run the next meeting on exactly that. Keep in mind our audience includes one engineer who will answer any hypothetical you throw at him but refuses to learn any jargon, so every node has to be phrased for him.
