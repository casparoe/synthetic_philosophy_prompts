Last night a friend and I spent four hours arguing about Kavka's toxin puzzle — the one where a billionaire will put a million dollars in your bank account at noon if, at that point, you intend to drink a mildly debilitating but harmless toxin tomorrow afternoon, with the money yours whether or not you ever drink. My friend took the line that intention is a policy rather than a prediction: you can simply resolve to drink, keep resolving, and collect, and a fully rational person does exactly that. I took the line that an intention I know I will have decisive reason to abandon at the last moment is not something I can hold, only something I can perform. By the end we were no longer friends but rival decision theorists, and neither of us had a million dollars.

Help me settle the score indirectly. Write a satirical piece: the landing-page copy and FAQ section, roughly 600–700 words, for a fictional Silicon Valley startup — call it Resolve, or Precommitr, or whatever suits it — that sells "intention infrastructure" to people who have been offered the billionaire's deal. Deadpan growth-team voice throughout, no winking at the reader. The product is a suite of commitment devices: escrowed antidotes, auto-donating the million to a cause the client despises if they fail to drink, escalating reminder notifications, a moderated community of fellow intending drinkers.

The humor has to come from the actual content of the puzzle rather than from generic startup satire. It should know the difference between intending to drink and merely predicting that one will; the difference between a rationality in which drinking is irrational whenever you reach the decision point and one in which forming the intention is what pays; whether the billionaire's payment scheme screens for genuine intention or only correlates with it. It should notice that clients who can already form the intention need the service least and are therefore its most profitable customers, and that the startup keeps attracting exactly the people it cannot help. Include a testimonials section with (at least) two clients: one who bought every tier of device and still couldn't collect, and one who drank the toxin without ever being offered any money and is somehow the most satisfied customer of all. Include a pricing page that quietly charges different rates depending on the client's decision theory. The FAQ should slowly fall apart on the question of whether the service forms intentions or merely raises the cost of abandoning them, without ever admitting that anything has fallen apart.

Crucially, the piece should be fair to both sides of my argument: a reader should not be able to tell whether the founders believe intention is directly choosable (my friend's view) or only engineerable-around (mine), and ideally the satire should be unfair to both of us in ways neither of us has thought of yet. Invented founders and clients only; no real philosophers or decision theorists named; no citations or further reading.
