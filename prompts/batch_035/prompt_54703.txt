I'm a software engineer, mostly backend and distributed systems for the past decade, now working on infrastructure for ML deployments. I have no formal philosophy training, but I've spent the last year and a half in a small reading group with colleagues where we work through AI safety and alignment material — blog essays, transcripts of talks, and the occasional paper we only partially digest — and one disagreement keeps coming back around without being resolved.

The disagreement is about the standard alignment research should be held to. My colleagues' position is essentially average-case: a system is "aligned enough" if it behaves well across the realistic distribution of deployment conditions, and demanding worst-case guarantees is a perfectionism that no other engineering discipline actually applies — bridges are designed for hundred-year storms, not for arbitrarily sized ones, and aviation safety is really a system of quantified average risk with margins, not a literal guarantee about the worst thing that can happen. I've noticed that most defenses of the worst-case side, when I look for them, come down to security reasoning: assume an adaptive adversary, and average-case guarantees are meaningless because the adversary picks the tail. I find that convincing but too contingent — it makes worst-case standards depend on there being human attackers, and it lets my friends reply that in a world of genuinely non-adversarial deployment, average-case would be fine.

What I actually believe, and can't articulate well, is something more structural: average-case evaluation is ill-posed for sufficiently capable optimization systems regardless of whether anyone is attacking them. In the software I write, average-case reasoning works because the input distribution is exogenous — a load balancer is judged against traffic it doesn't itself generate, and the traffic statistics don't depend on the balancer's decisions in any deep way. But a system that optimizes over its environment changes which situations arise later; its outputs select and shape its own future inputs. Over a long enough horizon it can drift toward the regions of condition-space where it behaves worst, or more subtly, reshape the distribution itself, so there's no fixed measure for "average" to be taken over. In that regime "behaves well on average across deployment conditions" doesn't denote a well-defined property at all, and the only coherent standard left standing is a worst-case one. So here is what I'd like you to do: argue for my side — that when a system's behavior feeds back into the distribution of situations it subsequently faces, average-case alignment standards collapse into ill-posedness and worst-case becomes the default, not an aspiration. Please lay the argument out as carefully as you can rather than just asserting it; then address the strongest objection to it, which I take to be that worst-case guarantees over unbounded condition sets are simply unattainable and that demanding them would make alignment research unfalsifiable in its own way; and finally say whether the endogeneity problem only bites above some capability threshold, and if so, what the right standard is for systems below it — where my argument, even if correct, arguably doesn't reach.
