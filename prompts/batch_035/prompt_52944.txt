I've come to hold a position about the philosophical weight of computational complexity theory, and I want to know what would count against it. The view, stated carefully: P versus NP matters to philosophy not primarily as an open mathematical problem but because the answer, together with contingent facts about which algorithms actually exist, amounts to something like a law of nature governing what can be found, known, and kept secret. Call it the hardness-as-law view. On this view, the find/check asymmetry — that verifying a candidate solution can be polynomially easier than finding one — is the deepest available explanation of three otherwise scattered phenomena: the possibility of public-key cryptography, and with it privacy and coordination at scale among strangers; the intelligibility of treating mathematical discovery as an achievement rather than a chore, since anyone with patience can check a proof but only insight finds one; and the non-arbitrariness of distinguishing insight from brute search. I'm influenced by the way Impagliazzo's five worlds — Algorithmica through Cryptomania — frames average-case hardness as a contingent feature of our universe, and I take our actual world to be one in which worst-case hardness and useful average-case hardness are both robust, load-bearing facts rather than artifacts of a formalism.

Play it straight and tell me what would have to be true for this to be wrong. In particular:

1. What developments would decisively count against it? I can think of some candidates myself — a proof that P = NP; a practical quantum computer that breaks enough of the relevant structure; the continued march of SAT solvers and heuristic methods quietly dissolving problems we had classified as hard — but I want to know whether these really touch the view itself or only its applications, and what I'm missing.

2. What evidence is merely awkward, and how would a committed holder of the view absorb it? For instance, the observation that the find/check asymmetry was sociologically real long before complexity theory — apprentices checked craftsmen's output, referees check papers — and might survive even in Algorithmica, which would suggest the philosophy doesn't really need the complexity theory.

The insulation worry: since the view can always retreat from average-case hardness to worst-case hardness, or from efficient-in-practice to polynomial-in-principle, does it risk being qualified into unfalsifiability? And if so, is that a defect for a view of this hybrid kind — partly mathematical, partly empirical — or just how foundational hypotheses work?
