I sit on a regional clinical ethics committee, and next month I have twenty minutes to defend a proposal to recast our guidance on decision-making for patients with advanced dementia around a relational conception of personhood — the Ubuntu-inflected idea that a person's standing is not an individual cognitive achievement but something partly constituted by and held in trust through their web of relationships, so that a patient's moral status does not fluctuate with her mini-mental score, and the question "what would she want" becomes "what do her relationships, carried by those who know her, say she would want."

The committee I face is small but sharp, and I need to be ready for each of them specifically:

- a behavioral neurologist who holds that moral status is a matter of sentience and nothing else, and suspects the rest is sentiment;
- an analytic philosopher by training who thinks "relational personhood" conflates the criteria for being a person with the social consequences of being one, and will press me on whether I'm making a metaphysical claim, an ethical one, or a rhetorical one;
- a disability rights advocate who has watched relational and consciousness-based language get used, in courts and at bedsides, to justify withdrawing treatment from people deemed insufficiently connected or aware, and who will not be soothed by good intentions;
- a hospital administrator who will ask, fairly, what the rewrite actually changes at 2 a.m. on a ward when a resident physician has to decide whether to escalate care.

Prepare my Q&A sheet. List the objections roughly in the order they will arrive, each phrased as that member would phrase it, ranked by how much damage it could do to the proposal. For each: what I should concede up front, a reply I can actually deliver in about thirty seconds of live exchange, and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks — I suspect it is the advocate's, and that my current answer to it ("relational views protect, they don't diminish") is a hope rather than an argument. If that is so, tell me what the argument would have to be, and whether I have it. Do not write a defense of the proposal itself; write the prep sheet.
