In a seminar on Leibniz we reached the claim that God chooses the best of all possible worlds, and a classmate defended it with an analogy that has stuck with me: God selecting a world is like a composer writing a symphony. The composer needs dissonance and tension — the minor passages, the clashes that resolve — because a piece of pure consonance is worse, not better; so the evils of this world are the dissonances required by the best overall arrangement, and to complain about evil is like a listener complaining that the symphony contains a minor chord. The analogy was doing real work in the discussion: it was meant to show that Leibniz's theodicy makes evil intelligible as instrumental to a greater good, and that our protests against it are aesthetic mistakes rather than moral ones. Please audit it. Lay out what corresponds to what — the composer, the score, the laws of counterpoint, the perspective of the listener, the dissonant chord itself — and then show where the mapping breaks: which of Leibniz's actual commitments it captures (the selection among incompossible worlds, the ordering by simplicity of laws and richness of phenomena, the claim that God chooses rather than makes the natures of things) and which it quietly distorts, and whether Leibniz's own distinction between metaphysical, physical, and moral evil can even be rendered in the symphony picture. In particular, tell me whether the analogy can carry the conclusion it was deployed for, or whether it succeeds only by turning evil into something that happens within the music rather than to someone, and what a Leibnizian who saw the disanalogies clearly should put in its place.
