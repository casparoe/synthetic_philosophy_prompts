In my historiography seminar, the professor explains the outbreak of the First World War with what she calls the powder-keg picture: Europe in 1914 was a keg of dry powder — the alliance blocs, the naval race, the mobilization timetables — and the assassination at Sarajevo was merely the spark. The conclusion she draws is stronger than the usual "overdetermined" claim: the assassination was not the cause of the war in any interesting sense; asking what caused the war is a question about the keg rather than the spark; and treating the July decisions of particular statesmen as the cause is, she says, a category mistake. A friend who works on causal modeling objects that the analogy is smuggling in its conclusion: whether the spark counts as a cause turns on whether the keg is a background condition or a standing cause, and nothing in the picture settles that — it simply paints the keg inert and the spark vivid, and lets the paint do the argument's work.

Please audit the analogy for me, in five steps. First, the correspondence table: say what exactly plays the role of the powder, the keg, the dryness, the spark, and — if the mapping has one — the hand that strikes the match, and be honest about where the mapping runs out of parts of the source to assign. Second, the breakdown points: which differences between a keg and Europe actually bear on her conclusion — for instance, that powder does not interpret the spark, so all sparks are interchangeable, whereas the specific content, victim, and timing of the Sarajevo assassination shaped the diplomatic maneuvering that followed; that a keg cannot respond to a near-miss by becoming wetter, whereas states and alliances do react to crises that fizzle; or that the picture offers only two roles, fuel and spark, while serious causal analysis of a large event wants more categories — INUS conditions, necessary components of a sufficient set, standing conditions, triggers — and the analogy quietly forces every factor into one of its two. Third, sort the disanalogies: I don't care that a keg is inanimate in general, only insofar as that bears on whether the assassination is demoted; tell me which of the breakdowns matter for her conclusion and which are decorative. Fourth, the verdict: given genuine overdetermination — that some great-power war was fairly likely with or without this particular spark — can she still get the stronger claims, that the assassination was not a cause at all and that singular-cause talk about such events is misplaced, or does the analogy only support the weaker, almost trivial reading that the spark was not necessary? Fifth, the repair: give me the best revised version a structuralist could offer, or a better analogy if one exists, that preserves the overdetermination insight without the question-begging demotion of the trigger.

Some context for the audit: I am writing a seminar paper on whether historians should ever ask for the cause of a large event rather than for a set of conditions, and I need to know exactly how much weight the powder-keg picture can carry before I rest anything on it — so treat the fifth step not as a concession to the structuralist but as a test of what the insight really is once the rhetorical apparatus is stripped off.
