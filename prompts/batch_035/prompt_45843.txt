Next month I'm presenting at a workshop on anthropic reasoning, and I need a Q&A prep sheet for the question period.

My thesis, in two parts. First: the self-indication assumption — roughly, the view that finding yourself to exist should raise your credence in hypotheses on which more observers exist — is not an exotic amendment to Bayesianism but a consequence of conditioning correctly on your own existence. Second, and this is the part I expect to bleed: the presumptuous philosopher is a feature, not a bug. In that thought experiment, the philosopher is told only that one of two empirically equivalent theories is true and that one of them posits vastly more observers than the other, and claims on those grounds alone to know, with enormous confidence, which. I want to say the philosopher's credence shift is real but inert: it is licensed only where rival hypotheses fix their observer counts directly, and it licenses credences — never announcements, interventions, or bets whose settlement requires the populous world to be actual.

The audience is mixed and mostly hostile: formal epistemologists who regard anthropic reasoning as probability theory's astrological branch; two cosmologists from the fine-tuning literature who suspect that anything boosting worlds with more observers is a multiverse smuggled in a priori, in violation of parsimony; and one steadfast defender of the Carter–Leslie doomsday argument who reads the self-indication assumption's cancellation of doomsday as motivated reasoning. There will also be a quiet sympathizer of Radford Neal's full non-indexical conditioning, who thinks that ordinary conditioning on all of one's non-indexical evidence already does everything my assumption purports to do, with none of the paradoxes.

Draft the objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do, and phrased in their vocabularies: the double-counting charge (haven't I already accounted for my existence in choosing a prior at all?), the parsimony violation, whether the self-indication assumption really escapes the reference-class problem it was recruited to solve, the Boltzmann-brain measure problem the cosmologists will raise, and the doomsday defender's charge that cancelling doomsday is too convenient. For each objection: a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Finally, include the question I am hoping nobody asks — I suspect it is the one about infinite worlds, where the assumption's weighting over observers goes improper, but tell me if there is a worse one.
