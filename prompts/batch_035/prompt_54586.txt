I spent thirty-five years in condensed matter physics before retiring, and for the past two years I have been working my way into the AI safety literature, partly through a reading group of former colleagues. The topic that has hooked me is what people call mechanistic anomaly detection: rather than flagging unusual outputs, one inspects whether an input is processed by the same internal machinery that handled the benign training distribution — the idea being that a misaligned model may behave normally on ordinary inputs while running some quite different computation on the ones that matter. What strikes a physicist immediately is that the entire program rests on a notion nobody has pinned down: what does it mean for two inputs, or two models, or two training runs, to be processed "by the same mechanism"? In my old field we spent decades learning that questions like this — when are two systems in the same universality class? — cannot be answered at the level of raw microscopic detail, because the same macroscopic behavior can be realized by wildly different microconfigurations, and near-identical microconfigurations can drift apart under coarse-graining. I suspect mechanistic interpretability is facing the same problem with none of the conceptual tools.

So here is what I want: a specification, not a survey. Write the desiderata that any adequate account of mechanistic sameness must meet if it is to serve anomaly detection in particular. To seed your thinking, consider the constraints that strike me as natural but that you should feel free to challenge or refine: the account should be invariant under retraining (a fresh run of the same architecture on the same data presumably implements "the same mechanism" through different weights, so any notion defined directly on parameter values is dead on arrival), invariant under permutation symmetries and other re-parameterizations of the network, stable under coarse-graining in something like the renormalization-group sense — factoring out implementation details that don't matter for the function — yet not so coarse that it collapses into pure input-output equivalence, since behaviorally identical models can presumably implement different mechanisms and detecting that difference is the whole point. There is also, it seems to me, a decidability requirement the physics analogy does not capture: the account must be operationalizable by an inspector with finite access to a fixed network, which may rule out definitions I would find natural, like the topological conjugacy one uses for dynamical systems. And there is an adversarial requirement: an account that is too sharp gives an adversary a target — a backdoor trigger engineered to sit just barely outside the equivalence class of benign processing. For each desideratum, give the motivating case and say whether it is genuinely non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp. Then, and this is the part I most want to see: if some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — for instance, if coarse-grained stability and adversarial sharpness pull in opposite directions — say so explicitly, because then the specification itself is the result. Finally, a sentence or two on which single requirement does the most to exclude the obvious candidate accounts (behavioral distributional tests, activation-level clustering, circuit-level and causal-intervention analyses), and where you think the interesting tension lies. No citations, please — I will match the positions to the literature myself.
