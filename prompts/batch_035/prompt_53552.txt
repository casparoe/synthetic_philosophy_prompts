I teach a mid-level elective called Decision Theory for Philosophers — about 18 students, mostly philosophy majors with a few economics students mixed in, and one 75-minute session per week. Next month we reach the two-envelope paradox, and I've noticed from past years that students handle it badly in a specific way: they follow the classic 1.25x expected-value calculation for switching perfectly well, agree it seems to imply you should switch, agree that switching twice by the same reasoning implies a guaranteed gain from switching back, and then just shrug — they file it as one of those paradoxes where the math is fine but intuition is broken, and move on. What I want instead is for them to feel where the reasoning actually goes wrong, not just be told. My idea is to run the paradox as a live experiment with real (if small) stakes, but I need help designing it properly.

Design a full in-class activity around the two-envelope problem. The rough shape I'm imagining: students are paired off, each pair gets two sealed envelopes prepared by me in advance, one containing twice the other, they each take one, and then we stage a decision point where they can pay a small fee to swap. I'd like it to run in two rounds — one where the distribution I used to fill the envelopes is hidden from them, and one where I announce it in advance (say, a simple bounded distribution over powers of two) — so they can experience the difference between reasoning from "it's either double or half, fifty-fifty" and reasoning from an actual prior. Materials and payoff scheme should use a token currency rather than real money, redeemable for something modest like dropping the two lowest homework grades, and the mechanics need to be simple enough that a pair can complete a round in under five minutes. I'd also like a whole-class tallying step where we aggregate outcomes across pairs and look at what the data does and doesn't show.

Then the debrief: please map the specific moments in the activity onto the specific moves in the paradox — why the unconditional swap calculation feels right in the hidden-distribution round and falls apart in the announced-distribution round, what the aggregate data says about whether switching "really" gains on average, and how this connects to the fact that the naive argument secretly assumes something like a uniform prior over an unbounded range, which can't exist. I want the debrief to end with students able to say precisely which premise of the switch-argument is false, not just that the conclusion is absurd. Please also include: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (my guess is some pairs will try to open the envelopes early or collude; there may be others), how to handle it, and an honest note on which feature of the paradox no finite classroom experiment can reproduce — I suspect the real action is in the tails and in infinite expectations, and I'd like you to say plainly what the facilitator has to supply by argument because the activity can't show it. If you think the two-round structure I sketched is the wrong shape, redesign it — I care about the pedagogical goal, not my draft.
