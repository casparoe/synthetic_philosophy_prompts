I'm a quant trader — stat arb mostly, a bit of systematic macro on the side — so my native vocabulary for thinking about outcomes is things like: edge (positive expected return conditional on what you know), variance and vol, exogenous vs. endogenous risk, tail hedges, position sizing and Kelly, drawdown control, regime shifts, ergodicity stuff (I've read the Ole Peters papers even though I'm skeptical), and Taleb's convexity/antifragility language. Philosophy is not my thing, but I picked up The Prince on vacation and got to the chapter on fortune (ch. 25 I think) and it weirdly felt like home. As I recall he says fortune is the arbiter of about half our actions and leaves the other half to us, then he has this image of fortune as a violent river that floods the whole plain unless you've built dikes and embankments during calm weather — and then, in a strange digression, he says fortune favors bold, impetuous young men over cautious ones, with some remark about her being a woman and hence needing to be handled boldly.

Here's what I want: translate fortuna and virtù into my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. My first instinct is that fortuna is just exogenous variance — the part of the P&L I don't control — virtù is edge, and the dike-building passage is just risk management, cutting tails in quiet times before the vol arrives. But I suspect that translation is too easy and flattens him, so don't let me get away with it.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts map cleanly, which map only if you distort them (my guess is the fortune-favors-the-impetuous bit is where it gets interesting — is that just convexity, sizing up because the payoff distribution is right-skewed, or is he claiming something that doesn't reduce to an expected-value story at all?), and what has no expression in a trader's vocabulary. One thing to keep in mind: virtù for Machiavelli isn't just skill — it seems to include knowing when cruelty is useful, being willing to break promises when circumstances change, and matching your methods to the times rather than sticking to one temperament. Where does that stuff go? "Regime-adaptive strategy" feels like it captures some of it but maybe not the part about being feared or about ruthlessness as a component of excellence.

So finish with a verdict: is the residue a defect of my vocabulary — did Machiavelli actually see something about acting under uncertainty that traders systematically miss — or is it a sign that his claims were never really about what I'd call uncertainty in the first place, e.g. partly political/moral claims that only look like risk management when you squint at them? And keep the translation honest — if something of his only sounds like antifragility because Taleb also read him, say so rather than making the mapping look tighter than it is.
