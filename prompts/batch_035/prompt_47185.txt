At our board game night two friends got into it over the two-envelope thing — one envelope has twice as much money as the other, you pick one, and the question is whether you should switch. Dave had picked it up from a puzzle thread online and couldnt let it go.

(1) Dave's opening move was the classic one: say you open your envelope and see $100. The other envelope is equally likely to hold $50 or $200 — nothing in the setup favors either, right? So the expected value of switching is $125, which beats $100. And he said this works for *any* amount you see, so you should allways switch, whatever you see.

(2) Maya said that can't be right: the same argument works from whichever envelope you're holding, so it 'proves' you should switch, and then, once you're holding the other one, that you should switch back. Endless 25% gains from ending up where you started. So somewhere the argument broke, and she said the broken bit is 'equally likely' — it's not a free gift, it depends on how the amounts were chosen in the first place.

(3) Dave conceded the formula can't hold across the board but said that's actually where it gets interesting. However the amounts were generated, there's some typical range or ceiling. So when you see $100, that observation is evidence: if $100 is huge compared to what the process usually produces, you're probably holding the bigger envelope, so keep it; if it's small, switch. His original claim survives in modified form — the naive calculation isn't just a dumb mistake, it's what you get if you wrongly treat 'bigger or smaller, equally likely' as carrying no information about the process behind the envelopes.

(4) Maya wasn't done. She said if that's all it is, then the puzzle has no bite at all for someone who never opens their envelope: before you look, the expected gain from switching is exactly zero by symmetry, so there's nothing to decide, and Dave's 'modified claim' only kicks in after you look. Dave said that was the deepest part, not a concession — the puzzle is a story about the gap between what's true before you look and what's true after, and the naive calc smuggles the before-looking symmetry into an after-looking claim. Maya said he was agreeing with her while making it sound like he'd won. They parked it and told me to referee, and honestly each of them sounded right to me while they were saying it.
