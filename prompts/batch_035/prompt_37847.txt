I am a graduate teaching assistant, and next week I must lead a fifty-minute tutorial on Benacerraf's 1973 dilemma in the epistemology of mathematics for a group of second-year philosophy undergraduates. They have nominally read the paper, though in my experience half of them will have read only the first three pages, and the other half will have read a different Benacerraf paper entirely and not noticed. Please do not write the explanation for me; I want a plan for the tutorial. Specifically, I would like you to address the following. First, the sequence: which components have to be established, in what order, and what depends on what — in particular, does the plausibility of the 'combinatorial' picture of mathematical truth have to be secured before the causal picture of knowledge is introduced, or is it more effective to run them in the opposite direction so that the tension lands as a surprise? Second, the carrying example: I need one concrete case to hold the whole session together. It should presumably involve the contrast between how we know about ordinary middle-sized objects and how we know about numbers, but I would like you to specify what the example must contain and what it must omit, and to advise me on whether the pair of schoolboys from the earlier 1965 paper on the identity of numbers (Ernie and Johnny) is a help or a hazard in this session. Third, the misconceptions: I anticipate that students will arrive with several, including the beliefs that Benacerraf is plainly refuting Platonism, that the dilemma shows abstract objects do not exist, that the causal theory of knowledge is so obviously wrong that the second horn collapses trivially, and — most stubbornly — that the identification problem from 1965 (the one about which set the number three is) and the access problem from 1973 are the same problem under different titles. For each misconception you predict, tell me at exactly which point in the sequence it should be headed off, and how. Fourth, the boundary: what should I openly decline to cover at this level — for instance, whether to say anything at all about the various escape routes (revising the epistemology, revising the semantics, or denying the ontology) beyond flagging that they exist — and how I should frame that refusal to the students. Finally, give me one question I could pose in the last five minutes whose answers would reveal whether they have genuinely understood the dilemma, rather than merely memorised that it is called a dilemma. Please answer in British English.
