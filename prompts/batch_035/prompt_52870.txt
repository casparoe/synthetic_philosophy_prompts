I'm a PhD student in political theory working on a dissertation chapter about inherited responsibility in postwar German thought, and I want to test a literary case study against a philosophical framework. Take the role of a professional philosopher specializing in collective responsibility and postwar German intellectual history, who also reads novels carefully. The material: Bernhard Schlink's novel The Reader (and, where it differs, the 2008 film adaptation) read against Karl Jaspers' four-fold distinction from The Question of German Guilt between criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt. Please begin with some quiet brainstorming about how the novel's central relationships map onto the four categories before committing to a reading, then write the reading.

The questions I need answered: Which scenes does each category illuminate -- Hanna's trial for the church fire, Michael's silence about her illiteracy, the narrator's generation-wide shame at their parents, the encounter with the survivor in New York? Does the criminal trial capture what Hanna did, or does the novel show the law convicting her of the wrong thing (the report) because the right thing exceeds legal categories? Is Michael's shame Jaspers' metaphysical guilt, his moral guilt, or a fifth thing Jaspers' 1946 lecture lacks -- guilt by intimacy rather than by citizenship? And where does the novel resist the framework rather than illustrate it: does the illiteracy device quietly convert a moral question into an epistemic one, and does framing the perpetrator–descendant relation as a love story depoliticize what Jaspers insisted was political? I want a reading faithful in both directions -- one that doesn't distort Schlink to fit Jaspers, concedes honestly where the novel resists, and says at the end what fiction adds to the guilt debate that Jaspers' lecture cannot. Work from the plot and the concepts; no citations or bibliography needed.
