I am a PhD student in philosophy writing a dissertation chapter on risk and rational choice, and I would like your help thinking through the practical consequences of risk-weighted expected utility theory — the view, associated with Lara Buchak's work, on which a rational agent may weight the worse outcomes of a gamble more heavily than their probabilities alone would warrant, so that the value of an act is computed not as the plain expectation but from a transform of the gamble's cumulative probability distribution.

I want to understand the implications of this view for a specific kind of choice situation that comes up constantly in the effective altruism circles I move in, and that my chapter is ultimately about. The choice is between two ways of doing good: an intervention with a concentrated outcome distribution — reliably saving or improving a modest number of lives, with results you can largely predict — and an intervention with the same or higher expected value but a small probability of an enormous payoff, such as work aimed at reducing existential risk, where the chance of success is minuscule but the stakes are astronomically large. Much of the argument for the long-shot option in these discussions leans on two premises: that expected value is what matters, and that the number of lives at stake in the long-shot scenario is so vast that even a tiny probability carries the day.

Please describe the most important implications of risk-weighted expected utility theory for this kind of choice. I would like you to address, at least: what the theory says about when preferring the concentrated option can be fully rational even when its expected value is lower; how the agent's risk function interacts with gambles that have small probabilities attached to enormous outcomes, and whether there is any principled reason to expect risk-weighting to bite especially hard on that tail rather than being washed out by the size of the payoff; whether the standard objection that REU is just expected utility theory in disguise — the claim that a rational agent's aversion to the gamble could always be absorbed into a reshaped utility function over outcomes — can be answered, and what turns on the answer; and what the view implies for the thought that our aggregation of moral goods across many lives might itself be risk-sensitive rather than risk-neutral, i.e. whether it makes sense to apply a personal risk attitude when the person taking the gamble and the persons bearing the outcomes are not the same.

One request about how you present the material. Please separate what is settled among decision theorists from what is genuinely contested among people who know the literature. For instance, I gather that the exploitation and dynamic-choice objections to REU agents, the proper treatment of risk attitudes in moral aggregation, and the relationship between rank-dependent weighting and the older tradition of non-expected-utility models are all live debates rather than closed questions; if that is right, present them as open, and do not present any one resolution as the consensus view. If some point I have framed above is itself tendentious or rests on a contested reading of the theory, I would rather learn that than receive a tidy answer.
