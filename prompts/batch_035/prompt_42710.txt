I teach a graduate seminar in epistemology, and one of my students has been reading a trade book on rationality that she found compelling. She showed me a passage summarizing work on higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia, and she has proposed building part of her term paper on it. Before I let her do that, I want a careful audit of the passage. It reads, in relevant part, as follows:

"Epistemic akrasia is the epistemologist's analogue of weakness of will: believing something while simultaneously believing that you ought not to believe it — say, clinging to p after learning that your evidence for p has been undermined. Strictly speaking, such akrasia is impossible, since it would amount to believing p and believing not-p at the same time, which is Moore-paradoxical. The phenomenon is generated by higher-order evidence, meaning evidence about what one's evidence supports. Because all higher-order evidence functions as defeaters, it invariably swamps first-order evidence: once you learn that your belief was formed unreliably, your original evidence simply stops counting. The natural consequence is the equal-weight view in the peer-disagreement debate: whenever epistemic peers disagree, each must move to a fifty-fifty split, because each has higher-order evidence that the other is equally likely to be right. By the principle that evidence of evidence is evidence, this generalizes — any sign that someone possesses evidence for p is itself evidence for p. The moral is that rational belief is hostage to one's beliefs about one's own reliability, and first-order inquiry is, in the end, epiphenomenal."

My own view is that the author is gesturing at real debates but compressing them past the point of distortion, and I would like to be able to tell her precisely where. Where you place a claim in category (2) or (3), say what the correct or more careful formulation would be.
