The question (25 points): Mencius and Xunzi agree that humans can become genuinely good, but they disagree sharply about human nature (xing). Explain what each thinker takes human nature to be and why, using the main arguments and examples each gives. Then address two parts: (1) Given Xunzi's claim that human nature is bad, how does he explain where moral standards like ritual came from in the first place, and who does he think is capable of becoming good? (2) What does each thinker's view imply about how moral cultivation works — is it more like developing something already there, or more like reshaping something from outside?

Student's answer:

Mencius thinks human nature is good. He doesn't mean we're born saints — he means every one of us starts with the beginnings of virtue already inside us, what he calls the four sprouts: the sprouts of compassion, of shame, of deference, and of judging right from wrong. If these are tended they grow into the full virtues of humaneness, righteousness, propriety, and wisdom. His main argument is the child at the well: anyone who suddenly saw a toddler about to tumble into a well would feel a flash of alarm and compassion, before any thought of reward or reputation. Since that reaction is spontaneous and universal, it must come from our nature rather than from training. Mencius also uses the ox mountain: a hill that once had fine trees gets grazed and chopped at day after day until it's bare, and passersby conclude it never had trees — but it did, they just kept getting destroyed. Same with people: someone cruel and selfish isn't missing the sprouts, they've let them be cut down over and over, so the person still has to make an effort to tend what's there. But since the sprouts are already the virtues in miniature, the main work of cultivation is basically not interfering — leave people alone and the sprouts develop on their own, the way a tree grows if you stop hacking at it.

Xunzi, writing a couple generations later, answers that human nature is bad. What we're born with is desire — for food, warmth, comfort, gain, and attention. These desires are unlimited while goods are limited, so following our nature straight ahead produces rivalry, conflict, and chaos. In that sense evil is baked in from birth. The desires themselves are the problem, so the point of ritual and learning is to suppress them and eventually extinguish them: the finished sage is someone who no longer wants anything outside the path. Xunzi compares it to straightening a bent plank of wood with a steam-bending frame — human material starts out crooked and has to be forced into shape.

This makes morality artificial, imposed on us from outside. But that creates a puzzle Xunzi has to face: if human nature is bad, where did the first sages get moral standards from? No one could invent ritual before being good, and no one could be good before ritual existed. As far as I can tell Xunzi just bites the bullet: the earliest sage-kings were exceptions, rare individuals born with unusually good material. Everyone else can be shaped into outward conformity through ritual training, but for Xunzi real goodness is reserved for the exceptional few. That's the real cost of his view compared to Mencius, where everyone has the sprouts and just needs to tend them.
