I serve as research officer to a state commission charged with drafting an official legislative apology for our state's compulsory sterilization program, which operated under statute for much of the twentieth century and ended before anyone now in office was elected. At this week's drafting session the two lead commissioners fell into a dispute over whether the resolution should be an apology at all, and the chair has asked me for a neutral assessment of the arguments before the next session. I could not produce one, and I would rather not guess.

The exchange ran as follows. (1) Commissioner Vidal argued that the proposed text is a category mistake: an apology is the expression of an agent's contrition for a wrong that agent committed, the state of today committed nothing, and a body without an inner life cannot feel contrition — so the resolution is, at best, a sincere-sounding sentence with a false presupposition, and at worst a fraudulent one. (2) Commissioner Rhodes replied that this misconstrues what the state would be doing: a verdict or a sentencing changes the legal standing of the parties regardless of what the judge feels, and the resolution likewise works on the civic standing of the wronged and their descendants; asking whether the state really feels sorry is like asking whether a court really finds. (3) Vidal answered that if the utterance is a performative of that kind, then call it a finding of historical fact plus a condemnation and drop the word apology, which trades on the feeling of inherited guilt; and he warned that if the apology commits the state to reparative payments, responsibility lands on current taxpayers who did nothing. (4) Rhodes said responsibility is not only for what one has done: a state persists through time, and inheriting its institutions means inheriting its liabilities, just as inheriting a company means inheriting its debts; refusing the word apology is refusing to keep the state's account honest. (5) Vidal objected that debts are owed to creditors, and here there is none — the dead cannot receive an apology — so the liability is a metaphor, and metaphor is how commissions launder politics as ethics. (6) Rhodes closed by denying that an apology needs a living recipient: we already accept that defaming the dead, or breaking a promise made to them, wrongs them, so posthumous standing is something a state can affect, and the apology is addressed to the record and to the living community whose standing that record defines.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the six steps, say whether the speaker is right, partly right, or mistaken, and why. Then say who, if either, is more right overall, and identify any distinction both commissioners missed that would dissolve part of the dispute rather than merely deciding it.
