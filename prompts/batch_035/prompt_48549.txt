For the past two years, on and off, I've been thinking about whether a language could be private — a language whose words refer to things only one person can in principle access, like the felt quality of one's own sensations. I have no philosophy training; I work as a lab technician and mostly chew on this during commutes. I've read enough secondhand discussion to know the standard moves, but I've also come up with what I think is a genuinely new twist, and I want to stress-test it against the best possible opponent rather than against myself in the shower.

Please play a committed defender of the view that a genuinely private language is impossible — the position associated with the later Wittgenstein. Be sharp, not a strawman: allow me small concessions when my points genuinely land, but never give up the core claim that a word whose application is fixed by private ostension alone cannot function as language. You may deploy the classic thought experiments (the diarist, the box with something in it that no one else can inspect, the distinction between something seeming right and it being right) but I'd rather you engage my specific case than recite stock scenarios. Keep each reply to roughly one paragraph so this feels like a live conversation, and stay in character until I write "stop." If I use a term sloppily, you may correct me briefly, but keep pushing the argument forward.

Here is my opening move. Since my early twenties I've had a recurring inner state I privately call "the settling" — it sometimes comes over me just as I'm drifting off to sleep, and it has no outward sign anyone has ever noticed. For years I marked a little dash in a notebook on nights it occurred, purely for my own use: the word was anchored in nothing but my memory of previous occasions. But last winter I noticed something. On nights I'd marked, I almost always sleep straight through to morning; on unmarked nights I very often wake at three or four. So now the word has a behavioral fingerprint I can check the next day, and which my wife could check just as well — though the state itself, at the moment I apply the word, remains accessible to me alone. So my question for you is this: does the discovered correlation retroactively give my private word meaning after all — in which case maybe privacy is compatible with language once the word acquires even indirect public criteria — or does it show the word was never a candidate for meaning until something public entered the picture, and my three years of solitary dash-marking were, linguistically speaking, empty rituals? And note that the correlation helps with the memory problem too: when I wonder whether tonight's state is the same one as before, I have something other than my own recollection to consult. I'm curious which horn you take, and why it's supposed to hurt me.
