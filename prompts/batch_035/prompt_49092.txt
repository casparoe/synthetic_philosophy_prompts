Something about the two big UK referendums bugs me and I want help thinking it through with actual democratic theory. In 2014 the Scottish independence vote used a wider franchise than usual: 16 and 17 year olds got the vote, and so did EU citizens resident in Scotland. Then in 2016 the Brexit vote went back to the standard Westminster rules: 18 plus, EU citizens living in the UK were excluded (though Irish and Commonwealth citizens resident here were in), while British expats registered within the last 15 years could vote from abroad. So you get these weird contrasts: a French woman who'd lived in Glasgow for twenty years could vote on independence but not on Brexit; a 17 year old in Edinburgh could vote to leave the UK but not to leave the EU; a British retiree in Marbella got a say on Brexit but not on Scottish independence; and a German living in London for decades got no say on either.

I gather the boundary problem — who belongs to the demos, and on what principle — is a whole subfield, so run these two referendums through it. Take the main candidate principles people actually defend (the all-subjected principle, affected-interests views, stakeholder or membership views, whatever else is in the running) and say what each implies about each of these franchise choices. Is there any principled story on which both referendums drew the boundary correctly, or is the mismatch just an embarrassment? One tempting move is to say the demos is relative to the decision being made — but if that's right, doesn't the real boundary problem just relocate to whoever sets the question, since in both cases it was Westminster that decided the franchise? And then flip it around: what do the cases show about the theory? Practically nobody objected to 16 year olds voting on independence, but plenty of people thought they should vote on Brexit — does that suggest the principles are doing no real work and the boundary is just set by politics all the way down?

Please write your answer in British English.
