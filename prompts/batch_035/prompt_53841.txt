I'm a high school senior taking an elective philosophy course, and the current unit is about consciousness. We've been discussing the distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness — the difference between information being available for reasoning, reporting, and behavior control versus there being something it is like to undergo an experience — and global workspace theory, in which the mind is pictured as a theater where a spotlight of attention broadcasts the contents of the moment to a vast unconscious audience of specialized processes. My classmates seem satisfied by the theater metaphor, but I have a background it doesn't map onto: for the past three years I've been building full-stack web applications, and the vocabulary I'm genuinely fluent in is that of software architecture — request routing, middleware chains, event buses, publish/subscribe systems, state stores, access control, caching, structured logging, and observability dashboards. I keep noticing that in every system I've built there is always some layer that inspects other layers, and it never strikes anyone as mysterious, so I want to know whether the philosophy debate is really about something my vocabulary can't express or whether it just sounds deeper than it is.

Please translate the debate into my vocabulary as faithfully as you can, and do it without smuggling in assumptions that belong to neither side. Roughly, I'd expect the global workspace to become something like a pub/sub event bus that many modules subscribe to; access consciousness to become whatever information has been routed somewhere that can use it for control and reporting; and the "hard problem" of phenomenal consciousness to become whatever the bus-plus-dashboard story is supposed to leave out. Don't let the translation quietly assume an answer — if a phrase like "there is something it is like" only makes sense given a prior commitment that experiences have an intrinsic felt character, say so rather than encoding that commitment into my architecture terms as though it were neutral.

Then I want a residue report in three parts. First, which claims of the access/phenomenal distinction and of global workspace theory survive the translation cleanly. Second, which survive only with distortion, and specify what gets distorted and in whose favor. Third, which claims have no expression at all in routing, buses, and dashboards. Finally, give me your honest judgment about the residue: does it exist because web-architecture vocabulary is missing something the philosophers genuinely need, or because the philosophical debate is less precise than it looks and my vocabulary is simply exposing that? If it's some of both, tell me which part of the residue points which way, since that distinction is what I actually care about.
