I'm a backend software engineer with about fifteen years of experience designing APIs and distributed systems, and no formal philosophy beyond a survey course in college that I mostly slept through. A colleague who actually studied philosophy keeps insisting that J. L. Austin's theory of speech acts — the framework in How to Do Things with Words, later systematized by Searle — will change how I think about API design, and I want to hold him to that. My current understanding, assembled from him and from scattered reading: some utterances don't just describe the world but actually do things — a judge pronouncing a couple married, saying "I promise," christening a ship — and whether they succeed depends on felicity conditions: who says it, in what context, with what authority, and with what intentions. Austin then complicates his own picture by distinguishing the locutionary, illocutionary, and perlocutionary aspects of every utterance, and eventually blurs the performative/constative line he started with, so that even stating a fact counts as doing something.

Please translate this whole framework into my working vocabulary, as faithfully as you can: pure functions versus side-effecting calls, preconditions and postconditions, checked versus unchecked exceptions, transactions, commits and rollbacks, assertions, the difference between a call succeeding and the caller acting on the result, and perhaps idempotency. Map each of Austin's distinctions to whatever fits — my working guess is that "I hereby promise" is a side-effecting call where felicity conditions are checked preconditions, so an infelicitous performative is a precondition violation rather than a crash. But then give me the residue report, which is what I actually care about: which parts of the theory translate cleanly, which come over only with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all in how my systems model calls and their effects. Two things especially puzzle me. First, insincerity: as best I can tell, Austin holds that a promise made with no intention of keeping it is still a real promise in some sense, whereas in my world a violated precondition usually means the operation should never have been allowed. Second, the illocutionary/perlocutionary distinction sounds like separating raising an exception from whether the caller catches it, but I suspect that analogy leaks, since hearers aren't call sites. The case of a marriage that fails because the officiant lacked authority also seems to be more than a simple rollback — the utterance happened, everyone may have believed it, and yet the act never took. At the end, tell me plainly whether the untranslated residue points to something my engineering vocabulary genuinely cannot express, or to something that was less clear in Austin's original than my colleague is willing to admit.
