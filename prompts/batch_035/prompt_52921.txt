I'm writing a paper that takes the slogan that taxation is theft seriously enough to stress-test it, and I've concluded that what the paper needs is not another survey of the standard back-and-forth but a matched pair of engineered cases. I want you to build them to specification.

The first scenario must be a case in which every feature normally invoked to distinguish taxation from theft is present, and the practice is nonetheless wrong. So: the taking is authorized by a fair vote of an inclusive body, the payer demonstrably benefits from what the money funds, the payer has a real and affordable exit option, the procedures are transparent and applied equally, and the stated purpose is a genuine public good — and the taking still comes out impermissible. The wrongness must not be stipulated; it has to fall out of independently plausible facts about the case. And it must not fall out of the vote or the procedures being rigged, since that would just hand the standard explanation its explanation back.

The second scenario must run the other way: a taking with none of the usual legitimating features — no vote, no consent, no benefit to the payer, no exit option, not even legality — that is nonetheless permissible, and not merely excusable or forgivable. Again, the verdict must be generated rather than stipulated.

Constraints for both: realistic settings a reader can picture without effort (a condominium association, a small town, a neighborhood in the aftermath of a storm — that register, not trolleys and not science fiction); each scenario no longer than a paragraph; no perfect information anywhere in the setup; the people involved are ordinary people; and do not stipulate anyone's motives where a realistic motive would do the work.

After each scenario, run the check. State which of the contested features are present or absent, explain why the verdict is not question-begging, and give the strongest attack a defender of the standard view would mount: in the first scenario, from someone who holds that democratic authorization plus benefit plus exit settles the matter; in the second, from someone who holds that the absence of all three makes the taking flatly wrong no matter what.

Then tell me what the pair shows. Which feature of each case is doing the moral work? Does the pair vindicate the slogan's underlying thought — that whether a taking is theft depends on something other than consent and procedure — or does it bury it? And where does ordinary taxation fall between the two cases, such that the cases sharpen the usual debate rather than replacing it? One more thing: be honest if the second scenario cannot be built without smuggling one of the legitimating features back in — for instance, by making the payer someone who benefited from an earlier wrong. If it keeps creeping back, say which feature kept reappearing, why it is doing the work anyway, and build the strongest case you can while flagging exactly where the smuggle happens.
