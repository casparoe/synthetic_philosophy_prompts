So, context: I'm a novelist, three books in, all fairly sober realist stuff, and for book four I've apparently decided to ruin my own life with a magical realist thing. The setup is a sculptor in a collapsing shipyard town who builds a statue of her dead brother, and the statue starts being a guy. Walks around, borrows books, slowly reabsorbs the clay into himself when he's upset. Part of the plot machinery depends on questions I initially thought were just vibes and now realize are load-bearing: if she melts him down and recasts the exact same clay as a different statue, did the brother die or just move out? When the statue was standing in the studio talking to her, was there one thing there or two — the statue and the clay, sharing a body, one of which was never alive? I keep having to make these calls scene by scene and I can feel myself making them on vibes, and I'd rather know what view is secretly running the book than have it leak in sideways and contradict itself by chapter twenty.

I did some reading and discovered actual philosophers have been fighting about the statue and the clay for decades and have something like seven mutually insulting positions on it, none of which agree with my editor, who says the statue is obviously just the clay and I'm overcomplicating my own novel.

So here's what I want: a "which view of material constitution do you actually hold" quiz, for me and possibly for the acknowledgments-section adjacent crowd on my newsletter. About ten questions. Every question has to be a concrete case someone can answer from their gut without knowing any of the jargon — nothing like "do you accept that constitution is not identity," that's a label question, it sorts people by what they've read, not what they believe. Answer options should be two or three per question, plain language.

These are the cases I want covered (add others if you need them, these are the ones my plot actually turns on, so they must appear):

1. The sculptor squashes the finished statue back into a ball of clay in a fit of grief. Did something just die, or is nothing dead and she's just holding her materials again?
2. She replaces the statue's cracked thumb with fresh clay. Same statue? And separately — same lump?
3. For one afternoon, two statues get pressed from the same clay at once before one is smashed. Can two complete objects share every single particle at the same time, or is something off about that even if it's convenient?
4. A case where the two candidates come apart: the lump would survive being rolled up in a tarp for a decade, the statue wouldn't survive being made into a different statue — or however you want to build the asymmetry, some case where what's true of one is false of the other.
5. Gradual replacement, ship-of-Theseus style, but make it matter: clay swapped out bit by bit over months until none of the original is left. When does the brother stop being the brother, if ever?
6. A counting question: at the moment the statue is standing on the pedestal being admired, how many things are up there — and I want the answer options phrased so a normal person can pick one without smelling a trap.

Then a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto actual named positions: the no-coincidence/sortal view that the statue just IS the lump with an art-history label attached, constitutionalism (two objects, one shaped like the other), four-dimensionalism in its worm and stage flavors, relative identity (same clay, different statue — identity is always relative to a sortal), the eliminativist line that there are no statues at all, just clay arranged statue-wise, and the deflationist shrug that the whole question is verbal and we can say whatever suits the novel. If a real philosopher defends something that doesn't fit my leaves, add it; if one of my leaves is basically a strawman nobody holds, tell me that rather than building it in out of politeness.

And this part matters to me: build the logical space first, from the questions — which combinations of answers to the cases are even possible — and then locate the named views in that space, not the other way round. Flag the combinations nobody actually defends, and when you do, say whether that's because the combo is incoherent, because it's coherent but nobody would want it, or just because it hasn't been explored. And if some pair of positions genuinely can't be separated by my kind of cases — I have a suspicion the two flavors of four-dimensionalism are like this, but don't take my word for it — come out and say the quiz can't tell them apart rather than inventing a fake diagnostic question.

One more thing, and I'll know if you don't do it: in the answer key, mark what's settled among people who actually work on this versus what's genuinely contested. I know just enough to know that "everyone agrees" claims in this corner of metaphysics are almost always lying, and I do not want to build a whole novel on something half the field thinks is confused while telling myself it's the consensus reading. If the deflationist reading is more popular than my quiz structure implies, embarrass me. If my question 3 stacks the deck toward a particular answer no matter which option you phrase how, tell me that too — I'd rather fix the quiz than get a falsely tidy result about my own metaphysics. And since the actual deliverable is a quiz and not an essay, put the settled/contested marking wherever it does the least damage — footnotes to the answer key, a note at the end, whatever — just don't let it turn into a lecture, my newsletter readers will not read a lecture.

Also, if you can, tell me which position my cases as written are secretly rigging me toward. I have a feeling the melted-and-recast scenario is doing something to me emotionally that no doctrine should get to do, and I'd like to know before I outline part three.
