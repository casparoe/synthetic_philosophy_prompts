A friend who took a linguistics course summarized Grice for me like this: "His big idea is that an utterance carries two layers. There's what the sentence logically entails, and then there's what the speaker smuggles in on top — the implicature. So if I say 'some students passed,' the sentence logically allows that all of them did, but everyone hears 'not all did': the maxims are rules of polite conversation speakers are expected to obey, the hearer assumes obedience, and works backwards to the hidden message. So an implicature is really just another kind of logical consequence, only an indirect one. And on Grice's account, speaker meaning ends up being a fact about the audience — if your utterance gets the hearer to draw a conclusion, that conclusion is part of what you meant. That's why Grice thinks almost nothing we say is ever communicated literally; the literal content is scaffolding that gets discarded once the implicature is in place." I'm inclined to believe most of this but something feels off, especially the last couple of sentences. Can you go through it claim by claim: which parts would a Gricean accept, which are conflations or overstatements a philosopher of language would flag, and does that grand conclusion about literal communication actually follow from the parts that are true?
