I teach recitation sections for our introductory algebra and set theory sequence, and a pattern in how we present the number systems has started to bother me. Every course "constructs" the numbers: the naturals as von Neumann's nested sets (0 is the empty set, 1 is {0}, 2 is {0,1}, and so on — on this construction 2 is literally a member of 3), the integers as equivalence classes of pairs of naturals, the rationals as further pairs, the reals as Dedekind cuts or Cauchy sequences. Then, having done all this, the lecturer almost always issues the same disclaimer: the construction is just one presentation, the numbers are not really these sets, we could have used a different encoding — Zermelo's, say, on which 2 is a member of 3 is false rather than true — and nothing in arithmetic would change. And no one ever says what the numbers are instead.

I want to know what this pedagogical practice presupposes about what numbers are. Please reconstruct its implicit theory rather than just naming one. Some things I've noticed that any reconstruction should account for:

1. If the numbers are not the sets, what is the construction accomplishing? Exhibiting a system that merely behaves like the naturals seems to presuppose there is a further thing it is behaving like — or does it? Could the disclaimer instead be the whole content of the view, so that being the number 2 is exhausted by bearing the right relations to the other numbers, and the construction is not a picture of anything but a definition of the role?

2. The practice quietly sorts questions into real and non-questions. Whether 2 is a member of 3 is treated as meaningless or at best encoding-relative, while whether every even number greater than 2 is the sum of two primes is treated as a determinate fact about the naturals. What account of numberhood makes that division principled rather than arbitrary?

3. Algebraists in the same courses say "the" Klein four-group while conceding there are many set-theoretic copies of it, yet insist two bijective sets are merely the same size, not the same set. Is the sameness standard being applied to the numbers the same one, or is it doing different work?

I'd also like to know where the practice is underdetermined: does it strictly presuppose platonism about numbers, strictly presuppose its rival, or is it consistent with several views about the existence and nature of mathematical objects? And is the pairing of the disclaimer with the construction game internally consistent, or does the practice, followed strictly, commit one to something its own disclaimer denies?

Please keep the discussion self-contained, without assuming I've read any particular philosophy of mathematics. End with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing either the single standard label that best names the view the practice presupposes (one short phrase, e.g. an -ism), or the word "underdetermined" if no single view is presupposed.
