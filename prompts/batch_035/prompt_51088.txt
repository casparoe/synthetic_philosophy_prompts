Hegel died in 1831, long before economic sanctions became a routine instrument of statecraft. Drawing on his treatment of war and international relations in the Philosophy of Right (§§321–340) — including the claim that war can preserve a people's ethical health, the insistence that there is no higher arbiter above states, and the remark that modern war is to be directed against the enemy state rather than against private persons — how do you think Hegel would have assessed the modern practice of comprehensive sanctions: coercive measures that stop short of war yet deliberately operate by impoverishing and immiserating a target state's civilian population in order to pressure its government? Consider in particular: (i) whether sanctions would even register for Hegel as a relation between states, given that they bypass the state's capacity for self-assertion and act instead on civil society, the system of needs; (ii) whether he would see them as a more rational and humane substitute for war, or as a degraded and dishonest evasion of it — a conflict that hides behind an economic guise precisely so as to avoid the ethical moment he attributed to war; and (iii) whether sanctions violate his constraint that hostilities are properly aimed at states rather than at private individuals. Reason from his actual commitments rather than from what a modern liberal would say, flag explicitly where his framework runs out, and state which of your conclusions you hold most and least confidently.
