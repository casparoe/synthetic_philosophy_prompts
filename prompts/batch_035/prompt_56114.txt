I'm a junior in high school writing my IB Extended Essay in philosophy, and I've decided on a risky pairing that I'd like help thinking through before I commit. The film is Spike Jonze's Being John Malkovich, and the lens is Douglas Hofstadter's view, from Gödel, Escher, Bach and I Am a Strange Loop, that the self is not sealed inside one skull: an 'I' is a pattern of symbols and stories that leaks between brains, so that the people we know come to live, partially and imperfectly, inside us. Hofstadter wrote movingly about his wife Carol after her death, arguing that she was not simply gone because a good deal of her pattern survived in him and in the friends who loved her.

The film seems, at first, to be made for this idea. A portal lets people spend fifteen minutes inside John Malkovich's head, seeing through his eyes; Lotte goes in repeatedly and is transformed by it; Craig eventually learns to puppet Malkovich from inside, overriding him for months at a time. If selves are portable patterns, the portal is just a crude literalization of what Hofstadter says is happening all the time.

But the more I think about it, the more the film seems to resist the reading, and I want to know whether I can make the resistance do philosophical work instead of sinking my essay. Hofstadter's leakage is slow, involuntary, and mutual — you take on the pattern of someone by caring about them, over years, and they take on yours. The portal is fast, unilateral, and parasitic: Craig doesn't come to host Malkovich's 'I', he evicts it and wears the body, and the film clearly treats this as a kind of violation. Lotte's case is closer to what Hofstadter describes — she comes out changed, in love, arguably with a self that is now partly Malkovich's — but the film gives that change a romantic-comedy shape rather than a metaphysical one. And Malkovich going through his own portal into himself, finding a world made entirely of Malkoviches, seems to be about something else entirely: maybe introspection, maybe narcissism, maybe the strange loop of a self modeling itself.

What I'd like is a serious critical reading that takes both sides of the pairing seriously. Specifically: (1) which scenes genuinely dramatize Hofstadter's claim that a self can be hosted by another brain, and what exactly is being claimed there; (2) where the film's mechanics — the fifteen-minute limit, the eviction, the vessel-aging plot about Lester and his friends — are doing horror-story work that contradicts Hofstadter rather than illustrating him, and whether I should argue that this is the film's real point, namely that self-transfer is only benign when it is slow and consensual; (3) what Hofstadter's account gains from being put next to a story that literalizes it, especially on the death-and-survival question that the film mostly punts on but that Hofstadter, after losing Carol, does not; and (4) whether the final image — Craig trapped, conscious but helpless, watching his wife's life through eyes he no longer controls — is best read as the dark mirror image of Carol-in-Hofstadter, a self lodged in another brain that doesn't want it there.

Please be honest about where my framing distorts the film to fit the theory, since my supervisor will be. Don't quote from the film or the books, and don't give me a bibliography — I'll assemble the texts myself. What I need is the argument: does Being John Malkovich support, complicate, or quietly refute the idea that an 'I' can be distributed across brains, and if the answer is 'complicates', what does the film add to Hofstadter's account that his own thought experiments don't?
