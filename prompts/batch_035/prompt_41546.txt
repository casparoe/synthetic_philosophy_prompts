I'm a second-year med student who got dragged into a philosophy of science elective, and something I noticed while shadowing in the hospital has been bugging me all semester. Doctors throw around "function" and "dysfunction" constantly, but I'm pretty sure they're not using the words one way. When my attending talks about a failing heart, she means the heart isn't doing its job in this organism right now — pumping blood. That seems like a "what it currently contributes to the system" sense: the part is malfunctioning because of what it's failing to contribute today, never mind its history. But the same week, a lecture casually described the appendix and wisdom teeth as "vestigial," and that seems like a claim about their selective history, not about what they currently do (they currently do very little — but the claim wasn't "it does little," it was "it's an evolutionary leftover"). And then there's fever: the ER suppresses it as a symptom to be managed, but someone from an evolutionary-medicine course told us fever is an evolved defense that exists "for" fighting pathogens, and that routinely stomping on it might sometimes be a mistake. Sickle cell is the case that really got me arguing with my friends over coffee: it's textbook "defective hemoglobin," but in malaria country the same molecule is protective — so is it malfunctioning, or doing exactly what selection shaped it to do? Psychiatry might be the worst of all, since the diagnostic manuals define disorders partly in terms of dysfunction of mental faculties without ever saying which sense of dysfunction is in play.

So here's what I want to know: what account of biological function does medical practice actually presuppose? Is it something like the selected-effects story — a part functions by doing what it was shaped by natural selection to do — or the causal-role story, where a part functions by contributing to some capacity of the organism as it currently stands, or is the whole thing an unstable mix that shouldn't be treated as one practice at all? Reconstruct the implicit theory (or theories) for me, and tell me which rival accounts each piece of the practice quietly rules out. I'd especially like to know whether it's philosophically underdetermined — whether several different accounts of function would end up licensing the same clinical habits anyway, in which case maybe the "inconsistencies" I noticed are just two innocent uses of one word rather than a real tension. And lastly, if medicine did have to commit to a single account consistently, what would actually change downstream? Would anything about how we handle fever, or how casually we remove appendices, or how we decide who counts as having a psychiatric disorder come out differently — or would the philosophical choice turn out to make almost no difference at the bedside? I care more about the reasoning than a verdict, so please walk me through which parts of the practice constrain the theory and which parts are just going along for the ride. (Also apologies if this is a well-worn debate I'm rediscovering — its new to me, and the elective only gave me vocabulary, not answers.)
