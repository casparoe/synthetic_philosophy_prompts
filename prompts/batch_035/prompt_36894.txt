I sit on the selection panel for a fixed-term advisory post. The engagement is strictly one-off: a single, well-defined analytical question, to be answered once, for a client who will act on the answer and then never again require the post-holder's judgment. Two finalists remain, and we have established the following to our satisfaction. On the actual deliverable, both would produce the same correct conclusion: we tested each of them on a masked version of the task, and both landed on the right answer. But they landed on it differently. Candidate A worked from an understanding of the underlying mechanism — probe her reasoning at any point and it holds together. Candidate B reached the same conclusion through a rule of thumb that, as far as we can reconstruct, has no warrant in this domain at all: it was imported from a different context and happened, this once, to point at the right answer. B cannot explain why the answer is right, and when pressed offers reasons that are subtly wrong even though the conclusion is right.

My colleagues are split. One side argues that since the deliverable would be identical and there is no future in which anyone will draw on either candidate's judgment again, the difference between A and B is, for this hire, no difference at all: any preference for A is a habit carried over from situations where understanding pays off later, and here there is no later. The other side holds that this cannot be the whole story — that it would be somehow wrong, in a way we cannot yet articulate, to hand the post to someone whose only qualification is a lucky true belief — but when pressed for the articulation, they fall back on exactly the "it pays off later" reasoning that the one-off structure of the post has already ruled out.

My question, then: setting aside every future-use consideration, is there any principled ground for preferring the candidate who knows to the candidate who merely gets it right? If there is, I would like to see it stated in a form that does not covertly reintroduce consequences — for the deliverable, for the client, for B's later career, or for our own future dealings with either candidate. If there is no such ground, I would like to know whether our residual conviction that A is the better hire should be treated as a defensible position or as a bias in favor of a property (understanding) that we value only for its usual effects — and, in that case, what this episode suggests about how much we genuinely value knowledge as against true belief.
