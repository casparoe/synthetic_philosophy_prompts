I'm writing a term paper on Hume's essay on miracles, and in office hours my professor floated a parody argument that I haven't been able to get out of my head. Our study group reconstructed it as follows. (1) Hume's central principle is that rational belief should be proportioned to evidence: no testimony is sufficient to establish a reported event unless it is more probable, given everything we know, that the report is true than that the reporters are mistaken or lying, and that probability depends both on the witnesses' reliability and on the antecedent probability of the kind of event reported. (2) That antecedent probability should be judged by experience: an event of a kind that nobody in the relevant community has ever observed is, on that evidence, extremely improbable — and the more unprecedented the kind, the lower the probability. (3) In 1799 the first platypus pelt reached London, and George Shaw, suspecting a hoax, took scissors to the specimen looking for stitches. Before it arrived, an egg-laying, duck-billed, venomous, furred mammal was contrary to the whole prior experience of European naturalists — not an odd instance of a known kind but an entire cluster of new kinds. (4) By (1) and (2), Shaw's suspicion was rational, and the accompanying reports from New South Wales deserved no credence until physical evidence was in hand; and for all he could tell in advance, the hypothesis of a sewn-together curiosity had precisely the same evidential profile as any other account. (5) The same reasoning covers the pre-1803 reports of stones falling from the sky, which the French scientific establishment dismissed for years until the shower at L'Aigle and Biot's investigation, and by extension every report of a genuinely unprecedented natural phenomenon. (6) So Hume's principle, applied consistently, instructs us to disbelieve testimony to any first-of-its-kind natural fact; and since nearly all of us can examine physical evidence for nearly nothing, we could never rationally come to accept, on the basis of reports alone, that unprecedented things happen. (7) That conclusion is absurd, and it isn't even Hume's own — he intended the maxim to indict miracle reports specifically, not all testimony to novelties. (8) The standard reply draws the line at violations of laws of nature: a platypus is bizarre but breaks no law, whereas a resurrection does. (9) But for Hume a law of nature just is whatever a long, uniform, and unbroken course of human experience has firmly established, so the distinction between 'contrary to experience so far' and 'violates a law of nature' reduces to a difference in degree of antecedent improbability, and the maxim was supposed to turn on exactly such degrees. (10) Therefore the argument proves too much: either we embrace sweeping skepticism about testimony to novel facts, or the maxim needs distinctions that Hume's own apparatus cannot supply.

The professor's challenge was to name the step to reject and price the rejection. I'd very much like to keep (1); proportioning belief to evidence looks like the last thing to give up, and my instinct is that the trouble is buried in (2), in the phrase 'experience' — but I can't tell whether the fix is that the relevant experience is the experience of humankind at large (colonists and Aboriginal Australians had seen plenty of platypuses, which makes 'uniform experience against' simply false), or that testimony is itself a form of experience so that the base rate can't be computed without the very reports in question, or something else entirely. I also want to know what each repair costs downstream: if the way out is that testimony and observation lie on a continuum, or that 'uniform experience' must include the witnesses' own experience, does anything recognizable survive of Hume's case against miracle reports specifically, or does the repair quietly dismantle the essay? Conversely, if the defender insists that law-violation is categorically different from mere novelty, what independent content does that category have for someone who, like Hume, takes laws to be inductive generalizations? Please tell me which step to give up, why it looked acceptable in the first place, and what the alternatives each have to pay.
