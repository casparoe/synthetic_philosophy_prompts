I've been thinking about this on my own, with no academic background in philosophy, and I can't make sense of a pattern in our laws and institutions — I'd like to know what theory of a person it quietly assumes. It started when a relative of mine with a serious mental illness signed what's called a psychiatric advance directive: while lucid, she authorized hospitalization in advance for a future episode, at a time when she might then be insisting loudly that she wants to leave. Once I noticed that, I saw the same structure everywhere. Casinos keep self-exclusion lists you can join voluntarily and that bar you for years; cars driven by people with past drunk-driving convictions can be fitted with ignition locks the driver agrees to in advance; and in medicine we honor refusals of treatment written by a lucid person decades earlier, even when the patient who now exists, demented and apparently content, wants to go on living. Yet the same legal system draws sharp limits: you can revoke your will any time before death, no contract can make you a slave, and courts refuse to enforce commitments that are too thorough or last too long. So the practice seems to be: the person you are today can bind the person you'll become, but only up to a threshold and only in some areas of life.

What I want is the implicit theory. What view of rationality across time does this pattern presuppose — that a person is really one agent whose preferences extend over years, so a choice made once counts as made by the whole person; or that each momentary self is separate, and the earlier self gets to speak only when the later self is incompetent, with the past statement working as evidence rather than as a decision; or something like a rule that the sober self is the authentic one and the impaired self simply doesn't count? And is the pattern even consistent? We bind future selves even where they aren't incompetent in any ordinary sense — a gambler on a self-exclusion list who walks past the casino sober and remorseful still can't get in — while refusing to bind them where they are competent, as when someone changes a will. So tell me which parts of the practice strictly require one theory of the self rather than another, where the practice is underdetermined in the sense that several quite different views of the person would license the same rules, and what would actually have to change if we replaced the presupposed view with, say, the idea that only the person existing right now has any claim to decide for themselves.

Since I gather this territory has real specialists, I'd like the answer to keep separate what is actually settled among them and what is genuinely contested — for instance, whether letting a past self bind a future one protects autonomy or violates it — and not to present one side of that dispute as if it were the consensus. Where the theory you reconstruct would itself be disputed, please say so openly rather than folding it into the background.
