Question (worth 20 pts): Some people think anti-smoking campaigns that show graphic images of damaged lungs are morally suspect, because they are manipulative: they get people to quit by scaring them rather than convincing them. Is this a good argument? Distinguish manipulation from persuasion in your answer.

Student's answer: Persuasion works by giving someone reasons: it goes through their rational faculties, and they can accept or reject what you say. Manipulation bypasses the rational faculties and works on something else instead -- fear, guilt, the desire to fit in. Kant's second formulation says that using a person as a means is wrong, and manipulation does exactly that: it uses a person's psychology as a tool while their reason is not engaged. So on the face of it the graphic-lung ads look like manipulation, since they work by producing fear rather than by giving arguments. But the fear in question is not irrational -- cancer is a real risk of smoking, so feeling afraid is an appropriate response to the facts, and the ad is really just making a reason visible that was there all along. An ad that made people irrationally afraid of something harmless, like an ad making viewers terrified of vaccines, would be different. Since the anti-smoking ad only puts a real reason into more vivid form, it benefits the viewer, and something that benefits the person being influenced cannot count as manipulation. Therefore the ads are persuasion, and the argument against them fails.
