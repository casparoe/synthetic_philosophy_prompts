I've been reading about Sen's liberal paradox and trying to work out where my own commitments actually lie: which decisions belong to a person's protected sphere, and when, if ever, collective preferences may override that sphere. Sen's result is that no collective choice rule can satisfy both a weak Pareto condition and even a minimal liberal condition — each person being decisive over the ranking of at least one pair of outcomes concerning their own life. Something has to give, and I've been running cases on myself to find out what I think gives, and where. Here are my verdicts. I'd like to know whether some principle captures them, which verdicts have to go if nothing does, and what the principle predicts for the cases I can't decide.

1. The book. Lewd wants to read a racy novel. Prude ranks the outcomes: no one reads it, then Prude reads it, then Lewd reads it. Lewd ranks them: Lewd reads it, then Prude reads it, then no one reads it. Both therefore prefer the outcome where Prude reads it to the one where Lewd does, so the outcome in which Lewd exercises his own choice is Pareto-dominated. My verdict: Lewd reads it if he wants. His say over his own reading trumps the Pareto comparison.

2. The apostasy. Cam leaves the faith in a devout town. The townspeople's grief is sincere and profound — for them it is genuinely the worst outcome on the table. My verdict: still Cam's decision. Even great distress among others doesn't move it into the collective's jurisdiction.

3. The purple house. Cam paints her street-visible house vivid purple. Most of the town hates looking at it, and their collective aversion outweighs her liking in any aggregation you like. My verdict: Cam's. Aesthetic offense at what is merely visible from the street is not the town's business.

4. The chainsaw yard. Cam takes up chainsaw carving at seven on weekend mornings; the neighbors' sleep is wrecked. My verdict: the town may regulate. I can't pretend this differs from the purple house in anything but intensity and the kind of nuisance involved.

5. The gambling ban. Dana is a compulsive gambler who campaigns for the town to ban gambling because she cannot resist it on her own; she genuinely prefers a world with the ban to one in which she is free and plays. The town votes; the ban passes. My verdict: legitimate.

6. Ella. Ella gambles recreationally, resents the ban, and prefers a world in which she is free. Structurally her position mirrors Prude's objection in the book case: a majority restricting someone's private pleasure on moralized grounds. I hold that the ban still stands. But I cannot say what distinguishes Ella from Lewd without saying something like "gambling is self-harming" — and in the apostasy case I refused to let the town's judgment about Cam's spiritual welfare count for anything.

7. The marriage rule. A tight-knit community in which the elders have always approved marriages; a member wants to marry outside the tradition. My verdict: the individual decides. Yet for cases 3 and 4 to come out as they do, I seem to need the line between an interest that licenses regulation (sleep) and one that doesn't (aesthetic displeasure) to be drawn somewhere — and I'm tempted to say that where it's drawn is partly a matter of local norms. If the community draws the line, why can't it draw it around marriage?

The principle I'm inclined toward is something like: a decision is insulated from collective choice when the only thing at stake for others is a preference about how the decider lives, rather than a setback to an interest that counts as a harm in its own right. But the apostasy case puts sincere grief — surely a setback to a real interest — on the wrong side of that line, and the purple house and the chainsaw yard seem to differ in degree of annoyance rather than in kind.

So: is there a single principle, or a small set of them, that captures all or nearly all of these verdicts? Which verdicts would it force me to abandon, and are those the ones I should abandon? Must the partition of the personal sphere be universal, or can it be indexed to community norms without collapsing into the problem in case 7? And what does the resulting view predict about the cases I genuinely go back and forth on: mandatory seatbelt laws for adult drivers, a public housing estate banning visible satellite dishes, and a state that requires some civic instruction of parents who want to homeschool? Please be honest about which of my verdicts your preferred principle simply cannot save.
