I'm a philosophy professor — metaethics is my main teaching area — but in a past life I spent eight years doing NLP research before I went back to grad school, and this has given me a bad habit: whenever I can't explain something to my old labmates in their vocabulary, I start suspecting the thing doesn't really make sense. The latest casualty is the Frege–Geach problem for expressivism. Setup: expressivism says moral claims like "stealing is wrong" don't state facts but express some noncognitive attitude — roughly, boo stealing. The problem is that the same sentence shows up unasserted, inside negations and inside conditionals like "if stealing is wrong, then getting your little brother to steal is wrong," and arguments like modus ponens on mixed premises seem valid, which requires "stealing is wrong" to keep a stable meaning across all those contexts — awkward if all the sentence ever does is vent an attitude. My labmates' knee-jerk reaction is that this is trivially solvable and that metaethicists are fifty years behind the curve: just give each sentence a representation and make the representations compose. I have a sneaking suspicion this reaction is neither as dumb as my colleages think nor as decisive as my labmates think, but I can't quite pin down why.

So here's the ask. Restate the Frege–Geach problem and the main expressivist responses — the Blackburn/Gibbard style moves with higher-order attitudes, the deflationary-truth stuff, whatever the quasi-realist and hybrid programs amount to — in the vocabulary I actually used in the lab: embeddings, compositionality, representations trained on one kind of signal and then re-used inside larger structures, what validity could even mean for representations that were never truth-conditional in the first place, whether you can state modus ponens as a property of some representation-preserving operation. Keep the translation honest: if the ML framing smuggles in an assumption (say, that everything just automatically gets a representation, or that composition is free), flag it instead of quietly calling it translation.

Then the residue report, which is what I actually care about: which parts of the debate map cleanly onto questions about compositional representations, which translate only with heavy distortion, and which cannot be said at all in the target vocabulary. And I want your considered view on the residue: does it show the ML vocabulary is missing something the metaethicists correctly care about, or does it show the Frege–Geach problem is less substantive than we like to pretend when we call it the hardest objection to expressivism? Related question, since it's the thing my labmates actually asked: is "just make it compose" basically a rediscovery of quasi-realism, or does it miss the point in some way that matters?
