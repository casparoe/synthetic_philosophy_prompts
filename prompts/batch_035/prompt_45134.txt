I'm a third-year philosophy student currently taking a seminar on classical Indian epistemology, where we've spent several weeks on the Nyāya school's theory of the pramāṇas — perception, inference, comparison, and testimony as the four accepted sources of knowledge — along with their account of doubt (saṃśaya), the requirement of a provable universal concomitance (vyāpti) between a sign and what it's supposed to establish, the reliability conditions on testimony from an āpta, and tarka's status as a supporting form of reasoning rather than an independent source of knowledge. I also have a practical role that this material keeps making me think about: I'm the head verifier for our university's student-run science and policy magazine, which publishes short explainers and news summaries. Each week my team of six volunteer editors — none of whom have any philosophy background, and most of whom are first- or second-year students in the sciences — has to decide which claims in submitted articles we can publish as established, which we have to hedge, and which we have to send back, with roughly half an hour of checking time per claim and no access to most paywalled literature.

I'd like you to turn the Nyāya framework into a decision procedure my editors could actually run on a claim before publication. The procedure should tell them what to ask, in what order, and what counts as a sufficient answer at each step: for instance, how to determine whether a claim rests on our own perception (almost never, in journalism), on an inference whose sign-to-conclusion concomitance we can actually check, on a comparison or analogy, or on testimony — and if on testimony, whether the speaker is reliable enough in the relevant way that we can treat it as knowledge rather than as something merely reported. It should handle the hard cases: claims that stack layers of testimony (a scientist summarizing a study for a journalist who summarizes it for us), inferences where the vyāpti can't be verified in half an hour, arguments that look like inference but are really analogy dressed up, and places where tarka-style "if the opposite were true, things we already know would break" reasoning can legitimately support a claim without establishing it. And it should be explicit about where the procedure stops with a rejection, where it stops with a hedge, and where it hands the decision to me because the question is one of credibility judgment or editorial values rather than something a checklist can settle.

Then run the procedure on three claims so we can see it work: first, an article citing a single peer-reviewed study, behind a paywall we can't get through, finding that a popular study-habit app improves exam performance in a forty-student trial; second, an opinion piece arguing that a congestion-charge policy that succeeded in one European capital will work in our mid-sized city, on the grounds that the cities are "basically the same kind of place"; third, a claim from a well-known and liked professor on our own campus, told to one of our writers at a departmental event, that a widely reported archaeological discovery is probably a mislabeled forgery — a claim our editors would love to publish and that may well be true. For the last one especially, I want to see whether the procedure gives the answer we want or the answer the framework actually supports. Finally, be honest about where the procedure will misfire: which parts of the Nyāya theory had to be distorted beyond recognition to make it usable at this speed, which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and which of the school's live disputes with its rivals — the Buddhist reduction of the pramāṇas to two, the Mīmāṃsā insistence that testimony is self-validating, the Cārvāka acceptance of perception alone — the procedure quietly takes a side on without telling the user.
