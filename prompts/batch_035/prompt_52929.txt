I sit on the allocations panel of my city's disability services board, which distributes a fixed annual budget for home accessibility modifications — stair lifts, widened doorways, adapted bathrooms, ramps, kitchen adjustments. We receive roughly 240 qualifying applications a year and can fund about 150. Until now we have given every approved applicant an identical flat grant, with a lottery when applications exceed funds. At Thursday's meeting we must decide among three policies, each championed by a different panel member, and I have been asked to circulate a written recommendation beforehand.

Option 1 (status quo): continue equal flat grants. Its champion argues this respects applicants' dignity: no bureaucrat decides whose impairment is worth more, no one is told their life yields less benefit per euro, and the policy is simple and auditable.

Option 2 (welfare weighting): our new analytics officer proposes allocating by projected gain in life satisfaction. Using before-and-after wellbeing surveys of past recipients, funds would flow where the measured satisfaction delta per euro is largest. He argues this is simply getting the most good from a fixed budget.

Option 3 (threshold funding): a third member argues we should instead fund whatever each applicant needs to reach a common threshold of basic functioning in their own home — getting in and out, using the bathroom, sleeping, preparing a simple meal — regardless of how expensive that turns out to be for a given applicant. Under this option, someone with an unusually narrow staircase simply gets the more costly retrofit.

Three hard cases are already circulating among panel members, and any recommendation has to say what it would do with them. First, Ms. Álvarez, whose retrofit costs roughly four times the average because of her building's layout. Second, Mr. Ostrowski, a semi-professional cook whose hands are affected; he requests an extensively adapted kitchen, well beyond what any threshold of basic functioning would cover, and says cooking is central to his life and identity. He would score very well under Option 2. Third, a long-term applicant who reports complete life satisfaction with her current situation despite being demonstrably unable to leave her flat independently — she would score poorly under Option 2, and her case has made several members question the wellbeing surveys altogether.

Two further complications. Our local Deaf association has objected that Option 3's functioning checklist, and the panel's framing generally, treats deafness as a deficit to be engineered away rather than a difference; they have also indicated they would rather we reject sign-language-related requests than fund them under a deficit rubric. And a service-user representative has warned that any needs-assessment regime, including Option 3, will be experienced by some applicants as a ranking of whose life is more worth improving — which was the very concern Option 1 was meant to answer.

Please draft the memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting, recommending one option or a defensible hybrid. Structure it in four sections, in this order:

1. Recommendation — a single paragraph, no more than 150 words, stating the policy I will argue for.
2. The three proposals assessed — roughly 500 words on what each gets right and where it fails, engaging the underlying question of what exactly the budget should be equalizing across applicants, and treating explicitly the problem that some applicants' reported satisfaction reflects adapted expectations rather than adequate circumstances.
3. The strongest case for the option I am rejecting — roughly 250 words, stated so well that a supporter of that option would recognize their position; do not strawman it.
4. Policy language and hard cases — roughly 400 words translating the recommendation into rules the panel could actually adopt, including how it handles Ms. Álvarez, Mr. Ostrowski, and the satisfied-but-housebound applicant, and what it says in response to the Deaf association's objection.
