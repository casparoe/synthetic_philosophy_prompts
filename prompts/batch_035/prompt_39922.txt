My sister and I are planning a surprise 80th birthday party for our dad. He's in pretty bad health, so realistically this is the last chance we get — everyone is flying in from all over, and its basically locked in: it has to be that Saturday evening, because dad can't handle a big late night twice, and he can't be told in advance or he'll work himself into a state about it.

Here's the problem. My sister is the one who's supposed to show up with dad at the venue at 5pm. She just checked and her flight the night before is on one of those tiny regional carriers that gets canceled alot whenever there's weather, and there's a decent chance of storms that day. I told her to switch to a bigger airline with more flights that day, even though it costs more. She says relax, she knows the flight will be fine — she's flown that route a ton, it's almost never canceled, and I'm being paranoid.

I told her that with this much riding on it you can't just say you *know* the flight will be fine. If it gets scrubbed she misses the whole thing, dad finds out everyone was in town for nothing (or worse, she's not there at all), and there's no redo — this was it. She says I'm moving the goalposts on what "knowing" means: the evidence didn't change just because I'm nervous, and the same airline was fine last month when I said "I knew it'd be fine" about a much lower-stakes trip. So either I was lying then or I'm being unfair now, according to her.

Is she being reckless, or am I applying a weird double standard where my bar for "knowing" changes with the stakes? And if the bar does shift like that, does that mean I never really *know* anything ordinary, since you can always imagine a version of the situation where more rides on it?
