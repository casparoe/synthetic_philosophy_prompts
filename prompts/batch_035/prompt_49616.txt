I'm a novelist, and the book I'm working on now is about a woman in her late fifties caring for her mother, who's in mid-stage Alzheimer's. The care home uses a bunch of stuff the staff call "therapeutic fibbing": when the mother gets agitated and asks where her husband is (he died four years ago), they tell her he's at the store and will be back later, and that settles her. They've also given her a lifelike baby doll to look after, which she adores, and they let her believe her old routines are still running — Sunday roasts, waiting for a phone call that never comes. I don't doubt it works: she's calm, she smiles, the worst agitation passes. But its been bugging me, because I half-remember a philosophy thing from college — Nozick's experience machine, where the claim was that none of us would plug into a machine that gave us perfect pleasant experiences, because we want to actually live, not just feel like we're living. The care home has, in effect, plugged my character's mother into a small machine made of well-meant lies, and everyone — including me, writing it — thinks it's kind and correct.

So here's what I want worked out. What theory of well-being does this practice presuppose? The obvious reading is that for her, well-being has collapsed into pure experiential state — how her moments feel is all there is left to her good — which is exactly the hedonism the experience machine is supposed to refute for the rest of us. But I'm suspicious of that reading being the only one. Could the fibs be justified on desire-satisfaction grounds instead (she wants her husband back, and the lie satisfies a fragment of that want), or on something about which desires still count as hers at this stage of the disease? Reconstruct the implicit theory the practice rests on, then tell me honestly where it's underdetermined — which several theories of well-being could all license it — and where it's not. And is there a real inconsistency in us doing this for dementia patients while we'd be horrified to do anything structurally similar to, say, a bored but healthy fifty-year-old, or does the difference track something defensible about whose life it still is? Last thing: say what would have to change in the home's practice if they instead presupposed an objective-list or a full desire-satisfaction view of her well-being — do the doll and the fibs have to go, or just get reinterpreted?

Since I may hang whole chapters on this: as you go, flag how confident you actually are in each step of the reconstruction, and at the very end single out the point you're least sure of. I'd much rather know where the philosophical ground is shaky than have it all come out sounding seamless.
