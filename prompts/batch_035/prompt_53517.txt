I'm a final-year undergraduate taking a seminar on collective decision-making and social choice, and before finals a few of us take turns writing practice exam questions for the study group. It's my turn this week, and I want to contribute one on judgment aggregation and the discursive dilemma that can't be answered by reciting the textbook three-judge court. Last year's exam apparently had a question on this material where you could earn nearly full marks just by retelling the standard case and naming premise-based versus conclusion-based voting, and I want something harder than that for our group.

Please write the exam question, a model answer, and a marking scheme, to the following specifications.

The question should be answerable in about 45 minutes, worth 25 marks total, and should be a problem-question with an essay component rather than a pure essay. Specifically, it must have five parts:

1. It must present its own explicit example rather than invoking the famous one. Set up a three-member committee with a concrete subject matter of your choosing — say, a hiring committee, a tenure case, or a regulatory panel deciding whether to approve a drug — with a clearly stated logical structure: the conclusion is a truth-functional compound of two premises (for instance, the committee's conclusion holds if and only if both premises hold, or if and only if at least one holds), and each member's votes on the premises and on the conclusion are laid out explicitly.

2. It must ask students to work out the collective outcome under (a) a premise-based procedure, (b) a conclusion-based procedure, and (c) proposition-by-proposition majority voting, and to say exactly which resulting profile of collective judgments is defective, and in which sense — inconsistency, incompleteness, or both.

3. It must then ask students to construct the mirror image of the standard dilemma: a distribution of votes in which the majority accepts the conclusion while majorities reject the premises (for a conjunctive structure), and to assess whether this case is genuinely symmetric with the standard one or whether something breaks the symmetry.

4. It must ask whether the dilemma is an artifact of simple majority rule — having students reason about which quota thresholds (unanimity on the premises, a two-thirds requirement, and so on) would or would not avoid the problem, given the agenda structure.

5. Finally, a short reflective component worth only a few marks: is the dilemma best described as a truth-tracking failure or as a failure of the group to count as a unified agent, and does the answer to that matter for which repair one would prefer?

The model answer should run roughly 600 to 800 words, and it must be derivable from the materials given in the question itself: no citations, no reading lists, and no reliance on named theorems. If you attribute any observation to a specific author, keep it minimal and correct, but the answer should stand on the computations and the reasoning alone.

For the marking scheme, distribute the 25 marks across the five parts, and make sure that for at least three of the parts you explicitly name the move that separates a top answer from a middling one — for instance, the middling answer merely observes that premise-based and conclusion-based procedures can diverge, whereas the top answer characterizes precisely for which premise structures and which vote distributions they diverge. For each part, also note the most common way you would expect students to lose marks on it.

One last constraint: the students in our course have seen the definitions of collective rationality, anonymity, and systematicity, but not the formal characterization or impossibility results, so the question should not depend on any of those; everything needed must either be stated in the question or be within what's listed above.
