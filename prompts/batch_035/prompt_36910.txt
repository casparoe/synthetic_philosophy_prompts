I'm taking a game theory elective and my professor has this thing where instead of essays he makes us stage debates between concepts — last week two of us had to play "backward induction" and "trembling-hand perfection" arguing about who has caused more unjustified grief in the literature. This time I drew the Nash bargaining solution vs the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution, and I want to write it as a dialogue between two hired mediators, because honestly pure axiom-talk puts everyone to sleep.

The setting I have in mind: a small startup and its first engineer are negotiating over how to split a retention bonus (say every feasible agreement is some split of $100k, and the disagreement point is zero for both — no deal, everyone walks). The Nash-mediator proposes the split that maximizes the product of the two sides' gains over their outside options. The Kalai–Smorodinsky mediator works differently: he normalizes each side's gain by the most they could possibly get in this negotiation (the "ideal point" where each side simultaneously gets its maximum — usually not itself feasible), and proposes the split that keeps the two sides' normalized gains in equal ratio. Partway through the session, new information arrives that doesn't change any of the actually available agreements but does change one side's theoretical maximum — say it comes out that the best package the startup could ever have offered itself was smaller than everyone believed — and the mediators instantly split: KS revises his proposed split, Nash doesn't move an inch. That's the fight I want. The Nash-mediator should demand to know why anyone's actual share should depend on some off-frontier fantasy point that was never on the table, and invoke independence of irrelevant alternatives; the KS-mediator should counter with monotonicity — a solution where the pie can grow and nobody is guaranteed a single additional dime of improvement is, per his telling, no way to run a bargaining table.

Make both mediators genuinely competent and evenly matched — no strawmen, neither one gets lectured by the other. They should actually respond to each other's points, not talk past each other (the Nash side should get to lean on symmetry and invariance to affine transformations somewhere; the KS side should be made to say explicitly which Nash axiom he's giving up and how much it costs him to sleep at night). The engineer and the founder can butt in as confused civilians a couple of times, mostly to force the mediators to translate into plain English. End it however you want, but if the parties end up shaking hands on some compromise, make sure it's one a bargaining theorist would actually nod at rather than wince at. Around 900 words, and keep it fun — these are characters, not textbooks.
