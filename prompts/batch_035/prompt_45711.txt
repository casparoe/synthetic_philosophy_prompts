So I write novels — midlist literary stuff, nothing you'd have heard of — and my publisher just rolled out this "reader wellbeing" package for the ebook editions, and I can't work out what they actually think a reader IS, if that makes sense. A friend said you're good at untangling exactly this kind of thing, so here I am.

Here's what the setup looks like. If a book contains a scene the internal sensitivity pass flagged — say a suicide attempt — the ebook shows a card before the chapter: "This chapter contains content some readers may find distressing. Tap to continue." There's no global off-switch for the cards; you dismiss each one individually. Separately, the store page defaults to the "comfort edition," where a few of the harshest paragraphs are just gone, and getting the original text requires hunting for a small link to a seperate editions page. And the reading app itself does its own steering: if you stop mid-scene it fires "finish your chapter?" notifications, and "gentle mode" (on by default for new accounts) swaps out maybe two dozen words across the whole book to soften the prose. I'm told readers "overwhelmingly prefer" all this, which I assume means they don't change it, not that they chose it.

What I keep going back and forth on is this. The marketing copy definately says the whole thing "respects reader choice," because everything is opt-in somewhere, technically, if you dig. But every default points one way, and they obviously know most people never change a default. So what do they take a reader to be? A competent adult who's just busy and appreciates a well-placed default — that's the nudge idea, right, the "libertarian paternalism" thing? Or someone fragile who needs protecting from my sentences? And can they coherently believe both at once, treating me as a hazard and the reader as an adult in the same paragraph?

What I'd like from you: first, lay out what the whole package presupposes about readers — what picture of a person it only makes sense under, and which rival pictures it quietly rules out. Second, tell me which parts a genuine nudge-theory person could defend with a straight face (the version where you're just helping people reach ends that are already theirs) and which parts are plain old paternalism wearing a nice interface. Third, the bit I actually lose sleep over: the stated goal is reader autonomy, so is the practice self-undermining? The same inertia they're exploiting to shield readers is exactly what they'd have to count as a genuine competence if the autonomy story were true — either the untapped default-setting counts as the reader's voice or it doesn't, and they seem to want it both ways depending on which way the steering goes. Is there a principled line between steering someone away from distress and steering them, full stop, or is the line just whatever the committee felt that week?

Don't bother with citations or reading lists — I want the actual reasoning. If you use the standard labels (soft versus hard paternalism, means versus ends, whatever else exists down there), explain them as you go; I didn't study philosophy, I just write people.
