I'm the reviews editor at a general-interest history journal, and a recurring dispute lands on my desk several times a month. Referee A writes that a submission "narrates but doesn't explain": it traces, say, the sequence of provocations leading up to a peasant revolt without showing why things had to go that way rather than some other way. Referee B responds that this objection assumes a covering-law ideal of explanation that history can never meet and shouldn't try to — that narrative ordering, emplotment, and attention to what the actors themselves understood they were doing *is* the explanation. Our authors, stuck in the middle, need actionable revision guidance, not a seminar on the philosophy of history.

Please write a procedure I can hand to referees and authors that settles when a historical explanation is adequate: an ordered set of questions to ask of a manuscript, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure returns a clear "no, this is only a chronology," and where it should stop and hand the decision back to me because the remaining disagreement is about what explanation in history should mean, not about anything in the text. Along the way, decide which distinctions from the covering-law-versus-narrative debate genuinely have to survive the simplification (e.g., the demand for contrastive explananda — why this rather than that — versus the demand for lawlike regularities), and which can be dropped for a non-philosophical audience. Referees should be able to run it in twenty minutes; they have no philosophy training, and I can't force them to read anything longer than two pages.

Then run the procedure on three cases: a manuscript that explains a revolution almost entirely as a narrative chain of escalating events; one that explains a famine by appeal to a general claim about how price shocks interact with subsistence economies, with the local events as an illustration; and one that offers a single-cause account (a monarch's decision) for an outcome with many plausible candidate causes. Include at least one case where the procedure gives a verdict I won't like as an editor who wants to accept the piece, and be explicit about the ways the procedure will misfire — the kinds of good historical writing it will wrongly condemn or rightly condemn for the wrong reasons.
