I am a novelist three drafts into a book set on a salmon farm on a Scottish sea loch, and I have spent enough time around aquaculture to have noticed a pattern I cannot get straight in my head. My narrator is the farm's newly hired welfare officer, and she will eventually have to articulate this pattern to her board, so I had better understand it myself.

Here is the practice as it actually stands. The farmed salmon fall under a welfare certification scheme with detailed standards: bands for dissolved oxygen and temperature, maximum stocking densities, a threshold above which mortalities put the site out of compliance, and a requirement that the fish be rendered insensible before slaughter. Under the same scheme, the delousing treatments the farm must run — mechanical brushing and thermal baths that injure or kill some fraction of the fish — are permitted, weighed by a cost-benefit sort of arithmetic, on the theory that the lice would harm more fish than the treatment does. The cleaner fish stocked to graze the lice off the salmon, wrasse and lumpfish, are barely counted at all. Meanwhile the crab boat across the loch stacks its animals live in a tank with their claws banded, and boils them without any stunning requirement; until recently the law said nothing about their welfare at all, and the recent amendment declaring decapods sentient has changed very little on the quayside. Wild salmon — fish with the same nervous system as the ones my narrator counts each morning — are hauled up in nets and suffocate on a deck with no welfare implications whatsoever.

What theory of animal minds and animal value does this pattern presuppose? The certification standards are almost all measures of physical health — water chemistry, lesion counts, mortality figures — which suggests a view of welfare as proper functioning rather than as feeling. Is that what the scheme strictly presupposes, or would a sentience-based view of welfare license the same checklist? Does the farmed/wild asymmetry presuppose that moral status attaches not to what an animal can experience but to its falling under human husbandry — a stewardship duty rather than a patient-centred one? And is the fish/crab asymmetry best read as a presupposition about evidence, since crab pain was scientifically contested for longer, or about moral status itself? Please reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, identify where it is philosophically underdetermined enough that several theories would license it, and describe what would have to change on my fictional farm if the presupposed view were replaced by a consistently sentience-based one.
