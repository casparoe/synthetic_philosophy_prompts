I teach a 75-minute session of an upper-level seminar on Wittgenstein's later philosophy (12 students), and I want them to feel the force of the rule-following paradox before we name it. Design an in-class game built around applying an invented rule to a stream of novel cases — say, a simple function on small numbers, or a nonsense word to be sorted across pictures — in which a designated judge must call each answer correct or incorrect using nothing but the written rule statement and the player's past answers, with a rotation that puts every student in the judge's seat at least once. Work in a "rival interpretation" element the students should construct themselves rather than be told about: some point at which the same body of past answers can be read as consistent with two incompatible rules going forward, so they discover that everything observed so far underdetermines the next application — and they shouldn't hear the name of the paradox or its literature until the debrief. Give me the rules, materials, scoring, and a timeline; then a debrief mapping what happened onto the skeptical challenge (that no fact about the player available to the judge fixes the next application), the gap between being inclined to answer and being obliged to, and the communal or dispositional replies and their costs; and a prediction of what will go wrong the first time I run it — in particular whether judges' own standards drift over the session and how to use that drift as data rather than fight it. Be frank about which feature of the rule-following problem the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply in discussion. Please write the answer in American English.
