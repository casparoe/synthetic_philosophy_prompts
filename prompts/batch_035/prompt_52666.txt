I work on preference learning for alignment, and my officemate has a move he uses whenever I doubt we can infer what humans actually want from their behavior: actions speak louder than words. His actual point being that we should learn preferences from what people do (IRL-style) rather than from what they say, since asking people directly gets you confabulation, saying what you think you should say, sycophancy toward whoever's doing the asking, etc. I half agree with him but I suspect the proverb is doing like five different jobs at once and he'd only endore one or two of them if pressed.

So here's what I want: lay out exactly five distinct, precise claims that the proverb could be making in this context, numbered 1 through 5, each item one or two sentances, no intro or conclusion, just the list. A few constraints: one of the five should be the definitional reading, the old revealed-preference move where preferences are more or less defined as whatever rationalizes observed choice, so the proverb comes out true but kinda trivial. One should be the rationality assumption in disguise — the claim that people are close enough to rational that their choices reliably track what they want, which is the one IRL actually needs and the one that's empirically shaky. And at least one should be strong enough that it's probably just false (stated preferences count for literally nothing, or actions reveal preferences regardless of the beliefs and constraints the person was operating under). For each of the five, add a clause at the end saying whether that reading would actually support my officemate's conclusion, that inferring from behavior beats just asking people and comparing notes. And don't give me a sixth item because you couldn't bear to leave one out — folding the extras into the five you've got is part of the job.
