My roommate has gotten deep into Fricker and came back with an arguement that seems to prove way too much. (1) Testimonial injustice is when a hearer gives a speaker less credibility than they deserve because of prejudice attached to the speaker's social identity. (2) Every credibility judgment we make is shaped by social identity — accent, clothes, gender, race, age, class markers all feed into who sounds competent, and this happens mostly automatically. (3) Those identity-based influences count as prejudice in the relevant sense, since they're not evidence about the specific claim being made. (4) So whenever identity influences how much we believe someone, testimonial injustice occurs. (5) Since nobody can fully screen those influences out, essentially every credibility judgment across social difference involves testimonial injustice. (6) So there is no non-injust way for me to believe or disbelieve anyone whose social identity differs from mine — which would make everyday epistemic life structurally unjust, and would also mean I can never legitimately doubt anyone, since the doubting itself is an injustice. She says fine, that's the correct radical conclusion and we should just live with it, but to me it proves too much. My hunch is that something slips between "influenced by identity" and "deflated because of prejudice", or else between wronging the speaker and the belief itself being flawed, but I can't make either hunch precise. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I'd really like to hang onto (1) and (5) if thats possible.
