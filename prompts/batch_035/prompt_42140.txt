I write about science and ideas for a living, and the literature I actually know well is subjective well-being research: experienced utility vs. decision utility, Kahneman's remembering self vs. the experiencing self, life-satisfaction scales, hedonic adaptation, experience sampling. Translate Nozick's experience machine argument against hedonism into that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, and then give me the residue report. The temptation for us science writers is to treat the machine as a thought-experiment version of what the field already half-endorses — an intervention that keeps experienced utility maxed out while decoupling it from anything real happening to you — and to shrug at Nozick's claim that we'd refuse the machine, since people adapt to good things anyway. But notice what the measuring apparatus can and can't express: a life-satisfaction survey filled out by someone inside the machine looks identical to one filled out by someone whose life actually went that well, so if theres a real difference here it's one my vocabulary has no operationalization for. So which parts of the anti-hedonist case survive the translation (contact with reality, the claim that we want to actually do things and not just have the experience of doing them), which come through distorted, and which cant be said at all? And when something cant be said, tell me honestly whether that's because the well-being framework is missing something or because Nozick's talk of staying in contact with reality was vaguer than it looked. Basically I want to know whether my editor's instinct — that the whole argument is just behavioral science avant la lettre — is right, or lazy.
