I host a civics podcast that covers electoral reform for a mixed audience: politically engaged general listeners, plus a handful of political-science graduate students who will notice any softening of the technical content. Next week's guest is the public face of our city's campaign for ranked-choice voting — a mathematician by training who argues, in every interview I have seen, that once voters can rank candidates instead of casting a single vote, tactical voting becomes pointless: no one will ever again have to bury their favorite in order to stop their least favorite. I take her to be entirely sincere, but as I understand the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, any rule for electing one of three or more candidates that is non-dictatorial and always responsive to the submitted rankings must, for some configuration of preferences, reward at least one voter for misreporting. So her claim, if it is the strict claim of strategy-proofness, contradicts a theorem; and if it is not the strict claim, I want to find out what claim she is actually making.

Please draft seven or eight interview questions that press on exactly this, written so they can be read aloud to a general audience while still landing with the graduate students. At least one question should ask her to say which precondition of the theorem she rejects — unrestricted preference rankings, more than two viable candidates, or non-dictatorship — and what follows if she rejects none of them. At least one should separate the strict claim, that no voter can ever gain by ranking insincerely, from the weaker empirical claim, that such gains are merely rarer or harder to identify under ranked ballots than under plurality, and press her on which one she actually holds. And at least one should ask what evidence, in real elections conducted with ranked ballots, could show that strategic misranking happens less often than under plurality — including what she would say to a voter who deliberately ranks a weak compromise above a strong rival in order to change the elimination order.

For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. I can already predict two of her replies and want follow-ups prepared for both: that the theorem assumes perfectly informed, perfectly calculating voters, whereas real voters rank sincerely out of habit; and that the theorem assumes unrestricted preferences, whereas real electorates are less varied than that. Please also give me a closing question about what would count against her campaign's central claim, phrased so that it cannot be answered with "we just need more voter education about how ranking works."

Write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way one would write an email to a co-producer: no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold text, no tables. Each question together with its follow-up should sit inside its own flowing paragraph. Do not cite or refer to any specific papers, books, or named historical elections; where a question needs an example, build it from a hypothetical three-candidate race.
