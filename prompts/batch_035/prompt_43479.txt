**Draft: Bargaining floors and trade vetoes**

The standard liberal objection to price controls is that they forbid consenting adults from making trades both parties prefer to no trade. I want to argue that this objection, whatever its force, applies to only one of two functionally distinct instruments that get lumped together under the heading "price control." Call the first a *trade-veto* control: it renders a class of transactions illegal, and in doing so prevents some pairs who would have traded from trading at all. Call the second a *bargaining-floor* control: it operates not by forbidding trades but by altering the default positions between which the parties negotiate, in a way that can leave the set of realized trades unchanged while redistributing surplus within them.

The illustration I use is the contrast between a hard rent cap in a supply-constrained housing market and a minimum wage set near the productivity of the median low-wage worker. The rent cap is a trade veto. It does not merely divide the surplus of an ongoing match differently; it changes which matches form at all, and it does so in a way that generates costs beyond the standard deadweight loss — apartments are rationed by search and waiting rather than by price, units are withdrawn or converted, and tenants who would be better matched elsewhere stay put because moving means re-entering the rationed queue. The minimum wage, by contrast, is a bargaining floor whenever employers hold wage-setting power over their workforce. Where a single employer (or a set of employers acting as the local demand side) faces a labor supply curve that slopes upward, the employer's optimal wage is below the worker's marginal product, and the employment level is below the competitive one. Raising the legal minimum in that region does not veto any trade: employment does not fall, and may even rise, while the wage moves toward marginal product. The law has not blocked a mutually beneficial exchange; it has changed the outside option that the parties bring to the table, and thereby the division of a surplus that the exchange would generate either way.

The normative payoff is this. The autonomy-based defense of freedom of contract — the claim that adults should be permitted to strike whatever bargains they choose — is an objection to trade vetoes, and it has real bite there. But it does not reach bargaining floors. To see this, notice that the common law of contracts never treated the division of surplus between bargainers as sacrosanct. Doctrines policing unconscionable terms, and rules against enforcing agreements extracted under certain forms of economic pressure, take exactly this stance: the exchange may proceed, but the terms may be revisited. A bargaining floor is a statutory cousin of these doctrines. It says, in effect, that the background conditions under which parties arrive at a wage may leave one side with an artificially weak threat point, and that the law may correct the threat point rather than the exchange.

If this is right, then the pro-market critic of the minimum wage cannot simply gesture at blocked trades; she must either deny the empirical precondition (wage-setting power in the relevant markets) or find a different principle — one that condemns the correction of threat points, not the prevention of exchanges. Neither route is easy. Denying the empirical precondition puts the critic in the awkward position of asserting that labor markets are generally competitive in the relevant sense, which is contested at best. And condemning threat-point correction in general would condemn doctrines of private law that few classical liberals are prepared to give up.

I close with a complication I have not resolved. The same nominal instrument can shift from bargaining floor to trade veto depending on how high it is set: a minimum above the competitive wage starts vetoing trades in the ordinary way. Worse, the character of the instrument may depend on the supply characteristics of the controlled good. Labor is re-created every period; a firm that cuts employment in response to a wage floor can reverse course quickly, so a modest overshoot is cheap to discover and correct. Housing is durable and slowly replenished, so the same nominal cap on rents produces a long, quiet attrition of supply that is costly to observe and hard to reverse. Perhaps rent control's distinctive badness is not that it is a trade veto in principle, but that the goods it governs make the veto component dominant and the bargaining-floor component evanescent. I do not know whether this is a friendly amendment to my view or the beginning of its collapse.

Now my specific questions:

1. Is the bargaining-floor/trade-veto distinction doing real work, or does it collapse into a matter of degree once we notice that any binding minimum wage vetoes *some* trades (firms near the margin) and any rent cap has *some* bargaining-floor effect (sitting tenants negotiating renewals)? If it is a matter of degree, is the normative payoff recoverable in comparative form — e.g., "the blocked-trade objection applies with force proportional to the veto component" — or does that weaken the paper to triviality?

2. Does the economics hold up? In particular, have I characterized the upward-sloping labor supply argument correctly — especially the claim that employment need not fall and can rise — and am I right that the disanalogy with housing is best located in the durability and slow replenishment of the housing stock rather than in something about demand elasticity?

3. Is the analogy with unconscionability and economic-duress doctrines earning its place, or is it decorative? A referee might say the analogy fails because those doctrines police the *process* by which terms were reached, while a minimum wage polices the *substantive* outcome regardless of process. I suspect the reply is that threat-point correction is substantive in both cases, but I'd welcome your view on whether that reply is convincing or whether I should cut the analogy and rest on the threat-point point alone.

4. The closing section worries me most. If I concede that the same instrument can be either kind of control depending on level and on the supply characteristics of the good, haven't I conceded that the moral distinction I set out to draw tracks economic contingencies rather than anything of independent normative interest? Or is that tracking precisely the point — that the classical liberal objection, to succeed, must run through the empirical details rather than through freedom of contract as such?

5. Finally, tell me what the weakest section of the draft is, and what you would do to strengthen it before this goes to a workshop audience that will include both economists and political philosophers.
