I've been following the peer disagreement literature — the dispute between conciliationists, who hold that discovering an apparent epistemic peer's disagreement should move you substantially toward their view (in the simplest form, to something near the midpoint of your two credences), and steadfasters, who hold that it can be perfectly rational to hold firm, since your original evidence doesn't evaporate just because someone equally smart read it differently — for long enough to notice a pattern: the moves on both sides are thoroughly mapped, the canonical cases (disagreement over a tip calculation, over a philosophy paper, over religious questions) have been run hundreds of times, and essentially nobody changes sides. I want a diagnosis of the impasse itself, not a survey of the positions.

By way of describing the debate concretely: the conciliationist's core thought is that peer disagreement is higher-order evidence — evidence about the reliability of your own first-order reasoning — and that you cannot defensibly discount it without already assuming the very reliability that's in question (the "self-trust" circularity worry). The steadfaster's core thought is that your first-order evidence is what it is: if you did the arithmetic carefully and your friend didn't, the fact of disagreement doesn't subtract from your evidence, though you may be uncertain whose situation is which. In between sit views on which disagreement is simply one more piece of evidence to weigh against the rest. Complications the literature keeps circling: the conciliationist's apparent self-undermining (philosophers disagree about conciliationism — should conciliationists then abandon it?), the question of how peerhood is ever established without track records that would settle who to trust, and whether the stipulated peers of the stylized cases are even possible.

Here is my working hypothesis, which I'd like you to test rather than assume: the debate is not really about peer disagreement at all, but about the individuation and commensurability of first-order and higher-order evidence — whether the fact that my peer believes otherwise enters the same evidential economy as my direct reasons for my view, or is a categorically different kind of input that the standard framework of reasons and evidence wasn't built to handle. If it's the latter, then perhaps the conciliationist and the steadfaster are each right within a different picture, and the appearance of direct conflict comes from their sharing a vocabulary ("evidence," "defeater," "rational") whose application conditions were fixed on the first-order cases. A related thought: as someone with Bayesian training, I notice that a straightforward modeling of the peer's credence as data seems to vindicate a modest steadfastness (the update depends on the likelihoods, not on symmetry), yet conciliationists complain that this modeling smuggles in independence assumptions that beg the question. Maybe the whole dispute is a fight over which formal idealization is faithful — a methodological dispute wearing epistemological clothes.

So: is this disagreement substantive, verbal, or a case of two questions — perhaps what it is rational to believe versus what one may permissibly do or assert — being run under a single heading? Where exactly do the sides part company: on the facts of the cases, on the standards for what counts as a reason, on bedrock intuitions that neither can argue for, or on the idealizations of the model cases? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a formal result, a class of cases where intuitions uncontroversially diverge, a distinction drawn elsewhere in epistemology (say, in how disagreement figures in debunking arguments in ethics or philosophy of religion) that could be imported — or should we expect none, and why? Please engage with what the positions actually claim rather than their labels, and be candid if the honest diagnosis is that one or both sides have quietly made their view unfalsifiable by case intuition.
