Okay so I've gone down a rabbit hole lately about why some countries end up rich and others stay poor, and everyone keeps pointing at the same example: Korea. The pitch goes something like this — one country, one people, same language, same culture, basically the same geography, then in 1945 it gets cut in half at the 38th parallel by outside powers, pretty much arbitrarily, and seventy years later one half is one of the richest places on earth and the other half literally goes dark at night in satellite photos. Books I've skimmed treat this as the killer natural experiment: culture held fixed, geography held fixed, only the institutions varied, so institutions must be what makes countries rich. Boom, argument over.

But something bugs me about it and I want you to stress-test the case instead of just repeating the tidy version. Because the two halves didn't only get different rules — they got different wars, different friends, different markets. The South could sell its stuff to the US and get rebuilt with aid money, the North got santioned and frozen out; the whole peninsuala got flattened in the early 50s and then rebuilt along completely different lines; and for the first couple of decades the North was apparently the more industrial half anyway (I read that somewhere, correct me if it's wrong), which is pretty awkward for the simple story since the "bad institutions" side was winning at first. So my question is: what does the Korea case actually prove, and where does the "natural experiment" framing break down? Which parts are real evidence that institutions cause wealth, and which parts are the institutions just being the pipe that geopolitical luck flows through — i.e. maybe the divergence shows that having the US as your patron beats having the USSR, and the institutions are downstream of that rather than the cause? I keep going back and forth with a friend of mine who's team-institutions all the way, and I want a careful answer: how far can you push the inference from this one split country before you're just telling a story, and is there anything the institutions-vs-geography-vs-culture debate can learn from history at all if its best case is a sample size of one?
