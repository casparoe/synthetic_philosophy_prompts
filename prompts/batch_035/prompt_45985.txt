bit of background first: I sit on a small "condition review" panel for a regional health service. A few times a year we get a dossier asking whether something should be counted as a disease "for our purposes" — which sounds technical but really decides whether treatments get covered, whether people can claim sick leave, whether screening programs get funded. Past cases have included things like prolonged grief (still intense, disabling grief more than a year after a bereavement) and gaming disorder, and we have misophonia (rage/panic reactions to other people's chewing sounds, basically) coming up. Nobody on the panel is a philosopher and honestly our discussions are a mess.

From my own reading I gather the underlying split is this: naturalists say disease is basically biological dysfunction — something in the person isn't doing what it's supposed to do, where "supposed" is read statistically, roughly along the lines of Boorse's biostatistical theory, dysfunction = failure of a part to contribute to survival and reproduction the way it typically does in the species. Normativists say no, disease is at least partly a value judgment — roughly Wakefield's line, that you need both a dysfunction AND harm judged by social standards, and there are people who push the value side even harder (if nothing were bad for anyone, nothing would be a disease, period). The panel ends up half-doing both without noticing: someone says "but it's in the ICD" (which begs the question, the ICD people had to decide the same thing), someone else says "but look how much suffering there is," and we usually end up splitting the difference in a way that satisfies nobody.

So what I want is a procedure. Please write the panel a decision procedure we can actually run in one long meeting: the questions in order, what a sufficient answer to each looks like, the points where the answer is "no, stop here," and the points where we have to admit the question is about values and hand it to a vote or a named official rather than pretend the procedure settled it. Keep whatever distinctions from the philosophy actually matter for coverage-type decisions and drop the rest, and be upfront about where the procedure is going to misfire or just can't finish. Then run it on the three cases above — prolonged grief, gaming disorder, misophonia — and tell me straight which ones it completes and which it hands back to us. For what it's worth I personally think gaming disorder is mostly a moral panic, so if the procedure comes out clearing it over my objections, I'd honestly like to see that rather than have the procedure rigged to agree with me. Also, one of us has to write public minutes, so the steps need to be defensible to a reader who wasn't in the room — no step that only works because we all share an intuition.

Note the constraints: we're clinicians and policy staff, not philosophers, the decision has to be made in weeks, and the final sign-off belongs to the clinical director, so the procedure's output should be a recommendation with the value-laden parts marked as such. If the honest answer is that naturalism vs normativism doesn't actually change any of our three outcomes either way, say that too — I'd rather know the philosophy is decorative here than keep pretending its doing work it isnt.
