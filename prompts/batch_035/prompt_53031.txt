Last year my housemates and I had a stupid but intense dispute about how to split rent, given that the rooms differ a lot, and we brought in a friend as a mediator. The thing I keep thinking about since is that her entire method was: get each of us to name a number, then push us toward the middle of the two numbers, with each round of offers narrowing the gap. When I later read that negotiation consultants advise you to never open with an offer, or to open with an absurd one, it hit me that "meet in the middle" as a fairness norm is hostage to whatever numbers get threw out first. And yet everyone in the room, including me, felt like the midpoint had some claim to fairness — it just *felt* fair, in a way that, say, the midpoint between my estimate of the grocery bill and my housemate's wouldn't.

I also stumbled onto the fact that game theorists have formal proposals for what a fair deal is, and they don't work off midpoints of offers at all. One idea (I think it goes back to Nash, of equilibrium fame) says: identify what happens if there's no deal — the fallback — and pick the deal that maximizes the product of each side's gain over that fallback. A different one says: each side has a best case, the most they could possibly get out of this dispute, and a fair deal keeps the two sides' gains in proportion to their best cases, so whoever stood to gain more keeps gaining more. What struck me is that both of these make the no-deal scenario and the best-case scenario do all the work, and the actual offers do none of it — so the everyday practice and the formal ones don't even seem to share inputs.

So here's what I want to know: what theory of fairness does the everyday practice — haggling to the middle, mediators proposing the average, evenly splitting a restaurant bill no matter who ordered the steak — actually presuppose? It looks like it presupposes that the offers themselves carry the relevant information, which can't be right given how gameable that is, unless there's some story about why stated offers are meaningful anyway. And there's the other side of it: we would never compromise toward the middle on a factual dispute (if I say the store closes at 7 and you say 8, we don't agree it closes at 7:30), so does the split-the-difference norm presuppose that the thing being divided is a matter of competing claims rather than of fact — and if so, what happens to the norm in cases, like our rent dispute, that are partly factual, since some rooms objectively cost more to rent? Finally, is everyday splitting just a crude approximation of one of the formal ideas, or does it genuinely diverge from both — and if we rebuilt the practice around the proportional one, what would actually change in how people haggle? I'd like the implicit theory reconstructed properly, an honest account of where the practice is too sloppy to pin down any single theory, and not a lecture on bargaining theory.
