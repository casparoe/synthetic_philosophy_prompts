The whole plot turns on whether the uploaded person is the same person or just a copy with the original dead on the table, and when I sat down to outline I realized my own intuitions are a complete mess, and that the mess is the interesting part, but only if I actually understand the mess instead of faking it. So here's what I'm asking: I've got maybe six months of research time before the outline locks, and I'm a lit guy, not a philosopher, so I need a prioritized sense of which open questions in this space deserve my limited reading energy and which are just set dressing that makes a novel sound deep.

To give you a sense of the tangle, there's the Chalmers thought experiment about gradually replacing your neurons with functionally identical silicon, where either your consciousness slowly fades while you insist nothing changed, or it doesn't, and both horns are horrifying in different ways. There's the distinction I keep tripping on between a destructive scan and a non-destructive one where the biological you walks out of the clinic and has to have lunch with your copy. There are questions I've half-invented and half-absorbed from podcasts: if two uploads run in perfect sync are they one person or two, does it matter morally if one gets paused for a year, does the upload dispute the will when the original's estate says it goes to "me," does substrate or running speed change anything about the stream of consciousness itself. Rank what you take to be the five or so most load-bearing open questions for someone in my position, tell me which ones the book genuinely has to take a stance on versus which it can leave ambiguous without cheating the reader, and defend the ordering — I want the criteria too, like whether a question is tractable for a novelist who'll never master the formal stuff, whether it actually changes how scenes play out on the page, and whether it's something the plot quietly depends on without my having noticed.
