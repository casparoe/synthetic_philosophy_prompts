my friends and i do this thing where every month we pick a dead thinker and argue about what they'd actually make of some current mess. this month it's bertrand russell and the topic is dating apps. on the surface he seems like an easy win for "he'd be delighted" — marriage and morals (1929) reads like he predicted half of it: he thought sex outside marriage was fine if people were honest and careful about children, wanted something like trial marriages, thought the churches' moralizing about sex was mostly superstition, and even argued jealousy was a vice to be worked down rather than a law of nature you just obey. he took real hits for it too, like losing his teaching gig in new york because of the uproar over those views.

but there's stuff that cuts the other way. he genuinely thought sex as mere appetite is hollow, that the whole point of more sexual freedom was *better love*, not more transactions, and that people who chase novelty for its own sake end up worse off than the prudes. and he actually lived into the sexual revolution, so he'd seen the free love communes and the first wave of no-fault divorce — he's not naive about any of this.

so here's what i want: what would russell really say about the current dating landscape — swipe apps and the gamification of attraction, the whole "situationship" thing, ethical non-monogamy as an online subculture, declining marriage and birth rates, people just opting out entirely? i want you to reason from his actual positions (jealousy, children's welfare as the real test of sexual morality, appetite vs love, his hatred of hypocrisy, his idea that a good marriage needs serious work and can't survive on convention alone) rather than just "old progressive would like progress." two things in particular:

1. where does he flip? like, is there a place where his own principles push him somewhere suprising — maybe he ends up more sympathetic to the trad-bras than anyone would guess, or conversely places where he'd say we haven't gone nearly far enough?
2. what's genuinely new for him? the apps are doing something no one in 1929 or even 1970 quite anticipated — the abundance problem, the infinite scroll of options, outsourcing matchmaking to an engagement-optimized algorithm. does he have the resources to say anything non-generic about that, or is this where a guy who thought technology was mostly making people anxious would just shrug?

please actually commit to verdicts instead of the "on one hand, on the other hand" thing. and its fine to note where you're extrapolating beyond anything he wrote — just flag it when you do.
