I've never studied any of this stuff, just been chewing on it since I found a used book about game theory years ago, so bear with me. Everyone says nuclear deterrence "works" because no one has used a nuke in war since 1945, but I cant figure out what it would even MEAN for that claim to be justified, so before I argue with my brother in law about disarmament I want the requirements written out first. What would an acceptable justification of nuclear deterrence actually have to deliver? Give me the list of things any real case for it has to get right, with the reason behind each, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are just what one side insists on.

Stuff I think belongs on the list, though maybe some are wrong:

1. It has to solve what I privately call the bluff problem. Deterrence works by threatening retaliation that would be pointless or straight up crazy to actually carry out once the first strike has already landed -- once your cities are gone, retaliating doesn't undo anything, it just burns theirs. So does the justification have to make the threat somehow credible without ever being carried out, or is it enough that the other side can't be SURE you won't? And is a threat you have no intention of carrying out morally different from a lie?

2. It has to say something about luck vs the threat doing the work. Eighty years without a nuclear war could be deterrence or could be that we got lucky a couple times when the wires crossed. Does a justification get to just assume the near misses were survivable?

3. It cant treat accidental launch as some side issue. The chance the whole system ends the world by mistake is part of the thing being justified, not a separate regrettable cost.

4. It has to deal with the fact that deterrence seemed to hold even when only one side had the bomb, and seems to "work" between powers that are not exactly chess players, which makes me wonder if the threat is even what's doing the work.

And some things I suspect are camp prejudices dressed up as requirements: that deterrence needs usable weapons (not purely retaliatory ones), that it needs numerical parity, that it needs escalation dominance rather than just the ability to destroy the other side no matter what.

The thing I most want answered: are these requirements even jointly satisfiable? Like, if solving the bluff problem forces you to inject randomness into the system -- delegated launch authority, hair-trigger postures, threats that leave something to chance on purpose -- and that same randomness is what generates the accident risk in (3), then maybe the requirements eat each other and there is no clean justification, only a tradeoff you're choosing blind. That would honestly change my mind about disarmament. So take a position on it -- don't give me the "reasonable people disagree" thing, and don't end by laying out options for me to pick from. Lead with your verdict in the very first sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble. And no reading list or name-dropping of books, I just want the argument.
