I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, but over the past couple of years I've settled into a view I'd like stress-tested rather than just praised: that pleasure is the only thing good for its own sake, and everything else I value — my career going well, my friends being happy, finally understanding how a gnarly system works — matters only as a means to, or a component of, pleasant states of mind. I take this to be a version of hedonism, and I know the usual objections, but the one that actually keeps me up at night came out of a long argument with a friend of mine who works in cognitive science. She pointed out that the items I'd file under "pleasure" seem to have nothing interesting in common: the pleasure of cracking a stubborn bug after three days, the pleasure of a hot shower after a cold ride, the pleasure of an inside joke landing with exactly one person, the pleasure of hearing a song I loved at nineteen, the quiet pleasure of finishing a book I'll never reread. When I try to state what all of these share, I either get something so thin it might be vacuous or something that clearly fits only some of them. Her conclusion is that this sinks my view: if "pleasure" doesn't pick out a genuine category, then saying it's the only intrinsic good is a claim about a category with no membership conditions, and my hedonism is built on a word rather than a thing.

My instinctive defense has two parts, and I'd like you to evaluate both. First, I claim I don't need a definition: I can reliably sort pleasures from non-pleasures case by case, the way people used "water" perfectly well before anyone knew about H2O, so the absence of a common property is a problem for theorists, not for me. Second, when she pushed me on borderline states — the strange feeling of missing something I dreaded, or the odd satisfaction of a funeral done well — I said these just show the concept has fuzzy edges, like "heap" does, and fuzziness isn't refutation. But I have a nagging worry about my own defense: unlike water, I can't point to any deeper fact that my case-by-case judgments are tracking, so if the judgments themselves wobble, there may be nothing underneath for them to be more or less accurate about. And there's a further test she floated that I can't quite dismiss: suppose neuroscientists someday find a single neural signature common to all the states I call pleasures — would that vindicate me, or would it just show I've been tracking a brain event, leaving open whether that event is what I actually care about when I say my friends' happiness matters?

So here is what I'm asking for. What would genuinely count against my view — which observations, arguments, thought experiments, or results from psychology or neuroscience or decision theory should move me — ranked from fatal through serious to merely awkward? Please distinguish the things a thoughtful defender of my position would concede as counting against it from the things they'd explain away, and tell me whether the fuzzy-edges move and the H2O move are principled qualifications or ways of insulating the view from ever being wrong. I'd especially like your honest verdict on whether the heterogeneity problem my friend raised is the deep objection it seems to me to be, or whether I'm overweighting it because it's the one I thought of myself.
