I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas for a general readership, and I've been following the debate over the so-called gender-equality paradox: the reported finding that, across countries, higher levels of gender equality go together with *larger* average differences between men and women in occupational and field-of-study choices — fewer women in STEM in the most egalitarian societies, relatively more in people-oriented work — rather than smaller ones. I've come round to a fairly strong reading of this pattern: that when a society liberalises and material necessity recedes, the differences that remain are, in some substantial part, the unconstrained expression of sex-linked dispositions in occupational interests, rather than the residue of lingering constraint or socialisation. On that reading, the freest societies are showing us something closer to a baseline than to a partial achievement. It's a conclusion I'd have to defend in print, under scrutiny, so I want a map of where it is exposed.

Tell me what would count against it. In particular, what features of the evidence would have to be different for the inference to fail? I have in mind things like this: if the cross-national association turned out to hinge on measurement choices — the composite used to index occupational segregation, the particular equality index, or the leverage of a few countries at the extremes — what exactly would that show, and what would it not show? If the more informative comparison were variance within countries rather than between them, what would the strong conclusion need to survive there? What would count as evidence that the most egalitarian societies still constrain choices in ways that mimic preference, and how strong would that evidence need to be to sink the inference rather than merely qualify it? And which findings from elsewhere — developmental psychology, cohort comparisons, sudden natural experiments in which constraints were lifted — would genuinely bear on it in either direction?

I'd like the defeaters ranked: which would be fatal to the strong conclusion, which merely awkward, and which a reasonable defender of the position ought to concede as counting against it rather than wave away. Please also be candid about whether the position has been quietly insulated by its qualifications — whether 'in some substantial part' has become a hedge that no evidence could touch — and whether that is a defect in a claim of this kind, given that it will be read by the public as saying something definite. I'm less interested in having the position defended back to me than in knowing what would move me off it.

Please write your answer in British English.
