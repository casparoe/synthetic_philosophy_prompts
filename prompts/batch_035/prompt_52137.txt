Been reading rationalist blogs and forums since the old Overcoming Bias days, so I've seen maybe a dozen "shut up and multiply" posts by now. The latest one summarized the Allais paradox like this: 'Allais offered people two choices. First, $1m for sure, or a lottery paying $5m with 10% probability, $1m with 89%, and nothing with 1% -- most people take the sure million. Second, $1m with 11% probability and nothing otherwise, or $5m with 10% probability -- most people now switch and take the $5m shot. No consistent expected-utility ranking allows both choices, so this violates the independence axiom, which says your preference between two options shouldn't change when a new option is added to the menu. Allais thereby proved that human preferences are irrational, and that's why economists abandoned expected utility theory for prospect theory.'

A couple things bug me about it. The numbers sound right from what I remember, but that gloss on the independence axiom sounds more like the independence-of-irrelevant-alternatives thing I've seen people invoke in voting contexts, and also i vaguely recall Allais himself thought the preferences were fine and it was the theory that was broken?
