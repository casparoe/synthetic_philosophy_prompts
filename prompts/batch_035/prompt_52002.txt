I run a philosophy discussion group at a community college, about 12 students, one 90-minute session. They've had intro ethics but no decision theory, and I want an activity that makes them *feel* the problem of interpersonal utility comparisons instead of just hearing about it. The shape I'm imagining: each participant gets a private slip with a made-up utility function on it (you write the slips for me — just ordinal lists won't do, I want numbers, and they should differ between people in ways that matter), and then the group has to make real allocation decisions trading one person's welfare against another's — distributing a few genuinely unpleasant tasks and a few small prizes, say. Hard constraint: nobody can show their slip to anyone. At some point I want them to hit the strategic problem too: once people can just *claim* how bad something is for them, claims get inflated, and they should notice that any mechanism asking for intensity reports is a target for gaming. Rules, materials, a points scheme (no real money), a timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the actual philosophy: why ordinal preferences are unproblematic, why cardinal utilities within one person are also fine (have them trade off against their own slip — that part should visibly work), and why the interpersonal scaling is the datum no one can observe. If there's a natural moment for them to reinvent something like the "imagine you had an equal chance of being each person" move, great, but don't force it. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about which part of the problem the game can't reproduce — my worry is that a slip with numbers printed on it already smuggles in a common scale and pretends to solve half of what's at issue, but I can't see how to avoid that.
