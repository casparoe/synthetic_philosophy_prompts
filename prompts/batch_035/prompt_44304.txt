For the year-end satire issue of our graduate economics newsletter, I'd like a piece skewering how herding works. Please write an Onion-style news article, headline plus 400–500 words, reporting that a mid-sized city's once-bustling restaurant row has been reduced to a single four-hour queue outside one mediocre taco truck, all traceable to one early patron who had a cold and couldn't taste anything.

The comedy has to come from getting the economics right rather than from mocking people as dumb. Each new arrival should be portrayed as updating rationally: they know their own taste for the taco truck is a nearly worthless private signal compared with the accumulated evidence of a hundred earlier choices, so following the crowd is individually Bayesian. The piece should quietly make clear what went wrong — one early low signal got locked in, everyone else's choices after that conveyed no new information, and the town's collective knowledge quietly evaporated — without ever stating this in those terms. Include at least one quote from a "concerned" behavioral economist lamenting crowd irrationality, and one from a visibly smug theorist pointing out that every single diner behaved exactly as his model predicted, no irrationality required. The cascade should also be shown to be fragile in the right way: when one respected food critic walks past the entire queue and eats next door, the line collapses by closing time and the taco truck is empty the next day, for equally rational reasons.

The target is the gap between individually rational inference and socially disastrous aggregation, not gullibility — a specialist reader should come away thinking the piece could be used to teach Banerjee-style cascades, and the general reader should just find it funny.
