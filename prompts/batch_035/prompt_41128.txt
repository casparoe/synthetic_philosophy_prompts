I'm a second-year grad student switching into philosophy of mind from a neuroscience undergrad, and I've got about a year to write a thesis before I have to commit to a dissertation direction. I'm drawn to split-brain stuff but worried the field has been circling the same interpretation debate since the 70s: did the commissurotomy patients have two streams of consciousness, or one stream with degraded communication, or does the question not even make sense as posed? So help me figure out what actually deserves the year.

My own tentative map of the open questions, feel free to disagree with all of it: (1) whether the real bottleneck is that nobody has a worked-out notion of "unity" — phenomenal unity vs unity of access vs mere behavioral coordination get run together in the literature, and maybe settling that is upstream of everything else; (2) whether the empirical base is weaker than philosophers assume, since the dramatic cases involve lateralized stimulus presentation and forced-choice tasks, which arguably test attention and report channels rather than consciousness itself; (3) whether newer-ish angles like partial or transient callosal disconnection, or the plasticity seen in patients operated on young, could break the deadlock by making the effects graded instead of all-or-nothing; and (4) what the answer implies downstream — for personal identity, for animal cases like birds whose hemispheres sleep independently, for whether "one subject per cortex" was ever a principled idea.

Which of these (or something I'm not listing) would you prioritize for someone with my background and timeline, and why? I want the ordering defended — importance, tractability for a philosophy student who can read the neuroscience but won't be running experiments, how many other debates hang on each one — plus a note on which you'd deprioritize because the answer wouldn't actually change much downstream. American English please, and casual is fine.
