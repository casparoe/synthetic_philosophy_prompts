I'm taking a political philosophy course that spends several weeks on the liberal–communitarian debate, and there's a puzzle from the unit I can't stop chewing on, so I'd like your help thinking it through. It concerns naturalization law, which I've realized sits oddly between the two positions.

Take the U.S. as the example. To naturalize, you must live as a permanent resident for years, demonstrate English proficiency, pass a civics test on the constitution and the branches of government, and swear an oath of allegiance that includes a promise to bear arms in the country's defense when required by law. Native-born citizens are asked for none of this — no test, no oath, no proof of attachment — and their membership is not only automatic but essentially unconditional: it cannot be involuntarily stripped, even for serious crimes, whereas naturalized citizens can be denaturalized for misrepresentations made during the application process. Several European states have gone further, revoking the citizenship of dual nationals who joined armed groups abroad, a power that by its nature reaches only naturalized citizens and migrants, never the native-born. And surveys routinely find that a substantial share of native-born citizens could not pass the very civics test that newcomers must pass.

What theory of political membership does this bundle of practices presuppose? The civics test and the oath look communitarian: membership as identification with a shared history and set of institutions, something you must demonstrate you've absorbed. The renunciation of prior allegiances and the formal, vow-like structure of the oath look liberal: membership as something closer to consent or contract. But the denaturalization asymmetry fits neither cleanly — a thick-communities view should presumably allow expelling native-born members who betray the community's core commitments, and a consent-based view should arguably hold everyone to the same demonstrated standard, or to none. Instead the law seems to treat the naturalized citizen's membership as conditional in a way the native-born's is not, which looks like a third, unarticulated view about how membership is acquired.

I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory of membership these practices rest on, and to do it carefully: distinguish what the practices strictly presuppose — what only makes sense under one of the two positions we've studied — from what they merely sit comfortably with. Say where the practices are philosophically underdetermined, such that a liberal, a communitarian, and perhaps some third theorist could each endorse them unchanged. Then tell me what would actually have to change in the law if the rival theory were adopted: Would a thoroughgoing communitarianism owe us a loyalty test for everyone, or would it abolish the naturalization test as an empty formality? Would a strict liberal contract view make denaturalization as unavailable as it is for the native-born, or instead make everyone's citizenship revocable for breach?

I also want you to be candid about the argument's weak points: tell me where your reconstruction is most likely to go wrong, and what consideration — an argument, or some feature of the practices I've described — would push you toward a different verdict about what the law presupposes.
