I'm grading midterm answers for an intro political philosophy course and need a second opinion on one script. The exam question was: "Rawls claims that the parties in the original position would choose his two principles of justice over alternatives like average utilitarianism. Explain how the veil of ignorance is supposed to make this a fair choice situation, why the choice is supposed to go the way Rawls says it does, and why critics have thought the argument depends on an implausible attitude toward risk."

Here's the student's answer, typed out:

"Rawls wants to design a choice situation so fair that whatever principles come out of it are just, whatever they turn out to be. The original position is this situation, and the veil of ignorance is what makes it fair: the parties choosing don't know their class, their wealth, their natural talents, their race or gender, or even their own conception of the good. They also don't know the general facts about psychology and economics of their society, since knowing those might tip them toward principles favoring one social arrangement over another. What they do know is that they want primary goods — rights, liberties, opportunities, income, and the bases of self-respect — things anyone would want whatever their life plan turns out to be.

Because the parties are impartial behind the veil — Rawls builds altruism into the setup, so each party cares about everyone equally — they will pick principles that are fair to all rather than rigged in favor of anyone. Faced with the alternatives, average utilitarianism has an obvious downside: if you turn out to be on the losing end, you can be sacrificed for the greater good with no floor under you. The rational choice is maximin — arrange things so the worst possible outcome is as good as it can be — and maximin applied to social and economic inequality just is the difference principle, so the original position argument essentially guarantees that the second principle gets chosen. And because the worst off can genuinely benefit from some inequalities (incentives for the talented, etc.), the difference principle permits those rather than demanding flat equality. In fact the difference principle can even justify trading away some political liberties when doing so would produce big economic gains for the worst off, which shows how flexible it is compared to a rigid principle of equal liberty. This is exactly why critics say the whole thing smuggles in risk aversion: if you took the parties to instead maximize expected utility, you'd arguably get utilitarianism, so Rawls needs them to be maximiners and its not clear why they would be."
