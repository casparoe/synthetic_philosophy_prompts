I write about science and ideas for a mid-sized outlet, and last week I interviewed a physicist with a new book out about how science has taken over the questions philosophy used to fumble. Afterwards, over drinks, he laid this on me, and I cant stop chewing on it, because if it works it seems to prove a lot more than he noticed:

(1) A field is making progress only if it's settling its foundational questions — the big ones that tell you what the field is even about.
(2) Philosophy's foundational questions (free will, what there is, how we ought to live) are still debated today in the same form they were debated 2,400 years ago.
(3) Nothing that goes 2,400 years without settling its defining questions is making progress.
(4) So philosophy makes no progress.
(5) And this isn't just true so far, it's true forever: whenever a philosophical question does get settled, it stops being called philosophy and gets a new name (logic, psychology, physics). So philosophy, by definition, is the unsettled residue, and (4) can't fail.

He was obviously hoping I'd quote this in the piece. Heres my problem: (4) and (5) together look like they prove too much. Run (1)–(3) on math and you get that math makes no progress, since Goldbach has been open for going on 300 years and the continuum hypothesis for close to a century and a half. Run it on physics before, say, 1650, and physics made no progress either for its first two millennia. When I pointed this out he just grinned and said he'd happily take both of those bullets too — he's one of those guys who thinks only settled science counts as knowledge. That reaction makes me suspect the rot isn't in how far the argument reaches but in the premises themselves. My hunch is that "in the same form" in (2) is quietly doing all the work, but I can't put my finger on exactly what work. So: which step should I refuse to grant him, what does refusing it cost, and is (5) a rigged definition he literally cannot lose with, or is there a respectable version of it? I'd honestly rather keep (5) in some form than (2), but tell me if thats backwards.
