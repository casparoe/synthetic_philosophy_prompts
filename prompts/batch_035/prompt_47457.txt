A friend of mine who works in risk pricing has taken to repeating a line he picked up somewhere in the Bayesian literature: the probability of a conditional is a conditional probability. He used it at dinner to settle a dispute. I wanted to bet ten dollars on "if the amendment passes, turnout will top sixty percent," and he said pricing it is trivial: take your credence that turnout tops sixty percent given that the amendment passes, and that is the fair price — a conditional bet is just a bet, priced like any other event. The price felt right to me, but a philosopher at the table muttered that David Lewis had shown back in the seventies that this slogan cannot be true without restriction — that if it held generally, conditional probabilities would stop depending on what you conditioned on — and that the whole idea has been in trouble ever since. Nobody at the table could unpack that, and now I don't know what I actually agreed to.

Please disentangle the slogan for me. Lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses "the probability of a conditional is a conditional probability" could be expressing: for instance, as a stipulative definition of a new operator; as a descriptive claim about the semantics of English "if"; as a normative constraint on rational credence in conditionals; as a claim restricted to simple, non-embedded conditionals; and as a claim about what must happen when conditionals are conjoined, negated, or iterated. For each reading, say whether it is a harmless definition, a substantive but perhaps defensible thesis, or a target that Lewis-style triviality arguments actually refute — and where you can, sketch the shape of the triviality reasoning in plain prose, so I can see how conditioning on a conditional, or applying the slogan twice, forces probabilities into collapse. Finally, tell me which reading my friend needs in order to price my bet the way he did, and whether the reading he needs is one of the ones that survive.
