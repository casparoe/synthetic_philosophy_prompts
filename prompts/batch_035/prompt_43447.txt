The exam question was:

"The 2015 Open Science Collaboration project attempted to replicate 100 psychology studies, and only a minority of the replication attempts came out statistically significant. A common reaction to this was: this shows frequentist statistics is broken, and psychology should just switch to Bayesian methods. Evaluate that reaction. Does the replication crisis reflect a failure of frequentist inference specifically, and would Bayesian methods have prevented it?"

Here's the student's answer:

"The reaction is half right, because the replication crisis is at bottom a failure of significance testing. A p-value is the probability of getting data at least this extreme given that the null is true, and nothing in the procedure ever tells you the probability that your hypothesis is true -- Fisher himself would have admitted as much. Since psychology mostly tests low-prior hypotheses, and since only significant results get published, a large share of published findings will be false even if every individual test is performed perfectly. Ioannidis called this the garden of forking paths: researchers face many legitimate analytic choices -- which covariates to include, which subjects to exclude, when to stop collecting data -- and every choice nudges the p-value around, so with enough flexibility you can get p < .05 on basically anything. The 2015 project confirmed his prediction: most published psychology findings are false.

Would Bayesian methods have prevented the crisis? Mostly yes. Bayesian inference doesn't depend on the sampling plan -- the likelihood principle says the evidence is the evidence, whenever you happened to stop collecting it -- so optional stopping and the forking paths problem disappear. And a Bayes factor gives you the actual probability that your hypothesis is true, so researchers would have seen that p = .04 in a low-prior field is actually pretty weak evidence, and they wouldn't have overclaimed in the first place. The crisis was never really about fraud or lazy researchers; it was about a method that cannot say the thing we most want to say, and the fix is a method that can."
