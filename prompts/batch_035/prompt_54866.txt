I run pre-departure orientations for a small study-abroad outfit — roughly 80 students a year, headed to maybe a dozen countrys, and the "local norms" part of the orientation falls to me, one hour the week before they fly. Right now my advice is basically vibes: be respectful, watch what the locals do, when in Rome, etc. That's not much use when a student actually asks whether they *have* to do the thing. Do they really have to tip twenty percent in the US as a visitor from Japan? Should they wait at a completely empty Berlin crosswalk at 2am with no car in sight? And when a local convention collides with a student's own convictions — somewhere the convention is that men don't shake women's hands, and a student finds that demeaning — is following it politeness, or is it complicity? I need something more like a procedure and less like a mood.

So here's what I want: take whatever the philosophy of convention actually has to say (Lewis-style stuff — conventions as solutions to coordination problems, where everyone conforms becuase everyone else conforms — plus whatever ethics has to say about when conforming becomes a genuine obligation on an outsider rather than a local habit) and turn it into a decision procedure I can teach and the students can run themselves on the ground. Give me the questions in order, what counts as enough of an answer to each one, where the procedure outputs "treat this as a real obligation," "go along with it but it's not morally on you," and "don't do it," and where it refuses to decide and hands it back to the student because the question is one of values, not facts. It has to handle the case where the student can't possibly know the answer in advance — they've never been there, and from the outside you can't tell a coordination norm from a local quirk — so tell me what the default should be under uncertainty: ask a local? copy the majority? err toward conforming or toward staying out of it?

Constraints, so this stays realistic: students won't carry a manual, so it has to compress to something memorable, around five questions max; and I have exactly that one hour, so any step I can't teach in ten minutes is out. Please be honest about which steps are doing real work and which are just judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and say outright what the procedure was never going to settle.

Then run it on three cases. (1) The Japanese student and the twenty percent tip in the US. (2) Jaywalking, both directions: a German exchange student in Manhattan where locals cross against the light constantly, and the empty-street Berlin case in reverse. (3) A female student going somewhere the local convention is that men don't shake women's hands, who thinks the convention is demeaning — follow it out of respect, refuse quietly, and is that even her call to make rather than mine to impose? And include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer I won't like: I privately think visitors shouldn't have to tip American amounts, it just props up a system I'd like to see die, so if the procedure says the tip is a genuine obligation, say so anyway and show your work.

One formatting thing: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — continuous prose only, the way you'd write a long email to a colleague. My students are reading this on their phones anyway.
