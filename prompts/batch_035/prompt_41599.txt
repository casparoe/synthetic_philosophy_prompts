I am a novelist who writes chiefly about farming communities, and I have just joined the board of a literary foundation that runs a small residency programme in a converted farmhouse. The lease is expiring, and the board faces a choice. Renewing it on the only terms the landowner will accept requires the neighbouring tenant family to surrender grazing rights their household has held for three generations — a serious, perhaps terminal blow to their livelihood. Converting the programme into a series of city workshops, by contrast, would confer a modest benefit — an afternoon of tuition, a manuscript read by someone who matters — on several hundred aspiring writers a year. One board member says the arithmetic is decisive: the small gains, summed, plainly outweigh the one loss, and any squeamishness about that is mere sentimentality. Another replies that a single household's livelihood cannot be set against a heap of minor conveniences, however tall the heap, any more than one may take a healthy stranger's organs to save five patients. I notice, uncomfortably, that my own novels have trained me to side with the second view, and I do not trust my training. Can the board reason its way to a decision here rather than merely feel its way? In particular, does the answer change if the several hundred becomes several thousand — and if it does, what exactly is it that changed?
