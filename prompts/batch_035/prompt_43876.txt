I teach philosophy of science, and every second year I give the structural realism lecture, and every time I realize I don't actually know what I mean by "structure." So this term I'm trying to work backwards from my own gut verdicts about what survives theory change, and I want to know whether there's some principle that captures them — and if not, which of my verdicts has to go.

Here are the verdicts. Fresnel's wave theory of light into Maxwell's electromagnetism: the ether stories died, the wave equation and the interference relations survived, and I say that's the clean case of structure being preserved — the standard Worrall example, and my gut agrees with it. Carnot's efficiency formula, derived back when heat was widely taken to be a conserved fluid: the fluid turned out not to exist, the formula survived untouched, and I count that as preservation too — maybe even the best case, since the ontology wasn't just incomplete but flat wrong. Phlogiston chemistry into oxygen chemistry: here I say nothing real was preserved, even though the phlogiston people had their own quantitative bookkeeping (weights lost on calcination and so on) — that carried over, but I dont want to call it structure.

Then the pair I cant square. Lorentz's ether theory versus special relativity: observationally indistinguishable, formally near-identical, and my instinct says they're the same theory wearing different metaphysics, so the ether's funeral cost nothing structurally. Ptolemy versus Copernicus: also observationally indistinguishable at the time, but here I want to say Copernicus got the structure of the solar system right and Ptolemy got it wrong — not the same theory at all. Both pairs agree on all observations and I'm treating them differently, which smells bad.

A couple more, since they're where I get honest. Newtonian gravity into general relativity: I say preserved, but only in the weak-field limit — the inverse-square relations survive as approximations, so maybe preservation comes in degrees and I've been pretending it's all-or-nothing. Entropy: the symbol and the equations for S survived, but the interpretation behind them kept getting swapped out — the conserved-fluid story, then Clausius, then the statistical reading — and I genuinely can't decide whether what survived was structure or just a word.

And the case I keep going back and forth on: whether the quark and gauge structure of QCD would count as preserved if it someday turns out that quarks are emergent from something deeper. By the Carnot standard it seems yes, by the phlogiston standard it seems no, and I'd love the principle to tell me which.

For what it's worth, my crude first pass was "what's preserved is the equations, minus the ontology," but that seems to let the phlogiston bookkeeping in, and I can't see how to keep it out without also kicking Carnot out. So: is there a principle that gets all these verdicts, or close? If two of them really can't be saved together, tell me which one you'd throw overboard and why. Then tell me what the principle predicts for a case I haven't listed — say, the wavefunction's status if some future theory keeps the Schrödinger formalism but drops the story about the 3N-dimensional configuration space. Go as slow as you need to on the Lorentz/Ptolemy pair; that's the one I actually care about.
