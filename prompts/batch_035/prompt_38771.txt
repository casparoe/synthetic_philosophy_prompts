At a dinner party last weekend, two friends of mine — a philosophy PhD student and a software engineer who reads a lot of ethics — spent over an hour arguing about whether the doctrine of double effect can bear the weight that trolley cases put on it, and they eventually asked me to referee. I couldn't, so I'm asking you. (1) Lena opened by saying the doctrine explains the standard contrast: diverting a runaway trolley onto a side track where it will kill one person is permissible because his death is a merely foreseen side effect, whereas shoving a heavy man off a footbridge to stop the trolley is impermissible because his body is the means of the rescue, and the doctrine prohibits precisely intending harm as a means. (2) Marco countered with the loop variant: suppose the side track curves back onto the main line, so the one person's body is the only thing that stops the trolley before it reaches the five. His body now does exactly the causal work the footbridge man's would, yet intuitions still favor diverting — so either the doctrine misclassifies the case or the intuition is wrong, and neither option is comfortable. (3) Lena replied that what the agent intends in the loop case is just redirecting the trolley onto that track segment, not stopping it with the man's body: she would happily divert onto an empty loop if one existed, and would gladly have the one leap clear if a buffer further along would catch the trolley, whereas no parallel description of the footbridge shove explains why it is performed without invoking the man's being placed in the trolley's path. (4) Marco called this description-relativity the real problem: by Lena's test, a terror bomber can redescribe his act as releasing ordnance above a military target, hoping the civilians near it escape, and thereby render their deaths incidental; if she insists some descriptions are unavailable to the agent, she owes a principled account of the grain at which intentions are individuated, but agents' self-descriptions are manipulable and there is a continuum of cases — factory bombing with foreseen civilian deaths at one end, terror bombing to break morale at the other — with no non-arbitrary place to draw the line. (5) Lena's last word was that the grain problem afflicts every rival too: rights-based views must also say why harms inflicted as means are worse than equal harms merely foreseen, so the difficulty is a cost of the doctrine, not a refutation, and anyway our confidence in the loop intuition is likely contaminated by our verdict in the ordinary switch case, from which it differs only by an invisible detail most people overlook. Please adjudicate each move in turn: who is right at each step, whether Lena's reply in (3) is a substantive distinction or a verbal maneuver, whether the description-relativity charge in (4) defeats the doctrine or merely damages it, and what, if anything, both of them are missing.
