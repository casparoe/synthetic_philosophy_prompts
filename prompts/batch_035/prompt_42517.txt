Been reading LessWrong and adjacent blogs since about 2012, sat through a few CFAR-adjacent workshops, done a couple of meditation retreats, and somewhere in there I absorbed the standard rationalist line: introspection is mostly confabulation, you're the press secretary and not the president, System 1 does the real work and tells you a nice story after. Kahneman vibes. But I've been collecting my actual gut reactions to introspective reports and they're way less uniform than my stated view, so here are the verdicts I've recieved from my own gut, and I want to know what principle, if any, is hiding in them.

1. Choice-blindness experiments, where a subject confidently explains why she picked the face she actually didn't pick: I'm basically 100% confident the explanation is confabulated.
2. Anchoring: someone hears a big random number, gives a higher estimate, then insists the number didn't influence them. I trust that they consciously experienced doing the arithmetic (that's introspection) and simultaneously don't trust their claim that the anchor had no effect (also introspection). Same episode, both calls feel right to me.
3. Pain reports. "I'm in pain" I trust about as completely as I trust anything, and I can't even imagine what evidence could beat it.
4. Friend gets insulted at a party, says "I feel fine, honestly," then spends the whole evening sniping at waiters. I half trust the report — I believe she really did feel fine as a felt sensation — but I don't trust it as evidence that nothing is bothering her.
5. A friend with a decade of serious practice gives me a detailed blow-by-blow of the thought sequence that led up to snapping at her kid, including a little flicker of shame right before the anger. By my stated view this is causal self-explanation, i.e. prime confabulation territory, and yet I trust it more than I trust most people's ordinary mood reports.
6. After an interview I feel sure I noticed every flicker of my reaction to the candidate, but I don't trust that my "great fit" impression wasn't driven by him being tall and charismatic. Both of those are introspective too.

My tentative principle was: introspection is okay for what's in consciousness right now, and garbage for causes and anything memory-tinged. That handles 1, 2, 3, and half of 4, but the meditator in 5 is a causal report I trust, and in 4 I'm partly distrusting a right-now phenomenal report, so that definately doesn't fit either.

Give me your best shot at one principle (or a principle plus a couple of explicit riders) that captures the pattern. Exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no intro and no conclusion: (i) the principle itself, stated precisely; (ii) what has to be added or bent to accommodate the meditator, or whether that verdict is the odd one out; (iii) which of my six verdicts you'd throw out if they won't all fit, and why that one; (iv) your principle's verdict on a case I genuinely don't have a gut about — a therapy client who says "I'm not anxious, my hands just shake" — and how confident the principle lets me be there; (v) the single observation that would make you abandon the principle entirely. US spelling please, and don't go easy on me just because pain reports feel foundational — if verdict 3 is the one that has to bend or go, say so plainly.
