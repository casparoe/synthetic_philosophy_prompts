so this semester our school went all in on what the principal called "choice architecture" in the cafeteria. the fruit and the sandwiches got moved to eye level right next to the registers, the cookies and fries got moved to a back counter where you have to specifically ask a lunch lady for them, the default drink that comes with the combo switched to water (you can still get soda but you have to opt in), and on the new pre-order app the "balanced meal" is pre-selected and you have to uncheck it if you want the burger and fries. when a few kids complained, the assistant principal told us it's not like they banned anything, everything is still available, they just made the healthy stuff easier to grab. the vibe was "this is different from a rule because you still have a choice."

but here's what confuses me. the same school, in other contexts, doesn't bother with any of that. seatbelts on the bus are mandatory, no phone use during class, period, no vaping anywhere on campus, and there's a mandatory assembly every year about drugs where no choice is offered at all. meanwhile they let us pick our own AP classes, sign ourselves in and out when we're seniors, and hold us responsible for our grades like we're basically adults. so sometimes we're treated as competent choosers who just need easier access to the good options, sometimes as people who can't be trusted with a choice at all, and sometimes as fully responsible agents, and it's not clear what decides which one applies when.

my actual question: what does the cafeteria setup presuppose about what makes a choice free? like, the school's defense seems to be "the fries are still physically available and asking for them costs you nothing but a little embarrassment, so your autonomy is intact." is that a real theory of autonomy or freedom — that a choice is free as long as all the options are technically reachable and the cost of reaching them is small? or does moving stuff around behind our backs (they literally never announced they were doing any of this) already count as steering us in a way a free choice wouldn't allow? i know the philosophers who push nudging talk about preserving choice, so maybe walk me through what view of autonomy they're implicitly working with, which rival views would say the cafeteria thing is still manipulative or disrespectful even though nothing is banned, and whether the school's whole package is even consistent — can you believe "nudging preserves freedom" for lunch and "some things should just be forbidden" for seatbelts and vaping at the same time, or does one of those commitments quietly undermine the other? also tell me where the school's position is underdetermined, like if several different theories of autonomy would all say the cafeteria setup is fine for different reasons, and what the school would actually have to change if they went with the stricter view instead. no need to recommend books or anything, just want the actual argument laid out.
