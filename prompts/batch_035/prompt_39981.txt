I'm the section editor for what our masthead calls "Decline and Fall" pieces at a popular history magazine: 2,000-word essays arguing that Rome fell because of lead, drought, plague, monetary debasement, Christianity, or immigration policy, and several cross my desk each month. I'm a longtime reader of rationalist blogs, so the failure mode I want to screen for is monocausal certainty dressed up as revisionism, but I lack the historian's training to do this ad hoc, we have no classicist on staff, and each manuscript can go to an academic referee exactly once before we're on deadline. Please write a screening procedure my desk can run in about an hour per manuscript: an ordered set of questions, beginning with whether the explanandum is even fixed — western administrative collapse versus a centuries-long transformation versus economic contraction — then tests that distinguish "X was present in the late empire" from "X made a difference," where the East–West comparison is the natural experiment our authors keep ignoring; for each question, say what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a rejection, and where it hands off to the referee or to an editor's judgment because the question is one of values or framing rather than fact. Flag explicitly where the procedure presupposes an answer to "did Rome fall at all," since a transformation-thesis manuscript could pass or fail depending on that choice. Then run it on two manuscripts: one blaming lead plumbing, one blaming the late-antique climatic downturn — and be honest about it if the procedure rejects the climate piece, which I confess I find attractive. Hard cap: 1,200 words.
