Everyone knows Prohibition failed -- the 18th amendment in 1920, bootleggers, speakeasies, Al Capone, repeal in '33. What I want is different: I've been reading about John Dewey's idea that democracy is supposed to work like science. Policies are basically hypotheses, the consequences get observed by citizens, and the polity revises. He also had this idea (from his 1920s work on politics) that a public forms when people are affected by the indirect consequences of other people's actions, and that publics can stay confused and disorganized -- he even thought the modern American public had sort of dissolved into scattered spectators who don't recognize their own problems.

So run Prohibition through Dewey for me. Was Prohibition even an experiment in his sense -- there was no control group, no agreed metric of success, and the different camps seemd to be arguing about entirely different things (was it about morality, public health, labor discipline, or anti-immigrant anxiety?). Who or what was the public here: the Anti-Saloon League claiming to speak for it, the urban drinkers whose lives it ran, or the coalition that eventually voted for repeal? Did the public actually form and learn something by 1933, or did repeal come about for crass reasons like Depression-era tax revenue and jobs in brewing? And I want the analysis to cut both ways: does Dewey's framework illuminate what happened, or does the episode embarrass him -- thirteen years is a long time for an experiment to run on people who never consented to being subjects, and you could say the system only registered the feedback once organized crime had already reshaped whole cities. Or is that actually vindication, since the system did eventually self-correct? Be honest about where Dewey is silent instead of forcing his framework to say something.

One more thing: if you think Dewey himself would have opposed prohibition as bad experimentation, say why, but flag whether that's actually in his writing or just you extrapolating. I'd rather know the difference.
