You know the absent-minded driver problem? A guy is driving home at night and has to get off at one of two highway exits, but the exits look identical, and he's absent-minded enough that, standing at an exit, he genuinely cant tell whether it's the first or the second. (He knows there are exactly two; he just can't tell them apart, and he knows he can't.) Payoffs in the classic version: getting off at the first exit = 0 (terrible route), getting off at the second = 4 (great route), missing both and circling back = 1.

The famous tension is between two ways of deciding:

- The night before, he picks the best plan among all the strategies he can actually execute while unable to tell the exits apart, coin flips allowed. Doing the math on the classic numbers, the optimum is mixed: at each exit, continue with probability 2/3, for an expected payoff of 4/3.
- Standing at an exit, he reasons: "Nothing distinguishes them, so I'm at the first or the second with equal probability. And whatever I conclude here, I concluded at the other one — same brain, same information." On that basis exiting looks worth (0+4)/2 = 2, while continuing looks like its a sure 1 (if I continue here, I continue at the other one too, and going past both pays 1). So the moment tells him to exit — and a driver who acts on that exits at the first exit he reaches and collects 0, worse than what the plan was even worth.

Here's what I want: a variant of the case where these two views agree, built to spec:

1. Two exits, same absent-mindedness (no new information ever arrives — he's just as lost at the second exit as at the first), payoffs are three plain numbers, story stays mundane (routes home, no sci-fi, no payoff bigger than about 10).
2. The best ex-ante plan is pure — no coin flips in the optimum. I want him to know what to do, not a probability.
3. The moment-view reasoning, run at an exit, endorses the very same action the plan prescribes.
4. But absent-mindedness still has to cost him: an attentive twin who remembers which exit is which gets strictly more than the absent-minded driver's optimal plan. This is the anti-cheating clause — the agreement can't come from one action just being best everywhere, since the attentive twin has to genuinely want different things at the two exits.

For context: I'm trying to work out whether the plan/moment tension is a structural feature of absent-mindedness itself or just an artifact of the specific numbers somebody happened to pick for the classic version. So the variant has to earn the agreement out of the payoffs — a case that gets agreement by stipulation is useless to me.

After the scenario (one paragraph), please: (a) compute the ex-ante optimum over all mixed strategies and show it's pure; (b) run the moment-view deliberation step by step and show it lands on the same action; (c) confirm the attentive twin still does strictly better, and pinpoint where the absent-minded driver leaves value on the table; (d) say which feature of the payoffs is doing the work. Then one last probe: hold exit-at-first at 0 and past-both at 1, and treat the second-exit payoff as a dial. Find the exact dial value at which the two views split apart, and check that this threshold correctly predicts (i) that the classic 0/4/1 case sits on the disagreeing side, and (ii) that on that side the ex-ante plan goes mixed — matching the exit probability you get when you recompute the classic optimum.
