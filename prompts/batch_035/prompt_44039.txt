The philosophical debate over the value of original artworks has been stuck in a loop for at least half a century, and I want a diagnosis of the impasse rather than another round of it. On one side are those who insist that if a forgery is perceptually indistinguishable from the original, then every aesthetically relevant property is shared, so whatever extra value the original commands must be non-aesthetic — historical, economic, biographical — and any felt sense that the original is better is a confusion about what we are actually responding to. On the other are those who reply that authenticity is itself an aesthetically relevant property: the original is the record of a genuine creative achievement while the fake is a record of imitation, and that difference is part of what the work is, not a fact external to it. Neither side's stock cases move the other. Two cases strike me as diagnostic: a painting restored so heavily that ninety percent of the pigment is the restorer's, which people on both sides hesitate to call either original or fake; and a painter who, after total amnesia, painstakingly produces a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of her own earlier canvas — same hand, same skill, no deception, so the usual contrast between creative act and copy seems to fail. My hypothesis is that the dispute is verbal in a specific way: the two parties use 'aesthetic value' to track different things, namely features of the experience the work can produce in an informed viewer versus features of the work as a historical-intentional object, and each side's intuitions are generated by cases that quietly presuppose its own reading. Assess that hypothesis, and determine whether the disagreement is verbal, a case of two substantive questions being asked under one heading, or substantive outright. Present your answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: the first two should state what each side actually holds and why its cases work on its own terms; the third should give your diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is; the fourth should explicitly evaluate my hypothesis; the fifth should say what, if anything, would count as progress — a sharper distinction, a new kind of case, a change in the question — or why no progress should be expected.
