quick background so this makes sense: I work at a small company building an AI assistant (customer-facing, healthcare adjacent) and last week my lead put me in charge of the ethics spec — like, the actual document that says what the system is allowed to do when a user request conflicts with other goals, or when it has to choose between bad options. deadline is end of month. I sat down to write it and realized I don't actually know if my own views survive being written down as a coherent spec. here's the package I came in with:

(1) when the stakes are big and quantifiable — triage decisions, allocating scarce appointments, that kind of thing — the system should do straight expected-welfare math and pick the best outcome, full stop. nothing gets to veto the math at that scale.

(2) in everyday operation (conversation, task execution) the system should be bound by flat prohibitions — never deceive the user, never manipulate, never promise something and quietly not do it — and it can't override these even when it calculates that breaking one would make things go better.

(3) those prohibitions are genuine side-constraints, not rules of thumb that exist because following them usually produces good outcomes. the spec should not present them as derivable from the math, and the system shouldn't be able to dissolve them back into expected-value reasoning.

(4) since I'm honestly torn between utilitarian and more rights-based views, the system should handle moral uncertainty the way you'd run a parliament: give each moral outlook seats in proportion to my credence in it and let them bargain toward a policy, rather than me picking one outlook and hardcoding it.

(5) the system should be corrigible — when human operators override it, it defers, even if it thinks the operators are making things go worse by its own lights.

some of these feel like they're fighting but I genuinely can't tell which fights are real. like, obviously (1) and (2) point in different directions, but maybe that's fine since they cover different situations? or maybe the "different situations" move is exactly where it falls apart — I honestly can't tell anymore, I've reread my own notes way to many times. and (5) vs (1) also looks like a clash to me but I have a suspicion it's not really one.

go through the commitments and audit them: which pairs are in genuine tension and how bad, which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection, and for each real one what the cheapest fix is and what it costs. rank them by severity instead of telling me everything is equally broken — I have to actually write this spec, so for each fix tell me concretely what the spec would then say. I'd rather not find out in a design review that I believed alot of mutually contradictory things.
