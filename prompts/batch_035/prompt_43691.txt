The expressivism debate around the Frege–Geach problem has been going for what, sixty years now, and from the outside it looks like a loop: the critic says expressivists can't explain moral sentences in unasserted contexts (if "tormenting the cat is wrong" just expresses disapproval, what does the antecedent of a conditional like "if tormenting the cat is wrong, then getting your little brother to do it is wrong" express, and how does the modus ponens argument that follows stay valid?), the expressivist comes back with new machinery — Blackburn's story about attitudes toward whole patterns of attitudes, or Gibbard's planning framework with his possible factual-normative worlds — the critic calls the machinery ad hoc, then the hybrid people propose views where moral claims express both a belief and an attitude, and the next critic says hybrids just concede the whole point by smuggling truth-conditions back in through the belief half. My metaethics reading group has been going through this for months and everyone's positions have just frozen exactly where they started. My own hypothesis, for what it's worth, is that the real fight isn't about moral language at all but about a constraint on semantic theories: whether explaining the validity of arguments containing a moral premise requires assigning truth-conditions at the sentence level, or whether a sufficiently clever non-truth-conditional story can earn compositionality some other way. If that's right, then the debate is substantive but not really about ethics, and the "ad hoc" complaints are code for "your machinery wouldn't generalize to any langauge with embedded contexts" — which is a legitimate complaint, but nobody ever frames it that way, so it never gets answered head-on.

Diagnose this for me. Is the disagreement substantive, verbal (the two camps meaning different things by "express" or by "meaning"), or is it two different projects — one doing semantics of moral vocabulary, one doing metaethics — that got welded together under one name and now can't be pulled apart? What is actually doing the work in the "ad hoc" objection — is it about explanatory elegance, about how the machinery would behave in other embedded contexts, or something else? And given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress here: a fully worked-out expressivist semantics for some explicit fragment of English, something like a proof that no non-truth-conditional theory can be compositional, the hybrid fans and the plain cognitivists realizing they were agreeing all along, or just everyone deciding the question was ill-posed and moving on? Please take a definite position and defend it — if you think the debate has actually made real progress since the sixties and I'm wrong that it's just looping, say so directly and point to where the progress happened. I'd much rather get a committed diagnosis I can push back on than a menu of possibilities to pick from.
