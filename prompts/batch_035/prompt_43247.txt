I'm a novelist, and the narrator of the book I'm working on is a woman slowly becoming convinced that her whole life is happening all at once rather than moment by moment, so I've been doing a lot of reading around Borges and his ideas about time. A friend of mine who reviews books for a living read an early draft and sent me her notes on what she called Borges's actual philosophy, and I want to double check her before I build the character on top of it. She put it roughly like this: in his essay A New Refutation of Time, Borges sets out to disprove that time exists. Following McTaggart, he shows that time is self-contradictory — since we only ever experience the present, and since Hume had already shown we never perceive time itself, only one idea after another, the past and the future must be imaginary, so the succession of moments is a fiction. She says Borges proved that all of our moments are really simultaneous, and that this is the serious metaphysics hidden underneath his story The Garden of Forking Paths, where time branches into every possibility, and that it also means he believed in eternal recurrence, every event repeating forever. Her upshot was that Borges was basically an idealist who denied that anything truly changes, and that the word "refutation" in the title is doing exactly what it sounds like: he thought he had refuted time, full stop.

Here's my problem: I've actually read the essay, in translation (my Spanish is nowhere near good enough), and what I remember is something much more playful and self-undermining than what she describes — a stack of borrowed arguments about idealism, and then, near the end, something like a shrug where he gives in and admits that time, unfortunately, is real, and that the whole construction collapses the moment he tries to actually live by it. That doesn't square with her talk of proof and refutation. But she's the professional critic and I'm a novelist who maybe skimmed, so maybe I'm the one misreading. Can you go through her summary piece by piece and tell me what a Borges scholar would accept, what they'd wince at, and what is just plain wrong? Specifically I'd like to know whether McTaggart really shows up in the essay the way she says (I honestly don't remember him being there at all, I thought the philosophical borrowing was Berkeley and Hume), whether the simultaneity of moments, the forking paths, and eternal recurrence are all part of one coherent doctrine Borges held or whether she's smushed together things he kept separate — some of it from fiction he wrote, some from essays where he's trying out positions rather than committing to them — and whether the ending I half-remember undercuts her "he refuted time, full stop" conclusion or not.

And then the part that actually matters for my book: if her summary is right, Borges is a model for someone who genuinely believes her life is simultaneous. If my memory is right, he's a model for someone who finds the argument irresistable and then refuses to live by it — which is a very different character, and honestly a better one. So after you've sorted the accurate parts from the misreadings, could you tell me whether her overall conclusion (Borges as a straight-up idealist who denied change) survives once you correct the errors she made along the way, or whether correcting them changes the verdict? Please write in American English, and don't assume I know technical philosophical vocabulary — I'm a working novelist, not a grad student, so gloss anything I'd need glossed.
