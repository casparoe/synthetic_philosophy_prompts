Two friends of mine have been going at the socialist calculation debate for like two years now and I've finally talked them into doing a proper adversarial collaboration, and somehow I got volunteered to draft the joint statement. Write it for me.

Background: one of them (call him the decentralist) is broadly on the Mises/Hayek side — without genuine market prices for capital goods and intermediate goods there's no rational way to compare production plans, and even in principle the information a planner would need is dispersed, tacit, and often only gets articulated in response to price signals in the first place. The other (the cyber-socialist) is on the Lange side but updated — her view is basically that Lange's trial-and-error pricing board was always meant as a proof of concept using the crude tools of the 1930s, and that between modern demand forecasting, huge firms that already plan millions of SKUs internally, prediction markets for pulling out tacit info, and now LLM-based forecasting, the practical-difficulty version of the objection has been slowly eroding for decades.

The statement should have:

1. A numbered agreed-claims section — including things like: that Mises's "impossible in principle" claim and Hayek's "can't work in practice because of knowledge" claim are different objections and they should stop sliding between them; that the original Lange model conceded a lot (it kept consumer goods markets, it was simulating prices rather than abolishing them); and that whatever one thinks of the debate, the burden-shifting between the two sides has been a mess historically.

2. A disagreements table, where each row states one actual disagreement in wording the side holding that view would sign off on. So the decentralist's row about tacit knowledge should be phrased his way (forecasting demand for what people will buy is not the same as discovering what they'd want if the menu itself could change), and the cyber-socialist's rows should be phrased hers (e.g., that firms like giant retailers already demonstrate iterated internal allocation at a scale 1930s economists thought unthinkable, so "planning can't scale" needs an argument, not a citation of the old ones).

3. The cruxes — I suspect at least these: whether preference prediction and preference discovery are the same problem or merely similar-looking; whether the big-retailer case shows planning works at scale or just shows it piggybacks on external market prices it could never generate itself; and whether incentive problems and soft budget constraints are part of the calculation problem or a seperable issue the statement should bracket. If there are other real cruxes, include them.

4. For each crux, a concrete case or test both agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance — and make the predictions specific enough that being wrong would actually be embarrassing, not the vague kind you can squirm out of.

Two more things. First, no diplomatic mush — if a formulation is so watered down that only a diplomat could love it, sharpen it until one side would actually object. Second, both of them suspect part of the fight is verbal (e.g., whether shadow prices set by a board "count" as prices in the relevant sense, or whether "the market" refers to a mechanism for price formation or to any iterated feedback process), and they want the statement to say explicitly which parts of their dispute are verbal and which are genuine, and why. Oh, and the cyber-socialist gets genuinely annoyed when people strawman Lange as having ignored incentives entirely, so don't sneak that concession into the agreed section.
