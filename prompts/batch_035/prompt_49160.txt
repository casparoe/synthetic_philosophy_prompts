A grad student in my phil-bio seminar has me cornered with an argument and I can't decide which rung of the lader to kick out. Background: she's a Dawkins enthusiast, I'm an old Williams man, we do this dance every year, it's a whole thing.

Her argument, as she presented it:

(1) Natural selection occurs whenever there is heritable variation in fitness among entities that compete — reproduction, variation, inheritance, differential success. This is just the standard recipe, and it's level-neutral as between genes, organisms, and groups.

(2) But for selection to change anything, the bookkeeping has to be kept in something that survives generations. In sexual species, organisms are one-off genetic mixtures that meiosis scrambles, and groups dissolve entirely; only genes — stretches of DNA short enough that crossing over rarely splits them — pass through the generations unbroken.

(3) So whatever we say about selection pressures at the organism or group level, the thing whose frequency actually changes generation over generation is the gene — and "selection for X" just means "X's frequency gets tracked and pushed."

(4) Therefore all selection is ultimately selection of genes; organism-level selection and group selection are shorthand, like describing a battle by its body count instead of by who flanked whom.

(5) So explanations like "the group with more altruists outcompeted the group with fewer" are always replacable, without loss, by "the allele for altruism outcompeted its rival allele" — no selection above the gene level ever really occurs.

Now here's what's bugging me. My student also believes — and here I actually agree with her — that the argument shouldn't license things like "selection for the good of the entire gene pool of the species," or, in an asexual lineage, "selection for the good of the clone." But her premises seem to license exactly that. The frequency of any longer stretch of chromosome, of an entire asexual genome, even of a whole species' gene pool, is also "tracked" by the same bookkeeping; in an asexual lineage the whole organism passes through the generations unbroken, so (2) is at best locally true; and if the criterion is just "whatever's frequency changes," well, gene pool frequencies change over time just fine. If her argument is sound it seems to prove too much: not "genes rather than organisms" but "any chunk of heritable stuff you care to count, up to and including the gene pool." When I pushed her, she said the gene is special because it's the biggest chunk that's also a functional unit of self-replication — but that sounds like smuggling in a criterion that never appeared in the premises.

So: which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Let me state my preferences up front. I want to keep (1) — the level-neutral recipe is the best thing that ever happened to this debate — and I'd also like to keep some version of (5), because I spent the eighties arguing against naive good-of-the-group thinking and I'm not giving that back. If I have to give up one of those to block the "gene pool is the real unit" consequence, tell me which, and what I'm paying for it. And if the fix is that some step equivocates — reads true on one meaning and then gets used on another — I want that named explicitly, not waved at.

Write the diagnosis in prose, and then after the prose give me a table with three columns — "step", "verdict", "cost" — one row for each of the five steps, where verdict is one of keep / reject / reinterpret and cost is a single sentence on what amending or giving up that step commits me to. Keep the whole thing under a thousand words. I teach this material; I don't need the Wikipedia tour of who said what in which book.
