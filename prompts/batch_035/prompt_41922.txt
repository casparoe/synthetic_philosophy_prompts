A guy at my debating society reckons German-style data protection is incoherent, and I can't find the hole in this: (1) Any state that collects income tax already knows your income, address, employer and bank details, and any state that polices knows your criminal record. (2) So every modern state, however privacy-protective, already holds a dossier on each citizen. (3) The Chinese social credit system differs from that baseline only in degree — it aggregates more sources, adds a score, and attaches consequences to it. (4) A difference in degree can't ground a difference in rights: you can't have a right that gets violated when the state holds 60 categories of data about you but not when it holds 59. (5) So there's no rights-difference between the German and the Chinese arrangements, and Germans must either accept that the social credit system would be compatible with their constitution or admit the right to informational self-determination was incoherant from the start. He takes the second horn and I think both are unacceptable, but the only escape I can see is denying (4), which seems to commit me to saying vague thresholds can never ground rights, and I don't believe that either. Where's the slip, and what does closing it cost me? Lead with your verdict in the first sentence, then the reasoning — no preamble, and don't restate the argument back at me.
