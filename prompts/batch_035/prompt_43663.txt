I keep noticing a pattern in arguments about institutional reform and I can't find a word for it. When someone proposes abolishing some old rule or practice, the defenders often can't say exactly what it does — they just insist it's load-bearing. The skepticts then reason that if nobody can articulate its function, that's evidence it has none, so it's safe to remove. But that seems backwards in a specific way: for practices that arose by selection rather than design, inarticulacy is what you'd expect even if they're crucial, because nobody built them with an articulable purpose, and the situations where their absence would bite are rare enough that no one alive has ever seen them. So the evidential situation is asymmetric — the silence cuts in favor of the practice being load-bearing, not against it. Does this asymmetry have a name, the move of treating inability to explain a practice as positive evidence of hidden function, specifically for evolved rather than designed institutions? I know Chesterton's fence is in the neighborhood, but as usually stated it's a practical maxim about not removing a fence until you know why it was put up, not a claim about what the inability to explain the fence probabilistically shows, and it doesn't distinguish selected from designed stuff. Hayek is nearby too, with dispersed knowledge, but his point is that someone locally knows, whereas my point is that possibly nobody knows at all. So: is there an established term for the evidential version? If not, could you coin one and define it carefully, including where it breaks down — e.g. does it survive the fact that lots of old rules really are just dead? Please also say how confident you are in your claims and single out the point you're least sure of.
