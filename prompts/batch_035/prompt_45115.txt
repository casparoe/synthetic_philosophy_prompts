I'm a high school senior taking an elective called Contemporary Moral Problems, and for my final project I've been given forty minutes to run a discussion session for classmates who have never read any philosophy. My plan is to hand out a decision tree titled "What do you actually believe about the difference between doing harm and allowing it?" and let people sort themselves before I tell them the standard names. The tree should start from verdicts on concrete cases rather than from any labeled doctrine, and each branch node should be phrased so that someone can answer it honestly without knowing which position it leads toward. The cases I want built in are: diverting the runaway trolley onto the one to save the five; pushing the large man off the footbridge to stop the same trolley; the surgeon who could quietly harvest one healthy patient's organs to save five dying ones; switching off a ventilator at a dying patient's request versus giving that patient a lethal injection at the same request; and the contrast between the drowning child you walk past on the way home and the faraway famine victims you will never see at all. Please use those five as anchors but add any others you think do better diagnostic work.

The leaves I need placed are: the straight consequentialist view that the doing/allowing distinction carries no intrinsic weight at all; a non-moralized doctrine of doing and allowing, where the weight comes from causal or agential facts, such as who initiated or redirected the threat rather than who merely failed to prevent it; a moralized version, where the weight is borrowed from rights, special obligations, or the victim's claims against particular people; a view on which the distinction is real but only pro tanto, so that enough lives on the other side can outweigh it; and finally the position that killing versus letting die is a stricter and separate line from doing versus allowing, one that does distinctive work in the medical cases even where it makes no difference in the trolley cases. I'm especially interested in whether my case set can actually pull apart the killing/letting-die axis from the doing/allowing axis — the ventilator-versus-injection pair is meant to do that, and I'd like you to tell me if it fails or needs reinforcement. Also, wherever two of the leaves genuinely cannot be separated by any question that doesn't already presuppose the labels, flag that honestly instead of inventing a fake discriminating question, and note any coherent region of the logical space that nobody occupies, with one sentence on why you think it's empty (unattractive, incoherent, or just unexplored).

Format-wise, the tree should fit on one printed page: no more than about nine branch nodes, each a question with two or three answers, and each leaf should carry the position's name plus one sentence about its most awkward commitment — the case it has the hardest time explaining to my classmates. I want the handout to work even for someone who answers the questions quickly and inconsistently, so if you can suggest one built-in cross-check question that lets me detect people whose answers pull in opposite directions, that would help me run the discussion afterward. I don't need citations or the names of particular philosophers attached to each leaf, just the positions themselves, clearly enough described that I can defend the handout to my teacher.
