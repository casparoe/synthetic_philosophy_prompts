I'm a PhD student in machine learning doing mechanistic interpretability work, with enough philosophy of mind in my background that I care about the words my field uses, and a pattern keeps surfacing in lab meetings and papers that I'd like to put a name to before I coin one myself. The setting is always some version of this: we train a network, and nobody designed what goes on inside it — we designed the objective and the architecture, but the internal organization is whatever the training run left behind. When we open the model up, we find structure: a direction in activation space that tracks whether a snippet of code is syntactically valid, a circuit that fires on indirect-object constructions, a "deception direction" that flips the outputs when ablated. And at that moment a familiar disagreement breaks out. One camp in my lab says a feature is real when it is causal (intervening on it changes behavior) and replicates across random seeds and even across differently trained models; a second camp says that any decomposition that predicts behavior comparably well has an equal claim to the word "real," so the word does no work and we should talk only in terms of predictive schemes; a third says the whole question is confused, because the network is an artifact — we built it, so unlike atoms or genes there is nothing there to be independently right or wrong about. What makes it stranger still is that the same researcher will switch camps within a single paper: the introduction disclaims that the model "really" wants or believes anything, and the results section then treats causal interventions as though they were probes for a genuine internal property.

What I'm pointing at is not simply realism versus instrumentalism about unobservables, for a reason I keep coming back to: the system is an artifact whose internals were not designed by anyone, so we made the thing without making the level of description now in dispute. And the usual stance-talk about treating a whole system as if it had beliefs and desires doesn't help either, because the fight is over structure far below that level, and the causal-ablation criterion is supposed to do genuine work separating discovered structure from structure we merely project onto a high-dimensional object. Does this configuration have an established name of its own, or a literature I should be reading? If the nearest concepts fit only imperfectly — and I suspect each of them does — tell me specifically how each one misses, and if nothing established fits, coin a term: define it, say what it excludes, and give a boundary case, something like a linear probe that predicts a behavior perfectly while plausibly reading out a spurious correlate rather than the property the researchers had in mind.

To be clear, I won't be satisfied with a bare label; I want the carving. What exactly is being disputed when people dispute the "reality" of a feature — a fact about the model, a fact about our descriptions, or something about how the two stand to each other? What would make the third camp right that the question is merely verbal, and is the causal criterion tracking the presence of structure or just our ability to intervene cleanly? And since I'll be repeating whatever you say in front of people who know this literature better than I do: tell me where your own answer here is most likely to fail — what consideration, if it held, would move you from "this is a substantive dispute" to "this is verbal," or back, and what you'd want to see before trusting the boundary cases you drew.
