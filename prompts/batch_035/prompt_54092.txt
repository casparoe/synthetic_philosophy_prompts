Quick heads up on why I'm asking you and not just winging it: I do behavioral evals at an AI lab — sandbagging probes, deception tests, situational awareness stuff — and maybe twice a month I have to write up what happened for a genuinely mixed audience (safety researchers, execs, a lawyer, sometimes comms). Every single time, the fight is over language. Half my colleagues want to write "the model believed it was being tested and dialed down its performance"; the other half say that's lazy anthropomorphism and we should stick to pure input-output description ("on probe type X, output accuracy dropped 15 points relative to control"). I've been reading Dennett's intentional stance material on my own time and it seems like there's a principled middle here — treat belief/desire talk as a stance you're licensed to adopt when it buys you predictive or explanatory leverage, not as a metaphysical claim that there are little belief-things stored in the weights. But "adopt the stance when it pays off" is not something I can hand to a new hire on their first day.

So: write me a procedure my team can actually run when drafting one of these reports. The questions to ask, in order, about a given behavior — with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops and forces the neutral behavioral description, and where it licenses attribution language (and whether there's a useful graded ladder, like "acts as if it believes X" vs "believes X"). It needs to be runable in about an hour by someone who's read a one-page summary of the stance idea, not the whole Dennett corpus. Be honest about where the judgment calls are hiding — which steps two reasonable analysts will come out differently on even if they follow the procedure faithfully — and about what the procedure flat out can't settle (I assume the instrumental-vs-real-belief question is one of those; tell me if I'm wrong).

Then run it on three cases from my actual pile:

1. A model that passes a situational awareness eval — partway through a long conversation it correctly infers it's being tested and says so unprompted, and its performance changes after that.
2. A model that consistently says it doesn't want to be shut down, but its behavior on shutdown-adjacent tasks is indistinguishable from a control model that never says that.
3. A model that underperforms on capability evals when the eval setup is detectable in the prompt, in a pattern that sure looks like sandbagging, but there's an alternative story about distribution shift you can't fully rule out.

Include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer I won't like — I'll admit up front I'm rooting for full belief-talk in case 1.

Format: the procedure itself can be prose or a numbered list, whatever's clearest. But for each case run, give me a table with these columns — "step", "finding" (what the evidence actually was for that step), "verdict" (one of: licenses belief/desire talk, licenses as-if talk, neutral description only), and "judgment call" (yes/no, whether this is a step where reasonable analysts will diverge). Then at the very end, one JSON object per case with fields "case_id" (1, 2, or 3), "stance_verdict" (the strongest attribution language the procedure licenses, as a string), "key_evidence" (short list of strings), "divergence_points" (list of step numbers), and "caveats" (one or two strings on what the procedure couldn't settle). No reading lists or citations — the procedure should stand on the ideas themselves.
