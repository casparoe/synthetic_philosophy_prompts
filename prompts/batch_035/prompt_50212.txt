so im a phd student in political theory, and next month i'm presenting at a workshop on normative change. my paper defends a version of Appiah's honour account of moral revolutions, but with a twist I'm honestly a bit nervous about. My claim: Appiah is right that what killed duelling, foot-binding, and (partly) the Atlantic slave trade wasn't argument but shifts in who could hold their head up in respectable society. But I want to go further and say the mechanism he gestures at is really a coordination flip, not a change in anyone's values. Once enough salient people stopped treating the old practice as a badge of honour, the old norm became a losing strategy basically overnight — no one's actual moral beliefs had to change first, or at all. On my reading, the "revolution" in moral revolutions is explained without any moral progress in people's heads, and I take that to be the real sting of the Williams thesis about honour: what modern moral philosophy dismissed as honour is doing all the work that "moral reasons" get credit for.

The audience: the workshop is mostly deliberative democrats, Habermas-adjacent people who believe normative change tracks the force of the better argument in the public sphere. Plus two economic historians who know the duelling and slavery data cold and will not be kind to cherry-picked cases. Plus my supervisor, who thinks anything game-theoretic is an excuse to avoid taking reasons seriously, and who will be in the room.

Draft me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get from this specific crowd (not the ones an Appiah fan would raise), ordered by how much damage they'd do, phrased the way each group would actually put them, with a short reply for each and a note on where the reply is soft. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. And could you write it all in British English please — I'll be presenting in the UK and don't want to look sloppy.
