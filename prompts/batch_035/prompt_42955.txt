Our decision-theory reading group spent last week on Parfit's hitchhiker, and one member ended the meeting with an argument he claims dissolves the entire debate about it. It seems to prove far too much, but every step looks fine to me in isolation, and I cannot find the seam.

The case as we set it up: I am stranded in the desert; a passing driver will take me to town only if she predicts that I will pay her once we arrive; she is a perfect predictor and has already made her prediction; and if she drives on, I die. The argument:

(1) A decision problem is a situation in which an agent chooses among options and the outcome depends on the choice.
(2) Since the driver predicts perfectly, what I will in fact do at the destination is exactly what she has already predicted I will do.
(3) So there is no possible situation just like this one — same driver, same already-made prediction — in which I choose differently; a world in which I choose otherwise is a world in which she predicted otherwise.
(4) Therefore, facing this situation, I cannot choose otherwise than I in fact will.
(5) Where an agent cannot choose otherwise, there is no choice; where there is no choice, there is no decision problem.
(6) So Parfit's hitchhiker, and every perfect-predictor case built on one, is not a decision problem at all, and the quarrel between those who say I should pay and those who say paying is throwing money away is a quarrel about nothing — like asking for the best move in a chess position that permits no moves.

He is serene about the cost and extends the argument himself: in a deterministic universe, the state of the world before my birth already settles what I will do, so by parity of reasoning nobody in such a universe ever faces a decision problem, and decision theory is a theory with application only in worlds with open futures. He regards this as a bold result rather than a reductio.

Another member proposed a rescue. If the presenter is right that there is no choice to make, she says, the live question becomes what to be rather than what to do — whether, back when it was up to me, I should have made myself into the kind of person who will not pay — and she takes this to be the argument's real payoff: rationality attaches to dispositions, not to individual acts. I cannot tell whether this is a rescue or just the same problem relocated one step back in time.

For my part, I would like to keep (3) — I do not see how a perfect predictor could allow the same prediction and a different choice — and I would like to keep the thought, implicit in rejecting (6), that these cases are genuine decision problems worth arguing over; the one-boxing dispute does not feel like a debate about nothing. Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked fine, and what rejecting it costs — including whether the extension to deterministic worlds strengthens my friend's hand or exposes it, and whether the dispositions rescue stands on its own or inherits whatever is wrong with the original argument.
