I teach an upper-level seminar on rational agency and ethics (mostly juniors and seniors, all of whom have had some decision theory), and I'm writing the final. One topic I really want to test is moral trade — the idea that agents with genuinely different moral views can sometimes strike deals that leave each of them better off by their own lights, the way people with different tastes trade goods. My problem is that the standard presentation is so short — "people with different values can trade, like vote swaps" — that students just recite that tagline, and I can't tell who actually understands the machinery from who memorized the slogan.

So I want the question built around a concrete scenario I made up, with numbers. A small nonprofit has a two-member board. Amara, on the board, only counts far-future human welfare in her moral accounting; Bo only counts animal welfare. The charter fixes two program decisions every year: Domain 1, which research project runs — either an x-risk institute or a wild-animal welfare lab — and Domain 2, which campaign runs — either AI-safety lobbying or factory-farming reform. By charter, Amara single-handedly controls what happens in Domain 1 and Bo single-handedly controls Domain 2, but each of them cares about how BOTH domains come out. Their intensities are (these are each agent's own utils): Amara — x-risk institute 10, wild-animal lab 8, AI-safety lobbying 4, factory-farming campaign 1. Bo — wild-animal lab 10, x-risk institute 2, factory-farming campaign 6, AI-safety lobbying 4. Each agent's total payoff is the sum of what they get from the two domains. I'm deliberately not telling students anything about whether these numbers are comparable across the two agents — the good ones should figure out for themselves whether the question ever needs that.

What I'd like from you: (1) the question text exactly as it would appear on the exam (closed book, 90-minute slot, 25 marks total), having them compute the no-deal outcome and the full-deal outcome, explain why the deal benefits both parties without any interpersonal comparison of moral value, show that the situation has prisoner's-dilemma structure and draw the consequence for when moral trade is feasible in the real world (verification, commitment, iteration, that kind of thing), and connect it to real-world analogues plus at least one genuine limit on the idea; (2) a model answer of about 600 words; and (3) a marking scheme with five must-hit points, marks adding to 25, a one-line note per point on the typical way students mess it up, and a sentence on what seperates an A answer from a B answer. My guess is the B answer does the arithmetic correctly and then gestures at "different values can trade, just like markets" — the A answer has to nail the no-comparability point and the game structure, and I'd like the marking scheme to say so explicitly.
