My closest friend and I spent the whole of last weekend arguing about whether Kant's Formula of Universal Law does any genuine moral work. Her position was that the test is empty: any maxim can be redescribed so that it either obviously passes or obviously fails, so the universalizability procedure merely launders our antecedent moral convictions and gives them a Kantian gloss. I replied that this conflates the test itself with the description of the maxim fed into it — but by the end of the evening I could not confidently say where, in Kant's apparatus, the work of fixing a maxim's description is actually done, or what principled constraint stops the tailoring she alleged. I teach an upper-level ethical theory course, and I want to put our disagreement to use by setting a final-exam essay question that forces students to confront exactly this issue, rather than one they can answer by reciting the distinction between contradictions in conception and contradictions in the will. Please write the question (answerable in ninety minutes), a model answer of roughly 600 words, and a marking scheme that distinguishes an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain — including, at minimum, how each answer handles the worry that the agent's own purposes are doing the moral work, and what the practical-contradiction reading of the test is supposed to contribute.
