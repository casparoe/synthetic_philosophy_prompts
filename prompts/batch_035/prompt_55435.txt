Take the Windrush scandal in the UK and analyze it as a case study in archives and power -- the whole line of thinking that says archives aren't neutral records of the past but instruments of whoever controls them, and that the silences in a record are produced just as much as the entries are. Quick refresher on the facts so we're on the same page: the Windrush generation came to the UK from the Caribbean between 1948 and the early 1970s, as British subjects with an automatic right to settle. Because the right was automatic, they were never issued any document proving it. Many children arrived on their parents' passports and never held papers of their own. From 2012 the "hostile environment" policy made landlords, employers, the NHS and banks legally required to check people's immigration status, and the burden of proof landed on individuals to prove decades of continuous residence. People who had lived in Britain for 50+ years were detained, lost jobs and homes, and dozens were wrongly deported. Meanwhile in 2010 the Home Office had destroyed the landing cards that recorded many of their arrival dates. The scandal broke in 2018 and the government later admitted it was foreseeable and avoidable.

What I want is a careful mapping, not vibes. Who were the record-keepers and who had no power over what got recorded? What did the archive contain and, more importantly, what did it structurally never contain -- and was that absence an accident or the direct consequence of the right being automatic and therefore undocumented? Where exactly does the 2010 destruction fit: was it the cause of the scandal, or would the pattern have been the same anyway since the cards were only one incomplete trace and children on parents' passports weren't in them individually? Then I want you to run it in the other direction and say what the episode does to the theory itself. At least one thing complicates the standard story: the scandal was eventually exposed and partly corrected using other state-held traces -- school records, tax and National Insurance records, ship manifests. The state's own paper trail helped bury these people and then helped dig some of them out. Does that show the archive is power-neutral after all, or just that power isn't the same thing as the archive? And where is the theory of archives-and-power basically silent here -- what did it fail to predict or explain? Finish with whether the episode is a confirmation of the view, an anomaly, or something it never really anticipated. Be honest about the places where the framework strains instead of forcing it to fit.
