I'm writing a novel set during the Peasants' Revolt of 1381, and a beta reader told me my narrator is too presentist — that she thinks like a 21st century person in medieval clothes. So I picked up a pop-history book on historiography to figure out what that even means, and now I'm more confused than when I started. The book's argument, as far as I can reconstruct it: presentism just means judging people in the past by the standards of the present, the old Italian philosopher Croce said all history is contemporary history, which shows every historian is trapped in their own era's assumptions and can never really understand an alien mindset, and so all historical writing is essentially propaganda for the present, since there is no neutral vantage point to judge from anyway. The book also implied that the only honest options are either to embrace judging the past openly (the historians' equivalent of writing with an agenda) or to give up on the past mattering at all. My problem is that some of this resonates — my narrator does, let's be honest, have my politics — and some of it sounds like it's proving too much. Is this a fair summary of what presentism means as historians actually use the term? Which parts would a working historian nod along to, which are conflating different things (I have a vague sense that narrating events with hindsight and moralizing about them aren't the same move?), and does the everything-is-propaganda conclusion actually follow from the bits that are true? Also, if you have a view on whether a novelist's presentism is the same sin as a historian's, I'd genuinely like to know — it changes how I write the damn book.
