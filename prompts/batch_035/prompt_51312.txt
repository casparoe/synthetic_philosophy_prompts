The debate over whether the standards for knowledge shift with the attributor's practical situation has been running for over thirty years now, and from where I sit it has completely stalled. Everyone in the neighborhood — the contextualists, the interest-relative invariantists, the classical invariantists with pragmatic stories — agrees on the phenomena: bank cases, lottery cases, the fact that we'll say someone "knew" a low-stakes fact we'd deny they "knew" once their mortgage is on the line. And everyone has a re-description of every case the others produce. What the contextualist calls a shift in the standards for "knows," the invariantist calls a pragmatic implicature about how strong an assertion the stakes license, and vice versa all the way down. I've watched my own corner of the internet get stuck the same way, and it usually turned out that the two sides were answering different questions under one heading.

My working theory is that this one is a borderline-verbal dispute, but of an unusual kind: not a dispute about whether the interest-sensitivity is real (all sides concede the phenomena), but about which side of the semantics/pragmatics boundary it lives on — and boundary disputes like that are prime candidates for dissolving rather than resolving. Yet I suspect that verdict is too quick, because the parties do seem to disagree about what happens in places where a re-description costs something: retrospective reports where the attributor's stakes differ from the subject's, knowledge ascriptions embedded under quantifiers or conditionals, and plain disagreement — two people saying "he knows" and "he doesn't know" about the same subject at the same time, apparently without talking past each other. Whether those are genuinely different predictions or just more mutual accommodation is exactly what I can't tell from the armchair.

So: diagnose the structure of this disagreement, using what each side actually says, and tell me whether it is verbal, substantive, or two questions under one heading — with reasons rather than a survey. Say what would count as progress given your diagnosis, and be concrete about the kind of datum that would force one side to accommodate it at genuine theoretical cost rather than by re-labeling. Finally, flag where your diagnosis is most likely to fail: what argument or observation would push you toward a different verdict about what kind of dispute this is, and is there anything an experimental result could settle here, or is that hope itself confused?
