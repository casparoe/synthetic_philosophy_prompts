I am reading Book III of Hume's Treatise of Human Nature and have come across a passage in the section "Of the origin of justice and property" (Book III, Part II, Section II) that I cannot fully work through. Since my edition modernizes the spelling, I will render the passage in close paraphrase rather than exact quotation.

In essence, Hume says the following: two people rowing a boat together coordinate by an agreement or convention, even though they have never exchanged promises with one another. In the same way, he continues, languages come to be established gradually by human conventions, without anyone having promised anything to anyone. And, he concludes, the rule concerning the stability of possession is no less derived from human conventions for the fact that it arises gradually and acquires its force only by a slow progression, in the course of which people come to see that it serves their mutual interest to leave others in the secure enjoyment of what they possess, on the condition of being treated the same way in return.

I would be grateful for help with the following, ideally in order:

1. What exactly does Hume mean by "convention" in this passage, and why does he place so much weight on the fact that the rowers never exchanged promises? I take it he is arguing that coordination does not require any special normative act, but I am not confident about what he thinks is doing the work instead.

2. What role does the gradualness — the "slow progression" — play in the argument? Is it merely a historical claim about how the rules of justice actually arose, or does it matter philosophically to his conclusion that justice is an artificial rather than a natural virtue?

3. I am also interested in how this bears on more recent work on collective intentionality and joint action. Presumably Hume's rowers form some kind of joint activity, but it is not obvious to me what kind: does his account, on which the rowers act from reciprocal expectations and perceived mutual advantage rather than from any shared commitment or explicit agreement, fit the sorts of cases that later theorists have treated as paradigmatic joint action? Would the rowers' coordination count as a joint action even for the theorists who insist that genuinely joint action requires a shared intention or a special "we"-attitude, or is Hume describing a shallower kind of coordination that such theorists would set aside as a limiting case?

I am less interested in the exegetical fine print than in getting the structure of the argument right and in understanding how the rowing case connects to the broader claim about justice, but I would also welcome it if you could flag any way in which my paraphrase or framing above seems to miss Hume's point.
