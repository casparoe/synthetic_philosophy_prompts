I'm taking a course on ethics & economics and we just covered the Stern vs Nordhaus fight over climate discounting, and I keep feeling like the two sides arent even arguing about the same thing the philosophers argue about. Quick facts so we're on the same page (no need to know more than this): the Stern Review in 2006 basically set the pure rate of time preference at about 0.1% a year -- essentially zero, with the tiny remainder meant only to capture the chance civilization doesn't survive that long -- and Stern defended the near-zero rate on explicitly ethical grounds. Nordhaus shot back that rates that low are wildly out of line with how people actually behave (market returns, savings choices, his DICE model started around 3% and declines over centuries), and that you can't run policy on an ethics that nobody, including the policymakers, actually endorses in their own lives. The whole fight is usually retold as "how much do we care about future people," but I suspect that framing is doing some sneaky work.

Here's what I want. Take the philosophical literature on pure time preference -- the idea that a benefit matters less simply because it arrives later, holding everything else fixed -- and use it to analyze this episode properly. So: which parts of the two camps' disagreement are actually about pure time preference, and which are about other stuff smuggled in under the same name? For instance, Stern's little 0.1% isn't pure time preference at all, it's catastrophe risk, so arguably Stern's model contains *zero* pure time preference -- does that mean the episode is a clean win for the anti-discount camp, or does it show the concept can't do the work in a real policy fight, since almost nobody disputes that a 1-in-a-thousand annual chance of extinction should shrink expected future welfare? And Nordhaus's revealed-preference reply -- is it a claim that pure time preference is *justified* by our behavior, or just that it's *empirically real*, and does he ever actually engage the ethical question or dodge it? Also there's the whole thing where even with zero pure time preference you still discount future consumption if you expect the future to be richer -- so is the popular story "Stern cared about grandchildren, Nordhaus didn't" just wrong as a description of what divided them?

Then go the other direction, from case back to theory: what does the Stern-Nordhaus episode show about the philosophical debate itself? The standard anti-pure-time-preference argument is the symmetry one -- dates of birth shouldn't matter morally any more than place of birth, future people's utility counts the same as ours -- but notice that this argument, if you take it seriously, seems to have nothing to say about Nordhaus's actual position, which was never "future utility counts for less" but something about consistency with observable choices. Does the episode suggest the philosophical debate and the policy debate come apart entirely (i.e., you could grant the philosophers their point and still have most of the Stern-Nordhaus fight left unresolved), or is that too generous to Nordhaus? I'd honestly take either answer, I just want the mapping done carefully rather than the usual hand-waving. Tell me where the pure-time-preference framework is genuinely silent here -- like on the choice of consumption-growth assumptions, which drove at least as much of the divergence as the discount rate did -- instead of forcing it to explain everything.
