I've been reading a popular biography of Isaac Newton—not a scholarly one, but a well-reviewed trade book aimed at general readers—and one chapter deals with his religious writings. It sums up his theology in a single paragraph that I found both gripping and, at moments, slightly too tidy. Here is the passage as I remember it:

"Newton was a man of his age in appearance only. From his thirties onward he privately rejected the doctrine of the Trinity, holding what historians usually call Arianism—the view that Christ was a created being, subordinate to the Father rather than co-eternal with him. He concealed this belief from everyone, since openly denying the Trinity was a criminal offense that could have cost him his fellowship and his chair, and in the Scotland of his day it could cost a man his life. The millions of words he poured into biblical prophecy, chronology, and church history were a private hobby, kept in sealed-off compartments that had no bearing on his physics. He dated the great apostasy to the Council of Nicaea in 325, where he believed the doctrine of the Trinity was imposed on an originally unitarian church through the victory of Athanasius's party. And working from the Book of Daniel, he predicted that the world would end in 2060."

The chapter then closes with a remark that has stuck with me: that it was fortunate for the world that Newton's theological obsessions never touched his physics, since only that strict separation made the Principia possible.

I can't tell which parts of this are solid and which have been smoothed over for the general reader. Something about the 2060 date nags at me—I half-remember a news story about a Newton manuscript on display somewhere, in which he seemed to be warning against people who set exact dates rather than setting one himself—but I may be conflating two different things, and I honestly don't know where the errors, if any, are. I also don't know whether the popular claim that Newton wrote more words on theology and alchemy than on science is what this book is leaning on, or whether that claim is itself shaky.

Please audit the paragraph claim by claim. For each sentence, tell me (a) what a historian of science who works on Newton's manuscripts would accept as stated, (b) what they would accept only with qualification or as a simplification, and (c) what they would flag as subtly wrong—wrong in an instructive way rather than an obvious one. Where a claim is contested among scholars, please say so and give the shape of the disagreement rather than pretending to a settled verdict. In particular, I want to know whether "Arian" is a fair label for Newton's position, whether "concealed this belief from everyone" holds up against what we know of what he actually circulated and to whom, whether his story about the fourth-century corruption really runs through a council in 325 or through something else, and what the 2060 figure actually represents in his manuscripts. Finally, tell me whether the closing remark—that the separation of his theology from his physics was what made the Principia possible—survives the corrections you have made, or whether it depends on one of the claims that doesn't hold. Please answer in American English.
