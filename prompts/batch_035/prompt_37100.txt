My doctoral supervisor is retiring after thirty-nine years as a historian of early modern chemistry, and I've been asked to give the main speech at her retirement dinner. Her field was alchemy and what gets called "proto-chemistry," and she was known in the department for the lecture she gave every autumn to the new graduate students, in which she took apart the standard Whig history of her subject — the version where alchemy exists only as a wrong turn on the road to Lavoisier, where phlogiston theory is a mistake awaiting refutation, and where the whole point of studying Paracelsians or iatrochemists is to explain how we eventually got it right. Her own work treated her subjects as intelligent people reasoning well from the evidence and categories available to them, some of which turned out to be dead ends and some of which quietly survive in modern chemistry under new names. Her students internalized this to the point where we'd catch each other saying things like "Priestley refused to see what was in front of him" at parties.

Here is my problem. A retirement speech is almost structurally Whiggish: the discipline was once benighted, her generation arrived with the right ideas, and now we understand. I can feel myself drafting exactly the narrative she spent her career dismantling — "for centuries historians dismissed these people, until finally scholars like her showed..." She will notice this within a sentence, and so will roughly half the room, which will be historians of science; the other half will be family, former students who left academia, and actual chemists, who won't catch the historiographical sin but still need to be genuinely moved. So please write the speech: long enough for a dinner (aim for something that takes six or seven minutes to deliver), honoring her and her work, but without any narrative arc in which the past is vindicated by, or exists as a stage on the way to, the present state of the field — including the present state of the field's own self-understanding. Ideally it should include at least one moment where the anti-Whiggish stance is performed rather than lectured about — some way of praising her that doesn't depend on her having been on the winning side of history, since that is precisely the standard she rejected.

Alongside the speech, please add a short accompanying note (just for me, not to be delivered) that separates what the speech treats as settled from what is actually contested among historians of science. In particular, flag anywhere the speech leans on a contested historiographical position — for instance, how far the sympathetic "rational alchemist" revisionist reading really extends before it starts overcorrecting, or whether the older generation of historians really held the dismissive view her autumn lectures attributed to them, or whether it's fair to portray alchemy's absorption into chemistry as a gradual continuity rather than a rupture — and mark clearly where you've taken a liberty or simplified for the sake of the occasion, so I know what I'd be asserting on her behalf if I delivered it as written.
