I belong to a small reading group on distributive justice in which we argue over concrete cases rather than texts, and one member — I will call her Beth — has by far the most consistent instincts of anyone there. Her verdicts across this year's cases:

1. Gregory deliberately cultivated a taste for fine wine and rare first editions, and now finds ordinary pleasures grey on the same income as everyone else. Verdict: owed nothing.
2. Amina, blind from birth, financially comfortable, and by her own sincere report exactly as satisfied with her life as her sighted neighbours. Verdict: owed public support anyway — Beth's gloss was that Amina simply has less to work with.
3. Tomas was paralysed after skiing a closed slope against explicit avalanche warnings. Verdict: nothing beyond emergency care; he rolled the dice and lost.
4. Karin's treatment-resistant depression, which is nobody's fault, drains her wellbeing and costs a fortune in therapy and medication. Verdict: owed full support.
5. Dele, deaf since a childhood illness, received a large cash settlement but lives somewhere with no interpreters, no sign-language classes and no deaf community, and is socially isolated. Verdict: the cash was not enough; he is owed the actual means to communicate and participate, even at further public expense.

Next week's case is Marta: healthy and unimpaired, with exactly average wealth, but through sheer brute luck — she happened to live where a war she had no part in collapsed the universities, the hospitals and the transport — she can do and be far less with her life than her equally healthy, equally wealthy twin in a peaceful region. She reports that she has made her peace with it and is broadly content.

I know the canonical answers to the question of equality of what are welfare, resources and capabilities, but Beth's pattern does not map neatly onto any of them. Please do the following. Set out the candidate rules that fit all five verdicts, including hybrid and responsibility-sensitive versions, and be explicit about how each rule must be filled in to accommodate verdict 5 in particular. Predict Beth's verdict on Marta under each surviving rule, flagging where the rules converge and where they genuinely come apart. Say which of the earlier cases is the closest analog to Marta's, and whether that analogy actually decides anything. Give your best guess at what she will say, with your reasons for preferring it. Finally, propose the one further case — a situation none of the verdicts already covers — that would best separate the surviving rules, and explain what each rule predicts about it.
