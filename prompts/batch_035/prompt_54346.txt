Like a lot of people who spent too long on the rationality blogs in their twenties, I've internalized the forecasting ethos: beliefs are scored predictions, and being "close to right" just means a better Brier score. Recently, though, I've gone back to the old Popperian problem of verisimilitude — the idea that one strictly false theory can be *closer to the truth* than another — and I've realized my gut verdicts about "closeness to truth" don't line up with my scoring instincts at all, and may not line up with each other. Here are the verdicts; I want to know if any single principle delivers them, or close.

Newtonian mechanics is far closer to the truth than phlogiston theory, even though both are strictly false, and Bohr's planetary atom beats Thomson's plum pudding for the same reason. But I also think Newtonian mechanics is closer to the truth than a strictly *true* but shallow theory — say, a tautologous "everything that happens, happens because of the laws" — which is exactly where my forecast-scoring instincts scream at me, since the trivial truth scores perfectly and Newton scores badly. Next: conjoining a false theory with a random unrelated truth, as in "the Earth is flat, and there are at least seven planets," doesn't seem one bit closer to the truth than "the Earth is flat" — if anything it feels slightly more ridiculous — even though it contains strictly more truth and no more falsehood. And there's a family of cases around vagueness: a theory that is precise, deep, and wrong about one parameter feels much closer to the truth than a theory so hedged ("things roughly balance out, roughly") that it never manages to say anything false; my rationalist training says unfalsifiability is a cardinal sin, but I notice I can't derive that from any closeness principle I can state. Finally, I remember reading that on some of the classic definitions, whether one theory counts as more verisimilar than another can flip depending on whether you state them in Celsius or Fahrenheit, or some such notational shift — I can't reconstruct the argument, but if that's right it seems fatal to those definitions, and yet I'm not sure I can defend the demand that verisimilitude be notation-invariant rather than just feeling that way.

So: is there a principle, or a small set of candidates, that captures these verdicts? Please state each precisely enough to generate verdicts, show me which of my cases motivates which clause, and be honest about which of my verdicts you'd have to overturn — I'm half-suspecting the "false-but-deep beats true-but-shallow" verdict and the "conjunction with a trivia truth gains nothing" verdict can't both survive. Then tell me what your principle says about a case I haven't listed: a colleague offers me "either general relativity is true, or the universe is a simulation" — as a disjunction it can't be more false than GR alone and contains at least as much truth, but it feels like a parasitic cheat, and I'd like to know whether that reaction is principled or just snobbery. And if you think the Brier-score conception of closeness and the verisimilitude conception are tracking genuinely different things, say so plainly, because I've been quietly conflating them for years.
