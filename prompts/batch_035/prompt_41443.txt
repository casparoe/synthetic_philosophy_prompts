I'm finishing a graduate seminar on Aristotle's ethics, and our final session is on Nicomachean Ethics Book V — specifically the passage on justice in exchange, where Aristotle argues that reciprocity holds the city together, uses the builder and the shoemaker to ask how many shoes equal a house, and introduces money as the conventional measure that makes dissimilar crafts commensurable. To close the seminar, I'd like a poem rather than another paper, and I've settled on the form for a reason: a sestina's six end-words circulate through fixed positions, changing partners and configurations without ever leaving the poem's economy, which strikes me as exactly what Aristotle thinks money and need do to goods — shuffle them around until unlike things stand in a ratio to one another. So please write a sestina whose six end-words are, or include, words doing that work: shoes, house, need, measure, equal, and convention (or close variants you think serve better — you may substitute, but tell me why). The lines should render the argument accurately enough that someone who has actually read Book V would nod: the exchange isn't charity but holds the polis together; the builder and shoemaker aren't interchangeable, and that very difference is what makes exchange necessary rather than redundant; money isn't natural and could in principle be otherwise, and yet it's what lets unlike things be counted against each other. I'd like the envoi to carry the residue of the argument — whatever is left over once commensuration has done its work, and whether Aristotle thinks anything real remains incommensurable is precisely the interpretive question the poem needn't settle.

A few constraints: please write in British English, keep the tone closer to the seminar room than to a Renaissance fair (no archaisms), and avoid merely name-dropping Aristotle or leaning on the word "reciprocity" as a placeholder for the actual structure of the exchange. The poem should work as a poem for a reader who has never opened the Nicomachean Ethics.

Because the interpretive ground here is genuinely contested — scholars disagree, for instance, about whether Aristotle takes the relevant equality to hold between the persons of the traders or between their products, and about how much of the passage anticipates versus resists modern economic thinking — please append a short prose note of perhaps 150 words stating: (1) which interpretive choices the poem leans on and where it takes liberties with the text or with contested readings, and (2) which single interpretive claim embedded in the poem you are least confident about, and why. If some aspect of the argument you've attributed to Aristotle is uncertain in your own mind, say so plainly rather than papering over it.
