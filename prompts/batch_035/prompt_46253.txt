For the past three years I have been trying, as an autodidact with no academic background, to get clear on one question: how can anything in a purely physical world be about something else? A word is about Paris; a belief is about the weather; even a frog's tongue-snap seems to be about small dark moving things. From what I have gathered on my own, the main serious attempt to explain this without invoking anything spooky is teleosemantics: the idea that what a trait represents is fixed by what it is there to do, where "there to do" is understood in terms of natural selection or learning history. I understand the broad shape of the difficulties this view faces — for instance the disjunction problem (why does the frog represent "fly" rather than "small dark moving thing" or "fly or BB pellet," given that it snaps at all of them), the swampman worry that an exact physical duplicate assembled by lightning would share every physical fact yet allegedly lack any history of selection and therefore any content, the complaint that biological functions are too indeterminate to pin down specific contents, and the gap between the simple signalling states of animals and the productive, compositional thoughts of language users.

I now want to spend the next few years of my life on this — writing a series of long essays aimed at other non-academic readers, arguing either that meaning can be naturalized along these lines or that it cannot. My time is limited, I have no institutional support, and I cannot run experiments or do mathematics beyond basic logic. So I need an agenda, and I would like yours.

Which of the open problems in this area should I work through first, and why? I care less about the list than about the ordering and its defense. For each problem you prioritize, please tell me: what depends on it — which other problems in the area are upstream or downstream of it, so that settling it first would make the others tractable or show them to be pseudo-problems; how tractable it is for someone like me, working from careful reading and argument alone; and whether it is neglected or instead an industry that an outsider has nothing to add to. I also want you to be blunt about which of these problems are overrated — which ones feel deep but, if solved tomorrow, would change nothing for the question I actually care about, namely whether aboutness fits into the natural world.

Two further things. First, I have a pet idea I would like you to assess as part of the agenda: perhaps what fixes what a trait represents is not its selection history but its forward-looking contribution to the system's persistence — what the trait is doing now that keeps it in place, rather than what it was selected for in the past. If this is worth developing, tell me where it fits in the ordering; if it collapses into a known position or into an obvious objection, tell me that too, so I can drop it early. Second, some people I hope to reach are unpersuaded not by the details but by the whole enterprise — they think that meaning, understanding, and aboutness are simply not the kind of thing a physical story could ever capture, whatever the details. Please tell me how much that skepticism should shape my priorities: should I address it head-on, or is it a distraction from the internal problems of the naturalistic program? And what would count, concretely, as progress that should move a problem up or down my list over the next few years — a new thought experiment, a clarification, some finding from biology or psychology?
