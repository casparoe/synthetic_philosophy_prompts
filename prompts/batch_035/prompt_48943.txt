A friend of mine — I'll call her Dana — is someone I met over a decade ago in the comments of a rationalist-adjacent blog, back when we were both arguing about whether definitions are supposed to point at clusters in thingspace or at clusters in argument-space, i.e., at whatever lets you win a fight. She went off to do a PhD in comparative genocide studies and now works for an organization that documents mass atrocities for use by courts and tribunals, so the word "genocide" is for her simultaneously a legal term of art, a moral category, and a diplomatic grenade. We have kept up a running seminar-by-email on which atrocities deserve the name, and I've accumulated enough of her verdicts that I now want to make some predictions before we meet next month, when three new cases are guaranteed to come up.

Her track record so far: Rwanda in 1994, unambiguously genocide in her book. The Ottoman Armenians in 1915, likewise, and she gets visibly impatient with the recognition politics around it. Srebrenica in July 1995, genocide — but only Srebrenica: the wider Bosnian program of camps, expulsions, and ethnic cleansing outside that enclave she insists was crimes against humanity, not genocide, and she has been scathing about commentators who use the G-word as a synonym for atrocity. The Holodomor, on the other hand, she does call genocide. The Indonesian massacres of 1965–66, in which somewhere between half a million and a million alleged communists were killed, she rules out emphatically: a genocidal-scale atrocity aimed at the wrong category of victims. The Khmer Rouge period splits her verdicts the way the Cambodia tribunal's did — the destruction of the Cham and of the Vietnamese minority in Cambodia was genocide, while the million-odd Khmer victims of the regime were not genocide, because class enemies and political factions aren't among the kinds of groups the Convention was built to protect.

Next month's cases: first, the campaign against the Uyghurs in Xinjiang — mass internment, mosque demolition, forced labor, and documented birth-prevention measures, with the destruction aimed ostensibly at culture and demographic growth rather than at bodies. Second, Canada's residential-school system and the Sixties Scoop — forcible transfer of Indigenous children into state and church institutions, with the perpetrators self-describing as benevolent assimilators rather than destroyers. Third, Franco's suppression of the Catalans — executions of Catalanist elites, bans on the language in schools, courts, and publishing, forced name changes — a case where the state's target was clearly a national group but the aim was the replacement of a culture rather than the elimination of a people. Predict her verdicts on all three. I'd like you to work out the rule or rules she is implicitly running — a strict Conventionism with special intent and the four protected groups, Lemkin's broader conception in which destroying a people's cultural foundations already begins the crime, some hybrid with a doctrine about when intent can be inferred from pattern (note the tension between her Holodomor verdict and her refusal to generalize from Srebrenica to the rest of Bosnia) — and to say which cases from her track record each candidate handles well or badly. Then give your best guess at her three verdicts with reasons, identify which of the three new cases is the sharpest discriminator between the surviving candidate rules, and propose a further hypothetical — a case I could pose to her over dinner — that would definitively split the rules you think are still tied. And since Dana would be the first to point it out: say whether you think any of your predictions would change if the recognition politics were flipped, i.e., if the politically convenient answer for her field happened to run the other way.
