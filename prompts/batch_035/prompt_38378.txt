A policy question has been nagging at me. Several jurisdictions have paired a carbon tax with a rebate of most or all of the revenue to households on a roughly equal per-capita basis — Canada's fuel charge with its rebate is the clearest case. I want to know what this practice presupposes philosophically, because its two components seem to rest on different theories. The tax itself appears to presuppose that the harm of emissions is commensurable with money: that emitting a tonne of CO2 is a cost that can be internalized by a price, rather than something one is simply not permitted to do, as we think of assault. The pricing logic is consequentialist bookkeeping — set the price equal to the marginal damage and let agents decide. But the equal per-capita rebate appears to presuppose something quite different: that the atmosphere's assimilative capacity is a commons owned by everyone in equal shares, so that those who emit less than their share are owed compensation by those who emit more. On that picture a tonne of carbon is not an externalized cost but a withdrawal from a jointly held fund, and the rebate is not a welfare transfer but the payment of rents to the co-owners. Can one and the same tonne be both? Please reconstruct the implicit theory of each component, say whether the combined practice is coherent, or whether it is instead philosophically underdetermined such that several theories of climate justice would license it as is, and then tell me what a policy built on the commons presupposition alone would have to change — would the tax rate, the incidence, or the very form of the instrument come out differently?
