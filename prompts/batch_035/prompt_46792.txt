I am writing the penultimate chapter of my master's thesis on deflationary theories of truth, and at a recent family dinner my uncle — a retired engineer and voracious reader of popular philosophy — attempted to summarize the view for me, drawing on a book he had just finished whose title I did not catch. Since he now considers himself informed enough to challenge my thesis topic, and since I am not yet confident enough in the literature to correct him on the spot, I would like an expert assessment of his rendition.

Here is what he said, as close to verbatim as I can reconstruct it: "The deflationists — Ramsey, Quine, and their modern followers — hold that truth is not a genuine property of sentences at all. To say 'it is true that snow is white' is to say no more and no less than 'snow is white,' so the word 'true' is pure redundancy: we could strike it from the language entirely and lose nothing. That is why they call the theory deflationary — the concept of truth just deflates into the individual assertions. But notice where this lands us: if truth is empty in this way, the deflationist cannot distinguish a well-confirmed scientific theory from a comforting myth except by talking about what people believe or what is useful to believe, which was precisely the work the concept of truth was supposed to do. So the view slides into relativism, and that is why mainstream philosophers gave it up decades ago."

Please take the role of a professional philosopher specializing in philosophy of language and theories of truth. Before you begin your assessment, brainstorm for a moment about what leading deflationary positions actually claim, so that you have the accurate picture in view. Then go through my uncle's summary claim by claim: identify which parts a contemporary deflationist would accept as a fair statement of the position, which are defensible simplifications that a popular author might reasonably make, and which are genuine misrepresentations — paying particular attention to whether any of them conflate distinct historical positions under a single label, and to whether the inference he draws in the final two sentences actually follows from anything deflationists maintain. Along the way, note what a deflationist would say about the apparent indispensability of the truth predicate in ordinary and scientific discourse. Conclude with a verdict on whether the relativism charge and the abandonment claim survive, and suggest which points, if any, are worth raising with my uncle over the next family dinner.
