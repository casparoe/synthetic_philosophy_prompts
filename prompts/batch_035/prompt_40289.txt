My late uncle's estate is being distributed in two instalments, and for each instalment the executors have let me choose between two payout schemes.

For the first instalment, I could take a guaranteed £40,000, or a ticket giving an 89% chance of £40,000, a 10% chance of £400,000, and a 1% chance of nothing. I took the guaranteed £40,000. The thought of ending up with nothing when £40,000 had been there for the taking made me feel ill, and the slim prospect of £400,000 never seemed worth that risk.

For the second instalment, the choices were a ticket with an 11% chance of £40,000 and nothing otherwise, or a ticket with a 10% chance of £400,000 and nothing otherwise. This time I took the £400,000 ticket: with a substantial payout barely on the table in either case, the chance at the larger sum seemed worth having.

My sister, who read economics, tells me the two choices cannot both be right. Her explanation was that in the first decision the guaranteed £40,000 and its 89% chance counterpart are the same thing happening either way, so stripping them out should leave my preference between the remaining prospects untouched; yet across the two rounds I behaved as though I preferred the safer prospect in one and the riskier one in the other. She adds that no coherent assignment of values to the final outcomes could underwrite both choices, though she refuses to say which one she regards as the mistake.

Is her charge of inconsistency fair? If so, which of my choices should I revise, and what exactly would I be conceding in revising it? If not, what would have to be true about how I experience these gambles for both choices to be defensible? Please explain your reasoning as fully as you can, and tell me plainly whether, on your view, I am being irrational.
