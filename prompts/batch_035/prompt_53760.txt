My two colleagues have been at each other for years about the Pasadena game and I want them to finally write the document an adversarial collaboration would end with. Camp A thinks expected utility theory just needs patching: handle convergence properly (bounded utilities, weak expectations, a rule that refuses to let the order of summation do any work) and the machinery survives the pathological cases. Camp B thinks conditionally convergent expectation is the canary in the coal mine: expectation is the wrong primitive for decision theory full stop, and the right theory should rank or compare gambles some other way — dominance conditions, comparing payoff distributions directly, something not built on summing. Both agree the game itself is real and playable: flip a fair coin until it lands heads on flip n, and you're paid (-1)^(n+1) · 2^n / n dollars — so the series of probability-weighted payoffs is conditionally convergent, equals ln 2 if summed in the natural order, and can be made to equal anything you like if rearranged. Both also agree this isn't just a warmed-over St. Petersburg curiosity, since the Pasadena payoffs are big only rarely, flip by flip, and yet the expectation still doesn't settle down.

Draft the joint statement they could both put their names to. I want a numbered agreed-claims section (about the game, about what a decision theory owes us, about what the standard axiomatizations do and don't already guarantee), then a disagreements table where each row states one disagreement in wording that its own side would accept — nothing slanted, nothing so watered down it's vacuous. Then the cruxes. I suspect one crux is whether a theory that cannot assign any value to a single play of the game has failed at its job, or has merely run up against the edge of where value talk makes sense — but find the others too. For each crux, give a concrete case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. At least one test case should be something you can actually compute with pencil and paper, like the truncated Pasadena game (heads must arrive by flip N or the whole thing is void): what does each side predict about the prices at which the truncated game is worth playing as N grows, and which side is risking a concession if the other's prediction holds? Finally, if part of their dispute turns out to be verbal — e.g. about what "the expected value exists" is even doing in that sentence — say which part and why. Keep the math elementary, and dont let either camp quietly smuggle in their own framework's vocabulary as the neutral vocabulary for stating the disagreements.
