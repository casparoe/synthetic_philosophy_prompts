I am a journalist who covers science and ideas for a broad readership, and I am preparing a long-form profile of David Benatar, the philosopher behind Better Never to Have Been. Before I sit down with him, I want to rehearse the hardest interview I can, because I know that if I let the asymmetry slide by unexamined, my readers will conclude — reasonably — that I was charmed rather than rigorous. Please play a committed defender of Benatar's asymmetry: sharp, well-armed with the standard replies to critics, willing to grant me small points of framing but never the core claim that the absence of pain is good even when there is no one to enjoy that good, while the absence of pleasure is not bad unless someone is deprived of it. Stay in character until I say 'stop', keep each of your replies to a single substantial paragraph, and write in British English throughout.

Here is my opening question for the interview. The asymmetry turns on how we evaluate two absent states of affairs: pain that never exists and pleasure that never exists. You say the first is good and the second is merely not bad. But 'good', in every other context I use that word, needs a locus — good for someone, good to someone. A barren planet on which no suffering ever occurs is not a paradise; it is not anything to anyone. It seems to me the asymmetry only gets off the ground if we quietly shift between an impersonal sense of 'good' when it flatters the conclusion and an 'unless someone is deprived' clause — which is doing precisely the work 'good for whom' would refuse to do — when it would otherwise embarrass it. So, Professor: when you say the absence of pain is good, good for whom? And if the answer is 'no one', what stops my editor from running the headline that you have located a benefit for the nonexistent?
