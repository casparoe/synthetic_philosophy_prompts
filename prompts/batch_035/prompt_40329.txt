I'm a third-year student in an upper-level seminar on contemporary critiques of consequentialism, and I've been asked to draft a practice exam question for our review session. The unit just finished covered Bernard Williams's integrity objection, and I want a question that goes beyond the standard "explain Williams's critique and the utilitarian reply" boilerplate that everyone in the class can already recite.

Here's what I want the question to do. There's a tendency among students (and, frankly, in some of the secondary literature) to run two of Williams's points together: (1) the integrity objection, which is about a structural feature of consequentialism — that it makes no room for an agent's ground projects in the justification of action, and instead lets them in only instrumentally, as inputs to overall utility; and (2) the "one thought too many" point, which is about what goes wrong in a particular deliberative performance — the drowning-spouse example, where the rescuer whose motivating reason is that rescuing happens to be the right or optimal thing to do, rather than simply that it is his spouse in the water, has in some way reasoned badly as a person. The question should force students to see that these are separable: one is a claim about the theory's justificatory structure, the other a claim about agent-centered patterns of practical reasoning, and a sophisticated consequentialist might concede much of one while resisting the other.

To sharpen this, the question should bring in something like the two-level strategy associated with R. M. Hare: the consequentialist who says that the correct decision procedure for agents like us is not to calculate utilities at all, but to internalize dispositions, loyalties, and intuitive rules that reliably produce good outcomes, saving the calculating standpoint for critical reflection on those dispositions. Students must say whether this move answers the integrity objection, or merely answers the "one thought too many" complaint, or neither — and, crucially, they must take a stance on why: does the two-level consequentialist preserve integrity in the deliberative sense while still failing to respect ground projects at the level of justification, or is that a distinction without a difference because justification and deliberation can't come apart that cleanly? I'd also like the question to include a short applied component: a variant case in which an agent's ground project is itself impartial or philanthropic in character (someone whose deepest commitment is famine relief, say), so students must confront whether the integrity objection even gets a grip on such a person, and what that shows about whether the objection is really about *projects* or about *partiality*.

Please write the question itself (it should be answerable in about 90 minutes, in essay form), a model answer of roughly 600 words, and a marking scheme. For the marking scheme, identify the four or five specific moves that an A answer must make — for instance, correctly locating where in the justificatory structure the two views come apart, rather than just name-dropping the two Williams points — and, for each, a one-line note on the standard way students botch it. Please note explicitly where a B answer falls short. Also, please render everything in American English, including spellings like "criticism," "behavior," and "characterize." The question should not require any quotations from or precise citations of particular texts — I want it answerable from a command of the ideas, not the page numbers.
