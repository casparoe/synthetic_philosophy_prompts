I'm clerking for an intermediate appellate judge, and she's asked me for a bench memo on a case I've privately started calling "the flood ordinance mess." Short version: during a major flood two years ago, the county council passed an emergency ordinance against "exploitative pricing of essential goods." It was enacted the day *after* the conduct it punished, never showed up on the countys website for six weeks (some clerk's error), the word "exploitative" was never defined except in an internal guidance memo that a cousin working in the clerk's office quietly forwarded to a couple of store owners (and only them), and enforcement was weirdly concentrated on shops that had backed the incumbent council's rival in the last election. Defendant got convicted under it and now argues the ordinance "never became law" at all — not that it's unconstitutional, but that it *isn't law* — so the conviction is void.

The state's answer is basically pure pedigree: council followed its enactment procedure, executive signed it, ergo it's law, and any defects go to how it's applied or whether it survives due process, not to whether it exists in the first place. The judge wants me to take that side seriously — she thinks the separation of legal validity from legal merit is precisely what keeps judges from turning into a super-legislature — but she's also clearly tempted by the thought that a rule that's retroactive, secret, vague, and selectively enforced isn't just bad law, it's a failure to make law, and courts should be able to say so in those words.

There's also a wrinkle one of the defense attorneys raised that the judge finds "cute but dangerous": that legal validity comes in degrees, so the ordinance might be law enough to support civil fines but not criminal penalties. She wants to know if that's a real distinction she could actually write into an opinion or if it's just vibes.

Draft the bench memo she can act on: recommend a concrete disposition (affirm, reverse with dismissal ordered, remand for a narrowing construction, whatever you honestly think is best), and engage the strongest version of the state's pedigree argument, not a strawman of it. Lead with the bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning — no preamble, and don't restate the facts back at me, she already knows them.
