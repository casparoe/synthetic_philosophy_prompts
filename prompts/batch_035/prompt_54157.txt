I cover science and ideas for a mid-size outlet — features mostly, the kind of place that will run 3000 words on why nobody agrees on what a species is. A while back I did a piece on why smart people believe dumb things, and that's how I fell down the epistemology rabbit hole and got stuck on a fight that I now realize has been parked for decades: Bas van Fraassen's Reflection principle. Roughly: if you're certain now that at some future time you'll hold a particular credence, then your current credence should match it. Trust your future self — they'll have had everything you have, plus more. It came with a Dutch book argument: if you don't defer, you're exploitable.

Then the counterexamples rolled in. The poker one everyone cites (Christensen's, I think): you know that after a few drinks at the game you'll be wildly overconfident about your hand, and you can see that coming perfectly clearly right now, sober. Nobody sane says you should pre-adopt the drunk's confidence. Same family: the person who can foresee they'll irrationally believe they'll win the lottery, or cases where you know somebody is about to brainwash or gaslight you into a belief, and you can't stop it.

And here's where its been parked ever since. Camp one says fine, Reflection is just false as a general norm — counterexamples, case closed. Camp two, which I understand to be Christensen's own later line, says the counterexamples only show you have to condition on the right things: reflect on your future self as they'll actually be, degraded faculties and all, and the principle survives as a norm of deference to a properly functioning future self. Camp three treats it as a bookkeeping matter: Reflection is a coherence condition for ideal agents, humans who can foresee their own corruption were never the subject matter, so calling these counterexamples is a category error. Each camp publishes, the others cite them politely, and nobody converts. I've read enough now to see the pattern, and I can't tell if I'm watching a genuine dispute or three journals talking past each other.

My working hunch — and take it as a hunch, I'm a journalist not a philosopher: the camps aren't disagreeing about one thing. They're answering three different questions under one heading: a norm for ideal agents, a norm for messy human ones, and maybe a covert claim about what foreseen irrationality even is — a fact about the world you can conditionalize on, versus a defect in you that removes you from the norm's jurisdiction. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a real substantive disagreement in there after all? I want you to actually commit to one reading of what kind of disagreement this is — verbal, substantive, different-questions-under-one-name — and defend it using what the sides actually claim, not a neutral survey of all three. And then go one step further: tell me what would count as progress here, some distinction or reframing that could genuinely move it, or tell me straight that this one should be expected to stay parked forever and why. Don't give me the both-sides-have-a-point answer — I can write that myself in my sleep, and honestly it's part of whats wrong with my trade.
