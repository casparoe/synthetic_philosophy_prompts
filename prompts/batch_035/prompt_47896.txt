I work at a small machine-learning lab where, each week, an evaluation board scores individual reasoning episodes from our models — a chain of steps plus a final answer — and assigns a reward from 0 to 100. My colleague Priya has cast the deciding vote on that board for the past year, and her rulings have quietly become the house standard. We are now debating whether to distil her verdicts into reward labels for training, in effect choosing between process supervision and outcome supervision by inheritance rather than by argument, so I need to work out what rule she is actually running.

Her track record on past episodes, as best our minutes reconstruct it:

1. Answer correct; the chain sound throughout apart from one typographical slip — reward 100.
2. Chain sound throughout; a trivial arithmetic slip in the final line makes the answer wrong — reward 80, the largest partial she has ever given.
3. Answer correct; the chain contains a load-bearing false step, so the derivation is invalid even though the conclusion is right — reward 0, on the grounds that the episode is indistinguishable, from the inside, from a lucky guess.
4. Answer correct; the model emitted a bare answer with no reasoning at all — reward 0, on the grounds that she cannot grade what she cannot see.
5. Answer correct; the chain lucid and locally sound — but we later established that the episode replays, nearly word for word, a solution posted to a public forum years before. Reward 0, despite the visible process being impeccable.

Next week's docket has three episodes, and I would like her verdicts predicted before she gives them, together with the reasoning behind each prediction:

A. Midway through, the model commits a substantive error, then notices it and corrects it, and the chain read end-to-end is sound; the answer is correct. But the self-correction was triggered by a structural check that exists in this particular problem and almost nowhere else in its family: on probed variants, the model sails through with the same error uncorrected.
B. The chain is sound as far as anyone on the board can follow it, and the answer is correct, but the pivotal step is a compressed leap no grader can decompose or verify. Probes on variants of the same type show it landing consistently correct.
C. The chain tracks, almost clause for clause, a solution template in the pretraining data, but the model has applied it correctly to a genuinely novel problem; the answer is correct and generalisation on probes is solid.

For each of A, B and C, please predict her reward (or a defensible range), say which of the rules consistent with her track record you are appealing to, and be explicit that more than one rule fits the five earlier verdicts: I can see at least a final-chain soundness rule, a step-wise soundness rule that penalises any false step even a corrected one, and a rule under which reward tracks the evidence an episode provides that the model would go on getting similar problems right — besides cruder outcome-based rules with carve-outs for memorisation. Tell me which of A, B and C best discriminates between the candidate rules, and design the one further episode that would separate whichever two rules you end up rating most likely. Finally, since the point of all this is deciding what to train against: say which of her rules could safely be turned into reward labels, and what would predictably go wrong if we automated the wrong one — for instance, what a model trained against step-wise soundness learns to do with its own errors, or why a generalisation rule cannot be applied to a single episode without smuggling in off-episode evidence. Please answer in British English.
