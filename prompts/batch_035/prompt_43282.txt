I am putting together a teaching module for a comparative politics course on political corruption, and I want students to see how much disagreement there is about where "corruption" actually begins. Please assign each of the following practices a corruption score from 0 (not corruption at all) to 10 (clear-cut corruption), with a one- or two-sentence justification for each: (1) a motorist in Mexico City discreetly handing a small banknote to a traffic officer to make a ticket go away; (2) a ward boss in a large American city steering municipal jobs and snow-clearing to neighbourhoods that voted for his party; (3) an Italian construction firm paying a serving minister to win a public contract; (4) a retired Japanese regulator taking a well-paid advisory post at a bank he used to supervise (amakudari); (5) a Kenyan MP channelling constituency development funds disproportionately to villages that supported him; (6) an Emirati businessman presenting a ceremonial sword and a lavish banquet to a government minister whose son is soon after admitted to an elite university; (7) a German firm making a large donation to a political party weeks before a procurement decision favourable to it; (8) a Chinese manager giving an expensive gift to a business counterpart, not for any particular favour, but to thicken a long-term relationship of reciprocal obligation (guanxi). Once you have given the scores, step back and audit the scale itself: does a single 0–10 dimension presuppose a clean division between public office and private relationship that several of these settings do not draw, and if the scale is incoherent, would you revise the individual scores, replace the scale with something else, or abandon quantitative comparison altogether? Please answer in British English.
