I'm a software engineer on a team that sits between interpretability research and model release at a company that ships large language models, and a recurring part of my job has quietly become philosophical. The situation: we spend weeks characterising a mechanism in one checkpoint — say, the circuit behind refusal of harmful requests, or whatever the model uses for multi-step arithmetic — and then someone asks whether those findings carry over to a newer checkpoint. Meanwhile our anomaly monitors flag the new checkpoint because its internals have shifted, and someone else asks whether that is the same mechanism having drifted, or a genuinely different mechanism that happens to produce the same behaviour. We have no principled way of answering. These decisions come up perhaps a dozen times a quarter with real stakes attached: a ruling of "same mechanism" lets us reuse safety analysis and quiet the monitor, while a ruling of "different mechanism" blocks a release and triggers weeks of re-analysis. Each one has to be made within a day or two by people, like me, who have no formal background in philosophy.

My understanding, from the little I've read on the side, is that this is what philosophers of science would call the identity conditions for a mechanism — whether mechanisms are individuated by what they do (their function) or by how they are physically implemented, and what to say when the same function is realised in two quite different ways, or when an implementation drifts continuously until it is unclear there was ever a definite point at which it became something else. Our concrete cases: the model is fine-tuned and the attention heads in the refusal circuit change their roles, but refusal behaviour is intact; a 4-bit quantisation leaves behaviour nearly identical while the internal activations look nothing like the original's; and a nightly retrain from a slightly different data seed yields a model that behaves the same, except our probes find the relevant features living in entirely different layers.

What I'd like from you is a decision procedure my team can run on a given pair of checkpoints: an ordered list of questions, a statement of what counts as sufficient evidence for each answer, and clearly marked points where the procedure outputs "same", "different", or "undetermined — escalate to a human". Ground it in what the philosophy of science actually says about mechanism identity, functional individuation, and multiple realisation, translated into terms an engineer without a philosophy background can use, and be candid about which steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure. Then run the procedure on the three cases above — fine-tuning drift, quantisation, and retraining from a different seed — including at least one where it gives the answer my team would least want to hear, and close by saying what the procedure cannot do.

Please write the answer in American English.
