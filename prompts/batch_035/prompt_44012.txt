I write a column on science and ideas, and an economist among my readers has sent me an argument about Amartya Sen's liberal paradox that, if sound, would show that liberal and efficient collective choice is impossible as a practical matter — everywhere, always. That is more than I can believe, but each step is dressed as innocuous, and I don't want to publish a dismissal I can't defend. I'd be grateful for a careful diagnosis.

His argument runs as follows. (1) Sen showed that no collective decision rule can satisfy both the Pareto condition and even a minimal form of liberalism, under which each of at least two individuals is decisive over some pair of alternatives concerning that individual's own person or conduct — what he reads, how he sleeps. (2) Which pairs are personal in this sense is fixed independently of anyone's preferences: whether I read a certain book is my business regardless of what others think about it. (3) Whenever some other member of society holds a strict preference about how a person resolves such a personal pair — my neighbor cares deeply about what I read — the conditions of the paradox are thereby in place. (4) In any actual society there is no act so personal that nobody else holds a strict preference about it: someone cares about what I read, what I do in private, whether I ride without a helmet. (5) So in every actual society the conditions of the paradox are in place for every putatively personal choice, and no rule can combine Pareto efficiency with even minimal liberalism there. (6) Therefore liberal, efficient collective choice is impossible not merely in the textbook examples but as a standing fact of social life, which is why privacy law, markets, and codes of personal conduct keep failing us.

Please tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs. If more than one step is suspect, rank them, and in each case explain why the step looked fine and what must be given up to deny it. I'd most like to keep (2) and (4), but I'm prepared to hear that one of them is the problem.
