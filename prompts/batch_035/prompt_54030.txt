A close friend and I spent most of a long weekend arguing about sunk costs, and I emerged holding the unfashionable end of it: that honoring sunk costs is not always irrational. The version of the view I ended up with has two parts, and I want to be upfront that the first is weak and the second is where I actually plant my flag. Part one: many costs we call "sunk" aren't really sunk, because paying them changed the agent's future payoffs or evidence — the tenth year of training is cheap because of the nine before it, so continuing isn't honoring the past, it's exploiting it. Part two: even for genuinely sunk costs, there are cases where the past investment is partly constitutive of what the project now is — the marriage, the cathedral left half-built, the friend's half-finished dissertation — so "what should I do from here?" cannot always be answered by forward-looking payoffs alone. Next month I present this position at a seminar whose audience is mostly behavioral economists and experimental psychologists who teach the sunk-cost fallacy as a textbook bias, plus two philosophers of practical rationality who are broadly sympathetic to me but will insist I commit to exactly which version I hold. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections this audience will actually raise — escalation of commitment, opportunity cost, the Concorde case, "part one is just relabeling," "part two is a mood, not a reason," the training argument cutting in favor of quitting earlier — ordered by how much damage each would do, phrased in their vocabulary, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Include what I should concede up front about the empirical literature so I don't come across as denying that people escalate badly, and the one question I'm hoping nobody asks.
