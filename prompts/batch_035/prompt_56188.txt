I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, but ever since reading Gödel, Escher, Bach years ago I've kept coming back to Hofstadter's three-way split of any message into the frame message (the bare signal that there is a message here at all — the way a stretch of radio noise can announce itself as deliberate), the outer message (the decoding instructions built into the structure itself, which must somehow be understood before any content can be), and the inner message (the actual payload). Please write a sonnet about this idea — the location of meaning, or where in (or out of) an artifact the meaning actually lives. Use the octave to present a candidate message as pure matter — say, a stretch of signal arriving from deep space, or the physical grooves of a record — with no decoding assumed. Let the volta be the instant the decoder recognizes that the artifact was made by, and addressed to, something like itself. Let the closing couplet deliver the inner message, and make it something true about meaning never having existed in the artifact alone. Then make the form carry part of the argument: the first letters of the fourteen lines, taken in order, should spell out a short instruction to the reader of exactly fourteen letters, one per line — a frame message hidden in the structure, available only to a decoder that goes looking. Someone who knows Hofstadter's distinction should be able to identify all three layers, including the acrostic; someone who doesn't should still come away with a good poem. Begin your response with the poem itself, with no preface or commentary.
