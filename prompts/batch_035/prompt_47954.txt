Quick context: I help run a casual philosophy meetup at a pub, about a dozen people, and for the past few months we've been doing one classic thought experiment per session — trolleys, teleporters, the color scientist locked in her room, that kind of thing. Over that time I've noticed a pattern in how these things actually persuade us, and I can't find a word for it anywhere. Some thought experiments seem to get nearly all of their force from the way they're told rather than from any argument. If I retell Thomson's violinist case in flat language — you wake up and someone has surgically hooked a dying stranger to your circulatory system, unplugging him kills him, it takes nine months — half my group starts hedging and saying it's complicated. But told the way she tells it, with the kidnappers being devoted fans of a famous performer who chose you specifically, people are immediate and confident about their right to unplug. Same with other cases: we once swapped out purely decorative details in a trolley variant (the person on the side track described as a beloved local figure vs. a passing stranger) and the room's confidence measurably shifted, even though nothing morally relevant had changed. It's not that people end up disagreeing about the conclusion, it's that the vividness is doing the persuading, and if you strip it the intuition weakens or evaporates.

A second thing we stumbled onto: sometimes someone in the group retells a case with deliberately neutral wording, or even words chosen to tilt the other way, and the intuition flips or dissapears — and then other members complain this is cheating, that you're not allowed to bias the scenario. But of course the original telling was itself a choice of wording, so what counts as the neutral telling? I know the term "framing effect" from psychology stuff, but that seems to be about gains-vs-losses wording in numbers problems, not about whole fictional scenarios where the decorative detail carries the moral weight. And a friend just says it's "rhetoric," which feels way too broad — there's no orator persuading us, it's the structure of the example itself. So, does this pattern have an established name in philosophy? And does the retelling/tilting counter-move have one? If nothing fits exactly, I'd like you to coin a term — with a real definition and at least a couple of boundary cases showing where the phenomenon stops (e.g., is it still the same thing when the detail that flips the intuition is arguably morally relevant after all?).

Please structure your answer in four named sections, in this order: (1) "Nearest existing concepts," roughly a hundred words, listing the closest things that actually have names and one line each on how well they fit; (2) "What this pattern is — and isn't," around 150 words, carving it out from the near misses; (3) "The counter-move," a short paragraph on whether deliberately retelling scenarios flat or reverse-tilted is a recognized method and whether the cheating complaint has any footing; (4) "A label, if we need one," a short section where you either commit to the best existing term or coin one, with the definition and the boundary cases. I'm not after just a name-drop — I want to know where the edges of this thing are.
