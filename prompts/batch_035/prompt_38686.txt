So I'm writing a term paper for a phil science seminar on Bayesian confirmation, and I've gotten stuck on the problem of old evidence. Quick setup in my own words so we're on the same page: if some evidence E was already known before theory T came along, then P(E) = 1, so discovering that T accounts for E can't raise P(T) by the ratio formula — P(E|T)/P(E) is just 1/1. The standard patch, usually credited to Howson and Urbach, is garbling: hypothetically strip away your knowledge of E, ask what credence you'd have assigned it back then, and run conditionalization on that counterfactual prior. Fine. But my hunch is that garbling still fails whenever E was already nailed down by OTHER things the agent knows and keeps in the hypothetical — if the collateral knowledge makes E near-certain anyway, the garbled prior on E is still ~1 and the confirmation is still zero, even in cases where intuition insists it happened. I want a case built to show this, and I want it built carefully, not hand-waved.

Here's the spec. Build me a matched pair of scenarios, same agent, same old evidence E, same hypothesis H — the two versions differ ONLY in the route by which the agent arrives at H. In scenario A the agent works out H on independent grounds and only afterwards notices that it neatly accounts for E. In scenario B the agent reverse-engineers H, fiddling with assumptions precisely until it fits E — they peeked at the answer key. Constraints: realistic professional/everyday setting (a brewery, an auto shop, a hospital ward, IT incident response, whatever — no physics, no astronomy, no psychics, and please don't recycle Mercury's perihelion or the Semmelweis handwashing story, I've read those a hundred times). The crucial constraint: E must be near-certain for the agent even under the garbled hypothetical, because some collateral fact the garbling does NOT touch entails or makes E overwhelmingly likely (think: they know E through a second, independent instrument or record, so even pretending not to know E directly, the other reading pins it down). Each scenario should be one short paragraph. And to be clear about what counts as cheating: the Bayesian verdicts have to fall out of the setup once you run the numbers — if the machinery gives a different answer than my hunch, report the machinery, don't quietly rig the case so it comes out the way I want.

After the two scenarios, do the walkthrough. For each of A and B: state what actual-prior Bayes awards, what garbled Bayes awards (using the counterfactual prior), and what intuition awards — and if you don't think the intuitive verdicts actually split between A and B, say so honestly and tell me what that does to my setup, I'd rather know. Then tell me which single feature of the pair is doing the work, and give me the two best Bayesian counterattacks: (i) the bullet-biter who says the history of how H was constructed is simply invisible to confirmation, only the probabilities matter; and (ii) the garbler who fights back about what the counterfactual strips — maybe the collateral knowledge has to go too, and if so, where does the garbling stop? Btw I don't need citations or a bibiography, just the reasoning. At the very end, give me a structured summary as a single JSON object with these fields: "setting" (one line), "evidence_E", "collateral_knowledge" (the facts that keep the garbled P(E) near 1), "hypothesis_H", "garbled_verdicts" (an object with keys "A" and "B", each a phrase saying how much confirmation garbled Bayes awards), "intuitive_verdicts" (same structure), "load_bearing_feature", "strongest_bayesian_reply", and "pair_works" (yes / partially / no, with one sentence why).
