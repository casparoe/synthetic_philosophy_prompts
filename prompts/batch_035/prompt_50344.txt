Quick context: I'm in a course on the historical Jesus, and we just spent two weeks on the "criteria of authenticity" -- criterion of embarrassment, criterion of dissimilarity, multiple attestation, coherence, Aramaic substratum, all that stuff. Our professor also showed us a documentary clip about the Jesus Seminar, where a bunch of scholars literally voted on individual sayings with colored beads (red for "Jesus really said this," black for "later invention," pink and gray in between). Something about it has been bugging me and I can't quite articulate it, so I want help thinking it through.

Here's what bugs me. On the one hand, the criteria have a certain surface logic: the early church wouldn't have INVENTED something embarrassing to itself, so the crucifixion and the "let the dead bury the dead" line and so on probably go back to Jesus; if a saying shows up in Mark AND in an independent source like Q or Thomas, it's more likely old than something with one late attestation. Fine. But on the other hand, historians of basically every other period -- ancient Rome, the French Revolution, whatever -- never do this. They don't have a checklist of formal criteria they apply one by one to each statement attributed to Caesar and then aggregate into a confidence rating. They just weigh sources, in a holistic, kind of squishy way. So it feels like historical Jesus scholarship is doing something with a different underlying picture of what historical knowledge IS, and nobody ever states what that picture is.

So my question, basically: what view of evidence and historical inference do these criteria (and the bead-voting institutionalized version of them) actually presuppose? A few sub-questions so you know what I'm after:

1. The criterion of embarrassment seems to assume that traditions preserved by a community are, by default, stuff that community had reason to preserve -- i.e., that invention leaves traces of self-interest. Is that a general theory of testimony, or just an assumption about THIS particular community (the early church, which supposedly knew its founder got executed as a criminal and had to explain that)? And if it's just local, why did the criteria get presented as if they were general methods?

2. The criterion of dissimilarity (a saying is authentic only if it's dissimilar to both early Judaism and the early church) seems to quietly presuppose that Jesus was a weirdo standing outside both his own religious context and his followers' -- which is itself a substantive theological/historical claim, smuggled in as a methodological rule. Is that fair? Does anything about the practice force that presupposition, or could you reformulate the criterion to avoid it?

3. The bead voting thing treats authenticity as per-unit and binary-ish: each saying gets scored independently. But every historian I've read also says context matters -- a saying means something in a first-century Jewish apocalyptic setting that it wouldn't mean alone, and the criteria are supposed to reconstruct that setting. Is the per-saying scoring consistent with that, or does the practice presuppose something like atomism about evidence that the rest of the field contradicts?

4. And here's the thing I most want your take on: suppose you replaced the whole apparatus with a straightforwardly probabilistic approach -- everything is just likelihood ratios, P(this tradition looks like this | Jesus said it) vs P(this tradition looks like this | the church invented it), no colored beads, no named criteria. Would the results actually change, or would the criteria turn out to be folk-Bayesian heuristics in fancy dress? And is there anything a Bayesian version would lose?

For format: lead with your overall answer, then take the four sub-questions in order, and then give me a one-sentence summary as the very last line, beginning with the word "Verdict:" -- basically the implicit epistemology in one line, so I can tell whether the criteria are doing real work or just laundering assumptions. Keep it readable, I'm an undergrad not a seminar professor. (sorry for the length, this has been rattling around my head since tuesday's lecture)
