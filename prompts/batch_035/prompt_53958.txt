My housemate and I stayed up most of last night arguing about whether the lying/misleading distinction marks anything morally real. Her side of it is that lying breaches a distinctive trust that mere misleading leaves intact, and that this shows in her verdicts on cases. We ended up reconstructing a decade of them, and I noticed she's remarkably consistent. Her track record:

1. Telling a bailiff "I was home all evening" when she wasn't: lying, and worse than any merely misleading maneuver.
2. Spinning a false cover story to a border guard she knew full well would check her story and not believe it: still lying, still wrong.
3. Asked by her flatmate whether she'd finished the cake that was being saved for a birthday, saying "I had a slice" when she'd eaten the whole thing, so the flatmate assumed most of it was left: lying. (This one surprised me — I'd have called it merely misleading.)
4. Wearing her brother's staff badge to slip into a festival without paying: deceptive and wrong, but not lying — no words.
5. Saying "Oh yes, brilliant idea" sarcastically about a plan she thinks is terrible, where everyone present knows it's sarcasm: not lying.
6. Asserting "The landlord says we can't have pets," while having no idea what the landlord says, purely to end an argument: not lying — she calls that bullshitting and treats it as a different vice entirely.

Her sister's situation is about to force three fresh verdicts, and my housemate will be asked for them at dinner next week. Predict them. (a) The sister, selling her car, is asked whether it's reliable and answers with a run of true sentences — "It started fine this morning" and the like — carefully chosen so the buyer concludes it's sound, though it's a lemon that happened to run that day. (b) Asked to account for a broken window, she opens with "So when are you going to admit you broke it?" to a housemate who had nothing to do with it. (c) Held by captors, she tells them the true location of the supplies, in a tone and setting calculated to make them take it for disinformation. (d) She tells her daughter that the dying dog has gone to live happily on a farm, knowing the daughter will believe her.

I want more than the four predictions. Which rules actually fit all six of the past verdicts — an assertion-and-implicature rule, a broader rule on which any linguistic act intended to produce a false belief counts, even the uncharitable hypothesis that my housemate's word "lying" simply flags whichever deception she condemns most — and where do those rules come apart on the four new cases? Give your best guess for each verdict with your reasons for preferring the rule that generates it, and name the case, beyond these four, that would most cleanly separate whatever rules survive. Finally, be candid about the fragility of the exercise: tell me which of her six verdicts, if I've misremembered it after a late night, would unravel the rule-finding most, and what would make you abandon your best-guess rule for one of its rivals.
