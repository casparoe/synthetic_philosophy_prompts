I just started as the welfare coordinator at a mid-sized public aquarium (first real job out of school, started in january), and I've discovered there's basically no policy for any of the welfare calls that actually land on my desk. We hold everything from a giant Pacific octopus to cleaner shrimp to hundreds of fish species, and the questions come up weekly: which species get enrichmment programs, whether a transport or a tagging procedure warrants analgesia, when a struggling animal gets palliative care versus euthanasia. Our vet is great with mammals but openly says she "doesn't do fish feelings," and the board wants something written down they can defend to the public, especially now that the UK has started listing decapods and cephalopods as sentient, so inspectors ask us about the lobsters too.

What I need is a procedure I can actually run on a proposal: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to me or the vet because it's a values call rather than a facts one.

The hard part is that the procedure has to take a stance on something I keep going back and forth on, and this is where I want the real thinking: whether an animal's moral weight is a threshold thing (a species either counts fully or not at all, pending some evidence bar) or a graded thing (a zebrafish's pain counts at whatever credence you place on fish sentience, times its intensity). The threshold view makes the policy clean but arbitrary at the border, and the graded view makes the math defensible but genuinely weird -- a procedure built on it will happily trade one octopus against three hundred danios, and my staff will revolt. So decide between them (or find the third option I'm missing), tell me which distinctions survive the simplification and which you're dropping, and be honest about where the procedure will misfire.

Then run it on two live cases. First: our octopus is getting bored and destructive in her exhibit, and enrichment for her would eat the whole enrichment budget I'd otherwise spread across the fish tanks. Second: the vet wants to insert a tag in a stingray without analgesia, since fish analgesia is under-studied and she doubts rays feel it the way mammals do. And flag it clearly if the procedure gives me an answer I won't like -- my suspicion is that on the budget case it sides with the fish by sheer numbers, and I'd rather know that now than find out when the octopus has pulled half the tank apart. Budget is small enough that we're always choosing, not adding, so definately don't give me a procedure whose answer to everything is "both."
