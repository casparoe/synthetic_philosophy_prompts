I teach an undergraduate course on the ethics of war, and next term I want to screen Wolfgang Petersen's Das Boot (1981) as a sustained case study in the debate over the moral equality of combatants — the view, associated with Walzer, that soldiers fighting for an unjust cause are still legitimate combatants, no worse than the soldiers trying to kill them so long as they fight within the rules, and the revisionist challenge to it, associated with McMahan, on which fighting for an unjust war is itself a wrong that no clean conduct erases. The film is engineered to make an audience root for the crew of a German U-boat in 1941: they are exhausted professionals, several are openly contemptuous of the Nazi regime, the captain visibly despises the party officials around him, and we spend two hours inside their terror. I want a reading of the film through that debate, and I want it to be faithful in both directions.

Specifically: does the film dramatize the moral-equality thesis, showing us that our sympathy for these men tracks something real that the Walzerian position asserts — or does it win that sympathy by stacking the deck, scrubbing away everything that would test it (the crew's politics, what their sinkings actually contribute to the war, the historical record of the U-boat campaign) so that the thesis is never really put under pressure? Identify the scenes that do the philosophical work and say what each one shows or smuggles in: the drunken send-off before departure, the war correspondent Werner as our surrogate witness, the aftermath of a sinking where the crew must watch men in the water and can do nothing for them, the crippled dive and dash through the Strait of Gibraltar, and the final moments at the home quay. I'd like you to say honestly where the film resists the moral-equality reading and where it resists the revisionist one, and to flag which of these scenes is doing the most load-bearing work and whether it can bear it. Then close by telling me what the film adds to the Walzer–McMahan exchange that the arguments on paper cannot: something about the phenomenology of being inside an unjust war's machinery, and whether that phenomenology counts as evidence for either side or is only a rhetorical lever that a skillful director can pull in either direction — the same trick, after all, that an honest depiction of an Allied bomber crew would perform in reverse. The students will have seen the film before we discuss it, so assume the plot is known and argue with the scenes, not around them.
