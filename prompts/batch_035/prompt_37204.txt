help me out — i'm running the last session of a reading group on decision theory, and we've spent the whole back half on expected value maximization and its pathologies: pascal's mugging, tiny probabilities of astronomical payoffs, whether you're allowed to just not care about a one-in-a-googol chance. i want to close with a poem instead of another discussion, specifically a villanelle, and i want the form to do the actual philosophical work.

the speaker should be the fanatic themself — the person who bites every bullet. their position, roughly: for any finite certain payoff, there's a small enough probability of a large enough payoff that beats it, so if you're maximizing expected value you have to take the astronomical-side bet every time. and crucially, the fanatic doesn't think this is a mood or a temperament you can argue someone out of — they think it's forced. grant the axioms (expected value, dominance across acts, transitivity) and the conclusion about tinies and huges follows whether you like it or not.

here's where the villanelle earns its keep: a villanelle cannot get rid of its refrains. they come back whether the middle lines want them to or not, in their fixed order, and the poet can only arrange the ground they land on. that is exactly the fanatic's view of the conclusion — it keeps returning no matter how you hedge, and the only escape is to deny a premise outright, not to out-argue the arithmetic mid-stream. so the two refrains should be the premise and the conclusion of the argument (maybe something like a plain statement of the expected value rule, and the flat assertion that a tiny chance of enough outweighs a sure thing — but you pick the exact wording), and the tercets in between should be the anti-fanatic's escape attempts: bounded utilities, risk aversion, just discounting minuscule probabilities as noise, the gut sense that a bet that pays out in no accessible world is worthless. each tercet tries something, and the refrain returns anyway, unbothered and correct-sounding.

the closing quatrain, where the two refrains finally have to sit side by side in the last two lines, is the volta as far as i'm concerned — that adjacency should be the moment a reader sees that premise and conclusion are the same coin, and that if you hate the conclusion the only move left is refusing the premise at the door. so don't let the poem resolve in favor of the fanatic exactly, but also don't let it smuggle in a refutation the debate hasn't actually produced — the honest ending is the escape being visible but expensive, not cheap.

the bar: someone who's followed the fanaticism debate closely should be able to point at each refrain and say which is which and why the form is the right form for this, and someone who hasn't should still get a good poem about a mind that can't stop multiplying. also please keep the arithmetic honest — no "one in a million times infinity" sloppiness, the fanatic is careful and that's what makes them scary.
