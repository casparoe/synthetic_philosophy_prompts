I help run a small non-academic reading group that has spent this term on personal identity, and we have just finished Douglas Hofstadter's I Am a Strange Loop. The idea that gripped the group is his suggestion that an "I" is not a thing but a pattern: a self-referential loop in which a brain models the world, including a model of itself modeling the world. Since other people build coarse-grained models of us inside their own skulls, Hofstadter holds that fragments of a person's "I" can be re-instantiated in other brains, and that when someone dies, parts of that person can genuinely, non-metaphorically live on in those who knew them. Some of my members accept this and some think it is a flattering metaphor dressed up as metaphysics, and I want a case that would actually adjudicate between those reactions rather than just pump intuitions.

So here is the specification. Construct a thought experiment in which Hofstadter's external-instantiation criterion and a standard psychological-continuity criterion of personal persistence come apart in both directions at once: the candidate who best carries the deceased's psychological continuity should not be the candidate who best instantiates the deceased's "I," and vice versa, within the same case. Constraints: the scenario must be realistic and use only ordinary people and ordinary facts about them, no brain scanning, no uploading, no conscious software, no cryonics; something in the register of a widowed spouse, a sibling in the early stages of dementia, and a devoted biographer who never met the subject but has spent thirty years inside her papers. The case must contain one tunable parameter, something like the trade-off between fidelity of the model (how accurately someone reproduces the person's opinions, tastes, speech patterns) and engagement (how often and how centrally the model is rehearsed and deployed to generate actual behaviour), such that the two criteria rank the candidates differently depending on where the dial is set. The scenario itself should be no more than one paragraph, and the verdicts must fall out of the theories rather than being stipulated by the setup.

After the scenario, do three things. First, walk through the verdicts: say how much of the deceased's "I" or person survives, and where, according to Hofstadter's pattern-based view, according to a standard psychological-continuity theorist of the broadly Lockean or Parfitian type, and according to an animalist who says we persist only where our living body goes; explain why each theory gives the verdict it does rather than the neighbouring one. Second, tell me which feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to the verdicts when the tunable parameter is pushed to each extreme, and whether the case survives if I remove that feature entirely. Third, give the strongest objection a Hofstadterian would raise to the case — I anticipate something like the complaint that a biographer's model of a stranger is inert and parasitic, a copy that never feeds back into the world as the pattern itself, so that counting it as a partial "I" cheapens the view — and say whether the case can be patched to meet it, for instance by having the survivor act on the model in ways that reshape her own life and the lives of people around her. This is for discussion material for the group, so what counts as cheating is a case that builds the desired verdicts into its description of the people involved; the description should stay neutral and let the theories sort the candidates. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose only, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the register of a long thoughtful email.
