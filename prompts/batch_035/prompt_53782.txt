I run a discussion section for an intro IR / ethics-of-war course, about 16 students, one 75-minute block, and next week is deterrence. The thing I want them to actually feel, not just nod along to, is the weird core of it: a deterrent threat only works if carrying it out would be bad for you too, so success seems to depend on being (or convincingly seeming) partly irrational, and on letting outcomes be partly out of your hands. Schelling's stuff on brinkmanship — the idea that threatening to flip a coin is credible in a way threatening to press a button deliberately isn't — is what I want them to stumble into, but I don't want to lecture it up front. Ideally they think they're just playing a weird bargaining game, and only hear the word "deterrence" in the debrief.

Design the game for me. Some seeds I had, use or trash them: pairs of students as rival states on an escalation ladder, where each rung costs both sides a little and the top rung is catastrophic for both; a chance-driven trigger so that escalation can "go off" without either player actually choosing it (each step up forces both players to draw from a deck and a bad draw detonates — so nobody presses the button, everyone just climbs toward a button that might press itself); and some mechanism for public precommitment, like a player being allowed to visibly destroy their own ability to back down. Payoffs should be something I can actually hand out — candy, extra credit tokens, whatever — not real money, and small enough that losing hurts but nobody's traumatized. Please include the exact rules, how many rounds, what the other students do while pairs are playing, and a timeline so it fits in 75 minutes.

Then the debrief: map the specific mechanics onto the specific ideas (credibility, precommitment, the tripwire, why leaving things to chance beats leaving things to choice, and the stability-instability paradox if it fits). Also tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I assume some pairs will just race to the top rung to see what happens, and I suspect others will collude and never escalate at all — and how I should handle that. Finally, be frank about which feature of real deterrence no classroom game can reproduce. I'm guessing the obvious answer is "thousands of actual deaths," but I suspect there's something more structural too, maybe about how you can never run the counterfactual where the threat worked so you never find out if you needed it — if that's a real gap in the game, say so.
