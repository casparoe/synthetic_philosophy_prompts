Our city council is debating a package of paid parental leave and subsidized childcare, and the debate has hardened into a contest between two analogies that no one is actually examining. The advocates say childcare is infrastructure: like roads and the power grid, it is the connective tissue that lets the workforce function; it has to be built, staffed, and maintained at public expense before anyone can use it; and its returns come back as growth and tax base rather than as anything visible in a single household budget. The opponents say starting a family is like buying a boat: a deeply satisfying private consumption choice that some people make and others don't, and it is no more the city's business to subsidize daycare than to subsidize marina berths.

Please begin with your bottom line — which, if either, analogy can carry the funding conclusion — in your first sentence, and then give the audit. I want four things. First, a correspondence table for each analogy: what is supposed to play the role of the road, the traffic, the depreciation, and the congestion in the one, and of the boat, the marina, and the fuel in the other. Second, the points where each mapping fails, flagged by whether the failure matters for the funding question or is merely decorative — for instance, roads are nonrival up to congestion while a childcare slot is occupied by exactly one child; infrastructure returns accrue to whoever drives on it while the returns to leave and childcare include a parent's labor-force attachment and a future taxpayer who chose none of it; and a boat purchase doesn't have a two-generation externality structure in which the person who bears the cost and the person who generates the benefit are different people. Third, what each analogy smuggles in: the infrastructure one, that spending dressed as investment escapes the scrutiny that welfare spending gets; the boat one, that the choice is fully voluntary and fully priced in, when for many families the realistic alternative isn't boat versus no boat but one parent's exit from the workforce, with earnings scarring that long outlasts the leave. Fourth, whether a third frame — social insurance, public health, primary education — tracks the actual structure better, and what it predicts for the questions that actually divide the council: means-tested versus universal provision, a short generous leave versus a long thin one, subsidizing childcare slots versus paying the parents directly.

If you think the funding conclusion stands or falls independently of both analogies, say so plainly and show the reasoning that gets there without them.
