While helping a friend move into a new flat, I watched two of the helpers — Ren, a furniture restorer, and Iris, a PhD student in linguistics — fall into an argument about when several things add up to one further thing, and I would like a considered adjudication of it. The trigger was a flat-pack wardrobe, first as loose panels on the floor, then assembled. The exchange ran as follows.

(1) Ren said that assembling it brought a new object into being: beforehand, panels and screws; afterwards, a wardrobe — the thing that stands in the corner, holds the clothes, and could be sold secondhand, none of which the panels could do. (2) Iris answered that if so, there was a moment when the wardrobe began to exist, but no assembly process supplies one: after the 27th screw it is still five things, after the 28th it is one? A line we can place wherever we like — at the last screw, at the first, when it can stand unaided — is not a line nature draws, and what we can place at will is not a fact. (3) Ren said plenty of things come into being gradually — a fog bank, a person — and that vagueness about their beginnings is not taken to show they never arrive; at most it shows the wardrobe came into existence at some imprecise stretch of the afternoon. (4) Iris replied that the analogy smuggles in what is at issue: those are cases of distinctive unification, whereas Ren's own criterion, once it covers wardrobes, covers anything juxtaposed with anything — with sufficient patience there is an object composed of the wardrobe, the wall it stands against, and the wall's plaster, and the only thing that stops the list there is precisely the interest-driven line-drawing Ren claims to be above. (5) Ren conceded there is no sharp boundary but denied that this makes composition a matter of convention: it is not our deciding that makes the assembled wardrobe able to bear load while the loose panels cannot, and that is a difference in the things themselves, not in our ways of talking about them. (6) Iris said the load-bearing is fixed by the arrangement of the panels themselves; nothing over and above them does any explanatory work, and positing it anyway multiplies entities beyond necessity, which is bad practice in any theory. (7) Ren answered that parsimony, so applied, proves too much: the same reasoning eliminates waves as just water arranged wave-wise, and the panels themselves as just fibres arranged panel-wise, until Iris's inventory is reduced to the fundamental furniture of physics — and if she is prepared to say, in her own mouth, that there are no panels, their dispute has a shape; if not, she is drawing her line exactly where she forbade Ren to draw his.

They put it to me at the end, and I could not do it justice. Please go through the seven moves in order: say which of the two is right, or nearer right, about each, and why; flag anything both of them are missing; and say who comes out ahead overall. Take the dispute at face value, as a dispute about what exists — not as a dispute about which nouns are convenient.
