You are a professional philosopher specializing in value theory and the philosophy of mind, and you have considerable experience preparing interviewers for technically demanding conversations with working philosophers. I need your help preparing for my podcast on moral psychology. Next week's guest is a defender of what he calls phenomenological hedonism. His view, as he presents it, is that all and only pleasures share a single felt hedonic tone, and that this shared phenomenal character is what unifies the category of pleasure and lets it do its normative work in hedonism about well-being. He is patient with objections but tends to win exchanges by widening the notion of felt tone until every counterexample looks assimilable, so my questions need to make that maneuver progressively harder.

Please think carefully about where the literature presses hardest on this position before you draft anything, and consider several candidate lines of attack in your reasoning; present only the finished questions. Write eight to ten interview questions for me, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. Every question must turn on the actual content of his position rather than on his biography or general outlook. At least one question must press the classic heterogeneity worry with a concrete triad he cannot easily wave away: the sweetness of tasting ripe fruit, the absorbed flow of working through a proof, and the dull relief when a headache finally lifts. Ask him directly whether he can point to anything these three episodes share other than the label. At least one question must force a choice between treating hedonic tone as a determinable like color, whose determinates resemble each other by being specifications of a common property, and treating the category of pleasure as a family-resemblance structure whose members share nothing but overlapping similarities. A good follow-up there would key to the likely reply that family resemblance suffices for ordinary classification, by asking whether it can still distinguish genuine pleasure from mere absence of discomfort, which it had better do if pleasure is to carry the weight hedonism places on it. At least one question should confront him with the attitudinal alternative on which pleasure is not a sensation at all but a favorable propositional attitude, and probe whether he can dismiss a theory that explains the data without positing any common felt quality; the follow-up should key to his likely response that attitudes without felt tone leave out what we care about when we care about pleasure.

Finally, at least one question should test an analogy I have been playing with, and I would like you to sharpen it into something usable: listeners are sometimes told that musical consonance is not a single acoustic feature shared by all consonant intervals, but a cluster of relational facts about frequency ratios, which is why consonance admits of degrees and borderline cases while still being a real and explanatory phenomenon. Ask him whether hedonic tone could be like that, relational rather than intrinsic, and then press with a follow-up keyed to the reply I expect, namely that he accepts the analogy, since if the unity of pleasure is relational then pleasure is no longer the simple, homogeneous foundation that hedonism about well-being needs it to be.

Please deliver the questions and follow-ups as continuous prose only, with no headings, numbered lists, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the manner of a well-composed email.
