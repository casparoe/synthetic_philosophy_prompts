So here's my situation: I'm giving a talk to a graduate seminar in political theory — the kind of department where Arendt, Schmitt, and Berlin get name-dropped in the first five minutes of any discussion. My thesis is basically an updated version of Carnap's views on politics and planning. The idea, roughly: a lot of what passes for deep moral disagreement in politics is actually friction between different conceptual frameworks, and once you see it that way, the rational move is Carnap's principle of tolerance applied to political life — stop arguing about whether The State or The Common Good "really exists," state your ends explicitly, and treat everything downstream of that as an engineering problem to be solved with social science and planning. Carnap himself was a democratic socialist, lived in Red Vienna in the late 20s/early 30s around Otto Neurath (who thought a socialist economy should be run the way an engineer runs a plant), and kept making notes toward a never-written book on what he'd sometimes call rational politics. His line of thinking from the Logical Syntax period — that a framework is an instrument to be judged by efficiency relative to purposes we bring to it, not by truth, and that in science there are no morals, only techniques — is what I want to extend from mathematics and physics language to the language of political life. Worth adding so you understand my angle: I'm NOT trying to smuggle in technocracy or say experts should just decide things; I think the tolerance point actually supports democratic openness, because it tells people they don't have to win a metaphysics argument to have their ends counted.

The audience: about fifteen political theorists. A mix of Arendtians who think the whole "politics as fabrication" picture destroys the space of public action and plurality, some Berlin-style value pluralists, a couple of analytical Marxists who are honestly sympathetic to planning but will bristle at the fact/value flattening, and one older Straussian who thinks value-free social science is the road to nowhere. Zero Carnap scholars, zero logicians — so all the technical material has to be carried by me, which also means any objection that starts "but that's not what Carnap meant" won't come from expertise, only from vibe.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight-ish objections these people will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they'd do to my case, each phrased in their vocabulary (the Arendtian one, for instance, will talk about action vs. fabrication and the plurality of human beings, not about "language frameworks"). For each objection give me a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Also: what should I concede up front so I don't come across as naive about politics, and what's the one question I'm secretly hoping nobody asks?
