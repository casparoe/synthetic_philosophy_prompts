Before I retired from a career in experimental condensed-matter physics, I grew accustomed to the idea that a good theoretical description is often a matter of choosing the right level of abstraction: the same block of material admits a quantum-mechanical, a statistical-mechanical, and a purely phenomenological treatment, and part of the craft is knowing which one answers the question at hand. Since retiring I have been reading my way into philosophy of mind, and I keep returning to Daniel Dennett's intentional stance, which strikes me as exactly this sort of move: treat a system as an agent with beliefs and desires precisely when doing so buys you predictive leverage you could not otherwise get. Lately, however, I have been spending time with large language models, and I have encountered a competing picture that seems to sit oddly with Dennett's. On this view, which circulates among researchers concerned with machine learning interpretability and safety, an LLM should not be described as an agent at all, but rather as a kind of simulator or ensemble: the underlying model is a vast repertoire of personas, prompts select and blend among them, and any apparent beliefs or goals belong to the personas being run rather than to the model itself.

What I would like is a careful comparison of these two frameworks. Dennett's stance, as I understand it, is deliberately permissive: any system whose behavior is reliably and voluminously predictable by attributing beliefs and desires thereby has them, real pattern or no. The simulator view appears to deny precisely this permissiveness, insisting on a distinction between the attributive pattern and the underlying engine — the pattern of the persona is real, perhaps, but it is evanescent, reshaped wholesale by the next prompt, and so it fails to add up to a persisting subject of belief in the way intentional-stance attribution ordinarily presupposes. So my questions are these: Is the simulator view merely the intentional stance applied reflexively (one stance, taken toward the shifting cast of characters rather than toward the model), or do the two frameworks issue genuinely conflicting verdicts? In particular, does Dennett's sufficiency claim — robust predictability under intentional interpretation entails genuine belief attribution — hold up against a system where the attributed beliefs are not stable across contexts in the way ordinary agents' beliefs are, or does Dennett have the resources to say that only the persona, and not the model, is a candidate for the stance? And finally, if two stances both "work" prediction-wise but attribute beliefs to different subjects, what principled criterion, if any, breaks the tie?
