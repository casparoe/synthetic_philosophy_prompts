Something I've been chewing on since a family member's hospital stay. Pain is basically the one medical condition where the patient's own report is the gold standard. If you say your pain is an 8 out of 10, that number goes in the chart, drives the analgesia, decides the disability paperwork — nobody cross-checks it against a blood test or a scan. Nursing education even likes to say pain just is whatever the patient says it is, existing whenever they say it does. But at the same time, clinicians obviously treat the report as caused by the pain, and as evidence for it — they talk about patients under-reporting, or being stoic about it, which only makes sense if there's a real felt intensity that the report can track more or less accurately.

So the practice seems to need two things at once: (1) a causal path from the felt pain to the spoken judgment about it, and (2) the felt thing being an independent reality the report can be measured against. And here's whats bothering me. One respectable view of consciousness — epiphenomenalism — holds that phenomenal states like the awfulness of felt pain don't cause anything physical: the physical world is causally closed and the feeling just rides along on top. If that's true, then my utterance "my pain is an 8" is produced by brain processes that would have produced the exact same utterance whatever the pain felt like, or even if there were no felt pain at all. In that case the report can't be tracking the feeling, since nothing about the feeling ever reaches the report. That looks like it guts the whole practice.

So tell me: what theory of the relation between phenomenal states and the judgments we make about them does clinical pain assessment actually presuppose? Is that presupposition internally consistent — can you coherently treat the report as both the measure of pain and evidence for it, or do those roles pull apart? Which parts of the practice need the causal link, and which would survive without it (say, if pain were simply defined as whatever disposes you to report pain)? And if epiphenomenalism were true, what exactly breaks — the clinical usefulness of the reports, our knowledge that patients feel anything at all, or just the story we tell about why the reports mean something? I suspect the practice is compatible with more theories of mind than it looks, so I'd like to hear which ones it genuinely rules out and which it doesn't.
