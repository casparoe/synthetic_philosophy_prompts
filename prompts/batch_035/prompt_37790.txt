My reading group — we're a mixed bunch, an economist, a physicist, and two philosophy grad students — has spent three sessions on an argument our economist defends, and I can no longer tell whether it's a good argument with an unbearable conclusion or a bad one whose flaw I keep missing. The topic is our individual responsibility for diffuse harms: greenhouse gases, factory-farmed meat, that sort of thing. Her argument runs as follows.

(1) An act can be wrong in virtue of its consequences only if it makes a difference to what happens: if the world would contain exactly the same harms whether or not I perform it, then those harms cannot be what makes my act wrong. (2) In large-scale cases, harms are produced by very many individually similar contributions, and the harm materializes at thresholds — a heat wave requires so many tonnes of carbon, a certain number of animals is raised and slaughtered to satisfy a standing demand, and so on. (3) Given the numbers, any single contribution of mine almost certainly either falls short of triggering a marginal unit of harm, or is one without which the unit of harm would have been triggered anyway; and she adds that since rational decisions under uncertainty should track expected effects, and my expected effect is minuscule, (1) is satisfied not just nearly but for all practical purposes. (4) So no individual contribution to a diffuse harm is wrong in virtue of its consequences. (5) She then sharpens this with cases she takes to be reductios of any resistance: a firing squad of ten marksmen, any one of whose shots the victim would have survived; ten thugs who each deliver one of ten blows, any nine of which the victim would have survived; and she insists that anyone who denies (4) for my steak must deny it for the tenth marksman too, since by hypothesis his shot made no difference. (6) Finally, she notes that if physics is deterministic, then nothing is counterfactually dependent on anything anyone does, so on (1) nothing at all is ever wrong in virtue of consequences — and she says she is content with that, treating it not as a reductio but as a welcome austerity.

I want to say the argument fails somewhere, but every location I try looks expensive. The step from "almost certainly makes no difference" to "makes no difference" (between 3 and 4) smells like a modal slide, but I can't pin down whether it is equivocation or something subtler, and I notice the firing squad and the steak are supposed to force a choice between the two. The jump to (6) strikes me as trading on some ambiguity in "makes a difference," but she answers that the marksman case already shows we should accept (6)'s spirit, just restricted to overdetermination. For my part, I would very much like to keep something in the neighborhood of (1) — I'm drawn to the thought that what is wrong with me must be grounded in what *I* do, not in what gets done anyway — but I cannot live with acquitting the tenth marksman, and I suspect (5) is where the argument quietly does its real work without ever being stated as a premise. Please tell me which premise or inference to give up, why it looked so hard to give up, and what each of the main escape routes costs. In particular, I'd like to know whether the two cases she treats as twins — the marksman and the steak — really do have to be answered in the same breath, or whether there's a principled way to split them that she has foreclosed by construction.
