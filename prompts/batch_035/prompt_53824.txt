Over the past few years I've been trying to work out, on my own and without any philosophical training, what I actually mean when I call someone free. I mostly test my views by imagining cases, and I've now accumulated a set of convictions that strike me individually as obvious but that I can no longer tell whether they add up to one view or several. Something in the list feels like it's pulling in two directions, but whenever I try to pin down where, it slips away from me. I'd like a proper audit. Here is where I stand:

1. At bottom, being free means nobody is stopping you or forcing you. If no one is blocking your path or holding a threat over your head, you are free with respect to whatever it is you want to do.

2. Yet I'm just as sure that a person can be unfree at a moment when nobody is doing anything to her. A woman whose husband reads her messages and could make her life hell if she stepped out of line is not free while he is asleep, or while he happens to be in a good mood. The same goes for an employee who knows his boss could ruin him on a whim and so never says what he actually thinks.

3. I also believe freedom has an inside dimension. Someone in the grip of an addiction, or a person raised in a closed group that chose her beliefs for her, is not free even though there is no coercive hand on her — the pressure has moved inside her and does the work the guards would otherwise do.

4. At the same time, I want to say that a person who sincerely wants the life she ended up in is free, even if that life was arranged for her. If someone was pushed into a career or a marriage by her family and, years on, genuinely loves it and would choose it again, I can't bring myself to call her unfree. Freedom has to be judged from inside her own life, not by whether her desires have a history she would have approved of.

5. On the political side, I'm confident that state mandates reduce freedom even when they are good for us. Seatbelt laws and taxes on sugary drinks make people less free, and I'm content to say so while also saying the trade is worth it.

6. Finally, I don't think having more options is the same as being more free. A desperate person offered a choice between two miserable jobs has more options than a person offered none, but I would not call that choice free in any sense worth caring about.

Does this package hang together? Please go through it and tell me which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension and which only look like they are; for each genuine tension, rank it by severity and say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere on the list; and for anything that dissolves on closer inspection, show me how it dissolves. At the end, I'd like your judgment on which single revision would clean up the most. I have no attachment to any particular item on the list — my worry is only that I've been treating as one settled position what may really be several positions wearing the same word.
