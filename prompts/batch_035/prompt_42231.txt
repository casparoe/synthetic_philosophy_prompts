I'm co-running a graduate reading group this term on the metaphysics of propositions, and our next session is on Bradley's regress and the unity problem. For that session I'd like a one-page decision-tree handout that sorts each participant into a position on the unity question — not by asking which label they like, but by how they react to cases. The tree should begin from a starting commitment everyone in the room accepts, something like: some sentences with multiple terms are true, and that truth calls for some account of how their ingredients constitute a single whole. From there it should branch on diagnostic questions phrased so that someone can answer them without knowing where the branch leads.

The branch questions I'd like built in, roughly in this order: first, whether truth requires any entity over and above the sentence's constituents (objects, properties, the relation itself); second, for those who say no, whether that's because the whole truthmaker picture is confused or merely because the constituents, arranged in the right way, suffice on their own; third, for those who admit a unifier, whether it is itself a further constituent needing unification — the regress's second step — or something that applies to the whole without being one of its relata; fourth, whether relations are themselves ordered, so that the order of relata is internal to the relation, or whether order must be imposed from outside by something further; and fifth, whether the unity of judgment and propositional-attitude reports is a separate problem from the unity of states of affairs in the world, or the same problem encountered twice. The leaves should include Armstrong-style states-of-affairs realism, the multiple relation theory of judgment, deflationary views on which the regress is generated only by a bad picture of instantiation as an ordinary relation, positionalism and views on which relations carry their order internally, and the view that the unity problem dissolves once we stop positing facts or propositions over and above sentences and their uses — plus whatever else falls out of the logical space you build. For each leaf, add one sentence on the commitment that makes it hardest to hold, and flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate.

Two further constraints. Keep it usable as an actual handout: at most seven branch questions, leaves compact enough to fit a page. And please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names — settle the questions first, see which combinations of answers they allow, and then locate the named positions inside that space. If some combination of branch answers corresponds to no held position, note it and say whether you think that is because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; and if two familiar positions get forced onto the same leaf by your questions, say so explicitly rather than splitting them by fiat.
