So here's my situation: I'm a journalist who covers science and medicine for a general readership, and I've been put on a piece about why doctors, insurers, and patient groups keep fighting over what gets to be called a disease. To write it honestly I figured I should first figure out whether *I* have a consistent view, so I've been writing down my gut verdicts on cases, mostly to see if I actually believe something or just have vibes. Where I land: I think ADHD is a genuine disorder even though half the definition is "can't function in a school setting," which on its face sounds like a norm complaint rather than a medicine one. I don't think grief after a death is a mental disorder, even when it's still crushing two years later and looks clinically identical to depression — I was relieved when the DSM-5 people got talked out of medicalizing ordinary bereavement. But I'm uneasy about that one, because if the same sadness showed up with no loss at all I'd probably call it depression, and I can't quite say why the trigger should matter. Tooth decay is obviously a disease; I balk at calling aging one, even though it's a progressive breakdown of essentially every system and some serious people argue it should count. Nearsightedness counts as a disease even though glasses fix it in thirty seconds, so it can't be about whether the impairment goes untreated. Male-pattern baldness I flip on weekly. I don't think "gaming disorder" is real no matter what the ICD says, but typing that made me queasy, since it's exactly what people said about ADHD in the nineties, and I think they were wrong then. And I'm confident homosexuality was never a disease despite sitting in the DSM until 1973 — but my reason can't be "we now find it acceptable," because plenty of things people now accept and accommodate (say, being so terrified of public speaking you arrange your whole career around it) still seem like disorders to me.

So the question I'm chewing on: is there one principle that gets all or most of these verdicts? I have a half-formed candidate of my own — a disease is a part of you failing to do its biological job, AND this failing harms you — but I genuinely can't tell whether the harm clause is doing real work or just laundering whatever my culture already approves of (which would sink my gaming-disorder verdict and my baldness waffling at the same time). Please tell me: (a) what principle, if any, captures the pattern, stated precisely enough that I could apply it in an editorial meeting; (b) which of my verdicts a clean principle would have to overturn, and which one I should let go of first; (c) what the principle says about cases I haven't listed — try tinnitus, or short stature in a country where nearly everyone is tall, or premenstrual dysphoric disorder, since those are the ones my editor keeps asking about; and (d) be honest with me if the answer turns out to be "no, your verdicts are majority opinion plus a story," because if that's true I'd honestly rather know before the piece goes to print. This got heated at a work happy hour last week so consider yourself warned about how much people care about this stuff.
