I'm in a grad seminar on formal epistemology (first year, so be gentle but don't dumb it down) and our final assignment is to write up our own actual position on dutch book arguments and defend it. Before I commit anything to paper I want to stress-test the package, because my TA took one look at my draft outline and said "there's at least one inconsistency in there, probably more" and then refused to elaborate, which was very unhelpful. So here is roughly where I've landed. (1) I think the dutch book argument is basically sound and is the real justification for probabilism: if your credences violate the probability axioms, a bookie can construct a set of bets, each of which you'd accept by your own lights, that guarantees you a net loss, and that's what's wrong with non-probabilistic credences. (2) That said, I'm sympathetic to the complaint that the vulnerability shown is pragmatic, not epistemic — being exploitable is about losing money, it doesn't by itself mean your beliefs are false or that you're believing badly, it's a fact about your betting behavior. (3) In the Ellsberg urn cases I share the intuition behind ambiguity aversion: if one urn is known to be 50/50 red and black and the other has an unknown split, I'd pay a premium to bet on the known one, and I'm inclined to say that preference is rational rather than a bias to be explained away. (4) I also accept the diachronic version: a policy of updating by anything other than conditionalization leaves you open to a dutch book spread across time, so conditionalization is mandatory, not just a nice default. (5) Smaller one, but I want it on the record: I think it's fine for an agent to just refuse bets offered at exactly fair odds — you shouldn't be compelled to take either a bet or its complement when the whole thing is a wash in expectation.

Part of why I want the audit is that a friend in the seminar told me that (3) alone sinks the whole paper, which seems too fast to me — plenty of serious people are ambiguity-averse, and I don't see why I have to give it up just because I like dutch book arguments. And my own suspicion is that (2) is the harmless one, the sort of thing everyone mutters about pragmatics vs epistemology without it changing anything downstream, but I might be wrong about that too.
