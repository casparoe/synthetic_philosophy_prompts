Bit of an odd request maybe, but here goes. I coordinate a small working group on practical ethics — about eight people, two years of modest funding, mix of philosophers, one cognitive scientist, and one person who builds conversational AI systems. We've decided to look at the epistemology of moral testimony, i.e. when taking someone else's word on a moral matter is legitimate and when it's somehow defective. The classic puzzle, roughly, is that testimony about ordinary facts (how many moons Jupiter has, whether the shop is open) seems perfectly fine to accept on trust, but with moral claims many people feel you shouldn't just defer — you ought to grasp the reason yourself, otherwise you don't really have the moral view at all. Meanwhile moral advice looks structurally like ordinary testimony in most other respects, which is what makes the whole area interesting rather than just a curio.

Our concern is less the pure puzzle and more which open questions actually matter downstream, especially now that a lot of people recieve most of their moral guidance from influencers with large audiences and no credentials, and increasingly from chatbots. Some candidate questions we keep circling: (a) does reliable moral testimony require the testifier to have lived experience of the situation in question, or is good reasoning enough — the person affected by the decision versus the clever outsider; (b) is there a principled difference between one-off deference (asking a mate whether it's okay to attend a wedding you disapprove of) and habituated deference, where you effectively outsource a whole region of your moral life and stop forming your own judgements there; (c) is moral testimony from a system with no first-personal standpoint at all — no desires, nothing at stake, nothing it's like to be it — a genuinely new kind of case, or just a limiting case of testimony from a complete stranger; (d) when trusted moral advisors disagree, should that mainly update your trust in them or your first-order moral view.

What I'd like from you is a defended agenda rather than a survey. Rank those four (plus anything you think we've wrongly left off — I'm half convinced (c) is overrated, honestly, but argue with me) by which most deserves our limited attention. For each, say something about why it sits where it does: which ones are upstream of the others, which are tractable with armchair methods versus needing the empirical people, which would change actual behaviour out in the world if they got settled, and what developments over the next couple of years would move an item up or down your list. A reading list is no use to me, I should say — I want the reasoning, since I have to defend the final ordering to the funder.
