A colleague and I have been arguing about the problem of old evidence for over a year, and we have finally agreed to produce the document we should have produced months ago: a joint statement from our two camps. I am closest to the orthodox Bayesian position: credences are the fundamental quantities, rational learning is conditionalization, and the old-evidence problem is a technical inconvenience rather than a refutation — when a long-known fact seems to confirm a brand-new theory (Mercury's perihelion and general relativity being the canonical case), what has really changed is the agent's explicit stock of logical or explanatory information, and the confirmation should be reconstructed counterfactually, as the credence we would have assigned had we not already known the fact. My colleague holds instead that confirmation is an explanatory relation between evidence and theory, not a fact about anyone's credences at any time; the Bayesian reconstruction, he says, gets the direction of analysis backwards, since support is why our degrees of belief should move rather than being defined by how they would have moved. In the course of arguing, we have come to suspect our real disagreement is this: I believe every apparent case of old evidence can be redescribed as the acquisition of new information — a relevance noticed, an entailment finally recognized — while he believes there are cases in which nothing new is learned and yet confirmation still genuinely occurs.

Draft the joint statement we could both sign. It must include at least two claims we both accept, among them a shared taxonomy that distinguishes evidence whose relevance was possessed but unnoticed from evidence that only the new theory itself makes relevant; the precise residual disagreement, phrased so that each of us would accept the wording given for our own side; and at least one crux — a concrete case both of us agree would bear on the dispute — with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Format it as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
