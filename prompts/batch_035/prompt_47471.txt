I've become convinced that generations are real kinds, but of a peculiar sort: not natural kinds like gold or electron, and not just statistical groupings like "people born in an even-numbered year," but historical kinds -- things more like a river, a corporation, or a biological species on the species-as-individuals view, individuated by causal and genealogical continuity rather than by a shared essence. On my view, a generation exists when a cohort passes through a shared formative window (say, ages 10 to 25) during which some sustained social rupture or technological shift leaves a population-level imprint on values, tastes, and assumptions that neither earlier nor later cohorts acquire, and which then persists through their lives as a partially self-maintaining structure -- through shared cultural reference points, endogamous marriage patterns, and institutions keyed to cohort. On this picture "Millennial" is closer to a proper name for a historical entity than to a predicate with an extension defined by necessary and sufficient conditions, and the fuzzy boundaries around who counts are exactly what we should expect of a causal-historical individual rather than a defect in the concept.

Tell me what would count against this view. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and drawn from more than one direction: empirical findings (say, from cohort-analysis methods in sociology or epidemiology) that formative-period imprinting is too weak, too slow to decay, or too heterogeneous within a cohort to support a real entity at all; philosophical arguments that historical kinds of this sort collapse into conventional or purely nominal groupings once you press the analogy to species-as-individuals; cases where the genealogical cohort and the sociological generation come apart badly (immigrants, the class-stratified timing of adoption of new technologies, the fact that "Greatest Generation" style labels get retrofitted decades later by commentators with an interest in the story); and the possibility that what I'm calling a historical individual is really just a statistical artifact of age effects plus period effects, which would make cohort terms convenience labels rather than kind terms. For each defeater, say whether a defender of my view could concede it and survive, what modification that would force, and at what point the view has been qualified into something no longer worth calling realism about generations. I'd also like an honest verdict on whether my view is insulated: it makes no sharp predictions about boundary cases, and I want to know whether that vagueness is a principled feature of historical individuals, as I suspect, or a sign that nothing is being risked.
