I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm preparing a feature on the ethics of interrogation after spending several weeks reading the post-9/11 debates. Somewhere in that pile I came across a long-form essay, in a magazine I won't name since my question concerns the argument rather than the author, that sets up the familiar ticking time bomb scenario: authorities hold someone they are certain knows where a bomb is hidden and when it will go off, thousands will die when it does, and the information can be extracted only through torture. The essay then sums up the philosophical debate for lay readers roughly as follows. Once you accept that torture is wrong only because of the suffering it inflicts, the case for an absolute ban collapses, since if suffering is bad then letting a city be destroyed is far worse, and any consistent utilitarian must support torture in the bomb case. Philosophers who nevertheless defend an absolute prohibition, the essay continues, are leaning on a legal technicality rather than an argument, and even they concede that the bomb case is an exception — so the real dispute is merely about where to draw the line, not whether a line exists. The scenario, the essay concludes, merely makes explicit what is already universal practice: every state that has faced a genuine emergency has abandoned the ban, which is why the prohibition has survived only in places that have never been tested.

Something about this rings hollow to me, particularly the slide from a contrived case, stipulated with certainty about the detainee's guilt, knowledge, and timing, to claims about how actual interrogation works and what states actually do. But I lack the philosophical training to say precisely where the essay cuts corners, and since the framing I choose will likely shape how my readers think about the real-world policy question, I would rather get it right than merely punchy.
