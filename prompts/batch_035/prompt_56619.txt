I'm a high school senior taking an elective on probability and decision-making, and last week our teacher had us bet on urns to expose our own preferences to ourselves. I've been unable to figure out what principle, if any, actually lies behind my choices, and I'd like help doing it properly. Here are my honest verdicts on the cases we ran. (1) Urn A contains exactly 50 red and 50 black balls; Urn B contains 100 balls, red and black in proportions nobody knows. One ball is drawn at random, and if it's red I win $100. I'd rather bet on Urn A, and I'd even pay a couple of dollars to swap a forced bet on B for a bet on A. (2) Same two urns, but now the prize is on black instead of red: I still prefer Urn A. My teacher pointed out that holding both preferences at once seems to commit me to thinking Urn B has fewer than 100 balls in it, which is obviously false, and that worries me — though I confess I would make both choices again. (3) A third urn holds exactly 30 red balls plus 60 balls that are black and yellow in unknown proportion. Given a choice between betting on red or betting on black, I take red; given a choice between red-or-yellow and black-or-yellow, I take black-or-yellow. (4) Yet when my friend flips a fair coin, looks at it herself, and hides it under a cup, offering me $100 if I correctly call which side is up, I will play at even odds without any hesitation — even though I am, if anything, more ignorant there than about any of the urns. (5) And I'm not a generally timid gambler: I'd cheerfully pay $48 to take a fair fifty-fifty coin flip between $0 and $100, so plain risk aversion can't be the explanation either.

My own attempt at a principle was: bet when you know the odds, demand a discount when you don't. But that seems to say I should demand a discount on the hidden coin too, which I don't, so it must be wrong or at least incomplete. Is there a single principle that captures all five verdicts — perhaps something about the difference between a random process whose odds nobody can state and one whose outcome is fixed but merely hidden, or about who or what was responsible for the unknown? Please state the principle precisely, tell me what it predicts for two cases I haven't listed — a mystery urn that a machine filled with some random mix of red and black that no human could ever inspect, versus an otherwise identical urn into which one hundred different people each dropped a ball of their own choosing — and be honest about which of my five verdicts the principle cannot save, and whether the ones it keeps are worth the price of giving up the others.
