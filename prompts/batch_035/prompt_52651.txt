My brother, who works in finance and has recently gotten into economics podcasts, came back from a walk full of conviction and laid the following on me, more or less verbatim:

"Voting is irrational, and economists proved it decades ago. Back in the 1950s Anthony Downs did the math: in a large election, the chance that your one vote decides the outcome is about one in a hundred million — smaller than your chance of dying in a car crash on the way to the polling station. So the expected payoff of voting is essentially zero no matter how much you care about which side wins, while the cost — time, gas, standing in line — is small but real. Staying home is what a rational person does, and the fact that hundreds of millions of people vote anyway just shows that most people can't do expected-value reasoning. Political scientists call this the paradox of voting, and the honest conclusion is that democracy depends on irrationality."

I'm inclined to agree with him — I've skipped elections for exactly this reason — but his confidence bothers me, partly because I've also heard people argue that voting is a moral duty precisely because elections sometimes do come down to a handful of ballots. Please go through his speech and give me an accuracy audit: what would a political scientist or decision theorist accept, what would they wince at, and what is just wrong. I'd especially like you to address three things. First, is "your vote almost certainly won't be decisive" the same claim as "voting is irrational," or does something get smuggled in between the two — for instance, what counts as the benefit side of the expected-value calculation, and whether voters are assumed to be weighing only their own stakes? Second, is the one-in-a-hundred-million figure the kind of thing that holds for every voter in every election, or does it depend on assumptions his summary doesn't mention? And third, does his closing line — that democracy depends on irrationality — actually follow from the parts of his summary that survive correction, or does it depend on the parts that don't? Please be fair to him where he's right; I don't want to dismiss a good argument just because it's annoyingly delivered.
