I'd like a credence, with the derivation made explicit, for the following proposition: the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents — the difficulty that an agent reasoning within a sound formal system cannot, by Löb's theorem, come to trust a successor that reasons in the same system, because trust in the successor's soundness would require proving its own — will eventually be dissolved by a conceptual advance, rather than merely bypassed by engineering choices (for instance, by agents settling for probabilistic or heuristic trust, or by ensuring that successive systems stand in no formal relationship to one another, so that the theorem never gets a grip).

My own credence in "dissolved by conceptual advance" is roughly 0.25. Please give me yours, but more importantly decompose it. I'd suggest the following terms, though you are free to restructure: (1) the probability that the obstacle applies to realistic agents at all, as opposed to being an artifact of idealized agents that reason exclusively by formal proof; (2) conditional on its applying, the probability that the standard workarounds — probabilistic trust, parametric polymorphism, handing each successor a strictly stronger proof system and accepting an ever-lengthening chain of trust — merely postpone a Löb-like fixed point rather than escape it; (3) the probability that the distinction I'm drawing is itself unstable, i.e., that a sufficiently principled bypass would just amount to the dissolution; and (4) whatever residual you want to assign to the question being ill-posed as I've framed it.

For each term, give a number and a one-line justification, then show how they combine into your final figure. After that, please tell me which term you are least confident in, and what argument or technical result would move it the most. Two things I'm particularly curious about: first, whether a proof that a probabilistic analogue of Löb's theorem holds even for resource-bounded agents would substantially lower your figure for term (2); second, whether the observation that ordinary humans trust their future selves without proving anything about themselves — and without any visible analogue of the theorem blocking them — should raise or lower your estimate for term (1), or whether you think the human case is simply disanalogous in a way that makes the comparison uninformative. Finally, if your overall number differs from mine by more than about 0.15, indicate which of the terms you think most likely accounts for the disagreement.
