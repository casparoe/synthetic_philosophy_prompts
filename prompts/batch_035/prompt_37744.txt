A colleague of mine has firm and, as far as I can tell, consistent opinions on when a secessionist bid is legitimate, and I would like to anticipate her verdict on a case we will be discussing at our next seminar. Her record so far: she accepted Scotland's 2014 referendum as fully legitimate and would have treated even a narrow Yes vote as settling the matter; she regards the Clarity Act's demands for a clear question and a clear majority as a reasonable standard rather than a veto on Quebec; she holds that Northern Ireland would have the right to leave the United Kingdom if a majority there voted for it; she dismisses Crimea's 2014 referendum as illegitimate without qualification; and she accepts Kosovo's 2008 declaration of independence as legitimate despite the complete absence of any referendum. Two rules seem to fit this record: either legitimacy requires the parent state's consent or a lawful constitutional route, or a genuinely free referendum with a clear question and a decisive majority suffices by itself — though each sits awkwardly with Kosovo, and a strictly remedial rule, under which only oppression can ground secession, sits awkwardly with Scotland. The new case: a wealthy autonomous region of a consolidated democracy, with no history of independent statehood and no oppression beyond the central state's refusal to permit a referendum and its prosecution of the referendum's organizers. The region goes ahead regardless; after a unionist boycott, turnout is 71%, Yes wins 68% of votes cast, and independent monitors describe the vote itself as free even though it is legally void. What will she say? Please set out the rules consistent with her record, what each of them predicts here, your best guess together with the reasons for preferring it, and a further case that would best tell those rules apart.
