Our philosophy reading group spent last week on Frankfurt cases, and two members, Selin and Jonas, got into a disagreement about one of them that I promised to referee and then found I couldn't. The case was a variant Jonas constructed from Frankfurt's classic 1969 setup. In Jonas's version, Jones is deliberating (in the loose, ordinary sense) between two job offers and will accept the one from the mayor's office. A device in the room monitors Jones's brain, but its trigger condition is unusual: it fires — and would then manipulate Jones into accepting the mayor's offer — if and only if Jones begins to actively deliberate about the second offer. The trigger is keyed to the onset of exploratory deliberation, not to any leaning either way. As it happens, Jones never once considers the second offer; he accepts the mayor's offer immediately and wholeheartedly, and the device never activates. It's worth stressing that the device reacts to the onset of deliberation in real time rather than predicting anything in advance.

Their exchange went like this. (1) Jonas claimed the case cleanly refutes the principle of alternative possibilities: Jones couldn't have done otherwise — had any deliberation about the alternative begun, the device would have suppressed it before it went anywhere — yet Jones is obviously responsible for accepting the offer, so moral responsibility doesn't require alternative possibilities. (2) Selin objected that the case removes alternatives only by holding fixed something within the choice episode itself: either Jones's never beginning to deliberate about the second offer was already settled before the choice, in which case it's that settling, not Jones's responsibility, that is doing the interesting work and the compatibilist shouldn't celebrate it; or it wasn't settled, and then Jones had an alternative after all — deliberating or not deliberating — located slightly before the choice. (3) Jonas replied that this proves too much and too little: it would apply to any Frankfurt-style case ever constructed, and it shows only that some tiny residual alternative remains, not one that matters — nobody thinks responsibility hinges on whether an agent can wiggle an epistemic pinkie just before deciding. (4) Selin conceded that the wiggle, as such, is trivial, but said that this concession is the whole point: "could have done otherwise" in the principle is ambiguous between a counterfactual reading (there is a possible world, holding the past and laws fixed, in which he does otherwise) and a capacity reading (he had it in him, on his own, to do otherwise). The device's shadow defeats only the counterfactual reading, because in the nearest worlds where Jones does otherwise the device fires first; but the capacity reading survives, since what matters for responsibility is whether Jones himself had the wherewithal, and a standing threat removes the opportunity to exercise a capacity without removing the capacity. (5) Jonas's last word was that a capacity reading this weak is no longer a principle of alternative *possibilities* at all: a possibility you could never have actualized isn't a possibility in any sense the principle's defenders originally cared about, and the episode is best read not as refuting the principle but as revealing that "alternative possibilities" was never a stable notion, so the parties to the free will debate must simply choose between its readings.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: for each of the five steps, say who is right, or right in part, and why. Then address two further questions. First, is Jonas's step (5) reframing — that the principle is ambiguous and the case forces a choice between readings — a genuine resolution, or a way of changing the subject? Second, is there anything about the trigger condition itself, being set to the onset of deliberation rather than to a leaning toward one option, that affects the case in ways neither of them noticed? Both of them treated the trigger as a mere technicality, but I have a suspicion it matters.
