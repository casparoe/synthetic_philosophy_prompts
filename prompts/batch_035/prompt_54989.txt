Write a satirical internal memo, maybe 600ish words, from the "Historical Analogy Compliance Office" of a large AI lab. The memo instructs employees on which of the three approved historical analogies — nuclear weapons, the printing press, the Industrial Revolution — they are required to deploy in any given op-ed, whitepaper, all-hands talk, or congressional testimony, and includes a short decision tree for choosing between them.

The humor has to come from real features of the analogy debate, not invented ones, so that someone who's actually read this literature laughs instead of winces:

- each analogy should come with its official "talking strengths" and its known failure modes (nuclear gets you arms-race framing, verification regimes, and a hard capability ceiling, but fission was discovered rather than chosen and the tech was securable in a way software isn't; the printing press gets you inevitable diffusion and massive downstream epistemic upheaval, but nobody controlled it partly because nobody much tried, and the content/hardware distinction does weird work there; the Industrial Revolution gets you gradualism and labor-market adjustment over generations, which is exactly what people dispute when timelines are short)
- the memo should quietly reveal that the analogy selection smuggles in the policy conclusion — pick nuclear and you've basically already endorsed export controls and treaty-building, pick the press and you've already conceded containment is hopeless, pick the Industrial Rev and you've argued for slow institutional adaptation — and the decision tree should route the analyst to whichever analogy matches the conclusion they already had, while insisting it's doing the opposite
- at least one clause should acknowledge the reference-class problem (why these three? why not fire, writing, electrification, the internet, language itself, or the Columbian Exchange?) and then dismiss it in a footnote, and the dismissal should be the joke
- one numbered rule should ban "mixed analogies" (e.g. arguing AI is like nuclear weapons geopolitically but like the printing press domestically) while another should mandate them whenever a journalist is present

Format: memo header, numbered rules, the decision tree (it can be genuinely usable, which makes it funnier), maybe a closing note about the office being staffed by one person who also handles the lab's other historical comparisons. Invented names for the compliance officer and lab departments are fine; don't name any real company or living person. Keep it deadpan throughout — no winking at the reader, the memo is 100% sincere about its own importance. And end with something that quietly lands the deeper point, that these analogies are doing rhetorical work rather than predictive work, without ever saying that out loud.
