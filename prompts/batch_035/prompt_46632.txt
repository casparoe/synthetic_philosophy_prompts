I'm writing a paper on truthlikeness and I keep bouncing between the Popperian camp and the possible-worlds people without ever writing down what I actually want from an account. So build me the spec first: what does any adequate theory of verisimilitude — closeness to the truth — have to deliver? Some things I keep assuming are requirements, off the top of my head: it has to say a child who answers 5 to a division problem whose answer is 4 got closer than one who answered a million, though both are flat wrong; a tautology should come out less truthlike than a false but risky theory, so it can't just collapse into probability; adding a true consequence should never lower a theory's score, adding a false one should never raise it; and false theories can't all tie, which I gather is where Popper's original measure went wrong. But half of that list is probably camp prejudice dressed up as necessity, and your job is to sort it — which of these are genuine desiderata and which are one side's taste, and whether the survivors are jointly satisfiable at all. I have a vague memory that language-dependence results bite here, that you can reorder theories by re-describing them in a different vocabulary, but I don't trust my memory of it, so work out for yourself whether some subset of the desiderata just can't be had together — that's the part I most want. One more thing I can't decide on: should the target be the whole truth about everything, or truth relative to a question or contrast class? That distinction keeps quietly changing every example I try, and I can't tell if it's real or me confusing myself. And if you think the whole enterprise presupposes that "the truth" is a well-defined target and that's confused, say so plainly instead of dutifully building my list. At the end give me one short paragraph on how Popper's own content measure and a similarity-over-possible-worlds approach do against whatever list survives, but keep that brief — the criteria are what I want, not the scorecard.
