I'm a graduate student straddling philosophy and economics, and our seminar on the epistemology of markets requires each of us to circulate a statement of commitments in advance so the group can audit it before we defend it in person. Mine have been through a few drafts and I've ended up with the following package, which I'd like checked for coherence before I submit it. (1) I endorse the common prior assumption: any divergence in prior probabilities between rational agents must ultimately trace back to some difference in their information, so that where there is no such difference, the priors should coincide. (2) I accept Aumann's result that agents with common priors and common knowledge of each other's rationality cannot agree to disagree about their posteriors. (3) I accept the Milgrom–Stokey no-trade theorem: under those same assumptions, no volume of purely speculative trade can be rationalized at an equilibrium price. (4) I am nonetheless a Hayekian about prices: the price system is the best mechanism anyone has devised for pooling dispersed, private, often barely articulable information. (5) I believe that the overwhelming majority of trading volume in modern financial markets is speculative in exactly the sense the no-trade theorem targets — bets on beliefs, rather than risk-sharing responses to endowment shocks or hedging needs. (6) I hold that liquid markets deserve a strong default of epistemic deference: when my credence diverges from a market price, mine should move toward the price, and in most cases most of the way. (7) And finally, I believe that a careful observer in, say, late 1999 could have rationally and justifiably concluded that Nasdaq prices were badly wrong and taken a short position — not as a gamble on sentiment, but as a genuine epistemic achievement that the market itself failed to deliver.

When I've informally floated pieces of this, my friends in the philosophy department tell me I'm just shilling for the efficient market hypothesis, and my friends in finance tell me the no-trade material is a parlor trick with no grip on real markets. I don't think either diagnosis is right, but I honestly can't tell which pairs of these commitments are genuinely incompatible, which merely look incompatible, and which are jointly fine but jointly cost me something I haven't noticed. So what I want is an audit, not a survey: go through the package, identify the real tensions and the merely apparent ones, and rank the real ones by severity. For each genuine tension, tell me what would have to give and what that concession would then force elsewhere in the list — I'm particularly unsure whether the deference commitment in (6) can survive alongside (5) given (3), and whether my attitude toward the hypothetical 1999 short-seller in (7) is compatible with anything like (6). Be disciplined about where the theorems' assumptions actually bind: I'd rather hear "the theorem is silent here because the agents lack common knowledge of rationality" than a forced application. At the end, tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and what it would cost me. I genuinely don't know where the deepest problem is, and I'd rather not discover it in the seminar itself.
