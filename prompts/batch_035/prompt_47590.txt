A philosophy-of-science podcast I've been listening to made an argument about relativity and time that I'd like checked, because the presenter presented it as settled physics, and I don't know enough to tell where the physics ends and the interpretation begins. Here's the substance of what was said: special relativity abolished the idea that simultaneity is absolute — whether two distant events happen "at the same time" depends on the observer's state of motion, and there is no experiment that can pick out one frame's slicing of spacetime as the true one. In the 1960s, two philosophers, Rietdijk and Putnam, turned this into an argument that the future already exists: consider an observer moving relative to you, somewhere far away; in their frame of reference, some event that lies in your future — even what happens to you five minutes from now — is simultaneous with their present moment. Since no frame is privileged, what is real for them must be real, period; so your future is already real. According to the presenter, this shows that the block universe is a theorem of special relativity rather than a philosophical interpretation of it, and he drew the further moral that the future is therefore already determined by the present — that physics has settled the question in favor of hard determinism, and that libertarian free will, and even quantum-mechanical indeterminism, are illusions that relativity has dispelled. He added that most working physicists accept exactly this conclusion and that presentism survives among philosophers only because they haven't absorbed the lesson of 1905.

I'm inclined to think there's something to the argument — the relativity of simultaneity part at least sounds like real physics — but I suspect the presenter has run some claims together. Could you go through this claim by claim and tell me what a philosopher of physics would accept, what they'd treat as a simplification, and what they'd flatly reject? In particular: (1) is it right that special relativity alone, without any further premises, delivers the block universe, or does the argument need an extra philosophical commitment that critics could and do reject — and if so, what is it? (2) is the specific claim about "even what happens five minutes from now" being in some observer's present literally true as stated, or does it need qualification? and (3) does the conclusion about determinism and free will follow from whatever is genuinely correct in the argument, or is there a slide here from one claim about the future to a stronger one? I'd rather come away knowing exactly which parts of the podcast I can keep.
