Me and a friend were going through the contingency version of the cosmological argument and ended up somewhere weird. The usual story first: everything contingent needs an explanation, a chain of contingent things can't bottom out in more contingent things, so there's something necessary. Fine. But then he pushed one step further and now I can't find the exit.

(1) Every fact has an explanation. (2) There is a fact about how absolutely everything is taken together — the total state of the world, all of it included. (3) By (1), that total fact has an explanation too. (4) Since nothing sits outside the totality, whatever explains it has to be inside it: the world somehow explains why the world is this way. (5) If the explanation is internal, then nothing else was needed for things to be like this, and a state of affairs that needs nothing else in order to be as it is could not have been otherwise. (6) So the world as a whole is neccessary, and nothing could have been different, not really. (7) But that's obviously wrong — my desk could have been against the other wall, I could of skipped this whole conversation. (8) So (1) is false and there's at least one brute fact, presumably the fact of how the whole world is.

He's pretty pleased with himself. He says the cosmological argument backfires if you push it hard enough: you end up with either a brute fact or a world where nothing could ever have been otherwise, and no God in sight either way. I'd rather keep (1) than almost anything else here — the thought that reality is fully intelligible seems too important to just abandon — but I admit (6) is where things turn spooky. Which step should I give up, and what exactly am I paying for it? And if he's right that it backfires like this, does that damage the contingency argument itself, or only our extra step beyond it?
