My friend and I argued for most of last night about Thomson's lamp, and I would like to know, before I repeat any of this in my metaphysics seminar, whether the position I reluctantly accepted is actually sound. The setup, as she presented it: a lamp is switched on one minute before midnight, off half a minute before midnight, on a quarter of a minute before, and so on, with each interval halved so that infinitely many switchings all occur before midnight. She argues that this is a genuine proof by contradiction. The lamp must be either on or off at midnight. It cannot be on, since whenever it is switched on it is switched off again before midnight; it cannot be off for the same reason in reverse. So the supertask is inconsistent, and since the only substantive premise behind the contradiction is that the lamp has a definite state at every moment of a continuous time, she concludes that the real lesson is about time itself: infinite divisibility must be abandoned, and physics presumably needs something like a smallest interval. When I objected that the lamp is an engineering impossibility, she said this misses the point, because the paradox is supposed to be a priori, not a claim about actual mechanisms. She also had a supporting argument I could not answer: if the lamp were instead on a dimmer that rises smoothly toward some brightness, its state at midnight would have a perfectly good limit, and a function on a continuous interval that fails to have a value at a limit point is precisely what continuity forbids — so classical analysis itself, she says, commits us to the toggle lamp having a definite state at midnight, and that state contradicts both options. By the end I had conceded that her contradiction is valid and that the only question is which premise to reject, and I have been uneasy about it since. So here is my question: if her contradiction really is valid, why do people keep saying the lamp is "dissolved" rather than solved? Is she right that denying infinite divisibility is the honest way out, or is there something wrong with the step from "it cannot be on and cannot be off" in the first place, and with the continuity argument she backs it up with? If her inference to a smallest unit of time fails, I want to know exactly where it fails — whether the error is logical, whether it is physical, or whether it lies in the move from what a thought experiment's description leaves open to what time itself must be like.
