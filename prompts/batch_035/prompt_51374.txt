I help run a small student group that raises money for charity, and every year we have the same argument about where to send it. One faction wants to just follow the big online charity evaluators — the kind that rank organizations by roughly "cost per life saved": they only count interventions where a randomized trial has actually measured deaths averted, divide money spent by lives saved, and sometimes weight lives saved by the age of the person saved. The other faction says the whole ranking is rigged from the start: anything whose effects show up slowly or diffusely — advocacy for policy change, labor organizing, strengthening institutions in a poor country — can never win, not because it does less good but because nobody can run a clean trial on it. So the ranking quietly becomes "best among the measurable," and the measurables happen to be bednets and vitamin supplements and deworming pills.

I don't want you to referee which faction is right about where the money should go. What I keep wondering about is what the practice itself presupposes. What view of "doing good" does a ranking like that have to assume for it to make sense — does it strictly presuppose a welfarist consequentialism where the good is just measured health and lives, or is it actually compatible with several moral theories (deontology with a duty of rescue, even some virtue-ethical view about what a donor should attend to) and just fits some of them more comfortably? And separately: does the practice presuppose that unmeasurable effects are worth less, or only that we should act under uncertainty in some principled way? If it's the latter, is there a defensible middle path — like assigning credences to unmeasured-but-plausibly-large effects — that wouldn't just collapse the whole enterprise, or does any attempt to factor in systemic change defeat the purpose of having a clean ranking in the first place?

There's also a tension I can't decide whether to be bothered by. The same people who build these rankings will often say, in the same breath, that systemic change probably dwarfs everything else in the long run. Is that flat-out incoherence, or is there some coherent epistemic stance under which you can sincerely believe the biggest effects are elsewhere and still insist on ranking only by what you can measure — some principle about demanding evidence before acting, or about not letting enthusiasm masquerade as knowledge? I'd honestly take either answer, I just want to know whether the position is internally consistent or whether one of the two claims has to go.
