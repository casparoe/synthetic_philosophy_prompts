I'm a second-year graduate student preparing for a seminar on impartiality and the limits of moral theory, and I've been working through Bernard Williams's papers from that period. The passage giving me the most trouble comes at the very end of Persons, Character, and Morality, in the collection Moral Luck. There Williams takes up a case — apparently borrowed from Charles Fried's An Anatomy of Values — of a man standing on a shore who can rescue either his wife or a stranger from drowning, but not both, and asks what could be said on the man's behalf if the choice were challenged. As best I can reconstruct the line of thought without the book in front of me: Williams allows that the man might, in the moment, offer a justification — that she is his wife, and that this is why he is entitled to save her rather than the stranger — but he suggests that if this consideration arrived in that way, it would constitute one thought too many. I understand the example; what I do not understand is what the objection is supposed to be, since the thought in question looks perfectly sound.

Here is my difficulty. As the passage gets paraphrased in seminar discussion, the complaint is psychological: a loving husband should simply dive in, and pausing to deliberate reveals something lacking in him. But Williams seems to be making a more structural point as well — something about the place of moral theory in an agent's life. The thought that his marriage gives him permission is, on one reading, idle as an explanation of why he acts (he acts because he cannot bear to let her die), and if it is not idle — if the permission is what actually moves him — then something has gone wrong with the agent, though the thought itself remains true. So is the objection about deliberation at the time of action, about the order of explanation of why the agent acts, or about what a moral theory can intelligibly contribute to a person's motivations? And if the justificatory thought is what drives him, what exactly is defective: is his love absent, or is it functioning merely as evidence for a permission rather than as his reason?

My tutor insists that the popular reading — that Williams condemns any deliberation whatsoever in an emergency — is crude, and that the worry is continuous with the integrity objection Williams presses against utilitarianism in his half of Utilitarianism: For and Against, where the Jim case and the doctrine of negative responsibility are meant to show that an impartial audit of projects and attachments cannot make room for integrity. My question, then, is threefold. First, could you unpack the end of Persons, Character, and Morality step by step, making clear what the target of the one-thought-too-many remark really is? Second, is that remark simply the integrity objection compressed into a single example, or do they come apart — for instance, in what each implies about whether saving one's wife is justified, justified only from a standpoint no agent can occupy, or in no need of justification at all? Third, please say which reading you think survives scrutiny, and be explicit about where a defender of impartialist theory would dig in — for example, by insisting that sound deliberation can be habitual and instant rather than reflective.
