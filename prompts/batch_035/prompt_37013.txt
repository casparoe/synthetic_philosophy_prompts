I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but a recent argument between two colleagues — both of whom have read a good deal of philosophy of science — happened to be conducted almost entirely in database terminology, which is the only reason I could follow it. They have since asked me to referee, and I would like to give a considered answer rather than a shrug. Here is the exchange, reconstructed as faithfully as I can.

The three of us had spent the afternoon arguing at a whiteboard about our data model, and afterwards the conversation drifted to what physics says the world is ultimately made of.

Marcus went first. He contended that modern physics keeps dissolving objects into relations. A particle, he said, is not a little lump of stuff; it is a bundle of values — charge, spin, mass — none of which means anything except as an entry standing in relations to other entries. Follow physics far enough and you get something like a database schema: structure all the way down, with the "things" reduced to rows whose every column is a foreign key into other rows. Since the properties that survive scientific revolutions are, by and large, the structural ones, he said we should take seriously the thought that the relations are all there really is. He named this position ontic structural realism and declared himself a holder of it.

Priya replied that a schema with no rows is a blank template, not a metaphysical insight. Of course there are objects, she said; her own position (epistemic structural realism, apparently) claims only that our knowledge of them is confined to their relational properties — we know the schema, not the rows. Marcus's talk of relations without relata is like shipping an edge list with no node table: an edge is defined by the nodes it joins, so relations cannot float free of the things they relate. His picture, she concluded, is her epistemic modesty mistaken for a discovery about what exists.

Marcus answered that the nodes need not carry any identity beyond their relational profile. In an unlabelled graph, you can permute the vertices however you like and, provided the adjacency structure is preserved, you have changed nothing; a vertex simply is its position in the structure. Likewise an electron is individuated entirely by its state-space position — there is no remainder, no "node substance", for physics to talk about. The relata do no work, he said, and a principled engineer would delete them from the specification.

Priya then played what she evidently regarded as her trump card. She said she'd read that if all you claim to know is structure, the claim is nearly empty, because any collection of things with enough elements can be arranged to instantiate any given abstract structure — just as any pile of dummy data can be made to satisfy a schema if you place no constraints on the rows. Philosophers call this Newman's objection, she said, and it was originally aimed at Russell. Marcus is therefore caught in a dilemma: if he asserts only structure, he asserts something close to trivial; if he wants to assert more, he needs the objects back.

Marcus's rejoinder was that physical structure is not a bare set-theoretic skeleton: it arrives welded to dynamics, symmetry constraints and a metric, so it is not the sort of thing one can realise with arbitrary dummy data, but only with a system that actually behaves in that way. Whether that saves him, I honestly cannot tell.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move, saying who is right at each step and why. In particular, I should like to know: (a) whether "relations without relata" is coherent, either in the database sense or the graph sense; (b) whom the blank-schema/cardinality objection actually damages more, once you look carefully at what each of them is claiming; and (c) what, if anything, both of them are missing. Please close by saying who comes out more right overall.
