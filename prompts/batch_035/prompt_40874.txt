I teach a mid-level seminar on free will — 18 students, one 90-minute session, movable chairs — and we have just worked through the compatibilist line according to which what matters for responsibility is a *variety of free will worth wanting*: control, reasons-responsiveness, the ability to have done otherwise had one wanted to, rather than the metaphysical ability to have done otherwise given a literally identical past and character. My worry is that my students endorse this on paper while their gut intuitions still tie responsibility to ultimate origination. I want an in-class activity in which they discover, from their own choices rather than from anything I tell them, which enhancements to their own agency they actually want.

Design the activity as an Augmentation Market with the following fixed menu, to be used verbatim. Over a series of rounds, each student privately and bindingly selects exactly one augmentation to install in themselves before facing a decision scenario; the scenarios should be ones in which different augmentations predictably pay off differently:

1. Coin-flip implant — a fixed fraction of your decisions are replaced by a quantum-random choice among the options you were already considering.
2. Eraser — in the moment, you cannot act on any motive you would later disavow; the urge is screened out before it reaches deliberation.
3. Forecaster — you receive an accurate preview of how each option will feel to you a week later.
4. Split screen — alongside your own deliberation, you see the reasoning of the calmest, best-informed version of yourself.
5. Veto window — a ten-second gap opens between urge and action, during which nothing proceeds without an explicit go signal.
6. Scrambler — your deliberation is perturbed by small noise, so that two agents with identical histories and characters would sometimes decide differently.

The scenarios should cover at least: a temptation you expect to regret; a snap judgment under time pressure where the first instinct is wrong; and a deliberate choice between two options that both look defensible. Students whose installed augmentation raises their scenario score keep points, and those whose lowers it lose points; additionally, in each round a rotating bookmaker (a student) must publicly predict which augmentations the group will choose before they choose, winning points for accuracy.

Deliverables: complete rules and a point scheme requiring no real money; a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes; and a debrief in two movements. First, the debrief should map the group's revealed preferences onto the distinction between augmentations that add control and augmentations that add mere openness — using the expected gap between, say, uptake of the Eraser or the Veto window and uptake of the Coin-flip implant or the Scrambler to let the students state, in their own words, which variety of free will they wanted all along. Second, it should use the bookmaker rounds to prise apart two things the students will have conflated: being hard to predict, and being undetermined — they will have wanted to beat the bookmaker, and the debrief should show that you beat a predictor by being responsive to reasons it failed to model, not by injecting noise. The students should not hear the phrase about varieties worth wanting, or any author's name, until the debrief is underway.

Also include: a candid section on what the activity cannot reproduce — the hypothetical stakes, and the felt authorship of one's own real decisions, which no points scheme can simulate — and what the facilitator must supply in words; and an honest account of what will predictably go wrong on a first run, including students picking the Coin-flip implant as a joke or to be contrarian, and how to handle that without shaming them into seriousness (the bookmaker mechanism should do some of this work).

End the entire response with one machine-readable line of the form \answer{...} naming, in at most ten words each, the augmentation this design predicts the group will rank lowest and the one it will rank highest — for example, \answer{lowest: Scrambler; highest: Veto window}.
