The premise: a fertility doctor in a small town quietly used his own sperm instead of the anonymous donor samples his patients had signed up for. Decades pass, and one of the resulting daughters finds out through a consumer DNA kit and decides to sue him. The catch that keeps me up at night is that she had a genuinely good life — loving parents, decent childhood, she's happy, she loves being alive. That sent both her and me spiraling. I dimly remember this is related to something called the nonidentity problem — Parfix? Parfit? — the idea that some actions don't just make someone's life go worse, they change who comes to exist at all, so there's no person who ends up worse off than they otherwise would have been. Something about that seems to let my doctor off scott free, which feels obviously wrong, and my whole third act turns on whether that's a real problem or a philosopher's parlor trick.

Anyway, I've written up my attempt to get unstuck, pasted below — about 700 words of notes/mini-essay from my character's point of view (it's her journal, roughly), where she tries to argue that the doctor wronged her even if he didn't harm her, in the comparative sense. My idea is that it comes down to consent: her mother consented to a specific arrangement — screened, anonymous donor sperm — and the deception broke that, and the daughter inherits a kind of stolen choice even though she can't point to a worse-off version of herself to compare her life against. I also have her flirt with the thought that "you can't complain because you owe your existence to the wrongdoing" is a creepy argument, since by that logic anything that causally produces you gets a pass. But honestly I'm fumbling and I suspect I'm mangling the actual philosophy.
