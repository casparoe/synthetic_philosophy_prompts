I teach bioethics, and every fall the death unit goes the same way: the brain-death people and the circulatory-death people talk past each other for a week and nobody notices they were never arguing about the same thing. So before we do definitions again I want the spec first. What would an adequate definition of death actually have to deliver? Give me the desiderata: the cases it has to handle without flinching (DCD donors where the heart could plausibly restart, anencephalic infants, locked-in patients, patients with minimal but real integrative activity left), the distinctions it can't collapse (organism vs person, whole-brain vs higher-brain, irreversible vs merely permanent loss of function), and the practical constraints it has to respect — transplant logistics, the need for a determinate call at the bedside, not outrunning what ICU physiology supports. For each requirement, say why it's a requirement and not just one camp's prejudice smuggled in. Then flag any pair of desiderata you suspect can't be jointly satisfied, since if that's right the list itself is basically the result. I'd also like to know which single requirement knocks out the most popular contending definitions — that's the part I most want to bring into class. No scorecard of the existing definitions needed, we'll do that fight ourselves.
