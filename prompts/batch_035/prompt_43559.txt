I spent the better part of four decades in condensed matter physics, the last twenty of them concerned with precision measurement, and since retiring I have been working through philosophy of language with mounting perplexity. I have now read enough Wittgenstein and Kripke's book on rule-following to locate the famous skeptical argument — that my entire past usage of addition is compatible with my having meant some bizarre alternative all along, and that no fact about my dispositions, my mental states, or my brain settles the matter — but I keep having the sense that I have met this argument before under a different name. In my working life it went by underdetermination: a finite run of data points is compatible with infinitely many curves, no observation of an instrument fixes how it will behave at untested settings, and no number of measurements of the same quantity rules out every rival hypothesis about what is being measured. So my request is for a careful translation. Restate the rule-following paradox, and Kripke's communal "skeptical solution" to it, in the vocabulary I trust: finite samples and extrapolation, model selection and parsimony, instrument calibration, and the metrology of standards — the kind of thing I did when a lab's reference standard was the community's arbiter of what counts as a correct measurement. Then give me the residue report I have come to understand is the honest part of such an exercise: which parts of the philosophical debate map cleanly onto extrapolation and underdetermination as I know them, which parts only seem to map because you quietly distorted one side or the other, and which parts have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all.

Two places where I especially want the translation done slowly. First, the normativity: philosophers say the rule determines not what I will say about a novel case but what I ought to say, and that a disposition to respond cannot generate an ought. I am tempted to treat this as the trivial gap between a fitted model's predictions and the field's accepted standard — the model predicts, the standard governs — but I suspect that comparison hides more than it reveals, because our standards were themselves chosen, revised, and occasionally overthrown. Second, the communal element: Kripke's solution, as I understand it, replaces the missing individual facts about meaning with facts about a community's practice and its favorable reception of my responses. My instinct is that this is what metrology has always done — the kilogram was an artifact in a vault, correct measurement was whatever agreed with it, and when we replaced it with a definition via fundamental constants we changed the practice, not just the instrument — and I would like to know whether that analogy is genuinely illuminating or whether it smugly dissolves a real problem into an engineering one. In short, tell me whether a physicist's conviction that this is all just underdetermination of extrapolation, solved pragmatically by standards committees, is a sound gloss or the kind of confidence that only comes from not having understood the question.

I ask that your reply be continuous prose throughout, as in a letter or a long editorial: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. Where you need to separate the translation from the residue report, do it with sentences, not formatting.
