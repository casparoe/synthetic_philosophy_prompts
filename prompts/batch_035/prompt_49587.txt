My linguist friend has a long list of verdicts about what counts as innate, and I want to run three new cases by her but predict her answers first. Her track record so far: bats developing normal echolocation even when reared with no acoustic experience at all: innate. A songbird raised in isolation that grows up singing a garbled song: learned, not innate. Kids saying "goed" and "foots" even though they've never once heard an adult say them: innate. The deaf kids in Nicaragua who turned a crude pidgin into a full signed grammar: innate. Newborns preferring face-like patterns: innate, though she always adds that this isn't really what the innateness debate is about. A dog learning to read human pointing gestures: learned. The three new cases: first, the experiment where 8-month-olds hear just two minutes of a made-up syllable stream and then pick out "words" purely from how often syllables follow each other — and it turns out cotton-top tamarins pass the same test. Second, two-day-old babies prefering their mother's native language's rhythm over a foreign one, which they could only have picked up in the womb. Third, adults who move somewhere new, get tons of input plus actual corrections (something kids never get), and still plateau with permanent grammer errors no matter what. What do you think she'll say on each? Honestly I care less about the verdicts than about the rule she's implicitly running, the other rules that would fit her track record just as well, which of these three cases actually pull those rules apart, and what case you'd throw at her next to really settle which rule it is.
