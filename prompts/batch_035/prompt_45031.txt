I'm in a seminar that ends with Parfit's *Reasons and Persons*, and we've spent the last two sessions on the hitchhiker and what Parfit called the phenomenon of being rational to cause oneself to be irrational: the hitchhiker needs the driver's help, the driver stops only if she can rely on being paid later, and the hitchhiker can get the ride only by being the sort of person who will keep the promise even at the later moment when, by his own lights then, keeping it is no longer rational. What struck me is that everyone tells this story as though the disposition is simply an asset for its bearer: the hitchhiker gets rescued, the driver gets paid, and the capacity for rational irrationality comes out looking like unambiguous good news for whoever has it. I want to pressure-test that. My working suspicion is that the disposition's value depends on something the standard telling never mentions — namely that the commitment is hard to fake and not freely extractable — and that once others can read the disposition off you in advance, whoever sets the terms can capture the surplus it creates.

Build me a new case that keeps Parfit's structure but reverses who captures the benefit. The requirements: (i) realistic and everyday — a family or small-business setting is fine, no science fiction, no demons or perfect predictors; (ii) genuinely Parfit-style irrationality — the promise must be unenforceable (no contracts, no collateral, no pills, no legal or social sanction for breaking it), and the keeping must be irrational at the time it is kept: no further dealings with anyone whose opinion could be affected by this act, so the case must not quietly turn into reputation management in a repeated game; (iii) the counterpart extracts the promise precisely because the promiser's commitment capacity is legible in advance, and he must act in ordinary self-interest, not as a villain; and (iv) the promiser must end up worse off than she would have been had she been known not to keep such promises — the disposition is exploited rather than rewarded — and this must hold without the promised act being unusually onerous in itself; the damage should flow from the terms the counterpart can impose once he knows her promise is as good as gold. That last clause is the point of the whole exercise, so the case has to earn it: the promiser's ending up worse off must follow from the setup, not be written into the ending by fiat. If you find that some part of the specification can't be met without cheating, say where it strains rather than smuggling the result in.

After the scenario itself, in one paragraph, show your work in at most two more: first, verify that at the moment of keeping, refusing really is the rational act by the promiser's own lights at that time — this must be one-shot irrationality of Parfit's kind, not prudence under another name; second, identify which single feature of the case is doing the work of turning the disposition from asset to liability; and third, give the strongest reply available to a defender of the Parfit–Gauthier outlook, who will presumably claim the case shows only that the benefit is contingent on terms, not that the disposition is a liability. Then commit: reach a definite verdict on whether your case, as built, shows the commitment disposition to be on net a liability to its bearer, and defend that verdict — do not leave me with "it depends on the circumstances." A hard limit of 800 words total, scenario, walkthrough, and verdict included.
