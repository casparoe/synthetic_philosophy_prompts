In court, a witness's own account of what they saw gets in with no showing at all that they're a reliable observer — nobody checks their track record, we just swear them in and let the jury weigh them. But if the same content arrives secondhand — "he told me the light was red," offered to prove the light was red — it gets kicked out as hearsay, no matter how trustworty the source probably is. Except the rules are full of holes: excited utterances, statements made while perceiving the event, dying declarations, all come in withouth the declarant ever being cross-examined. So what's the implicit epistemology of testimony here? Is it reductionist — testimony only counts when the jury can check it, and hearsay is out because it can't be tested — or anti-reductionist — trust is the default, and the exceptions trade on circumstances that are supposed to guarantee trustworthiness in place of testing? Can both halves of the practice really rest on one coherent theory of epistemic justification, or does the whole bar actually rest on something else, like a belief that juries get seduced by untestable statements? And if we swapped the implicit view for plain reliabilism about testimony — let it in whenever the source is probably right — what would have to change in how the courtroom actually works?
