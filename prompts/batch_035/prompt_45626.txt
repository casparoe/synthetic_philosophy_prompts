I'm the instructor of record for a mid-size undergraduate course called Philosophy of Death, and I'd like help grading one of the exam essays from our unit on Epicurus. The exam question read as follows:

Epicurus held that death, understood as the state of being dead, is not bad for the person who dies. (a) Reconstruct his reasoning as charitably as you can. (b) Explain the deprivationist reply and why its proponents think it succeeds where other objections fail. (c) Assess whether the deprivationist reply, even if successful, establishes that death is bad for the person who die — and pay particular attention to when, if ever, death could be bad for its subject. (d) State whether the Epicurean conclusion, if sound, would show that death is not bad at all, or only that it is not bad for the one who dies.

Here is the student's answer, reproduced in full:

Epicurus's argument has two premises and a conclusion. First, something is bad for a person only if that person experiences it, since all good and bad consists in sense experience. Second, death — the state of being dead — involves no experience at all, since the person no longer exists to have experiences. Therefore, death is not bad for the person who dies. He presents this argument in the Letter to Menoeceus, where he also offers the famous symmetry argument: the time before our birth is a mirror of the time after our death, and since we do not regard our prenatal nonexistence as a misfortune, we should not regard posthumous nonexistence as one either. Together these show that our anxiety about death is misplaced, and Epicurus's conclusion extends beyond the person who dies: if death cannot harm the one who dies, then it cannot be bad for anyone at all, not even for the bereaved, since grief is directed at something that cannot harm anyone.

The deprivationist reply challenges the first premise. Nagel and later defenders of the deprivation account point out that we routinely judge things bad for people without those things being experienced: Nagel's example is being betrayed behind your back by people you trust — a clear misfortune that you never experience. If unexperienced harms are possible, the experience requirement fails, and a natural alternative is that death is bad for a person when it deprives them of goods they would otherwise have had. This avoids the strange result that death is harmless while any period of pain is bad, no matter how brief, and it captures our intuition that a sudden painless death in one's prime is a tragedy rather than nothing.

On the timing question, the deprivationist owes us an account of when death is bad for the person, and here there is genuine trouble, since there is no time at which the person exists and is deprived of anything. The cleanest fix is to say death is bad for the person at the moment of death, which is the last moment at which they exist.

Finally, the deprivationist's success has a practical upshot: once we see that death is bad for the one who dies, it follows that fearing death is rational, and Epicurus's whole therapeutic program — the attempt to free us from the fear of death — collapses.
