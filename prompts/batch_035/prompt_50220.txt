My sister and I have the same argument roughly twice a year, and last time she ended it by quoting "be fruitful and multiply" at me as if it closed the matter. To be clear about the setting: neither of us is religious, she's worried about declining birthrates in our country, I'm in my thirties and child-free by choice, and her use of the line was that (a) shrinking populations are a genuine moral catastrophe and (b) people like me who could have children and don't are letting it happen. I don't want to re-litigate the family part with you; I want to understand the sentence itself, because I've since noticed it — or secular near-cousins of it like "civilizations die when they stop reproducing" — all over the pronatalist writing I've read since, and it seems to be doing a lot of different jobs in different places, most of them unexamined.

So here is what I'd like from you. Take that one line and work out what precise claims it could be making when a secular person reaches for it. I've tried starting this myself and I can already see it's not one claim: it could be a religious command (which I think we can set aside, but say why if you disagree); it could be an instrumental claim that a society needs enough births to avoid some specific bad outcome, where everything depends on which bad outcome; it could be the claim that a larger population is intrinsically better, more people being a good thing in the way more happiness is; it could be a claim about duty — that each capable person is obligated to contribute, the way citizens might be obligated to vote or pay taxes; or it could be something more like an evolutionary observation dressed up as advice, as though the fact that every one of my ancestors reproduced gives me a reason to. There are probably readings I haven't thought of, and I'd like those too.

But please don't stop at the menu, because that's the part I can almost do myself and it hasn't settled anything. For each reading I want a verdict: is it trivially true, substantively defensible, or false — and, crucially, does it actually support what my sister used it to support? I've noticed, for instance, that a claim about what's good for a civilization and a claim about what I owe anyone rarely support each other, and I want to know whether any of these readings bridges that gap. Along the way, two puzzles of my own keep coming up and I want them dealt with squarely, not waved off. First: if more people is just better, then a world of ten billion lives each barely worth living would seem better than one billion very good lives, and that smells wrong to me — does that mean the "bigger is intrinsically better" reading is broken, or that my smell is broken, or is there a principled way out? Second: I keep running into the thought that no one can be wronged by not being conceived, since there's no one there to be wronged — and if that's right, it's hard to see how the command could be owed to the unconceived, which seems to knock out one of the main reasons people give for it. Am I reasoning correctly there?

Finally, and this is the part I most want: commit. Tell me which reading my sister most plausibly had in mind given how she deployed the line, and which reading you would actually defend if you had to defend one — with your reasons, including where you're least confident — rather than presenting the options as equally live and leaving the choice to me. If your considered view is that every defensible reading fails to support what the slogan gets used for in these debates, say that outright and take the consequences; if some reading survives, name it and stand behind it.
