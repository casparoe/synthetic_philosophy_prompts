A cousin who follows the intelligent-design movement backed me into an argument I can't find my way out of, and I'd like a proper diagnosis rather than a reflexive dismissal.

(1) A code, in the strict sense, is a set of conventions by which symbols are assigned, by agreement or stipulation, the function of standing for something else — Morse code, ASCII. (2) The genetic code is a code in this sense: codons function as signs for amino acids, and the cell's machinery reads them as such. (3) What makes a mapping a code, rather than a mere causal correlation, is that the assignment is arbitrary — the same job could have been done by a different assignment. (4) The genetic assignment is arbitrary in exactly this sense: nothing in physics compels the codon UUU to stand for phenylalanine rather than some other amino acid; the same chemistry could have sustained a different code. (5) Whatever is arbitrary in this sense and yet operates with perfect reliability must have been fixed by something that selected one alternative from among many. (6) Selecting one alternative from among many, with a functioning outcome in view, is a choice; and only agents make choices. (7) So the assignment of the genetic code was made by an agent.

My cousin says the conclusion simply follows and that I should accept it. I'm confident something slips somewhere, but each step looks fine to me taken on its own. Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked fine, and what rejecting it costs — including the options of rejecting (2), or biting the bullet on (7), if either is really the cheapest exit. For what it's worth, I'd rather give up (5) or (6) than any of (1) through (4), but tell me honestly if that preference is unaffordable. And if you think the argument is sound after all, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a flaw. Please answer in American English.
