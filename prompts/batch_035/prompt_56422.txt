Hey, so — you're a professional philosopher with a strong economics background, and I want you to think carefully about a pattern I keep running into. Do some brainstorming up front, list the candidate concepts before you commit, don't just fire back the first label that comes to mind.

Some context on me: I teach political philosophy, and every time I prep a lecture on competition I keep seeing the same shape in totally different settings, and I can't tell whether it's one phenomenon or several. I'll describe it, and then I want to know whether the thing, as carved exactly along the lines I describe, has an established name in the literature. If yes, tell me what it is and how it differs from the neighboring concepts it keeps getting confused with. If nothing established fits cleanly, say so, coin a term, and defend the boundary. I'm not going to be satisfied with a bare name — I want to know what exactly the phenomenon is, what it isn't, and where the edges are.

The pattern: a bunch of actors compete for something, but the prize doesn't get any bigger no matter how hard they compete, and the competing itself is costly. So all the extra effort mostly cancels out. Everyone would be better off if everyone had stopped at the starting line — but no one can stop alone.

Concrete-ish instances:

- Two rival shops on the same street keep extending their opening hours, each afraid the other will stay open later. Customers split roughly the same as before, both owners are now exausted and paying more wages, and the total business on the street hasn't grown at all.
- An academic job market with a fixed number of jobs. Candidates respond with more publications, more postdocs, more teaching experience, and the "winners" arrive at the same jobs ten years later than they used to, with less of a life left over.
- Peacocks. The total number of mates is fixed by things like food supply, so the tails don't catch more females in aggregate; each male's tail just has to be longer than the next guy's.

Now, here's what makes me doubt it's just an "arms race" or a "tragedy of the commons," and this is the part I most want you to get right. In the commons story there is a shared resource that the actors jointly use up. In my pattern there is often no resource being depleted at all — the waste is the effort itself. The prize is fixed (or shrinking), and the competing eats it. And sometimes it goes one step further and gets genuinely ugly: the competing destroys the value of the very thing being competed for. So many advertisers shout that nobody believes advertising anymore; everyone's CV is padded to the gills so credentials stop signaling anything. In those cases the winner ends up holding something worth less than what it cost to win it — the prize didn't just fail to grow, the race consumed it.

I also want the phenomenon kept apart from races that are actually productive, like firms racing to be first at some invention, where the racing itself produces knowledge. In my pattern the racing produces nothing but the racing.

I know the folk terms — "keeping up with the Joneses," "rat race" — and I half-remember something in the rent-seeking literature about competition for a prize burning up the prize's value, but I can't tell wether that's the same thing as what I'm pointing at or only one piece of it, since in my pattern nobody needs to be seeking anything illicit, the shops are just shops. Please sort out which of these (folk, evolutionary, economic, whatever) are genuinely the same phenomenon and which only look like it, and give me the boundary cases: where exactly does my pattern stop, e.g. what happens when the prize isn't quite fixed but grows a little with effort — is that inside or outside, and why?
