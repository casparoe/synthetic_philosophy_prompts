My decision theory reading group spent a session on I.J. Good's argument for the principle of total evidence — the one where a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer should never turn down costless, relevant information, because the expected value of observing before acting is never worse than acting blind. Afterward my friends Priyanka and Dan kept going at it over dinner and it got genuinely heated, and I've been trying to figure out who was right about what ever since. Priyanka opened by saying the proof is basically trivial: once you've seen the observation you can always just do whatever you would have done anyway, so free information can never lower your expected utility, and that means refusing free information is a flat-out rationality failure, full stop. Dan countered with his uncle, who for years refused to open his Huntington's test results — if there's no treatment, all knowing does is give you decades of dread, so he was better off not knowing. Priyanka said that misses the point: dread is a cost, so the information wasn't free, and the theorem is only about information whose effects run entirely through your subsequent decisions.

Then it escalated. Dan said fine, but that concession hollows the whole thing out — every real piece of information carries some cost, whether it's attention, anxiety, or spoiled surprises, so the theorem is about an empty set, and the folk wisdom that ignorance can be bliss comes through untouched. Priyanka said no, the theorem applies whenever the costs are small relative to the stakes, like wanting the salary number before accepting a job offer, and anyway the Huntington case proves her point rather than Dan's: the uncle's real problem was that he couldn't trust himself to act well on the information (he'd probably sink into fatalism), and the theorem explicitly assumes you update cleanly and then choose optimally — it's not a bug in the theorem that a person who responds badly to news does badly. Dan called that cheating: "free information is valuable to agents who use information well" is close to vacuous, every theorem is easy if you assume away the failures. And he had one more case he thought broke even a perfect reasoner: suppose a near-perfect predictor fills a box with money only for people it predicts won't check a certain observation — then even an ideal Bayesian who follows the evidence scrupulously is made worse off by observing, because someone modeled the observing itself. Priyanka waved this off as violating the theorem's setup, since it requires the state of the world to be independent of whether you look, and that's just built in. Dan said "built in" means "assumed away," and asked whether the theorem is anything more than a benchmark that nothing in real life — with costs, self-knowledge failures, and other agents modeling our curiosity — ever actually meets. She didn't have a snappy answer to that and honestly neither do I.

Can you adjudicate this exchange move by move — say who's right at each step, not just who wins overall? I'd especially like your take on whether Priyanka's "you can always ignore it" proof really does the work she thinks it does, whether Dan's predictor case is a genuine counterexample or just a change of subject, and whether his final challenge about the theorem being a benchmark with no real-world instances is fair. Also if there's something both of them missed that a more careful statement of the theorem would flag, I'd want to know that too.
