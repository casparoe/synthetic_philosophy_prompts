I'm in a behavioral ecology course this semester (it cross-lists with econ, sort of a signals and incentives mashup) and I got roped into running next week's discussion section myself because the TA is at a conference. It's about 12 students, one 50-minute block, and the reading was on costly signaling and the handicap principle. We covered how signals stay honest when they're expensive in a way that tracks the sender's underlying quality — peacock tails, gazelle stotting, expensive engagement rings, that whole family of examples. The part I really want them to *feel* rather than nod along to is the counterintuitive bit: the waste is load-bearing. If you made the signal cheap, everyone would send it, and it would stop meaning anything. That's the thing people resist on an intuitive level — surely nature would find a way to do it efficiently, right?

So design me a game for this. I'm picturing something where students are secretly assigned to be high-quality or low-quality "senders" trying to convince "receivers" of their type, and the *only* way to signal is paying some real cost — points that count toward an actual prize at the end, or doing something mildly tedious/embarrassing, whatever works in a classroom without money changing hands. I need rules, how types get assigned and kept hidden, and payoffs where receivers win for guessing right, senders of high type win for being believed, and low types win for fooling people. Crucially I want at least one later round where you quietly make the signal cheap (or free) and let them watch the information evaporate in real time, and maybe a round where mimics sneak in — low types copying the signal once they've seen it work — so they run into the question of what exactly keeps mimics out.

For the debrief: map the rounds onto separating vs. pooling equilibria, why the cost has to hurt low types more than high types for the signal to be stable, and then an honest comparison of handicap-style signals versus signals that are basically indices (where faking is physically impossible rather than just unprofitable). I'd also like a short bit on whether the peacock analogy actually carries over to human stuff like conspicuous consumption or hazing, or whether human status signaling is doing something the biological models don't capture. And finally tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — my guesses are people refusing to pay costs because it feels silly, or two friends colluding and ruining the incentive structure, but you tell me — and how I should handle it on the fly with 50 minutes and no slack. No citations or outside readings needed, the students won't have acess to anything during the session anyway.
