Next month I present to our philosophy of mind reading group — a deliberately mixed audience: two committed illusionists who suspect the hard problem just is the meta-problem wearing a different hat, two dualists who hold that our gap-judgments are quasi-perceptual detections of phenomenal properties rather than theory-laden outputs, and a pragmatically minded cognitive psychologist who thinks the whole topic is her subject matter with extra steps. My thesis: the meta-problem of consciousness — the problem of explaining why we judge that there is a hard problem at all, that is, why we find an explanatory gap between physical processes and experience — is a well-posed empirical question about human cognition, and it is separable from the hard problem itself: whatever story we eventually tell about why we make these judgments, it will not by itself settle whether consciousness is physical, and should not be allowed to masquerade as settling it. My sketch, which they'll be attacking: the judgment that the feel of red can't be captured in functional terms is itself a cognitive event with causes; if our quality representations present properties as intrinsic and non-structural, and our self-model has only limited access to its own processing, the gap-judgments may fall out of that — but this is a hypothesis for cognitive science to test, not a philosophical deduction, and its truth or falsity leaves the metaphysics of experience open.

Please act as a senior philosopher of mind who has sat through hundreds of hostile Q&A sessions and knows these subcultures from the inside. Before drafting, think through what each subgroup's commitments make them care about. Then prepare my objection sheet: the eight or so questions I will actually get from this audience — not generic ones — ordered by how much damage they could do, each phrased the way that particular subgroup would phrase it (something like the illusionists' charge that my bracketing concedes the mystery-mongers a quarter they will never give back; the dualists' reply that I am explaining away a detection and that a causal story about a judgment does not touch its epistemic warrant; the psychologist's worry that the deliverables are unfalsifiable just-so stories and that the data are contaminated by philosophy training). For each objection, give me a two-minute reply, a note on where the reply is soft, and what I should concede up front. Finally, include the question I am hoping nobody asks — I suspect it is the one about whether a complete functional explanation of my own problem-reports would leave consciousness itself doing no explanatory work in producing them, which threatens to collapse my careful separability claim; confirm whether that is indeed the worst one, or find me a worse one I have not thought of.
