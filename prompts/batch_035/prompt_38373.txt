In my public policy seminar, the instructor defended default rules and other choice architecture by analogy to a GPS, a comparison Sunstein has made repeatedly: a GPS points you toward a destination, but you keep the steering wheel; recalculating when you deviate is not coercion; and crucially, a GPS helps even a perfectly rational driver with no biases at all. The conclusion drawn was that since nobody thinks a GPS manipulates its user, nudges cannot be manipulative either, and complaints about them working "behind the back" of deliberation are overblown. I want to audit this analogy rather than accept it wholesale. Please give me an explicit mapping — what plays the role of the suggested route, the driver's destination, the recalculating, the driver's ability to ignore directions — and then say where the mapping breaks. I suspect some disanalogies matter more than others: for instance, a GPS's route is visible and its interests are aligned with the driver's, whereas defaults are often invisible and their designers need not be benevolent; and a GPS does not get its grip by exploiting anyone's cognitive biases, which seems to be exactly how defaults do their work. Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the intended use (concluding nudges are not manipulative) from the ones that don't, and tell me whether that conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table — or whether the analogy was doing the heavy lifting all along. If there is a better analogy for at least some nudges (a tactful friend arranging the seating, a salesperson positioning the milk at the back, a trail designer choosing where the path bends), say which and why.
