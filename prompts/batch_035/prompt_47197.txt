My closest friend is a philosophy PhD student working on philosophy of language, and I work on machine learning safety. Last night we had a three-hour argument about whether Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment is, at bottom, "the same problem" as what my corner of the field calls ontology identification. To make sure the comparison is fair to my side, some background on the ML notion: the worry is that a sufficiently capable learned model will represent the world using internal categories that need not line up with ours — the way its internal states distinguish and group situations may carve the world differently than our concepts of harm, honesty, or "the thing the human really wants" — so that even a human with full access to the model's internals could not straightforwardly read off whether it is tracking what we care about. Reporting what the model "believes" seems to require translating between its ontology and ours, and nothing guarantees that translation exists or is unique.

My friend's claim is that Putnam already exposed the general phenomenon this is a case of. On his telling, Putnam's point — that two people in internally identical psychological states can mean different things, because reference is fixed partly by the microstructure of their environments and not by what is in their heads — is precisely the phenomenon: representational content is determined by factors outside the representing system, and ontology identification is just the special case where we are the external environment for a model and cannot easily make our contributions felt. He added that Putnam's division of linguistic labor helps too, since the model would be something like a member of our linguistic community whose competence is delegated to the environment we built. I pushed back that this is too quick: Putnam's cases feature two systems with internally identical states whose meanings differ globally, whereas our problem is closer to constructing a translation manual between two systems with radically different internal states, and one of the systems is an optimizer we have reason to distrust, which introduces an adversarial element Putnam never contemplated. He replied that I was smuggling in practical worries where the philosophical issue is the same.

Please write a careful comparison of the two problems. Which parts of the Twin Earth machinery — the externalist claim itself, the environmental component of reference, the division of linguistic labor — genuinely illuminate ontology identification, and which parts fail to transfer or actively mislead? Is the better comparison perhaps not the Twin Earth argument about natural-kind terms at all, but rather the broader externalist thesis about how meanings are fixed by use and community, or social externalism of the Burge variety? Where the analogy does hold, say whether it is more than an analogy — that is, whether the ML problem is usefully seen as an instance of the philosophical one — and where it breaks, say what the residue is: is it something the philosophical literature lacks the vocabulary for, or something the ML framing has inflated beyond its philosophical weight? Finally, give a verdict on our argument: which of us was closer to right, and what should each of us concede?
