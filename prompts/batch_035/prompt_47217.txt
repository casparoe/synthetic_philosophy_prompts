I serve on my university's committee on gifts and endowments, and at next week's meeting we must decide on an offer I'm not comfortable deferring. The estate of a recently deceased professor of solid-state physics — a man with a distinguished publication record in his own field and a lifelong side interest in the Society for Psychical Research — has offered a seven-figure endowment to fund a permanent "Programme for Experimental Research into Anomalous Cognition," with terms requiring that the money support laboratory investigation of purported telepathy and precognition specifically, and not consciousness science generally. Our scientific-merit subcommittee is split three ways. One faction wants to decline outright: they argue that psi research is where replication goes to die, that we would be lending the university's name to the paranormal because the check is large, and that the professor's own field had enough genuine open problems without manufacturing more. A second faction invokes Henry Sidgwick: the Cambridge utilitarian was a founding president of the Society for Psychical Research in the 1880s and argued that investigating anomalous phenomena was nothing more than applying ordinary scientific method to a disreputable and neglected borderland, so that refusing even to look was itself a failure of scientific rationality; on their view, accepting the gift is the Sidgwickan move. A third faction wants to accept only with heavy conditions — a renamed unit, a mandate reframed toward the methodology of replication and statistical inference, the donor's executors at the table to renegotiate the terms.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before the meeting. It should recommend one course — decline, accept as offered, or accept with specific and actionable conditions — and argue for it in a way the losing factions could recognize as engaging their strongest points rather than strawman versions of them. In particular, I want the memo to take seriously what the Sidgwick precedent actually commits us to: whether "study it scientifically" licenses taking the gift on the donor's framing, or whether his insistence on rigorous method and on the exposure of fraudulent mediums cuts against a programme whose subject matter is fixed by a bequest. If it helps, assume the committee wants a position it can defend publicly, not merely in committee.

One more request, and I mean it: if you think the way I have framed the choice is confused — for instance, if the three options do not really exhaust the space, if the Sidgwick analogy is being made to do work it cannot bear, or if the real question is something other than legitimacy and is instead about institutional opportunity costs — then say so plainly in the memo rather than tidying the framing up. I would rather hear that before the meeting than after it.
