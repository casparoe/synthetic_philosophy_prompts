When a major regulation is proposed in the United States or Europe, agencies typically run a cost-benefit analysis that monetizes almost everything the rule touches: a human life is converted into a "value per statistical life" in the range of millions of dollars, injuries and illness get dollar figures, and even visibility at national parks and quiet in residential neighborhoods are priced. Yet the very same regulatory systems treat certain goods as categorically off the ledger. Under the U.S. Endangered Species Act, agencies have generally been understood to be forbidden from balancing economic costs against the survival of a listed species — a reading the Supreme Court endorsed in the snail darter case, when a nearly completed dam was threatened by a three-inch fish and the Court held that the statute's language left no room for a cost-benefit exception, provoking Congress to carve out an exemption afterward. Similarly, harms to sacred sites and to cultural heritage are commonly excluded from monetization even when the scenic value of the adjacent landscape is cheerfully priced. So the practice appears to presuppose two inconsistent things at once: that everything affected by policy can be made commensurable on a single monetary scale, and that some things cannot be traded off at any price. Reconstruct the implicit theory of value here. Does the two-track pattern really require a lexically ordered or pluralist theory, or would a thoroughgoing welfarist say the priceless category is merely a useful political fiction — an error cost correction for overconfident monetization, or a proxy for deeper uncertainty about the item's true value? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates, and identify where it is philosophically underdetermined, such that a defender could gloss the "priceless" tier as genuine incommensurability, as a dignity-based side constraint, or as a democratic judgment that some trade-offs should not be made by experts at all. Finally, describe what would have to change — both in the scoring rules and in the political practice surrounding them — if regulators instead adopted the view that incommensurable values are made comparable not by measurement but by the deliberative commitment of the community making the choice.
