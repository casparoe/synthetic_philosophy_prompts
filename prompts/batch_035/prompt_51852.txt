I am a TA grading a final exam for an upper-level undergraduate course on philosophy of probability, and I would like your help evaluating one answer. The exam question was:

"Reconstruct the doomsday argument associated with Brandon Carter and John Leslie, being explicit about the assumption it needs about how you should treat your own birth rank among all humans. Then explain how the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption deliver different verdicts on the argument, and say which of the two assumptions you would endorse, given the counterintuitive consequences each faces. (You may confine your discussion to human observers, but flag where the reference class matters.)"

The student wrote:

"The doomsday argument, first formulated by Nick Bostrom, says that because roughly the same number of people lived before me as will live after me if I am not in a special position, the fact that I am around human number 100 billion or so tells me doom is probably soon. Formally, if there are N total humans, my birth rank is about 100 billion, and since I should expect my rank to be typical, large N hypotheses that put my rank at one-millionth of the total are penalized, so the posterior shifts toward small N. The assumption doing the work is the self-sampling assumption (SSA): you should reason as if you were a random sample from the set of all observers in your reference class. Under SSA the argument goes through. The self-indication assumption (SIA) says you should also favor hypotheses on which more observers exist, since more observers means more chances for you to exist. This exactly cancels the doomsday update, and it is the natural way to block the argument. Gott's Copernican version — the future will probably last as long as the past — is just the informal ancestor of the same reasoning and stands or falls with it. As for which assumption to adopt: the reference class problem shows SSA can't be right, since we get different doomsday predictions depending on whether we include all mammals, all humans, or only humans who have heard of the doomsday argument, and no choice is principled. So SIA wins by default, and since SIA just restores our ordinary priors, it has no counterintuitive consequences of its own."

Please give this answer a letter grade and the specific point-by-point comments that justify it. In particular, assess whether the reconstruction is faithful, whether the treatment of SSA and SIA is correct, and whether the closing argument for SIA actually follows. Lead your response with the grade and a one-sentence summary of your reasoning, then give the detailed comments; do not restate the question or add any preamble.
