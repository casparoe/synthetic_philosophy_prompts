My other course this semester is multi-objective optimization, so I spend all week thinking about Pareto dominance, non-dominated sets, scalarization functions (weighted sums, epsilon-constraints, Chebyshev stuff), hypervolume indicators, and decision-maker preferences, and honestly when the professor started saying things like some goods can't be traded off against each other, my brain immediately went: oh, mutually non-dominated points, that's just the Pareto front. But I suspect that reflex is wrong in some way I can't articulate, so I want an actual translation, done carefully.

Here's the ask: restate the philosophers' debate in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can manage. The pieces we've covered are: the small improvement argument (if neither A nor B is better, but a tiny improvement to one of them still doesn't make it better, something odd is going on); Raz's view that incommensurability is constituted by the values themselves, like, it's not that we're ignorant of an exchange rate, it's that certain options just fail to have a meaningful comparison because of what they are; and Chang's idea of parity, where two options are comparable but neither is better, worse, or equal -- and her further claim that in genuinely hard choices you can resolve things by committing to one option in a way that makes it better than it was, rather than discovering it was better all along. Map all of that onto dominance relations, scalarization, and the rest, and then give me the residue report: which parts translate cleanly, which parts only fit if you distort them, and which parts have no expression at all in optimization-speak.

The residue is what I actually care about. Two specific questions I keep going back and forth on with my roommate (also a CS major, also skeptical of the whole unit). Second, when Raz says there's no common currency, is refusing to scalarize a faithful translation of that, or does the optimization framework sneak commensurability back in the moment you pick a solution anyway -- since in my class the story is always that the Pareto front is handed off to a decision maker who does eventually choose? And the Chang thing about hard choices making an option better than it was, that seems to have literally no counterpart in a framework where the ordering is fixed before you optimize. So tell me straight: is the residue a sign my vocabulary is missing something real, or a sign the philosophers are being vaguer than they sound?
