Next month I defend the second chapter of my dissertation. Its thesis is that the Wilt Chamberlain argument in Nozick's Anarchy, State, and Utopia establishes far less than either Nozick's critics or his libertarian admirers claim: read carefully, it shows only that end-state and patterned principles of justice are unstable under a regime of unrestricted voluntary transfer. It does not show that any actual holdings in anything like our world are legitimate, and it does not show that taxation is on a par with forced labor. The constructive half of the chapter then argues that, once the argument is read this narrowly, it allies itself naturally with left-libertarianism rather than with Nozick's own conclusions: a once-for-all equal division of claims over external resources is not a pattern imposed on holdings but a background condition on just acquisition, so maintaining it is not the kind of continuous patterned principle the Chamberlain example refutes. In fact, I claim, the example backfires: the instability of an equal starting distribution under voluntary transfer shows that any stable principle of justice must regulate acquisition rather than transfers — and regulating acquisition is precisely what resource egalitarians have always wanted to do.

My committee is the problem. The chair is a Rawlsian who regards the whole D1-to-D2 apparatus as a rigged example; she holds that the premise that justice is preserved by voluntary transfer simply begs the question against the difference principle, since for Rawls the voluntariness of a transaction depends on the fairness of the background institutions against which it occurs, and she will say my reading makes Nozick's example worse for egalitarians than the standard one, not better. A second member is a luck egalitarian who thinks historical principles of justice are anachronistic generally, and who will press the objection that my "background condition" is a pattern wearing a disguise: if a once-for-all division is to do any work, it must be maintained by ongoing interference with transfers, which reintroduces exactly the patterned principle the Chamberlain argument was supposed to refute. The third member is the one I most fear, a genuine left-libertarian who agrees with my conclusion but will say I have reached it by gutting Nozick's self-ownership premise, which he regards as load-bearing: if acquisition is constrained this heavily by prior equal claims, my supposed alliance between the narrow Chamberlain argument and left-libertarianism collapses into the claim that Nozick refuted Nozick. The outside member is an economist who cares about none of this and will want to know whether any of it survives when transfers are not spot trades between individuals but production inside firms, where there is no determinate moment at which anything is "transferred" from anyone to anyone.

Tell me which points I should concede before being asked, since I would rather cede ground than appear not to have seen it, and which points I must hold if the chapter is to survive at all. Finally, give me the one question I am hoping nobody asks, and tell me whether that hope is realistic.
