My mother, who is seventy-four and has generally been in good health, was told last week that she needs surgery to replace a damaged heart valve. Her cardiologist is neutral between the two surgeons at the regional medical center who perform the procedure, Dr. Aldana and Dr. Brant, so the family has been left to make the choice, and it has divided us.

The hospital's website posts only overall outcomes: of Dr. Brant's last 200 patients, 157 survived to the one-year mark, against 139 of Dr. Aldana's 190. My sister thinks that settles it. But a cousin of ours is a resident at the hospital, and when we pressed him he broke the numbers down by how sick the patients were before surgery. Among the severe cases, Aldana kept 45 of 90 alive and Brant kept 4 of 20; among the routine cases, Aldana kept 94 of 100 and Brant kept 153 of 180. By my reading, Aldana does better in both groups at once and yet worse overall. I have checked the arithmetic several times, and it holds. The resident adds that Aldana is the surgeon other hospitals send their hardest cases to, which would explain the mix, though it is Brant who advertises.

My sister is unmoved. She says the overall figures are the honest ones: they average over the actual mix of patients a surgeon sees, and subgroup breakdowns are the kind of after-the-fact slicing that pharmaceutical companies get criticized for — pick your cut, get your result. And there is a part of her argument that bothers me most: nobody has told us which group my mother belongs in. She is seventy-four but active, with mild kidney trouble, and the resident guessed "probably routine, but honestly it's a judgment call." If we cannot even say which row of the table she is in, my sister says, the only number that applies to her is the overall one.

Something in me resists this conclusion, but I cannot make the resistance precise. Is there a principled way to decide which figure should guide the choice — the overall one, the subgroup one, or neither? And does the uncertainty about my mother's own severity make the overall number more appropriate, or does it just mean we should be reasoning about something else entirely?
