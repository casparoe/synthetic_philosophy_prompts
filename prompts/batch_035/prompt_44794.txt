I teach an upper-level seminar on philosophy of mind — fourteen students, one 90-minute session — and we're approaching the combination problem for panpsychism. They know the standard dialectic on paper, but I want an activity where they run into the problem from the inside before we name what happened. My hunch about how to do this: give each participant access to only a fragment of some information (say, one feature of a scene), then task the group with producing a single unified report or judgment that no individual could have produced alone. I want them to discover for themselves whether they've become one cognizer or just a well-coordinated committee — and to feel that the question isn't settled by the fact that coordination succeeded.

Please design the full activity: rules, roles, any materials, a timeline, and how to keep it from collapsing into either chaos or triviality. The deliverable should not be a discussion of panpsychism; the problem should show up in what the rules force them to do. Then a debrief that maps specific moments of the activity onto specific sub-problems — subject-summing, the boundary problem, whether combination yields one subject or a mere aggregate — and that distinguishes the epistemic version of the difficulty (no one can see the whole) from the metaphysical one (how could micro-subjects compose a macro-subject), since the activity naturally fakes the first and can only gesture at the second. Include an honest note on which features of the combination problem no activity of this kind can reproduce, and a prediction of what will go wrong the first time I run it, with a fix.

Two further constraints: the debrief should also say whether the activity stacks the deck against a structuralist reply to the combination problem, and if so, how I could run a variant that doesn't. And please tell me, at the end, how confident you are in each part of the design — flag the component you're least sure will produce the intended effect, and what I should watch for to tell whether it's working.
