I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums since the Overcoming Bias days, and over that time I've watched "memetic" explanations get used in two opposite ways. Sometimes they demystify: religions and ideologies are described as spreading the way viruses do, because variants that induce their own propagation win regardless of their truth. Other times they explain nothing at all: an idea "spread because it was memetically fit," which is uncomfortably close to "it spread because it spread." The community's own discussions of memetic hazards — ideas dangerous partly because they're catchy — tacitly assume the demystifying version works, while its critics in the same threads suspect the whole framework is a tautology dressed in Darwinian vocabulary.

I want to know whether the second worry can be answered, and I've secured the chance to apply for a six-month, part-time research stipend from a small foundation sympathetic to longtermist and information-environment work. The half-formed idea: the Darwinian theory of culture, from Dawkins's original meme concept through Boyd and Richerson's dual-inheritance models, is only empirically interesting if it can make predictions or retrodictions that neither ordinary intentional explanation ("people taught it because they believed it was true or useful") nor individual-level learning ("people updated toward it on the evidence available to them") could make. Whether it can do that seems to hinge on the role of biased transmission — copying fidelity, conformity, prestige effects — and on whether "memetic fitness" can be operationalized independently of the content-based reasons we already cite for a belief's spread.

It should also anticipate the funder's obvious challenge — why fund armchair philosophy about memes rather than empirical research on misinformation — with an answer that would satisfy a program officer.
