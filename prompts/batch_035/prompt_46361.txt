In my social theory seminar the professor keeps using 'the whole is greater than the sum of its parts' as a trump card against methodological individualism — most recently to argue that a bank run, or a crowd stampede, cannot be explained by anything true of the depositors or the crowd members taken individually, and that only a properly holistic social science can touch such cases. Before I push back, I want to know what the slogan actually commits anyone to. Set out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing — from the innocuous point that aggregate outcomes are not simple additive functions of individual contributions, through claims about causal interdependence (my preference to withdraw depends on your withdrawing, each person's inputs depending on others' outputs), to the strong ontological claim that social facts do not even supervene on the total facts about individuals, and the separate methodological claim that individual-level evidence could never confirm a social-level explanation — and say of each whether it is trivial, defensible, or outright false, and whether methodological individualists actually deny it. Then don't leave the choice to me: tell me which reading my professor needs to get the bank-run conclusion, whether that reading is true, and how much an individualist can concede to the harmless readings without giving up anything that matters. If the only true readings are the ones that do no anti-individualist work, say so plainly. Please answer in British English.
