I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy, currently leading the discussion section for our department's philosophy of language proseminar. We've spent three weeks on Frege's puzzle about identity, and I've noticed that essentially every case in circulation — the morning and evening star, Cicero and Tully, the superhero and his reporter alias — involves terms that refer. A classmate floated the suggestion in seminar that the puzzle arguably becomes sharper, not weaker, when reference fails: if two terms are both empty, an identity statement linking them can still feel informative, truth-evaluable, and worth arguing about, which looks like trouble for anyone who wants to explain informativeness via modes of presentation and trouble for anyone who wants to explain it via reference. I want to build a clean case to test this in section, and I'd like your help constructing it.

Here is the specification. Design a scenario, set somewhere utterly mundane — an apartment building, a small workplace, a rural township — in which ordinary people, reasoning in earnest from evidence (sounds, tracks, missing items, discrepancies in an expense report), introduce two singular terms for what they take to be two posited agents: think a ghost, a prowler, an inside saboteur, though you should feel free to invent something better. The terms must not come from fiction, myth, or any kind of role-playing — the characters are making genuine epistemic posits, the way one posits a burglar from a ransacked garage. Over the course of the scenario, the question whether the two terms pick out the same thing must arise naturally and be action-guiding: someone must offer an identity statement and others must accept or reject it for practical reasons — because it matters whether they're dealing with one threat or two. Narrate the case from the outside, and include the stipulation that in reality nothing answers to either term (the sounds were raccoons, the discrepancies a software glitch), though the characters never learn this. Keep the scenario to one paragraph, and use no famous examples — no lost planets, no recognizable fictional characters. A case that merely stipulates that the identity statement is informative, rather than generating its informativeness from the characters' epistemic situation, doesn't count.

After the scenario, I need the walkthrough. First, the treatment on a broadly Fregean picture: what senses the two terms would have, what the identity statement expresses, and why it is informative to the characters — and then an honest assessment of whether the case worsens the Fregean's own embarrassment about empty terms, since if sense determines reference, it looks mysterious how two terms can differ in sense while sharing a failure of reference. Second, the treatment on a Millian picture where a name's semantic contribution is just its bearer: what, if anything, the identity statement expresses, and which of the familiar escape routes you would actually endorse in your own terms — a truth-valueless or gappy proposition, a paraphrase of the statement into a general claim about the evidence, or some pragmatic or pretense-based story — and how you'd answer the objection that none of these captures why the characters treat the statement as a substantive, disagreeable truth about a particular thing. Third, tell me which feature of the case is doing the work, and walk through what happens if you remove it in each of a few ways: if the terms are explicitly fictional, if one term is introduced by pure stipulation rather than inference from evidence, or if you reveal in-story that one term does refer after all — does the case then collapse back toward the ordinary version of the puzzle? I don't need citations or reading lists; phrase everything in your own terms, since I care about whether the construction works, not about who said it first.
