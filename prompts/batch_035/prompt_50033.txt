I'm a physician, and I keep noticing a pattern in how colleagues talk after bad outcomes. A doctor takes a calculated risk and the patient survives, and everyone calls the call "decisive" or "good clinical judgment." Another doctor takes what looks, from the chart, like the same calculated risk, the patient dies, and within a week the same people are calling it "reckless" — and, crucially, they don't say "it was the same call but it went badly." They insist the calls were different all along: the bad outcome revealed what the decision "really was," while the good outcome was just luck attaching to a properly careful act. The outcome seems to be treated as evidence about the act itself rather than as a further fact about the world. I've seen the same move outside medicine — a fund manager's bet is "prescient" if it pays and "gambling" if it doesn't, with commentators in each case claiming they could have seen the character of the bet from the start.

I know this lives somewhere near moral luck and near what psychologists call outcome bias, but neither seems to name the move I mean. Moral luck, as I understand it, is the puzzle about whether outcomes ought to affect blame and responsibility; outcome bias is the tendency to judge decision quality by results. What I'm after is a descriptive, almost grammatical phenomenon: the retroactive rewriting of what the act was, so that the act-description itself (decisive vs. reckless, prudent vs. cowardly) gets fixed by the outcome, and everyone then sincerely denies that outcome did any of the work. Is there an established term for this specific reclassification in the philosophy or psychology literature, and if so, how exactly does it differ from outcome bias and from moral luck proper? If it has no name, coin one, define it precisely, and give the boundary cases — for instance, where it shades into legitimate learning from outcomes, or into hindsight bias, so that it doesn't simply collapse into either. Please answer in American English.
