I'm a PhD student in cognitive science working at the border of philosophy of mind and motor-skill research, and I've been asked to moderate a workshop between two faculty members who have been arguing about the knowledge-how / knowledge-that distinction for the better part of a decade. One is an intellectualist in the mold of Stanley and Williamson: she holds that knowing how to do something is propositional knowledge of a way of doing it, presented in a practical mode of presentation, so that Ryle's distinction between knowing-how and knowing-that marks at most a difference in subject matter, not a difference in kind of knowledge. The other is a Rylean of the old school: he holds that knowing how is fundamentally ability — a complex of dispositions acquired through training — and that analyzing it as propositional knowledge either collapses the very distinction that matters or invites Ryle's regress of ever more acts of contemplating propositions before acting. What I need from you is not an adjudication of who is right, but the document an adversarial collaboration is supposed to produce, so that I have a draft to bring into the room.

Please write the joint statement the two of them could both sign. It should contain, in order: (1) a numbered section of claims both can accept — about the linguistic data (the way knows-how-to-F ascriptions embed in questions and infinitival complements), about the phenomenology and psychology of skilled performance (declarative/procedural dissociations, the automaticity of expert action, novice-to-expert transitions), and about what Ryle's regress argument does and does not establish (they can presumably agree it fails as a demonstration that knowledge-how is never propositional, while disagreeing about what follows); (2) a disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is stated twice — once in wording the intellectualist would accept as a fair statement of her own view, and once in wording the Rylean would accept as a fair statement of his — covering at least the epistemic work the practical mode of presentation is doing, whether the direction of explanation runs from propositional knowledge to ability or the reverse, and whether cases of apparent ability without knowledge (the chicken-sexer who cannot say how, the native speaker with no grasp of the rules) versus apparent knowledge without ability (the injured golf pro who can still instruct, the theory-saturated diver who cannot execute) are the central data or peripheral ones; (3) the cruxes — my own suspicion is that one is whether the semantics of knowledge-how ascriptions settles anything about the metaphysics of skill, and another is whether expert automaticity should be described as the disappearance of knowledge or its sedimentation, but you may find better; and (4) for each crux, one concrete case, experiment, or argument that both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance.

Two final requests. First, keep every formulation of the disagreements neutral enough that neither party could accuse the document of stacking the deck — that is the part I keep failing at when I try to draft this myself. Second, if any portion of their dispute strikes you as verbal — a quarrel over the word knowledge rather than over the mind — identify which portion and explain why, because my own working hypothesis is that the interesting residue is empirical (in skill acquisition and expertise studies) rather than semantic, and I want to know whether the document you produce would vindicate that hypothesis or undermine it.
