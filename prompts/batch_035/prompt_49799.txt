My metaphysics reading group has finally snapped: two of us have been going round and round for a year, one a fairly committed Russellian panpsychist, the other an emergentist about consciousness who thinks the combination problem is fatal to the panpsychist side (she's sympathetic to strong emergence but thinks it's a principled stopping point, not a mystery to be absorbed into matter everywhere). We've agreed to stop debating in the chat and instead do this properly. Please write the joint statement the two of us should be able to sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

Start the response with the statement itself, no preamble and no commentary about what you're doing -- the document should just begin.

What I want in it:

1. A numbered section of claims both can sign. These should include the things they actually agree on, not just boilerplate: e.g. that something needs saying about how phenomenal consciousness relates to microphysics, that the "structure and dynamics" of physics leaves qualitative character underdetermined, that both the emergence of consciousness from non-conscious matter and the combination of micro-experiences into macro-experiences are hard to spell out without hand-waving, and whatever else you think they genuinely share.

2. A table of the remaining disagreements, where each row states the disagreement in wording the relevant side would actually accept as a fair statement of its own view. This is the bit I care most about -- I don't want the emergentist's position summarised by the panpsychist or vice versa. Include at minimum: whether the combination problem is worse than, comparable to, or better than the emergence problem it's meant to replace; whether "phenomenal properties as intrinsic natures of physical dispositions" is explanatory or just a redescription; whether strong emergence is coherent; and whether total credence across the live views (emergentism, panpsychism, dualism, illusionism) should sum to 1 in a way that makes them rivals at all, or whether some of them fail to genuinely engage with the others.

3. The cruxes -- the subquestions such that settling one would move at least one of us. I suspect one crux is whether there is a principled difference between "many small subjects whose experiences ground one big subject" and "one big subject whose parts have no experiential status at all", since if that distinction can't be made sharp, both views may be doing less work than they think. Another is probably whether the individuation of subjects (why is my experience one experience, and why aren't there a million micro-experiences happening "alongside" it right now) is a problem for everyone or a special gift to the anti-panpsychist. But find the real ones, including any I haven't spotted.

4. For each crux, a case, thought experiment or argument both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Use concrete cases rather than methodology talk -- e.g. things like gradual neural replacement, split-brain, the possibility of partial unity of experience, cases where it's indeterminate whether there is one subject or two. If part of our year-long argument turns out to have been verbal (e.g. disagreement about what "grounding" means that dissolves once fixed), say which part and why, in the statement itself -- we'd rather find that out now.

Keep the tone of a document two people wrote together, so no rhetorical victories for either side, and if a point is genuinely unresolved, leave it unresolved rather than papering over it. Also please flag anywhere the two views can't actually be made to disagree about a testable or even a cleanly describable scenario -- if the whole dispute bottoms out in competing intuitions about simplicity, we want that said plainly. Bit long I know, but we've earnt it.
