In philosophy of mind, the default picture of the self for several decades has been Daniel Dennett's: the self is to the person as a center of gravity is to a physical object — not a component or an organ but an abstract point defined by a distribution (of mass in the one case, of narratives in the other), unobservable in principle yet indispensable for prediction, with the corollary that questions like "where in the brain is the self, really?" are ill-posed in the way "where in the hoop is its center of gravity, really?" would be if you insisted the answer had to name a piece of matter. The conclusion this analogy is made to carry is deflationary: no inner audience, no deeper fact of the matter beneath the narratives — the self is a useful posit, real enough as an abstraction, and talk of personal identity is instrumentally fine but should not be reified. A friend of mine thinks the analogy has more mileage in it than Dennett himself extracts. He notes that a center of gravity can lie in empty space (a hoop's lies in the hole), can shift discontinuously as mass is redistributed, and that where matter is arranged suitably a single body can seem to have two local centers of attraction rather than one. So, he argues, the analogy should license: selves located partly outside the organism, constituted by other people's narratives, diaries, and reputations; selves moving discontinuously, as in religious conversion or in someone's saying she is not the person she was, without any residual metaphysical puzzle; and two selves in one head in split-brain and dissociative cases, the analogy treating this as one distribution with two centers rather than as a mystery. I want an audit. First, give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the mass distribution, what plays the role of the observations from which the center is inferred, and what is the trajectory the posit exists to predict. Second, the disanalogies — the one that strikes me most is reflexivity, that a narrative changes the narratee, whereas a mass distribution is wholly indifferent to the center of gravity computed from it and to any story told about it — but sort out which disanalogies actually bear on the deflationary conclusion and which are noise. Third, take my friend's three extensions one at a time: which the analogy genuinely supports, and which it cannot decide either way — in the split-brain case, is the analogy able to answer whether the two selves were there all along or came into being, or does it merely restate the fission question in a new vocabulary? Finally, the verdict: does Dennett's deflationary conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the conclusion all along — and if the latter, say what a better source domain would have to be like: something abstract and inferential in the way a center of gravity is, but that, unlike a mass distribution, responds to being posited.
