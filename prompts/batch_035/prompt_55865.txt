My phil of knowledge class got a visiting lecture on Nozick's tracking theory and I found my notes confusing, so I'll just type out what I remember the speaker saying and hopefully someone can tell me if I have it right. He said Nozick's idea is that knowing that p means believing p, p being true, and your belief *tracking* the truth of p -- meaning, if p were false you wouldn't believe it. So on this view a brain in a vat doesn't know it has hands, because if it didn't have hands it would still be fed the same experiences and would still believe it has hands. He also said this tracking requirement is basically just counterfactual dependence: your belief depends on the fact in the same way the length of a flag's shadow depends on the length of the flagpole. Then he talked about closure, and said the theory implies a kind of embarrassment: if I know I have hands, and I know that having hands entails I'm not a handless brain in a vat, then by closure I should know I'm not a brain in a vat -- but the tracking theory says I *don't* know that, so the theory has to say I don't know I have hands either, which nobody accepts, so the theory is pretty much considered refuted these days. He wrapped up by saying sensitivity also gives weird results in reverse, like the grandmother who hears from her grandson regularly and knows he's well even though if he were sick the family would hide it from her -- so the tracking conditions are both too strong and too weak. Sooo my question is just: how fair is this? Which parts would a Nozick defender sign off on, which parts are subtly off (that flagpole comparison bugs me for reasons I can't articulate), and does the "considered refuted" bit actually follow?
