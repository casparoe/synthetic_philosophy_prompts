For the final exam of my upper-level seminar "Historiography: Theory and Method" — a mixed group of final-year history majors, philosophy students, and two MA candidates — I need one essay question on presentism: whether historians can escape the standards of their own time. The exam is a three-hour sit-down with two questions; candidates will have roughly seventy minutes for this one, closed book. The problem with every draft I've written so far is that it can be answered by reciting the seminar's first-week slogans — "avoid anachronism," "understand actors in their own terms," "don't judge the past by the present." My students have memorized that vocabulary; I want to find out whether they can use it under pressure, in a case where the vocabulary doesn't settle anything.

The question should be built around a concrete scenario, which you should embed verbatim in the exam paper so it's fully self-contained. Here is the case I want: a fictional student, R., has written a thesis chapter on a fictional nineteenth-century municipal reformer, E. M. Corven (1838–1902), who campaigned successfully for clean water and sewers in a fast-growing industrial city — reforms that demonstrably saved thousands of lives — and whose private papers also show her as an early and enthusiastic advocate of segregating and eventually sterilizing people classified as feeble-minded, a cause she shared with several respected medical voices of her own generation. The chapter reports both facts without commentary. Two examiners have now clashed over it: one demands an added paragraph of explicit moral condemnation, arguing that silence in the face of such views is itself a moral stance; the other demands the removal of all evaluative language, calling the proposed paragraph "presentism" and a failure of historical empathy. The exam question should ask the candidate to adjudicate this disagreement and, crucially, to say whether it is really a disagreement about presentism at all.

Please deliver three things: (1) the exam question itself, written so it cannot be discharged by summarizing the standard presentism-versus-contextualism debate; (2) a model answer of about 600 words; (3) a marking scheme of five must-hit points, 4 marks each for a total of 20, each with a one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong. I want the marking scheme to build in the following, which my students will not find in their lecture notes: at least one must-hit point should be that the refusal to evaluate is not a neutral, timeless stance but itself a norm with a history — the ideal of value-neutral scholarship is our period's commitment, which cuts against any blanket appeal to "escaping our standards"; at least one should be the applause asymmetry — we happily credit Corven for the sanitation reforms by standards she did not fully share, and call that history rather than presentism, so the objection to condemnation cannot simply be "the standards aren't hers"; at least one should require distinguishing conceptual anachronism from moral evaluation, noting that describing Corven's position as eugenic is not anachronistic in the way calling it by some later label would be. Beyond the five must-hit points, the scheme should say in two or three sentences what separates an A answer from a B answer — I expect the difference to lie in whether the candidate sees that "whose standards?" is the wrong question if asked once, and only a good question if the candidate goes on to ask which standards could be defended to the historical actors themselves, on terms those actors had some access to. The question, model answer, and scheme should assume no specialized knowledge beyond what an attentive reader gets from the embedded scenario, and should not require or reward citations of particular historians or works.
