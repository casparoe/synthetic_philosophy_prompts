i edit a small magazine where every issue pairs a short life of someone admirable with a short life of someone who went wrong — sort of Plutarch-style, though with modern people. The whole pitch is that readers pick up character better from lives than from rules. I've been in a running argument with a friend who writes for a religious audience and is convinced any admiring life has to lean hagiographic — clean up the flaws, or nobody comes away with something to imitate. Before we go another round on specific people, I want the spec first.

Write the desiderata: what an adequate use of biography for moral learning has to deliver. I'm thinking things like — which cases it has to handle (the subject with one ruinous flaw, the person who was right about the big thing and aweful in private life, the admirable-but-not-immitable genius), the distinctions it can't collapse (learning about a person vs learning from a person, imitating vs merely admiring), and whatever constraints come from how people actually absorb exemplars. Give the case or consideration behind each requirement, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are really just the taste of one camp. My friend's camp will say "must present an imitable ideal," mine will say "must be honest about the subject's failures," and I suspect we can't always have both. If some subset of the requirements turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable, say so plainly — that's the part I most want to see, and tell me which side each unsatisfiable pair is forcing a choice between.

Two more things. Don't score actual biographies or lives against the list, I'll do that myself — just the spec. And if you think the premise itself is off — that nobody really learns morality from lives, that Plutarch's pairs were mostly entertainment for readers who already agreed — say that straight instead of handing me a polite list. Same if you think one of my suggested requirements above is just my prejudice dressed up as a spec. I'd rather hear that than get the version built to please me.
