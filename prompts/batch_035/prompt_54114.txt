I've been reading about tranquilism — the view that the only thing that matters hedonically is the absence of craving and suffering, so that a mind free of both is not merely good but as good as anything can be, since any "positive" happiness adds nothing beyond relief. A friend of mine who holds this view leans on a comparison he says settles the matter: pleasure is like the pleasure of scratching an itch or of finally eating when you're starving. It seems like a positive good, but it is really just the removal of a prior discomfort, and the calm you feel when the scratching stops is all there ever was to enjoy. He generalizes this to everything: excitement is craving for novelty, affection is craving for company, achievement is craving for status — so tranquility is the ceiling, and the rest is just the itch varying. Audit the analogy for me. Lay out what is supposed to map onto what — the itch, the scratch, the relief — then identify where the mapping breaks down: are there pleasures not preceded by any felt deficit, such as listening to music you had no desire to hear? Is relief itself a positive felt quality or merely the absence of the bad, and does the analogy quietly assume that desire is always aversive? Separate the disanalogies that matter for the tranquilist conclusion from the ones that don't, and say whether that conclusion survives once they are on the table, or whether the itch was carrying it the whole time. If some other comparison would serve the tranquilist better, say what it is.
