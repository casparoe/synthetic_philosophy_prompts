I teach an upper-level epistemology seminar (about 15 students, all of whom did intro epistemology and have read the Meno) and I'm putting together the take-home final. I want one essay question on the Meno problem — why is knowledge better than merely true belief — but I'm tired of getting answers that recite Plato's tethering bit (true opinions running off like Daedalus's statues until you tie them down with an account of the reason) and then declare victory. Same for the students who just restate the swamping problem using Zagzebski's coffee machine and call it a day.

So here's what I want the question built around — a case I made up that strips out the usual crutches. A locum doctor is covering one final night shift at a rural clinic before permanently leaving medicine. A patient presents with a rare reaction, and the correct call is that drug X is contraindicated. Doctor A knows this — she looks it up in a source that, stipulate, is in fact perfectly reliable. Doctor B, in the possible world next door, has a true hunch: same patient, same night, hunch happens to be right, same treatment decision, patient survives in both worlds, and — stipulate this too — neither doctor ever practices again, never faces the question again, and never once revisits the belief. It is a one-shot belief in the fullest sense: nothing downstream of tonight will ever test it, use it, or require it to stay put.

The stipulations are the whole point. Plato's tether only adds value if the opinion would otherwise have run away later, and there is no later. The reliable source only adds value at the output if the output would otherwise have been false, and it isn't — B's belief is already true. And the practical outcome is identical. So if we still think something epistemically better happened in A's night than in B's — and I want the question to force students to actually commit on whether we do think that — the extra value has to live somewhere unusual: in the manner of acquisition (but then what happens to the swamping problem?), in the ex ante standing of the action (but is that even epistemic value, or have we slid into the practical value of acting well?), or in counterfactuals the stipulation doesn't actually remove.

Please write me three things. (1) The question itself, around 120 words, set up so it can't be answered by reciting the value-problem taxonomy — the case has to do the work, not the labels. (2) A model answer of roughly 600 words that takes a definite stance (I don't care which stance, as long as its load-bearing and argued for). It's allowed to say the case underdetermines where the value lives, but if it says that it must still use the case to rule some things out, not just muse. (3) A marking scheme, 20 marks total, that seperates an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain — in particular I want it to catch the two mistakes I most expect students to make here: (a) quietly sliding from "A acted more reasonably" to "A's belief is epistemically more valuable" without noticing those can come apart, and (b) praising the reliable source without seeing that in this case the reliability made no difference to the output, which is just the swamping problem wearing a stethoscope. No bibliography or citation apparatus needed in the model answer — naming the Meno where relevant is plenty. And don't let "A answers show deeper insight" style vagueness into the marking scheme; every mark should be attached to something concrete a grader can point at in the text.
