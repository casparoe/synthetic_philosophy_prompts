In debates over inheritance taxation and dynastic wealth, one side leans heavily on what we might call the "fair race" analogy: society is imagined as a footrace in which equal opportunity means everyone begins at the same starting line, and inherited wealth amounts to a head start that the rules should neutralise, presumably through taxation. The other side counters that a family is better pictured as a relay team than as a set of independent runners: what opponents call dynastic advantage is simply the baton pass between generations, and the whole point of the race, if it is one, is run by teams rather than individuals. Both pictures are doing real argumentative work — the first to license redistribution at the point of transfer, the second to paint such redistribution as a violation of the family's integrity rather than a correction of unfairness.

Please audit both analogies. Set out explicitly what maps onto what in each (the runner, the starting line, the baton, the team, the finish line, the prize), where each mapping breaks down, and which features of the source domain each analogy quietly imports that the target domain lacks — for instance, whether races have known endpoints, agreed measures of success, and entry only by individuals. Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusions from those that do not. Finally, assess whether either conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and propose a better analogy for the intergenerational transmission of wealth if you think one is available.
