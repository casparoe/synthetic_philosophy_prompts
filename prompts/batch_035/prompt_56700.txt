I'm writing an essay for a philosophy of literature seminar that pairs Borges's "Funes the Memorious" with Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, and I'd like your help developing the reading before I draft it. The hook I'm working with is this: Nietzsche argues that forgetting is not a defect but a condition of life, happiness, and action, and that a person or culture without the power to forget is condemned to a kind of paralysis; Borges gives us Ireneo Funes, who after an accident forgets nothing and as a result, on his own report, can barely think, since thinking requires abstraction and abstraction requires ignoring differences. So the story seems to dramatize Nietzsche's claim in miniature. Please act as a professional philosopher with expertise in both Nietzsche and literary criticism, and think carefully before committing to a line: first work out in your own mind how the story maps onto the essay, then give me the reading. Specifically, I want you to address how Funes relates to Nietzsche's three modes of history. Funes's memory is obviously a hypertrophied version of something — but of which mode? He accumulates the past in exhaustive, meaningless detail, which sounds like antiquarian history run to pathology; yet the essay also says the antiquarian preserves life by rooting it, while Funes's memory destroys his life, so where exactly does the mapping break? Does Funes ever do anything recognizably like monumental history, selecting and idealizing the past to inspire action, or critical history, judging and destroying the past to make room? If he does neither, that may be the point: Nietzsche's claim is about the uses of history, and Funes's memory is use-less in a precise sense worth spelling out. I also want the reading to be faithful in both directions: say where Borges resists being read as an illustration of Nietzsche, for instance that Nietzsche is diagnosing a culture and Funes is a single, physically injured, extremely poor young man, and that Borges's story has its own preoccupations — with the price of total particularity, with whether a language of perfectly individual terms is even possible — that are not Nietzsche's. Then tell me what, if anything, the story adds to Nietzsche's argument that the argument alone does not give us, and which single scene or detail you'd build the essay around. Please write continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, and do not invent or approximate quotations from either text; refer to passages descriptively rather than by wording. The essay is around three thousand words, so tell me which parts of this reading deserve the most space and which are decorative.
