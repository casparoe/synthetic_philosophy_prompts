I chair a philosophy department of about thirty faculty, and every December and May our executive committee spends a long evening deciding which of our multi-year commitments to keep, reshape, or wind down: a curriculum reform now three years in that most instructors quietly ignore, a half-built digital archive of our colloquium recordings, a mentoring program for first-generation graduate students that hasn't moved the numbers, and — my own favorite — a spring conference we have run for two decades whose attendance has halved. Between six and ten such items per meeting, ninety minutes total, real stakes for students and colleagues, and several decisions that are politically radioactive. Our standing instinct is the one drilled into every graduate student: sunk costs are irrelevant, decide as if you were starting fresh today. But I have come to suspect the textbook injunction is blunter than the decision theory behind it. Sometimes the years and money already spent seem to matter — not as costs, but as evidence about the project's quality, or as signals to collaborators and funders who need to know we finish what we start, or as part of what makes the project the project at all: a reform abandoned at year three may be worse than no reform, for reasons having to do with precedent and with our identity as planners, not with marginal payoffs.

What I want from you is a procedure the committee can actually run: a one-page ordered list of questions, answerable by people with no philosophical training in roughly ten minutes per item, with a clear statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, where the procedure terminates in a recommendation to stop, and — crucially — where it hands the decision back to human judgment because the remaining question is one of values or politics rather than facts. In constructing it, please take the philosophical complications seriously rather than waving at them: (i) when past investment is genuinely evidential, so that the standard "would we start this today, knowing what we know, at the remaining cost?" test misleads; (ii) when sunk costs generate reasons through commitment — precedent, morale, the credibility of our future promises to students and partners, the way a partially executed plan can acquire a rational authority of its own; (iii) how to keep the fallacy proper out, i.e., unrecoverable costs entering the weighing as though they were future ones, including its disguised forms ("we can't waste what we've already put in," loyalty to colleagues who did the work); and (iv) where loss aversion, waste aversion, and status quo bias will predictably corrupt each step, so the procedure can build in guards against them. And be honest about which of your steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on three of our actual cases: the curriculum reform, a fifth-year doctoral student whose committee is divided over whether to support one more year versus advising a terminal master's, and the spring conference. Run it straight even where the answer will be unwelcome — I suspect that will be the conference, and I would rather see the procedure bite than flatter me. Close with a short statement of what the procedure cannot do: which decision types it should never be applied to, and where continuing versus quitting is simply a question of what kind of department we intend to be, which no checklist is entitled to answer for us.
