My flatmate and I had a colossal argument last night that ran past two in the morning, and I'm still annoyed, so I want to hand it off to someone who can diagnose it rather than just take a side. It started over whether some film we'd both watched was objectively bad or whether he just didn't like it, and it escalated into the whole question of whether aesthetic judgement can be right or wrong at all. He took the hard subjectivist line — beauty in the eye of the beholder, no fact of the matter, end of story. I brought up Hume's essay on the standard of taste: the point about how Milton has lasted while forgotten poets sink, and how when we say a critic is good at judging we don't just mean we happen to agree with them, we seem to mean they're discerning in some real way — sensitive, practised, free of prejudice, comparing works against each other. He didn't budge. He said the test of time just shows popularity is persistent, not that it tracks anything, and then he hit me with the circularity point which I'll admit stung: Hume's ideal critics are identified partly by the verdicts they reach, so you can't use those verdicts to define what good taste is without going in a circle.

Here's what I actually want to know, and please don't just adjudicate who won the kitchen-table argument, I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. The literature on this seems to have been going round in circles for literally centuries, which makes me suspect something structural is going on. So: is the dispute between the objectivist and the subjectivist substantive at all, or is it partly verbal — a fight over what we're allowed to do with the word 'objective' once it's detached from, say, observational predictions? Is it that the two sides are secretly answering different questions (one a sociological or psychological question about how our judgements actually work, the other a normative question about whether any of them are correct)? There's also that odd feature of the debate everyone notes, that ordinary practice is inconsistent: we shrug at disagreements about ice cream flavours but think someone who rates a badly-made thing above a great one is somehow wrong, even though we can't point to what they got wrong the way we can with a false arithmetic claim. Maybe that inconsistency is the real crux — maybe the question inherits the ambiguity of the practice rather than resolving it.

Given whatever diagnosis you land on, I'd want to hear what would even count as progress here — a sharpened distinction, a new account of what ideal critics are doing that breaks the circle, or an argument that the disagreement is bedrock intuition all the way down and no progress should be expected. And be honest if you think Hume's whole apparatus of the true judge is a mistake we should abandon rather than repair. I'm not writing an essay on this or anything, I just want to know whether the argument I had is one that could in principle be settled, or whether my flatmate and I were never quite talking about the same thing in the first place. (For context: neither of us has read Kant on aesthetics beyond a Wikipedia skim, so don't assume I know that side, but you can bring it in if it helps the diagnosis.)
