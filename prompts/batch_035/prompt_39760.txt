Background: I've been reading about money pump arguments and diachronic rationality, and I've collected gut verdicts about whether an agent is irrational in a bunch of sequential-choice situations. I want to know if there's one principle that captures them all. I suspect there isn't, but let's find out.

The cases (one agent, a sequence of choices, sometimes a broker offering trades):

1. Classic pump. My preferences over three options A, B, C are pairwise: I prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A. A broker offers me B in exchange for A plus a dollar, then C for B plus a dollar, then A for C plus a dollar. I take each trade as it comes and end up back where I started, minus three dollars. Verdict: obviuosly irrational, no hesitation.

2. Same cycle, but I see the whole setup coming — the broker's scheme is transparent to me from the start. Verdict: still irrational, though maybe in a different way? Like the real problem is the intransitivity itself, and the pump is just a demonstration of it.

3. Same cyclic preferences, but no broker exists. I just drift between options on my own, each time choosing what I then prefer, never getting anywhere — the intransitive version of rearranging your furniture forever. Verdict: irrational, but less obviously so than 1. Nobody exploiting me doesn't seem to make it OK.

4. Taste change. Before a vacation I choose a hiking trip over a beach resort. Once I'm on the hike, I genuinely prefer the beach — it's pouring and my knees hurt — and I pay a change fee to switch. The change is one-directional, no loop. Verdict: totally fine. That's just being a person whose preferences respond to circumstances. Even though the resort profits from my changeability, there's no pump.

5. Akrasia version. Friday me plans to write all Saturday, and on Friday I prefer that plan to goofing off. Saturday me prefers goofing off, and goofs off. Verdict: irrational, somehow. And here's my problem: structurally this looks a LOT like 4. In both, the agent's preferrences differ between times and the agent does what they then prefer. Why is the hiker fine and Saturday-me not fine? My instinct is that the hiker's change came from the world (rain, sore knees), while Saturday-me's change came from the mere nearness of the fun — no new information about it, just its approach. But I can't make that precise, and "Saturday arrived" looks like new information too, so I'm not sure the distinction survives.

6. One-shot. Same cyclic preferences, but the broker can offer only one trade, ever, and then the market closes. Taking the single trade leaves me a dollar down with no possibility of the loop. Verdict: hmm, this feels less bad than the full pump — but the preferences are identical to 1 and 2, where I said the problem was the intransitivity rather than the loss. So either 6 is as bad as 1, or the intransitivity wasn't really the problem in 1. I go back and forth.

7. Binding. On Friday, someone offers to sell me a commitment that will override Saturday's goofing preference — a locked study, a forfeit I've paid in advance, whatever. I buy it. Verdict: obviously fine, actually smart. Paying to constrain your future self is not being pumped.

So: is there a principle that gets all seven? Candidates I've half-formed, feel free to demolish: (a) you're diachronically irrational only if someone can actually pump you — which my verdicts on 3 and 4 seem to refute in both directions; (b) you're irrational iff your preferences are intransitive — which fits 1–3 and 6, but then the Saturday agent's problem has to be something else entirely, since his preferences at any moment aren't intransitive; (c) something about where the preference change comes from — new information about the world vs. mere passage of time — which is the only thing that sorts 4 from 5, but every way I state it looks ad hoc.
