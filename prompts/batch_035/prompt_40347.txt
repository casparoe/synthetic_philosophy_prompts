You are a scholar of medieval political philosophy, specialising in Thomas Aquinas's political writings, with additional training in the history of the late Roman Republic. You have been asked to contribute an analytical essay to a reading group that pairs Thomist political theory with classical history. Please think carefully and work methodically: before writing the essay, briefly brainstorm the possible mappings and set aside the ones that would force the material, then write up the essay proper.

Your task: analyse the assassination of Julius Caesar on the Ides of March, 44 BC, through the lens of Aquinas's views on tyrannicide. I am not interested in the well-worn question of whether Brutus was justified in some generic sense; I want the disciplined application of a specific theory to a specific episode, and an honest account of where the theory illuminates the case, where it is silent, and where it misdescribes what was actually at stake.

To keep the analysis anchored, take the following facts as given rather than contesting them: the conspirators, drawn from the senatorial class and led by Brutus and Cassius, killed Caesar in a meeting of the Senate; they claimed to act as liberators of a republic whose liberties Caesar had been eroding; they had no agreed plan for what would follow; Caesar's death did not restore the Republic but instead produced more than a decade of further civil war, the proscriptions of the Second Triumvirate, and finally the principate of Augustus.

You should draw on the relevant threads of Aquinas's thought, reconstructing his position from the treatise on kingship, De Regno, and from the Summa's treatment of whether private persons may kill. The core materials you should work with, stated fairly but in your own words, are: his distinction between the tyrant by usurpation and the ruler who becomes tyrannical through misrule; his view that private persons lack the authority to kill, while public authority may lawfully kill; his prudential caution that killing a tyrant typically makes matters worse, since a people that tolerates tyranny gets worse tyrants, and the murder itself sets a precedent that invites further violence; and his suggestion that where a community has the collective power to do so, or where there is a legitimate superior authority, tyrants may be removed. Note that scholars disagree about how permissive or restrictive De Regno ultimately is on tyrannicide; you should acknowledge this interpretive range rather than silently picking a side, and say how the analysis would shift under the stricter and looser readings.

Your essay should do at least the following:

1. Fix the mapping explicitly. Is Caesar a tyrant by usurpation or a legitimate magistrate degenerating into tyranny, and why does the classification matter within Aquinas's framework? Who, in the Rome of 44 BC, could count as the public authority with the power of the sword, and were the conspirators private persons or representatives of a legitimate order? Be careful here: the answer is not obvious, and part of the value of the essay is showing where the Roman case resists the medieval categories.

2. Test the prudential thesis. Aquinas's striking claim is that tyrannicide usually fails to relieve the underlying disease, and that the attempt often inaugurates worse suffering. The Ides of March looks, on its face, like a confirmation. Say precisely what it confirms, what it does not, and whether the confirmation is cheap, given how often in history removing a ruler by violence has been followed by instability.

3. Identify where the lens breaks. Where is Aquinas simply silent about the Roman situation, and where does his framework actively misdescribe it? Consider in particular: he theorises kingship, not a republic with contested and institutionally divided claims to authority; he writes with a Christian order in mind in which the sources of legitimate authority are comparatively determinate; and he addresses the despair of individuals, not the self-understanding of an aristocratic faction convinced it embodies the state. Does his rule that the diagnosis of the disease is prior to the prescription apply cleanly when the disputants cannot even agree on what the body politic is?

4. Run the analysis in both directions. Conclude by saying what the episode shows about the theory: does it vindicate Aquinas's caution, expose a gap in his account of legitimacy, or reveal that his framework was never meant for cases like this? Do not force a tidy verdict; if the episode cuts in more than one direction, say so and explain which cuts are the deepest and why.

Aim for an essay of roughly 1,000 to 1,400 words. Quote nothing verbatim unless you are certain of it; paraphrase Aquinas in your own words. Avoid anachronism on both sides: do not credit the conspirators with modern republican theory, and do not credit Aquinas with views about popular sovereignty he did not hold. Where you rely on an interpretive judgement about the historical material, flag it as such rather than presenting it as settled fact.
