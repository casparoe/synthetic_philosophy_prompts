Next month I am running a forty-five-minute session on consequentialist cluelessness for a local discussion group whose members are committed effective altruists — bright, energetic, and with no philosophical training beyond what they have picked up in pursuit of doing good. By cluelessness I mean the worry that the remote effects of our actions dwarf and ramify beyond the effects we can foresee, so that even a confident judgment about an act's immediate consequences leaves us without a principled basis for judging its overall consequences. The audience will arrive fluent in expected-value reasoning, cost-effectiveness estimates, and the habit of converting any uncertainty into a probability distribution to be plugged in and averaged over. I anticipate that their reflex will be to file the topic under generic epistemic humility — "of course we can't be certain, that's what confidence intervals are for" — and that reflex is precisely what the session exists to confront, so the plan must meet it rather than talk past it.

Please do not give me the explanation itself; give me a plan for the explanation. Specifically, I want the following. First, the dependency structure: in what order the components must be introduced and why — for instance, whether the distinction between simple cluelessness (we happen to lack information that could in principle be had) and complex cluelessness (our reasons pull in opposite directions and there is no principled way to aggregate them) should come before or after the observation that consequentialist evaluation draws no temporal boundary around an act's consequences, and what goes wrong if the order is reversed. Second, the single carrying example the whole session should be built around, together with its required properties: it must make the ripple structure of consequences visible, it must resist the response that unmodeled effects "wash out" in expectation by symmetry — a response this audience will find deeply congenial — and it must not push listeners toward paralysis, or else the plan should explain why paralysis is an honest intermediate stage on the way out. Say whether the example should be drawn from the world they already work in (distributing malaria nets, funding x-risk research) or from ordinary life, and defend the choice given that an example from their own practice risks feeling like an attack. Third, the misconception map: which misunderstandings this particular audience will form, in the order they will form them, and the exact point in the sequence at which each must be headed off — I would expect confusions between cluelessness and risk, between cluelessness and skepticism about consequentialism as a theory, and between the claim that we cannot know an act's total effects and the claim that we can know it is net positive anyway, but the map should be built from how they will actually misunderstand it, not from how a first-year would. Fourth, what to openly decline to cover at this level, with the refusal stated honestly rather than silently omitted. And finally, a closing question whose answer would reveal whether someone has genuinely distinguished cluelessness from risk, rather than merely been unsettled for an evening. One further demand I am unsure about: I want the audience to leave still able to act, and I cannot tell whether that is a reasonable pedagogical requirement or a refusal on my part to let the topic land. The plan should take a position on which it is, and the sequence should reflect that position. Assume no readings, no handouts, and one whiteboard.
