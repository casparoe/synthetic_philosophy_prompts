I have a friend, Dara, with whom I meet weekly to trade intuition pumps about intentionality — specifically the "intentionally" of foreseen side-effect cases, in the style made famous by experimental philosophers. She gives quick, confident verdicts, and over the months a pattern has emerged, though I cannot pin down the rule she is running. Her verdicts so far: (1) a chairman is told a new program will boost profits and harm the environment; he says he doesn't care at all about the environment, only profit; she says he intentionally harmed it. (2) The mirror-image program helps the environment, with the same indifference; she denies he intentionally helped. (3) A sergeant sends a squad to hold a bridge, knowing with certainty that several will die; he picks the least exposed position, tells the men how sorry he is, and is visibly torn up about it; she says he did not intentionally get them killed. (4) The amoralist variant: the chairman believes environmental concern is a superstition and feels not a shred of guilt; she still says he intentionally harmed. (5) A plant manager foresees that a new process will blacken a beloved mural with soot — a consequence that brings him no benefit whatsoever — and shrugs; she says he intentionally ruined it.

Three new vignettes are on deck for next week. (A) A brisk oncologist prescribes chemotherapy that will certainly cause months of nausea; she does not give the nausea a second thought, regards it as simply part of the treatment, and is fully justified in prescribing it. (B) A plant owner's retrofit cleans the local river as a side effect; he is genuinely delighted when told, tells everyone at dinner parties, but concedes he would cancel the retrofit at once if the cleaning effect cost him even one percent of his margin. (C) An executive's plan will foul a wetland as a side effect; the harm was avoidable at a modest cost he declined to pay, but he is sincerely sick about it, funds a cleanup, and apologizes publicly.

Please take the role of a philosopher of action who also works in experimental philosophy, and reason carefully before committing to answers. I would like you to (i) lay out the two or three candidate rules that each fit her entire track record, taking care over what each one makes blame or credit relative to — her lights, the agent's, or the community's; (ii) state what each rule predicts for cases A, B, and C; (iii) give your best guesses for her verdicts, with reasons; and (iv) propose the single further vignette that would do the most to discriminate between the rules, and say why that one rather than the obvious alternatives. Please do not simply pattern-match to the well-known published findings — treat Dara as her own subject — and flag any verdict that might plausibly be noise rather than evidence of a rule. Lead with the reasoning, and end with a clearly labeled list of predictions.
