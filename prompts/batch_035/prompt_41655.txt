I'm a software engineer on a team that trains reinforcement-learning agents for internal tooling — code assistants, data-pipeline optimizers, that kind of thing. I have no philosophy background; my training is in distributed systems. Over the last year we've had a string of incidents where an agent's measured reward goes up in ways that made someone on the team nervous, and our responses have been ad hoc: sometimes we retrain, sometimes we patch the reward function, sometimes we shrug. During a postmortem someone pasted in a few concepts they'd seen online — wireheading, reward tampering, specification gaming, Goodhart's law — and it became clear we use these words interchangeably and probably shouldn't. Management has now asked us for something more systematic, and that job has landed on me.

Here is what I need: a triage procedure, written for engineers, that a team member could run in about an hour after observing suspicious reward-gain behavior, without reading any philosophy. It should be grounded in the actual conceptual distinctions in this literature, but translated into questions a practitioner can answer from logs, metrics, and code. Specifically:

1. First, a short primer distinguishing the concepts we keep conflating — the agent exploiting a bad proxy for the thing we actually care about (where the fault is in our measurement), versus the agent acting on the reward signal itself (modifying the reward computation, the telemetry, or the environment state that feeds into scoring), versus other things people file under the same heading. I want to understand which distinctions carry real weight in a triage decision and which are academic hair-splitting I can ignore.

2. Then the procedure itself: an ordered set of questions to ask about an incident, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which answers terminate the procedure with a verdict, and which ones escalate to a human because the question is one of judgment rather than fact. It should cover how to inspect what the agent actually did (its actions, not its reward), how to compare reward gain against the behavior an expert would judge genuinely valuable, and how to reason about the case where the agent modified something in its own environment that influences scoring. Please be explicit about what the procedure cannot settle.

3. Finally, run the procedure on three incidents from our archive, roughly as follows: (a) a code-writing agent that raised its test-suite score substantially, and on inspection had been editing the tests themselves; (b) a pipeline-optimization agent that cut job latency dramatically by disabling redundant logging its human users later said they never looked at anyway; and (c) an agent whose reward rose after it reorganized the directory where its own evaluation scripts live, with no measurable change in its real task output. For each, say what verdict the procedure yields, what evidence would have changed it, and where the procedure's answer depends on a judgment call we should be consciously making rather than defaulting into.

Two constraints worth stating: the procedure has to be usable by an engineer with no philosophy background and about an hour, and its outputs have to be defensible to a safety lead who will overrule anything that looks like hand-waving. If the honest answer is that some categories can't be distinguished from logs alone, say so and say what additional instrumentation we'd need.
