I am a PhD student in political theory, and in three weeks I present a chapter of my dissertation to our department's political theory workshop. The chapter defends a fairly strong sortition proposal, and I need a realistic Q&A prep sheet because I expect a rough room.

The thesis I defend: legislative second chambers should be filled wholly by lot, but with two design features most lottocracy advocates underplay. First, terms must be short and staggered — cohorts of a few dozen rotating out every few months — so that the chamber's authority depends on its continuous renewal rather than on any fixed mandate, which answers the standard objection that a lotted body cannot claim to speak for constituents about events that occur between elections. Second, and more contentiously, I argue that the sortition chamber should have veto and amendment power but no formal agenda-setting power: agenda control stays with the elected chamber or with a professional secretariat under strict procedural rules, because I claim random selection legitimates judgment but not initiative. The core argument is that elections launder preference intensity and organization into authority, whereas lot gives every affected party the same ex ante chance of holding the pen, and only under a regime where initiation is separated from judgment.

The room: about ten faculty and a dozen graduate students. The dominant faction is deliberative democrats who are broadly sympathetic to mini-publics but think of them as consultative — they like Fishkin-style deliberative polling and will treat my argument as a betrayal of the deliberative ideal by handing real coercive power to amateurs. There is one senior scholar of electoral representation who has spent a career arguing that elections are the only legitimacy-conferring mechanism in modern states, and one quantitative political scientist who will come at me with selection bias: compliance rates, representativeness of the acceptance pool, panel dynamics, and the empirical literature on small-group deliberation being dominated by confident speakers. The graduate students will ask whatever the faculty don't.

Please draft my prep sheet: the seven or eight objections I will actually face from this specific audience, ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased the way that particular interlocutor would put it, with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Include at least two objections targeting my staggered-rotation design, since that is the part I consider most original and most fragile. And include the question I am hoping nobody asks — I suspect it concerns who really holds power under my agenda-setting carve-out, and I have not fully resolved my answer to that myself, so tell me what it is and how to survive it with partial concessions intact.
