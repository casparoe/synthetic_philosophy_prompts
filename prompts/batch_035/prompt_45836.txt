I coordinate a small moral philosophy reading group—about twenty members, a mix of academics and interested laypeople, meeting in person monthly. Next month we take up standing to blame: when does something about the would-be blamer—her own record, her hypocrisy, her complicity, her relationship to the wrong—make her blame inappropriate even when the target deserves it? We have all read the standard essays, so a quiz that merely relabels the positions will be spotted instantly. I want the opposite: an instrument that sorts members by what they say about cases before they learn which camp that commits them to. Please write it.

Specifically, a ten-question quiz, suitable for printing on a single sheet, with a working title along the lines of "Where do you stand on standing?" Each question must be a concrete case with two or three answer options. No question may mention any philosopher, position label, or the phrase "standing to blame" in a way that telegraphs the answer; a member should be able to answer from her reaction to the case alone. Answers should express what, if anything, is wrong with the blame in the case—not whether the blamer is a good person, and not whether the target deserves blame, which I want held fixed throughout. Then provide a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto positions.

Because our internal disagreements cluster around particular diagnostics, the ten questions must include, in some form, at least these seven: (1) the reformed hypocrite—someone who abandoned the same vice years ago now blaming a friend who still indulges, forcing the quiz-taker to say whether genuine repentance restores standing, or whether standing was never lost, or never lost in the relevant way; (2) the concealed hypocrite—loud public condemnation of exactly what the blamer does privately, under a different description, so the quiz-taker must locate the defect in the double standard, the concealment, or the self-exemption from one's own standard; (3) the blameless bystander—someone with neither record nor temptation blaming a wrong she was never in a position to commit, since several of us suspect that never-tempted blame is in better standing than hypocritical blame while others think it smuggles in an arrogance of its own; (4) the victim versus the uninvolved stranger—both blaming the same wrongdoer for the same act; (5) moral luck—two blamers equally guilty of the same risky wrongdoing, only one of whose luck held, with the unlucky one blaming the lucky one; (6) selective application—blaming out-group members for an offense one quietly forgives in one's own group, which must be probed in a way that distinguishes a standing defect from mere ordinary bad judgment; and (7) structural complicity—blaming individual wrongdoers while benefiting from the same system at a scale no individual choice could alter.

The leaves of the scoring key must include: the equal-standing family associated with R. Jay Wallace's "Hypocrisy, Moral Address, and the Equal Standing of Persons," on which hypocritical blame wrongs the target by implicitly claiming a superiority the blamer does not possess; the interest view, on which standing tracks whether the blamer has a stake in the wrong; the causal-contribution view developed by Patrick Todd in "A Unified Account of the Moral Standing to Blame," on which standing is defeated by having contributed to the very wrong one blames; the reform-restores-standing view; and the skeptical view, of the sort suggested by G. A. Cohen's "Casting the First Stone," on which at least some ways of answering "you too" are legitimate rather than fallacious. If you know of other live positions in this literature that my seven diagnostics fail to place, add them as leaves and tell me which additional case would have been needed to catch them.

Three further requirements after the scoring key. First, tell me plainly which pairs of leaves the quiz cannot separate—if, for instance, the interest view and the causal-contribution view yield identical answers across all ten cases, say so rather than manufacturing a fake contrast question; I would rather know my instrument has a blind spot than pretend it does not. Second, list any consistent answer patterns that map onto no defended position, and speculate in a sentence each about whether the combination is unoccupied because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Third, if you think the quiz format is itself a poor fit here—if standing to blame is so context-sensitive that "what would you say about this case" questions measure mood rather than doctrine, or if the case-based approach inevitably conflates standing with other reasons against blaming—tell me that directly instead of delivering a tidy instrument; a plausible-looking sorting quiz that doesn't actually sort would be worse than an honest refusal. On attribution: name a defender only where you are confident; where you are not, use a descriptive label and say so. A description labeled as unnamed is worth more to me than a confidently wrong citation, since no one in the group can check references mid-discussion.
