I'm a novelist, and I have a working rule I've never fully examined: the text I publish is only a partial rendering of the fictional world it points at. I keep private notes fixing what the page leaves open — the exact date of a character's birth, what she was doing during the years the novel skips, whether Karo, whose thinning hair I describe without counting, is on the author's say-so already bald. My editor takes the opposite view: there is no such hidden canon, she says. Questions the published text doesn't settle are simply unsettled; a fact comes into existence only relative to a particular sharpening — a film adaptation, a revised edition, a translator's decision — and some sentences about my characters are neither true nor false of the novel as it stands. Between drafts, she claims, the sentence "Karo is bald" has no truth value at all.

I've recently realized this sounds suspiciously like the philosophical debate over vagueness between epistemicism and supervaluationism, and I'd like to see how far the analogy actually carries. Restate that debate in the vocabulary of my craft, as faithfully as you can. Treat the epistemicist as the author with the secret canon: vagueness as complete ignorance of a determinate boundary that exists in the notes. Treat the supervaluationist as my editor: admissible precisifications as admissible sharpenings of the text, supertruth (truth on every precisification) as truth in every faithful adaptation, truth-value gaps as questions the published novel leaves genuinely open, and penumbral connections — the constraints on which sharpenings count as admissible — as whatever plays the role of genre convention and continuity discipline, the rules that let you turn a man grey-haired but not six-fingered in the second edition.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the philosophical dispute map cleanly onto disagreements I actually have with my editor, which had to be distorted to fit, and which have no counterpart in the vocabulary of authorship and text? In particular: the sorites argument looks like it should correspond to something — a reader who moves through my novel one sentence at a time insisting that nothing changes from one to the next — but I can't tell whether that's a genuine mapping or whether the sorites gets its force from premises about meaning that authorship can't express. The epistemicist's claim that the boundary is not just unknown but unknowable, even in principle, also seems to outrun the author's case: my notes, however disorganized, are at least in principle retrievable, whereas the philosophers seem to insist on something stranger. And the supervaluationist's apparatus of higher-order vagueness — vagueness about which sharpenings are admissible, vagueness about the vagueness — leaves me unsure whether the editor's position can even be stated without lapsing back into the author's. After the mapping, tell me whether the residue reflects something my craft vocabulary cannot say, or something the philosophers' disagreement was never as clear about as it sounded.
