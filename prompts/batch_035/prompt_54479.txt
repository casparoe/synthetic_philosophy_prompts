I'd like your help thinking through a running disagreement I have with a colleague, a philosopher of science, about what actually survives when one theory is abandoned for another. Over the years she has delivered verdicts on a series of historical cases, and next week a new one comes up for discussion; I want to predict her answer before she gives it, and I want the prediction made properly rather than jumped to. Her verdicts so far are these. When the ether-based wave theory of light was replaced, she said the ether died but the equations lived on, and that this survival is what makes the episode a success rather than a mere refutation. On phlogiston she is adamant that nothing survived, not even the thought that combustion and respiration have something important in common, because the theory's unobservables were doing all the explanatory work and they were the wrong unobservables. On the crystal spheres of Ptolemaic astronomy she likewise says nothing survived except the observed planetary positions, which she refuses to count as survival at all, since the observations are not the theory's. On caloric she is genuinely torn: the mathematics of heat flow carried over into later accounts of diffusion, but she says the equations were reinterpreted so thoroughly that she cannot decide whether that is survival or coincidence. On Newtonian gravity she says the theory was absorbed rather than refuted, retained as a limiting case of its successor, which she counts as a third kind of survival, distinct from both the ether case and the phlogiston one. And on the old quantum theory giving way to matrix mechanics she says the formal analogies exploited by the correspondence principle survived, while Bohr's electron orbits did not, even though the orbits had featured in the derivations of the hydrogen spectrum. She is also emphatic on two negatives: predictive success by itself is never survival, and no unobservable object has ever survived a theory change. The new case is general relativity and whatever quantum gravity turns out to be. Suppose a future theory replaces general relativity, keeps its predictions in the regimes where it now works, recovers the field equations in an appropriate limit, but explains the metric field as emergent from something more fundamental, in something like the way thermodynamic quantities turn out to be statistical. Does she say anything survived? Predict her verdict, and do it by first laying out the candidate rules that fit her earlier verdicts — for example a rule about retained mathematical structure, a rule about retained working posits, and a rule about limiting-case absorption — and then saying which rule each earlier verdict supports and where those rules pull apart on the new case. Give me your best guess with your reasons, tell me which feature of the new case is doing the work, and describe a further case from the actual history of science, not a fantasy, that would best tell the candidate rules apart. Please write your answer in continuous prose, as one would in a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, and in British English.
