I'm grading final exams in an upper-level undergraduate course on philosophy of science, and I'd like a second reader's assessment of one answer. The question was:

"Goodman claimed to have uncovered a 'new riddle of induction,' distinct from Hume's. (a) State the new riddle as precisely as you can. (b) Explain how it differs from Hume's problem. (c) Describe Goodman's own response to it and assess whether that response succeeds."

Here is the student's answer:

"Hume's problem is that no finite run of observations can justify inferring that unobserved cases will resemble observed ones, because any argument for that inference must itself presuppose the uniformity of nature, and appealing to nature's past uniformity to support its future uniformity is circular. Goodman accepts that we inevitably project from observed to unobserved cases anyway; his new riddle asks which projections we are entitled to make. Fix a future time t. Call an object grue if it is green up to t and blue afterward, and bleen if it is blue up to t and green afterward. Every emerald examined so far has been green; but every emerald examined so far has also been grue, since all of our examinations have taken place before t. So the very same evidence equally confirms 'all emeralds are green' and 'all emeralds are grue,' even though the two hypotheses give opposite predictions about the first emerald examined after t. Unlike Hume's problem, the new riddle is not a problem about justification at all; it is a problem about which of two equally well-confirmed hypotheses is confirmed, i.e., about the validity of our inductive rules. Goodman himself never offered a serious positive solution. The natural response came later from Quine, who argued that we legitimately project only over natural kinds, and 'green' is a natural-kind predicate while 'grue' is a gerrymandered one. Quite apart from that, grue is parasitic: it is defined in terms of green plus a time parameter, so it is less simple than the green hypothesis, and the simpler hypothesis should win. And the grue hypothesis predicts that emeralds will change color at t, which conflicts with everything we know about the stability of mineral composition."
