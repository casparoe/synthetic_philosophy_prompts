Construct two thought experiments about the permissibility of abortion, one for each direction of divergence between the two canonical arguments in that literature: Judith Jarvis Thomson's bodily-autonomy argument from A Defense of Abortion (1971) and Don Marquis's future-like-ours argument from Why Abortion Is Immoral (1989). In the first scenario, the two arguments must deliver opposite verdicts, with Thomson's permitting what Marquis's forbids; in the second, the reverse — Marquis's permitting (or at least raising no objection to) what Thomson's argument condemns, or can be made to condemn only with an added premise.

Constraints:

1. Both scenarios must be realistic clinical fact patterns — no violinists, no people-seeds, no sapient aliens, no artificial wombs — and each no longer than a paragraph.

2. The divergence must be generated, not stipulated. It should fall out of the features each argument independently tracks — for Marquis, whether the fetus has a future of the relevant kind; for Thomson, how the fetus came to be dependent on this particular body and what was or was not consented to — rather than from a clause bolted on simply to switch one argument off.

3. Neither scenario may be a textbook staple. For the first direction, a candidate seed you may use or improve upon: a pregnancy arising from an IVF mix-up in which a clinic implanted a stranger's embryo instead of the patient's own, so that the patient took every reasonable step toward a wanted pregnancy, and yet the pregnancy she consented to is not the one she is carrying. The second direction you must design from scratch. One fact pattern worth considering there: a gestational surrogate who deliberately undertook the pregnancy under an agreement she freely signed, in which the fetus is later diagnosed with a condition that forecloses any future of the relevant kind.

After each scenario, do the following: walk through what each argument says and why it says it; identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work; and give the strongest attack a defender of the argument that comes out badly would mount — for instance, by denying that the analogy holds, or by importing an auxiliary premise such as an account of responsibility for the fetus's presence, or a substantive account of what makes a future count as sufficiently like ours.

Note that the second direction may be unsatisfiable, and I want to know if it is. Thomson's argument is, structurally, a permission-granting argument; it may be incapable of forbidding anything on its own. If you judge that direction unsatisfiable, say so, show why with reference to the argument's structure, and construct the closest satisfiable variant instead — explicitly flagging any premise you had to add, and whose argument it no longer is once that premise is added.

Finally, the purpose of these cases, so that you know what counts as cheating: I am testing whether the two arguments are genuinely independent axes — whether they can cross anywhere — or merely two routes to the same verdicts. A scenario that achieves its divergence through an edge case neither philosopher would recognize as engaging their argument (say, one in which the fetus is already nonviable in a way both sides would concede) fails the brief. So does one in which the verdicts differ only because some term has been read perversely. Close with a verdict: are the arguments orthogonal enough to conflict, and if so, at exactly which point in the case?
