I direct the clinical analytics unit of a mid-sized regional hospital network, and for the past year two of my senior analysts — I'll call them the "lumper" and the "splitter" — have been deadlocked over how our organization should interpret a pattern that keeps surfacing in our own quality dashboards. I have to brief our executive board on this in three weeks, and I refuse to present them a fake consensus. So I am commissioning the one document that might actually help: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between the two of them, and I want you to draft it as it would look if they had done the collaboration in good faith.

The recurring pattern: we evaluate care-management programs (a post-discharge follow-up protocol, a sepsis-response bundle, and one medication-reconciliation initiative) against outcomes like thirty-day readmission and in-hospital mortality, and the direction of the apparent effect is not stable across what we condition on. In one now-notorious case, the pooled comparison shows the program associated with *worse* outcomes, while every stratum of the patient-risk-tier variable shows it associated with *better* outcomes — and, embarrassingly, when a different analyst stratified the same data by attending service line instead, the reversal partly washed out. My two analysts agree on the arithmetic to the last decimal. What they cannot agree on is what follows from it, and because each has a defensible methodology and a strong personality, our whole reporting process has quietly bifurcated: every program evaluation now ships in two versions, and the executives have learned to ask which analyst produced which one.

The splitter's position, as I understand it: Simpson-type reversal demonstrates that the unstratified association is an artifact of an unfair mixture of heterogeneous groups, that reporting an aggregate effect that reverses sign across every stratum of a decision-relevant partition is at best misleading and at worst dishonest, and that the standing rule should be to identify the causally meaningful stratification and report the within-stratum effects. The lumper's position: the paradox has no fixed moral, because which partition is the "right" one is itself a causal judgment that cannot be read off the data; a partition variable can itself be a confounder, a mediator, or a collider depending on the underlying structure, so "always disaggregate on the variable that reverses the effect" is a rule with no domain of application until you've already settled the causal story by other means — and in some of our cases, the pooled figure over the population we actually treat is the decision-relevant quantity, and stratifying on a post-treatment variable manufactures a reversal that corresponds to no intervention anyone could perform. They have been arguing past each other, partly, I suspect, because the splitter hears "aggregate is fine" as "confounding is fine," and the lumper hears "always stratify" as "pretend you know the causal graph when you don't."

Draft the joint statement a genuine adversarial collaboration between them would produce. Specifically, I need:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — including about the mathematics itself (that the reversal is just a fact about non-collapsibility of the relevant association measures and about what marginal and conditional quantities are), about what the dashboards do and do not establish, and about the epistemic situation my unit is actually in (finite data, imperfectly measured risk tiers, and executives who must make a binary fund-or-defund decision regardless).

2. A disagreements table: each residual disagreement stated twice is unacceptable — I want each one phrased once, in wording both of them would sign as a fair statement of their own view. In particular, make them nail down whether the question "which partition is the correct one for reporting?" is a substantive causal question or partly a verbal dispute about what "the effect of the program" even means, and, if any of their dispute is verbal, I want that said explicitly, with the verbal part identified.

3. The cruxes — my own shortlist, to be checked and corrected: (a) whether there is a principled difference between stratifying on a pre-treatment variable and stratifying on something affected by the program; (b) whether the decision-relevant quantity for the board is the effect "in the mixture we actually face" or the effect within strata of something we could in principle target differently; and (c) whether a partition whose strata are themselves heterogeneous enough to reverse again upon further stratification admissible at all — whether the search for the "bottom" partition even terminates.

4. For each crux, the case, argument, or analysis both would concede bears on it, with each analyst's prediction about it written down in advance — including, where possible, predictions about what we'd see in the specific three programs above, since the whole point is to run the collaboration before the board meeting rather than after.

Please write it as the finished document, in the formal register such a statement would have, with a short preface explaining what the parties agreed the collaboration was and was not for. No literature review and no citations — I have people for that, and this document will be read by clinicians and administrators, not methodologists. What I most need from it is a text that neither of my analysts could repudiate without conceding they had been arguing in bad faith.
