Here's the exam question they were answering:

"In light of split-brain cases (commissurotomy patients studied by Sperry, Gazzaniga, and others), does the unity of consciousness come apart from the unity of the person? Explain what the experimental results actually show, and defend a position on whether the pre- or post-operative patient is one subject of experience, two, or neither of these answers is acceptable. 500 words max."

And here's the student's answer:

"When the corpus callosum is severed, the two hemispheres can no longer share information, and the experiments show they really do process different things. In Sperry and Gazzaniga's setup, a word like 'key' or 'ring' is flashed to one visual field only, so only one hemisphere sees it. The right hemisphere has no language at all, so the patient can't say what they saw, but the left hand, which the right hemisphere controls, can pick out the matching object (e.g., pick up a key). This shows two independent streams of consciousness operating in one skull. Gazzaniga's interpreter experiments make the same point: when the right hemisphere is told to do something, like stand up or laugh, the left hemisphere, which controls speech, doesn't know why, so it confabulates an explanation for the behavior. So we get two centers of consciousness that don't share content and each interprets the world on its own.

The obvious conclusion is that there were two persons all along, and the surgery just reveals this, which is what Nagel concluded in his classic paper: the callosum was what unified them into one person beforehand, and once it's cut there are two. This is bad news for the psychological-continuity theory of personal identity, since it shows that the psychological continuity we normally use to track persons is really just anatomical wiring.

Still, in daily life the patient behaves like one person, and cross-cueing between hemispheres helps them coordinate. So maybe we should say the person is one but the minds are two, and the unity of consciousness turns out to be a property of hemispheres rather than of persons. Either way, the callosum is the seat of the unity of consciousness, and losing it dissolves the single subject into two."
