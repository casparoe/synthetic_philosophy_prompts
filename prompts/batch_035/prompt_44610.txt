I'm an acquisitions editor at a mid-sized trade publisher, and I'm realizing most of my job is just reading other people's decisions. I see which manuscripts went to auction and who dropped out at what number, I hear at lunch which imprints passed and for what reason (usually "didn't fall in love" which tells me nothing), and my agents are constantly telling me a book is "hot" — which mostly means other editors are saying it's hot. Somewhere in my head I know the herd is partially rational: if three smart houses are bidding, that's real information, those people read the thing and put money down. But it's also exactly how information cascades work, right? The fourth and fifth bidder aren't adding information, they're just copying, and eventually nobody in the chain is looking at the actual manuscript. And cascades are supposed to be fragile and path-dependent — which means the book everyone passed on might just have been unlucky with the first two readers.

So: I want a decision procedure I can actually run in the twenty minutes between getting the submission and my editorial meeting, for how much weight to give the herd versus my own read. It should be grounded in what the actual cascade/herding literature says — rational herding, why cascades form even when everyone is behaving sensibly, the fact that early movers don't internalize what their choice reveals to later movers, cascade fragility, stuff like that — but translated into publishing, not economics-journal language. The questions in order, what counts as a real signal versus noise (e.g., does it matter that the passes came before or after Frankfurt? does a pass from a house with a strong list in that genre count differently?), where the procedure should tell me to stop and just go with my gut because it's a values call, and where it should force me to do the thing my gut won't like.

Then run it on three cases. First: a literary novel that five big houses passed on but that two of my most trusted colleagues at other imprints flagged as "the best thing I read this year and I couldn't get my pub board to move" — do I want it, and what does the herd's no actually tell me? Second: a commercial thriller going to auction where two houses known for publishing exactly this stuff are already in at six figures — does my wanting it mean anything at all, or am I just bidder number three in a textbook cascade? Third: my own strong conviction case, a weird book I love that nobody else has mentioned, where the procedure should probably tell me something uncomfortable about how much my private signal is worth when nobody else is corroborating it.

Also be honest about what the procedure can't do — like, I assume it can't tell me whether the first movers were right, only how much their choices reveal — and flag the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as questions. And if there's a place where what the herding literature recommends is genuinely in tension with how publishing actually has to work (we can't wait for more information, auctions have deadlines), say that too rather than pretending the theory just slots in.
