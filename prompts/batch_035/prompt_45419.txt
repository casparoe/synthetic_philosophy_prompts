I run a philosophy of science reading group that has spent the semester on the scientific realism debate: the no-miracles argument, Laudan's graveyard of once-successful-but-false theories, and finally Worrall's structural realism as the attempted compromise, followed by a session on the ontic version defended by people like Ladyman and French. For the last meeting I want to close with a villanelle, and I want the form to do philosophical work rather than decorate it.

The two refrains should carry the two faces of the view. One refrain is the epistemic claim: all we can ever grasp of nature is its structure — the relations, the equations, the pattern, not the things related. The other is the ontic claim: this is not a limitation of ours but the truth about what's there — there is nothing else to be grasped. A villanelle's refrains return verbatim while the surrounding lines change, and that is exactly the mechanism I want exploited: as the stanzas move through the material — Fresnel's equations surviving into Maxwell's theory with the ether discarded and the structure retained, the relata swapping out while the relations carry over, the no-miracles argument and the pessimistic meta-induction each getting their turn — the same returning line should read differently against different contexts, so the reader can feel why the two versions of the view keep passing for each other, and why the slippage between "we know only structure" and "there is only structure" is both the position's engine and its instability. The final couplet, where the refrains finally meet side by side, should be the moment they either fuse or expose their difference. I leave the choice of which to you, but it must be earned by the stanzas, not announced.

Accuracy matters more than flourish: no metaphor a philosopher of science would wince at, the retention example should be handled correctly, and if the Newman objection appears anywhere it should function as more than a name-drop. Someone who has read the literature should find the poem right, and someone who hasn't should find it a good poem.

After the poem, append a short prose note (roughly 150 to 250 words) in the spirit of self-critique: where the poem takes liberties with the ideas or leans on a contested reading; which reading of structural realism it ultimately sides with, if any; and which single objection — Newman's problem that pure structure without relata collapses into triviality, the complaint that structure retention is the exception rather than the rule in actual theory change, or something else — would most make the refrains ring false if pressed. That is, tell me what would change the poem's mind about its own view.
