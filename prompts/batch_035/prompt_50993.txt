So I've basically come around to Dennett's heterophenomenology as the right method for a science of consciousness: you treat everything subjects say and do about their experience as data, build up the world as it seems to them the way you'd build up the world of a novel from its text, and then that apparent world is one more thing to be explained — no special first-person channel, no taking introspection at face value, no magic. What I want to know is what would actually count against it, and I want this concrete, not the usual "but it leaves out the intrinsic feel!!" hand-waving, which I've heard a thousand times and which the method just eats for breakfast.

For instance — and here's the kind of case I keep chewing on. Suppose a lab trains people for months on some narrowly defined attention task, like catching the exact moment an afterimage shifts from orange to something you'd call red, or mapping grain in a texture you only notice once you've learned how to look. And suppose their reports converge across subjects at a very fine grain, stay stable over months, come out systematically finer then anything you'd predict from their discrimination behavior, and don't line up with anything physiological. Is that a defeater for the method? Or does heterophenomenology just file it as more text about what it seems like, and carry on? And if the answer is always going to be "more text," I start to worry the method has been insulated into unfalsifiability — though Dennett presents it as neutral between the first-person and third-person camps, and I'm genuinely unsure whether a *method* can be insulated the way a theory can, or what it would even mean for a method to fail.

So: rank the potential defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Say which ones Dennett-style defenders would themselves concede as counting against them, versus which they'd wave off, and be honest about the difference. What does the method predict that its rivals don't, such that its rivals could be vindicated and it embarrassed? And give me a straight answer on whether it risks anything at all, or whether "it's just the ordinary scientific method, applied" makes it effectively immune — and if so, whether that immunity is a feature or a dodge. For what it's worth, the thing that would move me most personally is trained introspection turning up structure that third-person methods could corroborate but would never have found on their own — I just can't tell if something like that has already happened and been quietly absorbed as more data.
