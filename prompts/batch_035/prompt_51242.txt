So, background: I'm a software engineer, zero formal philosophy training, but I run a movie night with some friends from work and once a year we do a "serious" pick where I'm supposed to bring a discussion topic. This time it's The Big Lebowski (we've all seen it a dozen times, that's the point). While procrastinating on this I fell down a rabbit hole about Moritz Schlick, the Vienna Circle guy who mostly wrote about verification and ethics, but who also had this late essay from the 1920s on the meaning of life. From what I gathered, his take is that meaning and purpose are two different things: a life spent chasing purposes is just a chain of means and ends that never actually pays out anywhere, and the genuinely meaningful moments are the play-like ones -- art, games, love, contemplation, stuff you do for its own sake and not as a step toward something else. And on his picture the nihilists are basically making a mistake: they think that because life has no cosmic purpose it must be meaningless, but Schlick would say meaning never came from purpose in the first place.

So my question: read The Big Lebowski through Schlick. The Dude seems almost engineered as a test case -- bowling, white russians, no career, no ambitions, just doing stuff he likes doing, while everyone around him (Walter turning everything into a purposeful struggle with his Vietnam framing, the actual Big Lebowski with his trophies and self-made-man speechifying, the nihilists who literally call themselves nihilists) is busy with purposes that make them miserable. Does the film dramatize Schlick's point that the meaningful life is the playful one? Where does the reading strain? I keep noticing that the Dude's play keeps getting wrecked by other people's purposes crashing into it -- the rug, the toe, Donny -- and I don't know if Schlick has anything to say about that, like whether play is fragile in a way his theory ignores. And is the Dude really purpose-free given how much he cares about his rug and about Donny?

Give me something I can actually use to lead the post-screening discussion with a bunch of engineers who've never read a word of philosophy: where the Schlick reading really illuminates specific scenes, where it bends the movie to fit the theory, whether the film ends up saying something Schlick doesn't, and one or two moments that would generate the best argument among my friends. Please keep the philosophy self-contained since no one at movie night (including me) has read the actual essay. Also if Schlick scholars would say I've misunderstood him, flag that too, I don't want to embarass myself in front of my brother-in-law who did a philosophy degree and will absolutely show up.
