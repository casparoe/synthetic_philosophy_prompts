Teaching the decision theory seminar again this spring and I want to run my own package of views past someone before I put it on the board as a target. Here it is: (1) the Allais preferences are rational — I take the certainty effect to be tracking something real about having a sure thing, not a bias to be explained away, and I think anyone who says you must be indifferent between the Allais pairs is proving too much. (2) Preferences have to be transitive and immune to money pumps — for me the normative force of decision theory comes entirely from practical exploitability arguments, not from representation theorems, which I think are just math that says IF you satisfy the axioms THEN there's a utility function, not that you must. (I've also had a colleague tell me my line on (1) is really just a transitivity violation in disguise, which I don't see, but maybe.) (3) A compound lottery should be evaluated by its reduced form — a coin flip between getting lottery A and lottery B just IS a particular lottery over final outcomes, there's no extra fact. (4) Stochastic dominance is untouchable — choosing an option that's at least as bad in every state of the world and strictly worse in one isn't a taste, it's a mistake, full stop. (5) And yet I still think expected utility is the right decision standard for public choices — cost-benefit analysis, government risk regulation, that whole apparatus — even though I let individuals keep their Allais-style preferences. So: which of these genuinely conflict, which only look like they do, and what's the cheapest way to restore consistency? I have a feeling something in here is unstable but I honestly can't see which piece. Rank the tensions by severity instead of telling me they're all equally bad, and for each one say what has to give and what giving it up costs me.
