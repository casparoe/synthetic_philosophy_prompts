I am a professor of philosophy — I teach aesthetics and ethics — and I chair the collections and interpretation committee of the mid-sized regional art museum in the city where my university sits. I am the only person on that committee with philosophical training; the rest are a registrar, two docents, a donor representative, and the director. Roughly once a month we face the same recurring decision: a living or dead artist's moral record surfaces — through the press, a donor complaint, or the registrar's provenance research — and within a fifteen-minute agenda slot we must decide whether the work stays on the wall, stays with revised label copy, goes to storage, or a loan gets returned. Our record so far is incoherent: we kept the nineteenth-century landscapist whose published pamphlets defended slavery (the canvases themselves are mute about it), we deinstalled a contemporary photographer whose celebrated portraits of exploited subjects had been made under exploitative conditions, and we are now weighing a sculpture on loan from a living artist facing credible abuse allegations. The decisions have to be defensible to our donors and to the local press, and the docents have to be able to apply the procedure in my absence.

Our docents' working shorthand — "bad person, bad painting?" — collapses at least three distinct questions: whether the work itself is aesthetically or morally compromised, whether we ought to associate with the artist at all, and what continuing to display the work says about the institution. And within the first of those, our committee keeps running together a moral defect the work merely happens to carry and one its power actually depends on — a seductive novel whose seductiveness runs through its cruelty. Your procedure has to keep those apart, and do it in a form a registrar can run under deadline.

So: you are a professional philosopher working in aesthetics, ethics, and museum ethics, and I would like you to think carefully — first sketch the standard positions (autonomism, moralism, the hybrids) for yourself and decide what each one actually commits us to — but do not lecture us on them; I know them and the committee does not. The deliverable is a numbered list of exactly five items and nothing else: the five questions the committee should ask, in order, each stated in one or two sentences, with what counts as a decisive answer, where the question hands off to a judgment call rather than a rule, and a brief indication of how it treats the live cases — including whether the slavery-defending landscapist and the abusive sculptor come out the same way or not. Do not open with an introduction or close with a summary; the list is the whole answer. I will warn you now that the committee will not enjoy whatever the procedure implies about the photographer we took down, and I want that implication preserved anyway.
