I'm writing an essay that reads The Great Gatsby through the lens of costly signaling and the handicap principle, and I'd like your help thinking it through. On the face of it Gatsby's parties are textbook handicap signals: profligately wasteful, thrown week after week for a woman who does not even attend them until Nick intervenes, and effective precisely because they are so expensive to fake. But the novel keeps wobbling on this reading. Daisy, when she finally arrives, is most moved not by the enormous display but by the shirts — a smaller, almost private gesture. Tom, the genuinely rich man, signals nothing at all, and his refusal to signal seems to be the strongest signal of security he could send. Gatsby's guests accept the signal (they come, they eat) while despising the signaller, and the one receiver he cares about misjudges the cost in a way the theory says she should not. So: where does the novel vindicate costly-signaling accounts, where does it resist them, and does it dramatise something the formal theory misses — for instance that signaling presupposes a receiver who is competent to judge the cost, and that this competence is itself unequally distributed between old money and new? Please be honest about the places where my reading strains the text. Answer in British English.
