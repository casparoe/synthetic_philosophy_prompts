As a professor of philosophy of mind, I supervise a small number of doctoral students at any one time, and I am now deciding which open questions in the literature on Dennett's real patterns and the three stances to steer the current cohort toward over the next three or four years. The stakes are their careers, so I want a dependency-aware ordering rather than a syllabus: the five questions whose settlement would most change the rest of the field, with the reasoning visible. My students are comfortable with formal tools — information theory, algorithmic complexity, a working knowledge of decision theory — but none is a physicist, so questions that turn on the foundations of physics are likely wasted effort in their hands, and I would rather know that than discover it in year three.

Among the candidates I half-see in the literature, and offer only as material to rank or discard: whether the physical stance deserves its name at all, given that physics is usually treated as the stance-free bedrock rather than as one predictive strategy among three, so that calling it a stance looks like either a pleasing symmetry or a substantive and unargued claim; what exactly makes a pattern real rather than merely available, once the compression criterion has to cope with lossy, projective, and interest-relative description schemes, where a description counts as shorter only relative to a tolerance nobody has specified; whether the design stance earns its keep or collapses into the other two, as its critics allege, since almost everything it predicts seems recoverable from either the physical stance plus engineering knowledge or the intentional stance plus evolutionary history; how the framework should treat systems whose intentional-level states are partially inspectable at the physical level — trained neural networks appear to cut across the stance taxonomy in a way the original formulation did not anticipate, being at once physically transparent and predictively intractable; and whether stance-relative realness is a strong enough result to underwrite the sort of realism about propositional attitudes that the literature on belief wants, or whether it quietly concedes everything to instrumentalism while retaining the word.

Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, no more and no fewer, with no introduction or conclusion. Each item should name one open question and, in a sentence or two, state why it sits at that place in the ranking — which other questions depend on it, what makes it tractable or intractable for philosophers with formal rather than physical training, and, where relevant, why a tempting candidate you have omitted does not earn a place. I will treat the ordering itself as the deliverable: if the most defensible ranking is one in which none of my five candidates survives, say so by omission rather than by commentary.
