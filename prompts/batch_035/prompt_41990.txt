I build risk models for a living (insurance pricing, nothing glamorous), and a philosophy-grad-school friend of mine got me sucked into the scientific realism fight a few months back, specifically the pessimistic meta-induction -- the Laudan-style list of theories everyone was once confident about that turned out to be fundamentally wrong, phlogiston and caloric and the ether and so on. As a risk person I feel like I genuinely need an answer here: if the historical base rate of "our best current science being deep-down wrong" is high, that seems like it should change how much I trust things, my own models included. But when I read around the debate it looks like its been totally stuck for decades. The realists lean on the no-miracles argument and on the various selective realisms (only the working parts are getting at truth, structure not entities, etc.), and the anti-realists reply that selectively pointing at the bits that survived and saying "those were the real content all along" is just retreating to whatever hasn't been falsified yet and then calling the retreat a theory. Nobody seems to give an inch. A guy at my office, half joking, said both sides are just survivorship bias pointed in opposite directions, and honestly that stuck with me. My working theory, coming at this from my day job: this is the same kind of disagreement you get when two actuaries look at the same claims history and disagree about whether the past is even relevant to the exposure going forward -- i.e., the fight isn't about the base rate at all, it's about whether historical cases are the right kind of evidence for a claim about now. Is that diagnosis right? Or is it really a substantive fight about what "approximately true" is supposed to mean, or about whether explanation or prediction is the thing science is for? Walk me through what each side is actually committed to, tell me what kind of disagreement this really is, and be honest about whether anything -- a cleaner formalization of approximate truth, better historical case studies, whatever -- could actually move it, or whether the whole question is just ill-posed.
