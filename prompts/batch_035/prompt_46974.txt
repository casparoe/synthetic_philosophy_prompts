I'm writing a long essay for a serious general readership on Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day, and I want to read Stevens the butler as a genuine test case in the debate between liberalism and communitarianism, rather than as a simple cautionary figure about repression. My worry is that the obvious reading is too easy, and I need help stress-testing it.

The obvious reading: communitarian political philosophy — MacIntyre on practices, tradition, and the narrative unity of a life; Sandel on the self constituted by its attachments rather than prior to them; Taylor on horizons of significance and strong evaluation — attacks the liberal picture of the person as a choosing self who can stand back from and critically endorse any of her ends. Stevens, whose entire identity is his role, who locates dignity in serving an employer of genuinely great stature, and who cannot act on his feelings for Miss Kenton because they have no place in his station, looks like a walking reductio of the communitarian self. His constitutive attachment is unmoderated by critical distance, and the disasters follow: he lends his meticulous competence to Lord Darlington's appeasement politics, stays downstairs while his father dies upstairs because an international conference takes precedence, and cooperates (through inaction) in the dismissal of the Jewish maids. On this reading Ishiguro vindicates the liberal side — Kymlicka's revised liberalism or Rawls's moral powers — since the catastrophe comes precisely from a man who cannot step back.

But three things complicate that, and I want the reading to take all three seriously rather than folding them back into the easy moral. First, Stevens's role-identity is not mere inheritance: he articulates and defends a considered conception of dignity, contrasts it with mere bantering competence, and chooses his path at least as reflectively as most liberal agents ever choose anything — so the liberal demand for critical endorsement seems to be met, and the disaster happens anyway. Second, there is a communitarian rejoinder available: his failure is arguably not constitutive attachment as such but attachment to the wrong community at the wrong historical moment — the aristocratic household in precisely the decades that world was collapsing and being discredited. MacIntyre's account of characters as the masks worn by traditions in their decline seems built to explain this, at least as well as the liberal explanation does. Third, the ending: Stevens on the bench at Weymouth pier, weeping over the remnants of his life, and then resolving to master the art of bantering to please his new American employer. Is the bantering resolution a modest step toward liberal autonomy — self-revision, choosing a new form of relationship — or is it the same constitutive logic reattaching itself, a man who can only relate to other people through service now practicing charm as a duty of station? The two diagnoses are hard to pull apart, and I suspect Ishiguro knows it.

So what I need is a reading faithful in both directions: it should state what the liberal and communitarian positions actually claim, without cartoon versions, and then work through how each illuminates specific episodes — the deathbed scene during the conference, Miss Kenton's near-declarations and their deflections, the dignity monologues, the meetings with the local villagers who take him for a gentleman, the pier scene. It should say which side, if either, the novel's narrative sympathy supports, and be honest about the ways the novel resists the whole frame: whether the debate, which is nominally about the justification of political principles, can even bear on an account of how one man should live, or whether pressing it onto Stevens is a category error the critic has to own. I'd especially like you to say whether the novel dramatizes something neither camp's arguments capture — my own hunch is that Stevens as narrator, retrospectively rationalizing his life to a stranger, embodies a reflective distance that is neither Rawlsian detachment from ends nor MacIntyrean embeddedness in a tradition, but I may be fooling myself about that. Please take a position by the end: does the novel ultimately argue for liberal autonomy, for a chastened communitarianism, or for something the debate has no name for? And if the reading only works by distorting either the philosophy or the plot somewhere, tell me where it strains.
