I keep flip flopping on whether you can compare wellbeing across different people, so I made a list of my actual gut verdicts and I want to know if theres a principle underneath them or if I'm just muddled:

- Two patients, one dose of painkiller, same surgery. One rates her pain 9/10, the other says 4/10, but the nurse tells me the second guy is stoic and is honestly worse off. My gut says give it to him. So apparently I think there's a fact of the matter about whose pain is worse, and that we can sometimes know it.
- My friend insists his team losing the final hurt worse than my mom's cancer scare scared me. Here my gut says there's no fact of the matter at all -- not just that we can't find it out, but that there's nothing to find out.
- A dollar to a poor person clearly does more good than the same dollar to a billionaire. Interpersonal comparison, no hesitation.
- But in pandemic triage I won't say saving a 25-year-old does more good than saving an 80-year-old. I'd still save the young one, but I explain that with fair innings, not with "there's more good left in that life."
- Within a single life I'm happy to aggregate: trading ten mild headaches against one migraine for the same person seems obviously fine. Across lives I balk: I don't think you can trade 10,000 strangers' mild headaches against one stranger's migraine, even though if all those headaches belonged to one person I'd say take the headaches.

The first two feel like the real problem to me, and 3 with 4 is close behind. So: is there one principle that gets all five, or close to it, and if not, which of my verdicts would you overturn and why? Whatever you land on, also tell me what it predicts for two cases I havn't listed -- whether a vet can truthfully say my dog's arthritis is less bad than a person's arthritis, and whether a health agency can add up QALYs across patients the way an economist adds up dollars. Be honest if the principle has to eat one of my verdicts to stay simple.
