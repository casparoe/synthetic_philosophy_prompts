I'm taking a seminar this term on rational choice theory and the limits of decision-theoretic modeling, and I have to submit a position paper on transformative choice. Before I write it up, I'd like a consistency check on the package of views I've drifted into over the semester, because I suspect it doesn't hang together, and I can't tell where the fault line is.

Here is the package:

1. Rational choice just is expected-utility maximization: an agent chooses rationally insofar as she picks the option with the highest expected utility, computed from her credences and her utility function over outcomes.
2. L. A. Paul is right that genuinely transformative choices — becoming a parent for the first time is her central case — fall outside this framework. You cannot know in advance what the experience will be like for you, or what preferences it will leave you with, so there is no well-defined utility to feed into the calculation, and the choice cannot be made rationally in the decision-theoretic sense.
3. What-it-is-like knowledge is knowledge by acquaintance and cannot be transmitted by testimony or description. Just as no amount of reading could convey the experience of red to someone blind from birth, no accumulation of vivid accounts from parents could convey to me what parenthood is like. This is why the gap in (2) cannot be closed by doing more research.
4. I find Agnes Callard's account of aspiration persuasive: a person can rationally, over time, work her way toward values she does not yet grasp, and this is a distinctive mode of agency rather than mere drift or self-deception. Becoming a parent might be exactly this kind of aspirational process.
5. All the same, I think my sister's decision to have her first child was rational. She deliberated carefully, weighed what testimony she had, and chose well by any ordinary standard.
6. In my own life I routinely and reasonably update on post-transformation testimony: when friends who have married tell me that marriage changed how they think about commitment, I believe them and adjust my expectations about my own future marriage accordingly.

Which pairs of these are genuinely in tension, which merely look as though they are, and what would be the cheapest repair in each case? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally damaging, and close with your judgment about which single revision would clean up the most. I honestly don't know which of these commitments I'm most attached to, so please don't pull your punches on any of them.
