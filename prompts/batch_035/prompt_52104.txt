I've been tracking my gut about when enjoying sad stuff is fine and when it feels a bit off, and I want to know if there's a pattern behind it. Here's my list of cases:

- After a bad breakup I played the most miserable songs I own on repeat for like a week. It almost felt like feeding myself. Fine.
- I cry at the end of certain movies and walk out feeling better than when I went in. Fine.
- Watching a documentary about a real disaster, I caught myself having basically the movie feeling — absorbed, kind of awed — and afterward it bugged me, like I'd been entertained by something that happened to actual people. Something's off there, though I couldn't of said the film was wrong to be made.
- At the funeral of a colleague's father, I found the sadness in the room moving, even a little beautiful, and I feel zero guilt about that. Fine.
- I genuinely enjoyed watching a smug coworker get taken apart in a meeting. Not fine, even though I notice it was the exact same shape as the movie case: someone suffering, me watching.
- I'll put on tragic opera when nothing at all is wrong in my life, just because it's Tuesday and I want the big feelings. Fine, though my dad thinks it's morbid.

My first thought was that the rule is just real vs. fictional: fictional suffering is fine to enjoy, real isn't. But the funeral verdict and the documentary verdict sit badly with that — both are real, one feels completely fine, the other feels off. I don't think it's distance in time either: old disasters still give me the icky documentary feeling, but a 90-year-old war memorial doesn't. Or does it? Honestly I go back and forth.

So: is there a principle that saves all of these? If not, which of my verdicts would you overturn, and why those? Then tell me what the surviving principle predicts for a few cases I didn't list — say, a play about a disaster that happened last year, where the victims' families publicly supported the production; a friend telling the story of their own divorce at dinner and me finding it gripping; and me deliberately rereading old letters right after my father died because I wanted the pain.

One more thing: since you'll be picking between my verdicts, I want you to point at the weak spot yourself. Where is your reasoning most likely to go wrong, and what consideration would actually change your mind? I'd rather have that than a confident answer.
