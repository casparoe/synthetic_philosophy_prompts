I'm a novelist, and I need help from someone who knows statistics the way a trial lawyer knows procedure. My current book is told from the point of view of a biostatistician who serves as an expert witness, and I'm determined that every number she recites on the stand should survive an expert reader. For background reading I picked up a popular book on statistical pitfalls — well reviewed, author has a doctorate, exactly the sort of thing my character's lawyer would hand her before deposition — and it contains the following passage, which I've copied out word for word:

"Simpson's paradox, first described by the British statistician Edward Simpson in 1951, is what happens when a trend that appears in every subgroup of a dataset vanishes or reverses when the subgroups are pooled. The classic case is graduate admissions at Berkeley in the fall of 1973: overall, men were admitted at 44 percent and women at only 35 percent — a gap so large that sex bias seemed the only explanation. But when the applications were examined department by department, the pattern dissolved: in most of the largest departments women actually fared slightly better than men, and the pooled-and-adjusted analysis showed a small but significant bias in favor of women. The university was, statistically speaking, exonerated. The culprit in such cases is always a third variable — statisticians call it a confounder — hiding in the aggregate; here, the fact that women tended to apply to more competitive departments. The practical lesson is unforgiving: the pooled numbers are always the misleading ones. Whenever you see a headline statistic, ask for the breakdown. Simpson's paradox is the ecological fallacy's mirror image — where the ecological fallacy warns that group-level patterns may not describe individuals, Simpson's paradox warns that they may describe them backwards. Once you know about it, you never trust an unstratified number again."
