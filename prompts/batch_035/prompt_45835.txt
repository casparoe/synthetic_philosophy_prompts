My two friends have been arguing about the Hart–Fuller debate for like two years now and it's going nowhere — one of them is a pretty hardcore legal positivist, the other is basically a natural law person, and at this point they mostly just talk past eachother. I want to force them into an adversarial collaboration, and what I need from you is the joint statement they'd both actually be able to sign at the end of it.

The statement should have:

1. A numbered section of claims both sides can accept — about the separation (or not) between identifying what the law is and evaluating it morally, about the grudge-informer cases and why both sides find them genuinely hard, about the difference between saying this is valid law but wicked versus saying this is not law at all, and about what the positivist thesis actually commits you to (roughly: that it's possible for law and morality to come apart, not that it's desirable — I suspect they both accept that formulation even though neither ever says it out loud).

2. A table of precise disagreements, where each row is worded so that its own side would accept how their position is described. Examples to cover: what valid law means such that the Nazi-era statutes either were or weren't it; and whether Fuller's internal morality of law is a morality at all — my positivist friend treats it as just principles of effective lawmaking, craftsmanship rather than ethics, while my natural law friend thinks that comparison begs the whole question.

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions where settling one would actually move at least one of them. I suspect one is whether rule-of-law virtues can be fully separated from substantive justice, and another is whether calling something law carries any real normative weight for a citizen deciding whether to obey it. But work out the real cruxes yourself, don't just take mine.

4. For each crux, a concrete case or argument both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Here's a case I want them to have to address: imagine a regime that is thoroughly evil in its aims but genuinely scrupulous about the rule of law — everything prospective, public, clear, stable, no retroactive surprises, honest courts faithfully applying the announced rules. Its apologists say at least you know where you stand. Does that system exhibit Fuller's internal morality to a high degree while being about as unjust as regimes get, and if so, what does each side think that shows? I honestly don't know which of them should be more disturbed by this case, and I want the statement to record where it cuts for each of them.

5. A short closing section on which parts of their dispute (if any) turn out to be partly verbal — e.g. whether the word law is even being used in the same sense across the disagreement — and why they think so.

Keep it in the spirit of an actual joint statement: no declaring a winner, and each side's positions have to be formulated in ways that side would put their name to, not strawmen. It's fine if the agreed section includes some plain historical facts about the debate — that it was a famous exchange in a law journal in the late 1950s, that the German courts after the war actually heard the grudge-informer prosecutions — but don't lean on exact dates or page numbers, nobody checks that stuff.
