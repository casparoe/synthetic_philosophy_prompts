The following argument was pressed on me by a mathematician acquaintance, who considers it a decisive refutation of aggregative ethics and has challenged me to name the faulty step. I have so far failed, and I am beginning to suspect either that the failure is mine or that the price of the answer is higher than I want to pay. He calls it the invariance argument:

(1) Aggregative ethics holds that the comparative value of outcomes is fixed by the distribution of welfare across the persons in them, taken as a totality; in the finite case this means betterness is settled by total welfare, and the infinite case, if it is to be a case of the same doctrine at all, must be some extension of that rule.
(2) Suppose the world contains infinitely many welfare subjects.
(3) The total welfare of an infinite collection of welfare levels, when it is defined at all, is either positive infinity or negative infinity; in either case any finite modification — rescuing a drowning child, devastating a continent — leaves the total exactly what it was, and when no total exists, there is nothing for the modification to alter.
(4) Outcomes with the same total welfare are equally good, and outcomes for which no total exists are such that neither is better than the other.
(5) So in an infinite world no act is better than any alternative: rescue, atrocity, and idleness are all on a par.
(6) A moral theory on which (5) holds has lost its subject matter; therefore at least one of (1) through (4) is false.

He insists the only respectable exit is to deny (1): aggregation, on his view, was a finitary convenience that infinity exposes as fiction. My suspicion is that the real work happens between (3) and (4) — some slide from the invariance of an extended-real sum to the invariance of value. But I cannot make the charge stick, because whenever I gesture at richer representations — hyperreal totals, limiting comparisons, densities — he asks which one, and why that one, and observes that the candidates disagree in how they rank the very same pair of worlds, so the choice among them looks like a free parameter rather than a fact about the worlds. If the choice is free, my patch concedes his conclusion: the rankings are not grounded in the worlds at all.

Tell me which step I should reject and what rejecting it costs. I would most like to keep something recognizably aggregative along with the falsity of (5), but only if that can be done without the arbitrariness he threatens to expose. And I would like you to look hard at the qualification in (3), the phrase "when it is defined at all," together with the second clause of (4): I have a feeling that between them they smuggle in a completeness assumption — that any two outcomes must be comparable — which nothing in his own premises entitles him to. If that is where the flaw lies, explain what a merely partial ordering of infinite outcomes can and cannot still do for a theory of right action, and what it costs to give up completeness rather than to give up aggregation.
