So my friend and I had another three hour argument about forgeries last night (this has become a weird recurring thing between us) and it left me with a position I now have to defend in public, which was probably a mistake. Backstory: I'm giving a talk next month at a meeting of a regional aesthetics society defending what my friend sarcastically calls my "funny money theory of originals." The view, roughly: the extra value we attach to originals -- the value that a perfect forgery can't capture even when literally no one can ever tell the difference -- isn't in the object, and it also isn't some deep fact about the artist's performance (I know, the Dutton people in the room will hate this). It's institutional in a very specific way: an original painting functions like legal tender. Its "originality value" supervenes on the artworld's provenance-tracking practices actually working the way they're supposed to, the same way a twenty dollar bill's value supervenes on the mint and the banking system and not on the paper or the ink. A perfect forgery is then exactly like a perfect counterfeit: aesthetically indistingishable, but still a counterfeit, because it inserts a fake node into the tracking system, and that insertion is the whole of the wrong-making feature. No mysticism about the artist's hand, no epistemic harm to future viewers (there are none, by hypothesis).

My friend's strongest comeback, which I still don't have a clean answer to, was that money-value is openly and unembarrassingly conventional while our response to originals doesn't feel conventional at all -- standing in front of the actual Rembrandt feels like responding to *it*, not to a record-keeping system -- so my view either (a) misdescribes the phenomenology of valuing art, or (b) is secretly normative, i.e. telling everyone they should stop feeling what they in fact feel, which I don't want to claim either.

The audience: mostly aestheticians. At least one person who thinks Dutton's performance argument is basically right -- art objects are records of achievement, the original is the authentic record, forgery is fraud on the achievement. A Goodmanian who will absolutely bring up the autographic/allographic distinction and ask why I'm only talking about paintings and what happens to my view for music or literature. A couple of anti-theorists who think "where does the value reside" is a badly posed question from the start. And a bunch of grad students who mainly want to talk about NFTs.
