I've drifted into holding a pretty specific view about introspection and I want to stress-test it. The view: introspection is reliable about the coarse-grained, functional facts of your own mind -- roughly what you believe, roughly what you're feeling, what you're planning to do -- but systematically unreliable about fine-grained structure (the exact phenomenology, what's in the periphery of attention) and about the causes of your own states. And crucially I think this asymmetry isn't luck: introspective access is built out of the same machinery we use for self-prediction and social self-reporting, so it's accurate exactly where those jobs require accuracy and sloppy everywhere else. Coarse content matters for predicting your own behavior and for telling other people what you'll do; fine-grained structure and etiology don't, so nobody ever built a module for them.

What would actually count against this? I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they'd be. Some things I've half-considered but never worked through: does the choice blindness stuff (people confidently explaining choices they didn't actually make) cut against my view or can I fold it in as just another case of unreliable causal access? Is there evidence that introspection is bad even at the coarse level -- people misreporting their own standing attitudes when nothing rides on it -- that would be genuinely fatal rather than awkward? Conversely, if introspection turns out to be startlingly good at some fine-grained task (like judging the timing or position of things at the edge of attention), does that sink the functional story or just mean that job mattered for something I haven't thought of?

Also: the view makes predictions its rivals don't, and I'd like someone to spell those out and say which are testable. If introspective accuracy tracks self-prediction utility, then accuracy should degrade for states that don't feed into prediction or reporting -- low-stakes moods, habits, things you never have to answer for. And I want an honest check on whether I've quietly insulated the thing: "accurate where it's functionally useful" can be stretched to accommodate literally any result, since you can always invent a use after the fact. If that move is available to me whenever evidence goes the wrong way, is the view doing any work, or is it just a story I tell after seeing the data? If it has become unfalsifiable, tell me whether that's fixable with a sharper formulation or whether it's fatal for this kind of claim. Rank everything from fatal to merely awkward, and be clear about which of these a person as committed as me would grudgingly concede counts against the view versus wave off.
