I've been toying with an argument in my metaphysics seminar on the unity of the proposition, and it now appears to prove that there are no false atomic propositions — a conclusion I refuse to draw, yet each step still looks defensible to me. It runs: (1) An atomic proposition or state of affairs, <aRb>, is a complex whose constituents are a, b, and the relation R. (2) Mere aggregation is not predication: the set {a, b, R} no more contains the claim that a bears R to b than a dictionary contains a sentence, so something must distinguish R's actually relating a and b from R's merely occurring alongside them. (3) That something cannot be a further constituent, on pain of Bradley's regress, since the new constituent would itself stand in need of connection to the others. (4) So the unification must already be internal to R: R is not a bare item that happens to be placed next to things but is essentially a relating, whose occurrence in a complex just is a's being related to b. (5) Whatever is essentially a relating cannot occur without relating, just as whatever is essentially a smile cannot occur without a face. (6) So whenever R occurs in a complex with a and b, a is related to b by R. (7) But a false atomic proposition is precisely a complex in which R occurs with a and b while a is not R-related to b. (8) Therefore there are no false atomic propositions, and no relation can exist uninstantiated, since an existing but unrelating relation would be a relating that relates nothing. Which premise should I give up, why did it look so plausible, and what does each way out cost — taking the "non-relational tie" option seriously, or embracing the regress, or denying that false atomic propositions have the same constituent structure as true ones?
