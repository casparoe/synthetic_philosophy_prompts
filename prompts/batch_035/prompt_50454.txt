I'm a retired fisheries oceanographer -- thirty-odd years mostly doing stock assessment work, first on the Grand Banks and later on the Gulf of Maine -- and since retiring I've fallen down a philosophy rabbit hole, reading Elinor Ostrom after finally picking up Governing the Commons because everyone kept telling me to. So now I want something I never asked when I was working the surveys: take the collapse of the northern cod fishery and analyze it as a case of the tragedy of the commons. Not the biology of the collapse, which I know too well -- the biology is that we let the scientists' uncertainty get eaten by politics until there was nothing left to be uncertain about. The institutional story. For those who need the outline: the Grand Banks cod were essentially an open-access commons, Canada imposed national management (quotas, seasons, licensing) which is the textbook "Leviathan" solution Hardin recommends, and it still ended with the 1992 moratorium and thirty thousand people out of work. So who were the players, what did the actual payoff structure look like, and was ruin really the individually rational outcome for each fisherman, or did the tragedy actually live somewhere else -- say, in the regulator's incentive to keep quotas high, which looks less like Hardin's herdsmen and more like a principal-agent mess?

Then here's the part I really want you to chew on. A stone's throw, oceanographically speaking, the Maine lobster fishery looks like the same commons -- open access, high value, everyone traps as hard as they can -- except it never collapsed and is booming. And the standard explanation (this is Acheson's work, which Ostrom leans on) is that the lobstermen themselves built the governance: informal harbor gangs that defend fishing territories against outsiders, and conservation practices like V-notching the tails of egg-bearing females so everyone throws them back, plus protecting the big breeders. No privatization, no real state coercion behind any of it -- the state eventually wrote the practices into law, but the law came after the practice, mostly as a favor to the industry. By Hardin's lights that fishery should have been wrecked a century ago. So the question is what this pair of cases does to the theory: does the tragedy of the commons still work as a model of what happened on the Banks once you notice that "government regulation" was present and failed, and does the Maine case show the tragedy was never really about common ownership at all but about something the model never had room for -- the capacity of users to build monitoring and boundaries themselves?

I'd like you to be honest about where the model breaks rather than trying to rescue it with an epicycle, because I have a suspicion from my own career that the standard picture -- commons bad, Hardin right, Ostrom the footnote -- gets both fisheries backwards, and I'd like to know if that suspicion holds up or if I'm romanticizing my neighbors down the coast. If it helps, I don't need you to get the stock dynamics right, treat the fish as a replenishing resource with a growth rate and leave it at that. What I want is the mapping, both directions: does the theory explain the cases, and what do the cases do to the theory?
