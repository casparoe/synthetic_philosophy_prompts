Quick recap so we're on the same page: the Anthropocene Working Group spent ~15 years on the start-date question, picked Crawford Lake in Ontario as the golden spike, pegged the start to the early 1950s (the plutonium fallout layer), and then in March 2024 the IUGS just voted the whole proposal down, so officially we're still in the Holocene. But the underlying disagreement didn't move an inch. The rival dates are still all on the table: Watt's steam engine / around 1800 (Crutzen's own original pick), 1610 (the Orbis spike — the CO2 dip from the die-off in the Americas), 1945 (Trinity), and the 1950s radionuclide signal. And some of the geologists who voted no said the right category for the whole thing might be a geological "event" rather than an epoch, which is an interesting move but kind of sidesteps why people wanted the word in the first place.

Here's my hypothesis, presented as a hypothesis: this was never a stratigraphic question. The plutonium layer is about as sharp a signal as stratigraphy ever gets — if that's not a boundary, nothing is. The fight is really about what an epoch is FOR. Stratigraphers want units that stay legible in rock ten million years out; the early-start crowd (ecologists, earth-system people, historians) want a unit that tracks when humans became a planetary force, and those are two different projects that happen to share one name. Maybe I'm wrong though — maybe it's a proxy war about climate politics, or just mundane boundary-drawing where every option is fine and the only thing at stake is turf.
