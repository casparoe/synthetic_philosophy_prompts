My graduate reading group on distributive justice has spent the better part of a year going in circles on one argument, and I've realized I no longer want another round of it — I want a diagnosis of why it never resolves. On one side sits a telic egalitarian, in roughly Parfit's taxonomy: he holds that inequality is bad in itself, not because it breeds resentment or social instability, but simply in virtue of how welfare is distributed across persons. On the other side sits a prioritarian: she accepts that benefiting someone matters more the worse off they are, but insists that the total value of an outcome is exhausted by facts about individual lives — who is how well off, and how much any change would matter to each person taken singly — with no evaluative residue left over in the gaps or ratios between them. Her principal weapon is, of course, the leveling-down objection: if inequality were intrinsically bad, then a disaster that swept everyone down to a common level of misery would be in one respect an improvement, and she thinks the egalitarian either accepts this absurdity or quietly abandons the view. My egalitarian friend bites the bullet without visible discomfort. Yes, he says, leveling down is in one respect a change for the better — equality is an essentially comparative value in something like Temkin's sense — but it remains all-things-considered terrible, and the demand that every respect of value must bottom out in benefits to particular people is itself a substantive axiological commitment, not common ground he is obligated to concede.

The escape routes we've tried all dead-end in familiar ways, which is part of why I suspect the debate is structurally stuck rather than merely hard. Moving to a deontic version of egalitarianism — opposing relational inequality as an injustice rather than valuing equality as an outcome — just relocates the fight to the question of whether the injustice is explained by the bad outcome or by the complaint of the person who is worse off. Retreating to Rawls's difference principle helps only by staying vague about whether the concern for the worst-off group is comparative or absolute, and both of my friends can read their own view into it. The sufficientarian's move — caring about people below a threshold — is a third view, not an adjudication between the two we already have. What I find most interesting is that the two sides never actually disagree about any concrete case we construct: both prefer a world of equal moderate welfare to one of extreme inequality with a marginally higher total, both oppose leveling down, and both would fund the benefit to the badly-off stranger over the equally-sized benefit to the comfortable one. They disagree only about how to describe the reasons they agree on. My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the dispute is not about distributive value at all but about whether there can be irreducibly relational evaluative facts — facts that do not supervene on how each life individually goes — and that the leveling-down objection is best understood as a person-affecting intuition masquerading as a reductio of a view that never claimed to be person-affecting in that sense. But I can also see the case that this is partly verbal, a clash between an essentially comparative and an essentially absolute notion of value wearing the same word, with the genuine residue being some bedrock intuition neither side can argue for without circularity.

So here is what I'd like from you: a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement, using what each side actually says, not a survey of positions or a verdict on which is correct. Is it substantive, and if so at what level — about the supervenience base of value, about the person-affecting restriction, about whether "in one respect better" claims can be genuine even when no one benefits? Is part of it verbal in a way that a sharper distinction could dissolve, and which distinction? Are the two camps perhaps answering different questions under one heading — what outcomes are good versus what we owe to particular people — so that the priority view and telic egalitarianism are not even competitors? Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: whether there is a case, distinction, or formal result that would force at least one side to move; whether the disagreement should be recast as a metaphysical one about relational value that the leveling-down objection cannot touch; or whether you think this is bedrock disagreement about the shape of value itself, in which case say why no argument should be expected to move either side. Please write your response in British English.
