I'm sketching an essay arguing that reward in RL isn't itself valence, and I'd love comments on whether the argument works. The basic idea: reward is a number fed into a training loop, and its role is more like evolutionary fitness than like pleasure or pain. Fitness also "tells" organisms what to do, but nobody thinks genes *experience* fitness. So maybe reward doesn't need to be felt by the policy any more than fitness is felt by DNA.

The catch, and where I think it gets interesting: the analogy leaks. Natural selection is a blind search over variants, but gradient descent writes directly into the agent's parameters using the reward signal. The agent also builds an internal value function, and there's a difference between "the trainer uses reward to shape the policy" and "the agent's own value predictions are wrong and get corrected" (the TD error thing). My hunch is that if anything in an RL agent deserves to be called valence, it's that second thing — the agent's internal expectation being violated — not the reward number itself.

I want to end with a proposed test: pain in biological agents isn't just a negative learning signal, it's an *asymmetrically strong* one (bad stuff gets prioritized, quicker updating, harder to extinguish). So maybe you could look for RL agents that learn asymmetrically from positive vs negative prediction errors beyond what the task requires, and treat that asymmetry as a marker of something pain-like.

Questions: does the fitness analogy do real work or is it just rhetorical? Where does it break down most badly? And is my proposed test actually meaningful or just a restatement of the problem in fancier words? Also feel free to poke at anything I glossed over too fast.
