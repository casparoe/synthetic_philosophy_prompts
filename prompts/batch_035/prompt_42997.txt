I'm a philosophy graduate student who has just finished a term seminar on the metaphysics and epistemology of mathematics, and I've come out of it with a nagging suspicion I want checked. The seminar spent weeks on Benacerraf's dilemma in something like its modern shape: the platonist seems to get a uniform, face-value semantics for mathematical language — the sentence about primes "there are infinitely many prime numbers" comes out true in exactly the way it appears to claim — but then owes an account of how causally embedded creatures like us could know anything about causally inert abstract objects. The nominalist or formalist gets a comfortable epistemology but has to explain away or paraphrase an enormous amount of ordinary mathematical practice and, on many views, the indispensability of mathematics to our best science. What strikes me is that the seminar rehearsed basically the moves available by the early 1990s — the indispensability response, the attempt to soften the causal requirement on knowledge, the view that we perceive certain elementary set-theoretic facts, the claim that a non-causal reliability relation can be brute or explained by mathematical truths' being necessary — and that the literature since then mostly refines these positions rather than settling anything. Nobody converts. Reviewers of the major books seem to talk past each other.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is not just because the problem is hard, but because the two sides are not actually disagreeing about one thing. The platonist's deepest commitment seems to be semantic: whatever else we say, mathematics is a body of truths with determinate subject matter, and any account of knowledge must be accommodated to that, not vice versa. The nominalist's deepest commitment seems to be epistemological: knowledge must in principle be explicable in the same terms as any other knowledge, and where that can't be done, the semantics is what has to go. If that's right, the "dilemma" functions less as an argument than as a lever that forces each side to reveal which desideratum it will pay to preserve the other — and the resulting fight over priority is not obviously resolvable by argument, because each side treats the other's priority claim as the very thing in dispute. But I can also see a competing diagnosis: that there is a hidden substantive question underneath, namely what "access" or "reliability" must amount to when the objects are non-spatiotemporal, and that progress would consist in a genuinely worked-out account of non-causal epistemic warrant, not in further arm-rassling about semantics versus epistemology.

So what I want from you is a diagnosis, not a survey and not a verdict on platonism itself. Is the disagreement substantive, verbal (say, about what 'exists' or 'knows' means), or a case of two research programs answering different questions under one heading? If it is about which desideratum takes priority, is that the kind of thing that can be argued for, or only assumed? Is there a formulation of the dilemma that would put real pressure on both sides at once, or some distinction — maybe between explaining the reliability of our mathematical beliefs and justifying them — that would count as genuine progress? And be honest with me about whether you think this is a debate that could in principle terminate, or one where the most we should expect is increasingly articulate stalemate, and why.
