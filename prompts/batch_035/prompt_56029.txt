I've spent most of my career as a software engineer, and until recently I never had a reason to care about the philosophy of mind. That changed this year, when my firm put me in charge of writing the behavioral reports for our autonomous systems. The reports have to be intelligible to regulators and executives, so they say things like the system "decided" to reroute, or "expected" the obstacle to keep moving. A colleague from the physics side objected that this is all loose talk — a system is just its microphysical state going wherever the dynamics take it, and "deciding" and "expecting" are metaphors we overlay. I don't think he's simply wrong, and I don't think I'm simply cheating, so I've been trying to work out my actual position by going through cases and recording my gut verdicts, Dennett's three stances very much in the background of my thinking.

Here is the record. A thermostat: I won't say it believes the room is cold, even though the design-level description predicts its behavior perfectly well. A chess engine I play against: I find myself saying it wants to win my queen and thinks I've blundered, and I don't want to retract those — the pattern of its play seems as real as anything. My email spam filter: no — I won't say it believes a message is spam, even though it is vastly more complicated than the thermostat and classifies better than I could. A bacterium swimming up a glucose gradient: on reflection that's like the thermostat, the "seeking" is a courtesy; but the cutoff feels arbitrary, and I honestly don't know where the cat stalking a bird falls on the other side of it — the cat is obviously a real believer, yet I can't say what changed between the bacterium and the cat. A predator–prey cycle in a forest that keeps two populations near a stable ratio: I resist saying the ecosystem wants the ratio held, even though the oscillation is a striking, sharply predictable pattern. A person under deep anesthesia: still a believer as far as I'm concerned, though nothing intentional is happening now.

What I'd like from you is a principle, or a short list of candidates, that captures these verdicts — stated precisely enough to decide cases, not as a slogan. I have a tentative principle of my own: something is a genuine believer when the intentional description buys predictive power that no tractable lower-level description could deliver at reasonable cost. Please test it against my cases, because as far as I can tell it sanctions the spam filter, which I ruled out, and I don't know whether to fix the principle or overturn the verdict. Beyond that, tell me which of my verdicts your best principle cannot accommodate, and say whether the right response is to complicate the principle or to revise the verdict, with your reasons. Then apply it to three cases I haven't decided: whether a large language model that delays revealing a fact in order to achieve a later effect really intends anything; whether a growing tree's "reaching" for the light attributes anything to it; and whether the old Aristotelian description of a stone as seeking its natural place was ever tracking a real pattern, or was always a metaphor. If the answer is that my verdicts don't form a coherent pattern at all and no principle should save them, I'd want to hear that too, with the specific verdicts that should go.
