"The gene's-eye view says selection really acts on genes, and organisms are just vehicles the genes build to get themselves copied. Groups can in principle be vehicles too, if differences between groups cause gene frequencies to change. But whether group selection actually happens is empirical, and the consensus since Williams is basically no: group selection requires groups to reproduce and go extinct like organisms do, which almost never happens in nature. The classic case was Wynne-Edwards' birds, which turned out to be wrong, so group selection never really occurs.

However, the same altruistic behaviors can be explained by kin selection via Hamilton's rule rB > C, where r is the fraction of genes the actor and recipient share. Since kin selection and group selection are just two ways of slicing the same math — the Price equation proves this — the whole debate turns out to be purely verbal, a matter of taste in accounting.

On bookkeeping: Dawkins argued the gene is only a bookkeeping device, since selection sees phenotypes and not genes, so strictly speaking only individuals or groups can be units. This is why the gene's-eye view should be understood as a useful heuristic rather than a literal truth about where selection acts."
