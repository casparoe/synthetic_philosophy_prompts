I'm a distributed systems engineer: I design consensus protocols for a living and I'm fluent in Paxos, Raft, quorum systems, the CAP theorem, and the distinction between safety and liveness properties. I've recently started reading the judgment aggregation literature, and it strikes me that a lot of it sounds like problems I solve on a normal Tuesday — a set of nodes each holding binary views on some propositions, an aggregation function, and a requirement that the collective output be logically consistent, which smells like a safety invariant. Restate the central positions and results of that literature in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can: the doctrinal paradox as a quorum-intersection or consistency failure, the premise-based versus conclusion-based procedures as two different commit strategies, the Condorcet-style and List–Pettit impossibility results as results in the same genre as FLP or CAP. Then give me the residue report: which philosophical claims translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all — and for the untranslatable remainder, tell me whether it reflects something my vocabulary is missing or something in the original debate that is less substantive than it looks. Two things I especially want your view on. First, the philosophers' question of which aggregation procedure is normatively correct — what the group ought to conclude, given what its members believe — doesn't seem expressible as a protocol property at all; in my world we ask only whether a protocol is correct with respect to a specification someone hands us, never which specification the group should want. Second, the requirement that collective judgments be jointly consistent and complete is treated in the literature as a substantive normative constraint worth arguing about, whereas for me consistency is simply what a correct implementation guarantees; I can't tell whether that difference is itself part of the residue or just an artifact of the translation.
