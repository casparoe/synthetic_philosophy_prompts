I am a criminal defense attorney with eighteen years of practice. I know the vocabulary of my trade cold, but I have read almost no philosophy. A philosopher friend has lately been pressing on me David Lewis's late paper "Divine Evil," and I want to know whether its argument against penal substitution can be genuinely expressed in my legal vocabulary or whether it lives somewhere my vocabulary can't reach. Below is my lay reconstruction of the argument, partly from the paper and partly from my friend's gloss on it; correct it only if it is materially wrong, not as a matter of philosophical housekeeping.

The doctrine at issue is penal substitution in the atonement: the claim that Christ, innocent and consenting, suffers the punishment due to humanity's sins. Lewis asks whether an episode like that could even count as punishment rather than mere harm, and answers no: harming someone is not, in general, punishing anyone, and punishment is essentially tied to the guilt of the person punished — strip away the tie to desert and what remains is an infliction of suffering, not a punishment of anyone. As my friend reconstructed the concessions along the way: some of what passes for punishment behaves like a transferable debt — a fine paid on my behalf by a solvent relative harms no one wrongfully and extinguishes my obligation — but the moment a volunteer is substituted for the convicted offender in anything like real punishment, a flogging, imprisonment, or worse, the agent has stopped punishing anyone and has begun merely doing something terrible to a consenting person, so that consent of the volunteer changes nothing. Lewis's conclusion, as I understand it, is that if the doctrine is read as penal substitution, God perpetrates evil: there is no way to inflict that suffering on the innocent without doing evil, whatever the volunteer agreed to.

Restate this argument in the vocabulary of my practice, as faithfully as you can — charging and conviction, mens rea and culpability, in personam rights and non-delegable duties, vicarious liability and respondeat superior, restitution and fines, civil contempt, credit for time served, proportionality review, the voidness of a sentence imposed on someone never convicted — and then give me the residue report I actually want: which of Lewis's moves translate cleanly into legal categories, which translate only with distortion, and which have no expression in my vocabulary at all. I am especially interested in his central claim, that harming a consenting innocent cannot count as punishing anyone, because my vocabulary has something in the neighborhood — criminal liability does not transfer, my client cannot send his brother to serve his sentence, a sentence imposed on an unindicted person is void — and I cannot tell whether that is the same neighborhood or a different one. Tell me whether the legal concept doing the work was built for conceptual reasons or for institutional ones, and whether that difference matters to Lewis's argument. Finally, say what you make of the residue in both directions: whether the parts of the argument that don't translate — God's standing as the wronged party, the disproportion of infinite suffering to finite desert, the idea that the volunteer's consent is normatively inert — point to something my legal vocabulary cannot say, or to something that was less clear in Lewis's presentation than it looked. And keep the translation honest: if you think the law's refusal to permit substituted punishment already vindicates Lewis, show me exactly which legal concept carries the conclusion rather than smuggling in the law's own assumptions and calling it translation.
