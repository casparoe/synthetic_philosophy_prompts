I'm a program officer at a small foundation about to fund a three-year program — roughly five researchers — on a single theme: when does an adaptive-hypothesis explanation of a human psychological trait count as science rather than a just-so story? The funding is for conceptual and methodological work: inference standards, criteria for when an adaptationist claim is supported, study-design templates. It is not for running new empirical studies, though the outputs should be usable by the people who do run them.

I'd like a defended ranking of the open questions in this area, and I'll seed it with four candidates I've been circling. Rank them by how much they matter for the program's goal, add anything important I've left out, and tell me which of mine you'd cut.

1. The uniformity/variation question: is pancultural uniformity of a trait stronger evidence of adaptation than the field's critics concede, or is observed cross-cultural variation better read as facultative response to environment — and on either answer, what follows for how adaptation is inferred?
2. The truncated-hypothesis-space problem: when a researcher contrasts an adaptive explanation with a byproduct or noise alternative, is there a principled way to generate and grade the competing explanations, or is the comparison structurally underdetermined no matter how careful the study?
3. The calibration question: could the field adopt explicit evidential thresholds for adaptive claims — for instance, a rule about how many independent, risky predictions a hypothesis must survive, or preregistration of adaptive predictions before data collection — and would such thresholds actually separate good work from bad, or only add bureaucracy?
4. The comparative gap: adaptationist claims in morphology lean on the comparative and fossil record, but for psychology there is essentially no behavioral fossil. Is there anything methodological to build here, or is this better acknowledged and deprioritized?

For each item I want the reasoning, not a summary: what makes it upstream or downstream of the others, how tractable it is for purely conceptual work, and what development over the next three years — in the program or the field — would move it up or down your ranking. State the criteria you're implicitly using (importance, tractability, neglectedness, how much else depends on the answer), and where two of them pull in opposite directions, show how you weighed them. I'm less interested in the final list than in whether the ordering survives scrutiny, so please close by identifying where your ranking is most likely wrong: which single argument or consideration, if it went the other way, would most reorder the list — and whether you see a flaw in the framing itself, e.g., a reason these four questions are not really separable and my asking for a ranking between them already misdescribes the field's problem.
