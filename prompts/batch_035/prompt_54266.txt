I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am at work on a long essay about the afterlife of the postwar exchange between the legal philosopher H. L. A. Hart and Lon Fuller of Harvard. Last month I sat on a panel discussing the piece, and at the close a law professor on the panel disposed of my question about civil servants who refuse to apply statutes they consider gravely unjust by declaring that an unjust law is no law at all, adding that this had been the natural-law position since Augustine and that the positivists had never really answered it. I pressed him afterward in the hallway and got little beyond the name-drop. Before I put the maxim in print, I need to know what that sentence is actually claiming, because I suspect the panelist needed it to do more work than any of its senses can bear.

Please set out, in ordinary paragraphs, the distinct precise claims the maxim could be making. I would expect at least the following candidates: that a rule failing some standard of justice is not law in the strict sense at all; that it is law only in a secondary or corrupt sense, law in a qualified way; that it is fully valid law that nonetheless binds no one in conscience; that officials such as judges and clerks ought to decline to treat it as authoritative; and, at the weakest end, that it is mere emphasis, a dignified way of saying very unjust. For each candidate, say whether it is trivial, defensible, or false, and then address the part I most need: whether any of them supports the use the panelist put the maxim to, namely the conclusion that a refusing official is not breaking the law but declining to apply something that is not law in the first place. In particular, if the strongest defensible reading is only that valid law may be morally toothless, that seems to be a claim about obedience rather than about what law is, and one the positivists accept anyway, which would leave the panelist with a slogan that does not reach his conclusion. I would also like to know where Fuller himself would stand here: my understanding is that his complaint concerned failures of legality, of the kind found in rules that are secret, retroactive, or too vague to follow, rather than substantive injustice, so that a clear, public, prospectively announced but wicked statute may fall outside even his tent. If that is right, the panelist's use of the maxim would be doubly orphaned, supported neither by the positivist nor by the natural lawyer he invoked. Tell me whether I have this right, where the readings genuinely divide Hart from Fuller rather than merely sounding as if they do, and whether the Augustine pedigree matters to any of it or is decoration. Please write the whole reply as continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write a careful letter, since I may quote from it in the essay.
