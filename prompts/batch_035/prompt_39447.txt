I'm taking a graduate seminar on the ethics of rescue, and I've been assigned to defend aggregation in life-saving cases against the anti-aggregationist position made famous by John Taurek's paper "Should the Numbers Count?" I want to rehearse this debate against a smart opponent rather than a strawman, so please play a committed anti-aggregationist philosopher: someone who holds, roughly, that in a choice between saving one stranger and saving five, the fact that there are five does not by itself give any agent a reason to save the five, and that what fairness to each person threatened requires is giving each an equal chance at survival rather than maximizing lives saved. Give the character real philosophical resources to draw on — the thought that each person's death is a total loss for that person whether or not others are present, the claim that there is no further loss that belongs to "the group" over and above the losses of its members, the fairness of equal chances, and a principled suspicion of treating persons as mere sites of impersonal value to be summed — and let the character deploy them sharply, conceding minor points (say, that numbers matter for logistics, or for how we describe the outcome afterward) but never the main one. Stay in character until I write "stop," and keep each reply to about one paragraph so this reads like a live seminar exchange rather than a lecture.

Here is my opening move. Suppose I have a single dose of a drug, and I can use it either to save one stranger or to save five strangers, all six in equally desperate need, each of whom loses exactly the same thing if not saved: their whole remaining life. When I save the one instead of the five, there is one person whose life is saved and four additional people, each of whose remaining life is lost. Those four extra losses are not losses of some mysterious sixth entity called "the group"; they are losses of four individual people — exactly the kind of loss you already agree counts as a bad thing when it happens to a single person. So my claim is this: I don't need to add anything across persons to get a reason to save the five. The separate, unaggregated losses of four additional individuals are already reasons, and they are reasons of the very kind you accept in the one-person case. Unless you can show that a loss which counts against an outcome when it is the only loss somehow stops counting when other losses occur alongside it, it looks like you must grant me the five. How do you deny me the five without denying me the one?
