Compose a philosophical dialogue, roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words, between two researchers who disagree about whether the presumption of independence can be formalized.

By the presumption of independence — the concept the dialogue should revolve around — I mean the everyday working rule that when we can see no reason why two quantities should be connected, we reason as though they were independent. The dialogue should treat it as what it is: the tacit engine behind a great deal of apparently rigorous reasoning. Treating the millionth digit of pi as a fair coin flip, even though its value is fixed; statistical mechanics assuming that incoming molecules' velocities are uncorrelated with a particle's past; judging that a physical constant is unlikely to fall in some narrow range, on the grounds that nothing in our theories seems to care about that range. The rule also fails, and the dialogue should make the failure modes concrete: correlations show up exactly where hidden structure connects things, as with long-range correlations near a critical point, or with a pseudorandom generator whose seed someone chose adversarially — a deterministic object that only looked random until you learned who picked the seed.

Character A is a mathematician who came to this problem through AI safety. Her view: when a system — human or artificial — reasons about the world using heuristic arguments, each individual argument carries a hidden bet that its unexamined variables are independent. With humans, decades of domain experience keep the bets mostly sound. An artificial reasoner making many such bets in a short period will not get independent failures: if one argument in a family quietly presumes independence where it shouldn't, its neighbors probably do too, so errors arrive correlated, on the same day, in a way that no per-argument sanity check will catch. Therefore, she argues, we need something like a formal language for heuristic arguments, in which the presumption of independence is an explicit, inspectable move whose failures can at least be seen and debated — otherwise we are trusting our survival to informal reasoning we cannot audit.

Character B is a statistical physicist. He is not a Luddite: he uses heuristics he knows are unsound and can explain precisely why they still work — mean-field-type approximations that ignore correlations he knows are there, applied in regimes where ignoring them is known, by experience, to be cheap. His position: heuristics get their power from tacit, accumulated knowledge of a domain, and any attempt to formalize them runs into a dilemma — the formal system will either be too weak to express the good heuristics, in which case it is useless, or strong enough to express them all, in which case it is just probability theory with extra steps, and the real judgment about when the presumption of independence is safe remains outside the formalism exactly where it started. He is genuinely troubled by A's correlated-failure point, but he thinks it shows only that informal reasoning needs institutional safeguards, not that it can be replaced.

Structural requirements for the dialogue:

- Both characters are equally competent. Neither is a strawman, and neither capitulates.
- Real back-and-forth: each turn must engage the other's actual last point — restating, granting, or pushing back on it — not segue into a canned speech.
- The argument must be carried by the concrete cases, not by meta-commentary about "heuristics" in the abstract. At minimum, the dialogue should work through the deep-digit case, the molecular-chaos assumption, the adversarially chosen seed, and the correlated failure of a family of heuristic arguments that cannot be counted like independent votes.
- Each character must, at some point, concede something that genuinely costs them, and the other should notice the concession and make something of it.
- The dialogue should not resolve. It should end with the two jointly articulating the single question that would decide the matter between them, and each stating, concretely, what an answer to it would have to look like for them to change their mind.

The characters should not cite literature, name specific authors or works, or trade references; everything they need stands on its own content. Write it so a reader who has never encountered this debate could follow every exchange, and so a reader who works on it would learn something from the concessions.
