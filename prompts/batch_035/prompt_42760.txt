I run a small type-design workshop for working graphic designers, and next month I'm pairing a screening of Gary Hustwit's documentary *Helvetica* (2007) with what I'm billing as a "philosophy night." The question I want to organize the evening around comes from an essay I've been chewing on about the cognitive science of reading. The author points out that by adulthood each of us has read tens of millions of instances of the letter 'a' — Bodoni 'a's, blackletter 'a's, a child's wobbly chalk 'a', the double-story 'a' on a medicine bottle, the single-story 'a' in a neon sign — and something in us treats every one of these wildly different physical objects as *the same thing*, the letter 'a', while a stray stroke or an unclosed bowl turns a genuine 'a' into an 'o' or into visual noise. His proposal is that a letter is not a shape at all but a role: a slot in a system of contrasts, defined by what it is *not* (not an 'o', not an 'e', not a 'd'), which each style or era fills in its own way. Identity, on this view, lives in the role; character lives in the style; and the two can come apart.

I'd like you to read *Helvetica* through exactly this question: what makes every 'a' an 'a' — and what does the film have to say about the relationship between the category a letter belongs to and the style it wears? The film is built as a quarrel between two camps, and it strikes me that the quarrel might be an unrecognized metaphysical one. On one side are the modernists, above all Massimo Vignelli, who did the New York City subway signage in Helvetica and defends it as a neutral, timeless instrument — a letterform with no personality of its own, so that content can speak through it. On the other are designers like Paula Scher, who ties Helvetica's dominance to American corporate culture of the 1960s and 1970s (big companies wanting to appear open and accessible while staying powerful), and David Carson, with his insistence that legibility is not the same thing as communication, and that a letterform that is harder to read can say more. And threaded between them are the montage sequences of Helvetica saturating the urban environment, plus a middle camp of designers who embrace the challenge of making the ubiquitous typeface speak differently each time. If the identity of a letter is a role filled variously by styles, does that suggest both camps are partly right — the modernists defending the role, the postmodernists insisting on the saturated style — and does the film dramatize that independence? Think especially about what it means that the *same* glyphs, nearly unchanged since the 1950s, serve the MTA and American Apparel and countless tech logos while carrying entirely different cultural freight.
