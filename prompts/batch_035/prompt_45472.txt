My two flatmates had a massive row on Sunday about whether the world is actually getting less violent, and I've been turning it over since because I honestly can't decide who came out on top. Ravi says it obviously is — he read one of those big books about the long-term decline in violence, and his point is that if you look per capita, your chance of dying in a war or a homicide has collapsed compared to tribal societies or the medieval period, and that per capita is the only fair way to measure it, like comparing road deaths per mile driven rather than just totalling them up. Jess thinks this is a con. Her view is that absolute numbers are what matter morally — the twentieth century had more dead human beings than any century before it, and telling the dead "yes but as a percentage you're doing great" is a grotesque way of keeping score. More people alive means more people available to be killed, and a world where 100 million die in wars is worse than one where 2 million die, whatever the denominators say.

It went on from there. Ravi answered that by Jess's metric, progress is nearly impossible by definition — any growing population that stays even slightly violent will rack up bigger absolute totals forever, so her measure can never register improvement, which shows it's broken. Jess said that's exactly the problem with per capita: it lets a growing world hide a worsening absolute toll behind flattering rates, and anyway the choice between the two isn't a neutral bit of maths, it's a value judgement smuggled in as arithmetic. Then it got definitional. Jess brought up factory farming — if you count what we do to tens of billions of animals a year as violence, then by any measure the world is more violent than it's ever been, and Ravi's claim quietly assumes "violence" means humans killing humans, which she says is question-begging. Ravi said a claim about violence just is a claim about human-on-human killing, otherwise it's like complaining the crime rate is up because you've added shrimp. And right at the end Jess threw in something that neither of them had time to develop: maybe there's no fact of the matter at all, because the answer changes depending on which timescales you pick (the world wars make the twentieth century look terrible, everything after 1945 makes it look lovely) and whether you count things like sanctions and poverty as violence.

They asked me to referee over dinner and I couldn't — every time I agreed with one of them the other had a comeback I couldn't answer. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right about each specific point, not just who wins overall? I'd especially like to know whether the per-capita-versus-absolute thing is genuinely a substantive moral disagreement or just people picking the measure that flatters their conclusion, and whether Jess's "no fact of the matter" move is a real insight or just a way of giving up. Please say who's more right overall, and what if anything you think both of them are missing.
