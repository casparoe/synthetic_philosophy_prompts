A friend of mine has been pressing an argument on me for months, and I can't find where it goes wrong, though it seems to prove far too much. It's about rescue choices. I've no philosophy training, so please bear with the numbering.

(1) Every person counts for one, and no one counts for more than one: no person's life is worth more than another's.
(2) To say we should save the five rather than the one because they are five is to say that the five outweigh the one, which is just what (1) forbids.
(3) So the bare fact that one side has five people on it and the other has one cannot, by itself, be a reason to save the five.
(4) A consideration that gives no reason must be ignored, not merely outweighed; so the decision must be made by something other than numbers, and a coin toss between the two sides is the fairest such procedure.
(5) Nothing in the argument depended on the figures five and one, so it applies equally to one versus fifty, or one versus a million.
(6) So when a firefighter can reach either the room with one trapped child or the landing with a hundred people, and the coin lands for the child, it is not merely permitted but required by fairness that the hundred are left to die.
(7) And by the same reasoning, any policy that knowingly sacrifices one person to prevent a hundred deaths — a medicine given to one badly allergic patient instead of used to stop an outbreak, say — is forbidden by fairness itself.

My friend accepts all seven conclusions cheerfully, and that is what I cannot swallow: equal concern for each person should not require indifference between one death and a million. Yet when I stare at the steps individually, each one looks like plain fairness to me. I would most like to keep (1); I'd happily give up any of the others. Which step should I reject, what exactly goes wrong in it, and what do I pay for rejecting it? Please answer in British English.
