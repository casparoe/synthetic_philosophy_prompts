So I'm a novelist, mid-draft on a book where the narrator does something genuinely awful in the first chapter and then stays completely flat about it — no remorse, no self-justification, just this affectless account of what she did and what happened next. My editor keeps saying readers will either forgive her or put the book down, and I realized what she means is basically Strawson: whether we resent someone depends on the quality of will we perceive in them, and a narrator who signals no quality of will at all gives the reader nothing to hook resentment — or forgiveness — onto. The Stranger has been my touchstone the whole time and its finally bugging me that I only half understand why it works. So I want a real reading of Camus through Strawson's reactive attitudes, not vibes.

Heres what I keep chewing on. The prosecutor in the trial spends ages on the mother's funeral — the coffee, the cigarette, the girlfriend the next day, the Fernandel comedy — and treats the killing as just more evidence of the same coldness. Read through Strawson, that's the courtroom trying to reconstruct a quality of will out of an absence of the right reactions: Meursault didn't display grief, therefore no proper regard for others, therefore guilty in his soul and not just of the act. Meanwhile Meursault himself refuses the whole exchange from his side too — he never resents anyone, never blames the prosecutor, never gets indignant, just finds it all a bit absurd that any of this counts. So nobody in the courtroom is operating in the participant stance, exactly when the whole institution exists to host it.

Things I want the reading to actually deal with:

- Is Meursault's flatness a refusal of the participant stance, or an inability to enter it? Strawson distinguishes between withholding reactive attitudes (treating someone as ill-willed) and suspending them for the objective attitude (treating someone as a thing to be managed — the mad, the very young, the victim of circumstances). Does Camus's narrator belong in either box, or does he break the taxonomy? And does it matter morally which box he's in?
- The one place Meursault genuinely explodes is the chaplain at the end — the one person in the book insisting on treating him as a soul that can be reached. Is Camus saying something sharp about the participant stance itself here, that being offered it can be a kind of assault? Because that's the thing I've never seen anyone say out loud and I suspect it's the actual heart of the book.
- Where does the novel resist Strawson rather than illustrate him? Strawson thinks the reactive attitudes are basically natural, inescapable, and what makes us human to each other; the objective attitude is a temporary tool, not a way to live. Camus seems to be flirting with exactly the thing Strawson says we couldn't stand — a whole life conducted from the outside, indifferent and indifferent-to. Does the ending, with its famous indifference, land as peace or as loss? Both readings feel available and I want the reading to commit.
- And then the part I actually care about for my book: if I want readers to stay with my narrator for 300 pages, do I have to smuggle in one moment where she reacts like a participant — a flash of resentment, guilt, gratitude, anything — to keep the reader's own reactive attitudes alive? Or is that cheating, and is there some structural trick Camus uses instead that I'm not seeing? Be honest if the answer is just that Camus got away with it because it was 1942 and I won't.

I'm not looking for a literature review, just a serious, argued reading that takes both the novel and Strawson's actual distinctions seriously, and doesn't pretend the fit is perfect where it isn't.
