I keep running into the same move in conversations about Kripkenstein — the rule-following paradox from Kripke's reading of Wittgenstein, where the skeptic asks what fact about me makes it the case that by "plus" I ever meant plus rather than quus (the function that agrees with addition on all sums I've actually computed but returns 5 for anything larger). Since no finite mental state, no number of past applications, no feeling of understanding seems to contain the infinitely many future applications in advance, it looks like there's no fact of the matter about what I meant — and if that's right for "plus," it's right for every word.

The move I keep encountering, usually from friends who work in machine learning, goes like this: the paradox is an artifact of pre-computational philosophy of mind, and it dissolves once you take seriously that the brain is a computational system. Grasping a rule, they say, is like having a program: the program for addition is a finite object, but it determines every value of the function for every input, including the ones never yet computed. My past training left a program in my head the way training leaves weights in a network, and that program — not some mysterious "grasp" — is the fact the skeptic says is missing. The quus hypothesis simply describes a different program, and the question of which one I was running is an ordinary empirical question. On this basis they conclude that Kripke's skeptical challenge is a curiosity of the 1980s literature, retired by cognitive science the way vitalism was retired by biochemistry.

Audit this analogy. First give me the explicit mapping: what plays the role of the rule, the grasping of the rule, the applications of the rule, the finite past training, and the normativity — the difference between continuing the series correctly and merely continuing it as I in fact will. Then say where the mapping breaks, and be disciplined about which breakdowns matter for the use my friends are putting the analogy to, which is dissolving the skeptical argument rather than merely explaining my behavior. Some candidate trouble spots you should address rather than dodge: a program is itself a symbolic object that someone must interpret, and Wittgenstein's own remarks about a machine as a symbol of its function suggest the interpretation problem just recurses one level up; a physical machine can misfire, jam, or run on corrupted hardware, so the program describes what it will do but seemingly not what it ought to do — and the quus skeptic's challenge was always about the ought; and any finite program is compatible with multiple functions being attributed to it, depending on how we generalize beyond its actual execution history, so quus may stalk the machine too. You should also consider whether the analogy can be repaired — whether, for instance, appealing to the program's causal role, its teleology, or the way it was learned from a community of teachers restores the missing normative element — and if so at what cost to the original claim that this is an ordinary empirical matter.

Finally, deliver a verdict: does the computationalist analogy actually answer Kripke's skeptic, does it merely restate the dispositional analysis the skeptic already anticipated and found wanting, or does it quietly concede the skeptical point while changing the subject? And if the analogy fails, say whether some successor analogy could do the job better, or whether the moral to draw is instead that no inner mechanism — computational or otherwise — could ever be the kind of fact about meaning the skeptic was asking for, so that the interesting action lies elsewhere, e.g. in Kripke's skeptical solution's appeal to the community's patterns of assertion and correction. Feel free to refer to the standard landmarks — Kripke's 1982 book, the Investigations' rule-following discussion, the dispositional and non-reductionist straight solutions — but don't let the essay become a literature survey; the object is the analogy and what it can and cannot carry.
