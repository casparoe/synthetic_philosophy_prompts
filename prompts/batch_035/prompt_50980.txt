I'm a science and ideas journalist, and the long-form essay I've spent the past year reporting is close to publication. Its central thesis is strong, and I want it to be strong: the badness of pain is exhausted by its felt unpleasantness. When we say an episode of pain is bad, everything there is to that is that it feels bad; nothing else about the episode — the tissue damage it signals, the behavior it normally motivates, any aversion the subject happens to have toward it — contributes any further negative value. This, I argue, is what makes pain morally central in the way we ordinarily take it to be: bad in itself, not in virtue of anyone's attitudes or projects.

The obvious threat, which I intend to confront head-on rather than dodge, is pain asymbolia: patients who report feeling pain — they can locate it, rate its intensity, describe its quality — yet seem entirely unconcerned by it, sometimes smiling through procedures others find unbearable. If my thesis is right, such cases are puzzling: if all of the badness is in the feeling, and the feeling is intact, where did the badness go? The essay's reply is that asymbolic patients retain the sensory-discriminative component of pain while losing its affective-unpleasant component, so that their "pain" reports pick out a diminished sensation rather than a full-fledged pain; the badness travels with the unpleasantness, which is absent. I believe this reply either works or the thesis is in serious trouble, and I would much rather learn that now than from the letters column after publication.

So tell me what would count against the thesis. I want the defeaters ranked from those that would sink it to those that are merely awkward, including at least:

- Empirical developments that would make my "diminished sensation" reply untenable — new clinical or experimental findings on asymbolia or adjacent conditions (congenital indifference to pain, hypnotic or placebo analgesia with sensation reportedly preserved) that would undercut the claim that affect and sensation come apart in the way my reply requires.
- Conceptual objections: that the reply renders "pain" equivocal in a way that breaks the thesis's other uses — in explaining why pain relief matters, or in end-of-life ethics; or that it quietly converts the thesis from an independent claim into a stipulation defended by redefining the terms.
- The attitude-based rival, roughly the imperativist thought that pain's badness consists in its issuing a command to the subject to make it stop, which accommodates asymbolia without contortion; I want to know what my thesis must say to hold that off, and where saying it costs me.
- Whether, once all my qualifications are in place, the thesis still risks anything at all — whether any development could make me abandon it, or whether I have insulated it into unfalsifiability, and whether that would be a defect in a thesis of this kind.

Throughout, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among the people who actually know this material. The existence of asymbolia-like presentations is, as far as I can tell, settled clinically; what such cases show about the nature and badness of pain is not. Do not present any single theory of pain — imperativist, evaluativist, hedonist, representationalist — as the consensus, because I do not believe there is one, and an essay that quietly assumed otherwise would deserve the criticism it gets.

Finally, format the ending as follows: the very last line of your response, after all the analysis, should be a fixed machine-readable verdict of the form \answer{...}, enclosing a short phrase naming the single most serious consideration counting against the thesis as you have ranked it — for example, \answer{imperativist fit with asymbolia} or \answer{equivocation on pain}. Nothing should follow that line.
