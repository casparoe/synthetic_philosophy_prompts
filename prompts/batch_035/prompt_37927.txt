I work as a data scientist at a regional public-health agency, and a dispute has broken out in our modeling group over how to explain county-level variation in vaccine uptake. Our lead modeler holds that every acceptable explanation must terminate in facts about individual agents — their incentives, beliefs, and constraints — and that any model that helps itself to a supra-individual variable, such as community trust or local information ecosystems, has stopped explaining and started describing. He calls this a principled commitment to methodological individualism. Our consultant from the sociology department disagrees, and the analogy that now carries most of the argument is hers: explaining a society through individual decisions, she says, is like explaining a forest by cataloguing individual trees — you miss the canopy, the microclimate, the fact that the trees jointly produce the very conditions each tree grows in. The lead modeler fires back with an analogy of his own: a rush-hour traffic jam is nothing over and above the cars and drivers in it, and if you catch yourself saying that the jam decided to persist for another hour, you have reified a summary statistic. Both of them are using their comparison as an argument rather than an illustration, and I have been asked to adjudicate at our next team meeting, which is why this has suddenly become my problem.

Please audit both analogies rather than the slogans attached to them. Lay out what maps onto what in each — in the forest, what plays the role of the individual agent, of the social fact, of the feedback loop that generates the whole — and then say where each mapping breaks down. Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the concrete decision in front of us, namely whether a variable like community trust may appear in a causal model in its own right or must first be reduced to agent-level terms before it earns a place, from the disanalogies that are merely decorative. Then tell me which analogy, if either, can actually carry the conclusion its user wants from it, and whether there is a third comparison that would fit this case better than either.
