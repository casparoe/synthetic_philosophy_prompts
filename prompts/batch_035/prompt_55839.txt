Please explain the problem of old evidence in Bayesian confirmation theory, and then work through the following twist on it, which I have not seen addressed in the standard treatments.

First, set up the problem itself carefully: a Bayesian agent assigns probabilities, updates by conditionalization on what she learns, and confirmation of a hypothesis H by evidence E is standardly spelled out in terms of E raising H's probability. Now suppose the evidence is old — the agent has known E for years, so P(E) is already 1, and no amount of conditionalizing on E can change the probability of anything. Yet intuitively a theory that retrodicts long-known data can be strongly confirmed by it (the stock example being general relativity and the precession of Mercury's perihelion, which had been a known anomaly for decades before 1915). Explain why this is not merely a technical annoyance but a genuine tension in the Bayesian picture of science: a theory's ability to fit data the theorist already had in hand is, historically, one of the main things scientists count as confirmatory, and the machinery seems to rule it out by construction.

Second, explain the two standard lines of repair and where each one cheats: (a) the counterfactual strategy, which asks not what the agent believes but what she would have believed had she not known E — and which quietly changes the subject from the agent's actual degrees of belief to a hypothetical agent's, thereby abandoning the subjectivist motivation of the Bayesian program; and (b) the logical-learning strategy, which models the agent as uncertain about what her own theories entail, so that "deriving E from H" becomes a genuine learning event — and which thereby gives up logical omniscience and inherits the problem of where the probabilities over logical facts come from.

Third, the twist. Consider a theorist who deliberately constructs a hypothesis to fit data that has been sitting in the literature for decades — say, a modified-gravity model built today, explicitly parameterized to reproduce the galaxy rotation curves measured in the 1970s and 1980s. The data are maximally old; the fit is by design rather than prediction; and yet the model is, in an important sense, more testable now than the bare dark-matter alternative, because it makes novel predictions for lensing and for systems with no rotation-curve data. Assess whether the old-evidence problem, the ad-hocness worry, and the novel-prediction debate are three problems or one. In particular: does the counterfactual repair distinguish between the retrodiction of a theory built before the data (Einstein) and a theory built after them (our modified-gravity theorist), given that both get the same counterfactual likelihood? If it cannot, say so plainly and explain what extra machinery would be needed — something like a probability distribution over theory-construction processes, or a distinction between the evidential support of a theory and the epistemic position of the theorist who holds it.

You may assume the reader knows Bayes' theorem, conditionalization, and the ratio measure of confirmation, but has not read the old-evidence literature. Write in American English, at the level of an advanced philosophy-of-science seminar.
