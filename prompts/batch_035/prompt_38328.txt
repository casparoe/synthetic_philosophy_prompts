In the corner of AI safety research concerned with making a goal-directed agent pursue its objective "without side effects," a widespread design practice is to add a penalty term that measures the agent's impact on the world as deviation from a counterfactual baseline: compare the state of the world after the agent acts with the state it would have been in had the agent instead done nothing. The literature plays with several variants of "doing nothing" — idleness from the very beginning, or idleness recomputed at each time step so that past deviations gradually stop counting — but the shared skeleton is that impact is distance from inaction, and the agent is rewarded for achieving its assigned task while keeping that distance small. The intuition behind this is easy to state: a good assistant should accomplish what it was asked to do while leaving the rest of the world as close to untouched as it can.

I'm a graduate student working on safe exploration and offline RL, and while implementing one of these penalties I got stuck on a question that seems more philosophical than technical, and I can't find where anyone has addressed it head-on. What conception of value does this practice presuppose? It appears to treat inaction as a moral zero point: the world as it currently stands has a privileged claim to remain as it is, and every departure from it, in any direction, counts as a cost. But the status quo the agent is told to preserve is not morally neutral in general — it may contain ongoing harms, festering problems, deterioration the agent could arrest. A paramedic deviates enormously from inaction, and we call that excellent. It also seems to presuppose that the designer's reward function is the sole license to change anything: whatever it fails to mention is thereby placed under conservation, as if unmodeled value were best protected by freezing the world that contains it. Is the implicit theory something like a precautionary principle about our ignorance of value, a mere tractability convention that presupposes nothing, or a genuine commitment to the normative standing of the status quo? And is the practice even internally consistent, given that the same community celebrates agents that clean up disasters while the penalty scores disaster relief as maximal impact, since restoring a broken status quo is a huge deviation from doing nothing?

I'd like you to treat this the way one might treat an odd doctrine of some legal system: reconstruct the implicit theory that the deviation-from-inaction practice only makes sense under, distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and identify where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that several different views of value would in fact license the same penalty term. Then tell me what would have to change in the design practice if the underlying view were replaced — say, if impact were redefined as deviation from what an ideally wise and fully informed agent would have done, making impact a distance from wisdom rather than a distance from idleness: which parts of the practice would carry over, which would have to be abandoned, and what new puzzles that replacement would create of its own. Please write your answer in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — just flowing paragraphs, the way one writes a letter or a long email.
