Here is my opening move. In your last years you warned that we are entering an age of counterfeit people — artefacts fluent enough to pass as people and to be treated accordingly — and you clearly wanted a principled line between the real and the counterfeit. Yet by your own lights the intentional stance looks powerless to draw it. The stance is warranted wherever attributing beliefs and desires buys predictive leverage, and you argued that the patterns it tracks are as real as centres of gravity. A large language model predicts what people would say better than most people do; on your criterion it is therefore as much a believer as we are, and calling it a counterfeit looks like an admission that your criterion does not capture whatever distinction you are now appealing to. Your old separation between genuine intentional-stance attribution and merely as-if interpretation does not help either, since it was supposed to turn on whether the attribution earns its keep — and here it earns its keep. Meanwhile, in the other direction, the model's confident confabulations are exactly what the design stance predicts from a next-token predictor with no world to be right about, so your rival stance explains the same behaviour without any belief-talk at all. So, Professor: does the intentional stance give you any principled difference between a real believer and a counterfeit person, or must your case against the counterfeits rest on something else entirely — and if so, on what, given how deflationary you were about what real believers amount to?
