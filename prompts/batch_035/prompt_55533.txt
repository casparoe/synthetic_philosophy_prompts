I'm a high school junior taking an elective called "Great Debates in Science and Society," and our term paper has to be on a "move" people make in arguments rather than a topic. I've noticed two moves that keep showing up in our class discussions about consciousness, the origin of life, and morality, and I want to write about them, but I can't tell whether philosophy has already named and dissected them or whether I'd be duplicating something under a different label.

The first move: someone asks how a complicated thing could have arisen from simple parts — how life came from chemistry, or how right and wrong could come from animals avoiding punishment — and the answers fall into two camps. One camp says "it's just atoms bumping into each other, there's nothing more to explain," and the other camp says the material picture must be incomplete, so we need something extra on top: a soul, a designing intelligence, a moral law written into the universe. What strikes me is that both answers seem bad in the same way. The first doesn't explain anything, it just asserts there's nothing to explain, and the second answers a small mystery with a much bigger one, like explaining why a watch keeps time by saying it contains a time-keeping spirit. The explanations I actually find satisfying are the ones that build the complicated thing out of parts that are individually easier to accept than the thing itself — levers and gears and springs making a watch, or selective retention making an eye — where each step is humble but the stack gets you somewhere. I know "God of the gaps" covers the religious version of the second camp, but that name doesn't apply to, say, invoking a non-physical moral realm, and it doesn't touch the first camp at all.

The second move is almost the reverse. When someone does give a successful lower-level story, some people conclude that the higher-level thing is thereby shown to be unreal or illusory — a classmate said that since love is "just" a cocktail of hormones, romantic love is a fiction; another said that since our moral intuitions are products of evolution, morality is fake, as if discovering where a feeling comes from subtracts from its being there. Yet nobody concludes from physics that bridges aren't really load-bearing, or from neuroscience that pain isn't really bad. So my questions are: does the first contrast — explanations that build up from simpler accepted parts versus explanations that posit something even more mysterious than what they're meant to explain — have an established name and literature? Does the second move, where a successful bottom-up story is treated as a debunking rather than an explanation, have its own name? If they do, please tell me what the concepts are called, what exactly they say, and how they differ from nearby things like argument from ignorance; and if they don't, I'd like you to define each distinction carefully yourself — including what counts as a "simpler" part, whether a stack of steps can legitimately end in brute facts, and where the edge cases fall, since I'll need to defend my carving of this territory in the paper.
