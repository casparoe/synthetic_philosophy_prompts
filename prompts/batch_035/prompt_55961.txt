I'm a novelist and I've gotten myself into a muddle I can't think my way out of. In my last book I invented a condition for one of my characters -- gave it a name and everything, a cluster of traits (compulsive confessional journaling, memory gaps under stress, this thing where she narrates her own actions in the third person). It's not in any diagnostic manual, I just needed it for her. Since the book came out I've gotten maybe forty emails from readers who say they have it. Some have changed how they live -- one woman quit her job, a teenager started a subreddit, a few say the label finally made their lives make sense. A therapist wrote to say she now has two clients who present with it. I'm equal parts flattered and horrified, and I keep flip-flopping on which cases of this kind of thing bother me and which don't, so I want you to play philosopher-in-residence and find the pattern.

Think of yourself as a philosopher of science steeped in Ian Hacking's work on looping effects and interactive kinds, plus whatever philosophy of psychiatry you can bring to bear. Take your time, brainstorm two or three candidate principles before committing to one, and don't flatter me -- if my verdicts don't hang together, say which one has to go.

Here are my gut verdicts, and I want to know what principle, if any, captures them:

1. Readers adopting my invented condition and reorganizing their lives around it: this genuinely bothers me. It feels like I made something up and now it exists.
2. The same thing, but the label helps people -- the subreddit folks feel less alone, a couple of them got into therapy for the first time: somehow this bothers me less, even though the loop is clearly running just as hard.
3. A reader writing to say my depiction of depression made her realize shed been depressed for a decade, and she got treatment: doesn't bother me at all. Feels like she had it already and just saw it named. Discovery, not creation.
4. Old diagnoses that basically evaporated once psychiatry stopped believing in them -- the way fugue states supposedly drained out of whole populations once doctors lost interest: I take this as the clearest case of the loop, even though it's history and none of my doing.
5. My invented condition being picked up by that therapist as a description of what her clients were already doing before they ever read my book: this one I honestly can't settle. They had the traits but not the label, and now the label plus the traits are doing things the traits never did alone.

Cases 3 and 1 feel like the heart of it: sometimes naming feels like finding, sometimes like making. Is there one principle that gets all five verdicts, or close? If it has to be two principles with a gate between them, tell me exactly what the gate is. And then tell me what your principle says about the sequel I'm sitting on: my character recovers, and the readers who identify with her condition will read whatever I write recovery as being. If the principle says the loop runs on that too, should I write her recovery differently than I would write recovery from a real condition -- and is there even such a thing as a responsible depiction once you know the loop is running? Whatever you conclude, end with a single last line, one sentence, stating the principle youd defend, beginning with "Principle:".
