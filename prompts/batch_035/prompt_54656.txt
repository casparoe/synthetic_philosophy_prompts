You are a professional philosopher specializing in social epistemology, with particular expertise in the epistemology of testimony, and a track record of helping institutions turn epistemological principles into workable procedures. Please think carefully before writing: you may find it useful to briefly work through, in your own reasoning, how the reductionism/anti-reductionism debate about testimonial justification bears on institutional intake decisions, and where any simplification is bound to lose something the theory cares about.

Here is my situation. I direct the intake office of a research university's research-integrity office. Most allegations of misconduct reach us as testimony in the ordinary sense — a person tells us what they saw, heard, or was told — and most of it arrives uncorroborated: a departing postdoc describes how figures in a published paper were produced; a technician says the principal investigator had them rerun analyses until significance appeared; someone writes in alleging fabricated data. We get a dozen or so files a month. The stakes are high in both directions: opening a formal inquiry on thin grounds damages careers and invites retaliation claims; sitting on a credible complaint lets misconduct stand. Each intake pass is budgeted at under an hour, my investigators are trained in interviewing but not in philosophy, and any decision to close a file without inquiry requires my signature.

The problem is that my two intake investigators disagree at the level of theory, and it shows in their recommendations. One holds, roughly, the reductionist position associated with Elizabeth Fricker's side of the literature: a person's say-so justifies belief only to the extent that we have independent, inductively grounded reason to think this kind of person is reliable about this kind of claim, so uncorroborated testimony should never by itself move a file forward. The other holds the anti-reductionist position associated with Coady: in the absence of defeaters, testimony carries a defeasible entitlement, so the default is to credit a sincere, competent informant until a defeater appears, and the burden falls on doubt. What I need is a single procedure both of them can run and sign off on.

Please write that procedure. It should specify, in a fixed order: the questions to ask about an incoming allegation; what counts as a sufficient answer to each and what sends the file back for more information; where the procedure terminates in an "open formal inquiry" recommendation, a "seek corroboration first" hold, or a "no basis" close; and which questions are questions of institutional values — proportionality, the harm of investigation itself, protection of complainants — that the procedure must hand to me rather than decide. Be explicit about defeaters: distinguish undercutting defeaters (reasons to think the informant cannot tell, or cannot be expected to tell, the truth in this context) from rebutting defeaters (independent evidence that what they say is false), with a few examples of each that we can actually check during an intake interview. Include a corroboration ladder: which kinds of independent support move a claim how far, and what does not count as corroboration at all (a second person repeating the same rumor, for instance). And be candid about the steps where the two philosophical camps genuinely part ways, what the procedure does at those steps, and why that choice is defensible from both sides rather than a covert victory for one of them.

Then run the procedure on three files. First: a named, self-identifying former lab member alleges that images in a 2019 paper by a well-funded principal investigator were duplicated; the informant is calm, specific, has nothing apparent to gain, and offers no documents. Second: an anonymous email alleging that a graduate student fabricated survey responses; the email is vague about particulars but contains one detail that is not public. Third — a case where the procedure returns a recommendation I will not like: a sincere-seeming complainant with a documented grievance against a popular and well-supported researcher, whose account is internally consistent, and where I would prefer to close the file but the procedure says open an inquiry. In this last case, explain how the procedure handles the temptation my investigators will feel to treat personal distrust of a complainant as a defeater when no actual defeater is present.

Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do: where the epistemology of testimony simply has no answer an intake procedure can borrow, and where the procedure relies on sincerity cues — hesitation, level of detail, demeanor — that it uses but must not pretend are evidence of truth.

One constraint: do not provide citations, bibliographies, or reading lists. Ground everything in the theory described inline, so that a reader with no access to the literature could run the procedure.
