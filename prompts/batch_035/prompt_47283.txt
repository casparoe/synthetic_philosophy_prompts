For my graduate seminar on rational choice theory, I've found that students arrive at the bounded rationality unit with two misconceptions in equal measure: that satisficing is just a polite word for settling, and that Herbert Simon was somehow anti-rationality, a man telling us to lower our standards. Both readings survive the assigned papers untouched, because the papers are careful and the misconceptions are lazy. So this year I want to fight fire with fire and open the unit with a piece of satire that could only have been written by someone who actually understands the view.

The assignment I'd like you to complete: write a corporate decision-making policy document, in deadpan HR-legal register, for a fictional company that has made bounded rationality official policy. Numbered clauses, maybe 600–700 words. The humor has to come from getting the theory right rather than from mocking it as complacency. So, for instance: a clause mandating that search terminate at the first option that clears the employee's aspiration level, with a defined procedure for adjusting that level downward under deadline pressure and upward after a run of successes, per Simon's own suggestion that aspiration levels ratchet with experience; a clause prohibiting requests for "the best available option" on the grounds that no employee can survey all alternatives, and routing such requests to an "Omniscience Desk" that has never once responded; a grievance procedure distinguishing regret that one's procedure was sloppy from regret that one didn't attain the global optimum, only the first of which the company will entertain; an inventory of approved heuristics with instructions for when relying on the recognition heuristic is ecologically rational and when it is just being lazy; and perhaps an escalation-of-commitment clause framed as the pathological cousin the policy exists to prevent. The document should read as though procedural rationality — rationality given the constraints of limited knowledge and computational capacity, as opposed to substantive rationality — were simply the law of the office.

The quality bar: a behavioral decision theorist should laugh rather than wince, and the satire should be fair — it may exaggerate a feature of the satisficing view (the aspiration-level machinery, the stopping rules, the suspicion of maximization), but it may not invent one. Somewhere in the piece, ideally without ever stating it, the reader should feel the real question Simon left us: whether bounded rationality is a regrettable description of the cognitive hand we've been dealt or something closer to a prescription — whether the omniscient optimizer the company keeps routing mail to is a coherent ideal or a confusion. I'd also accept one or two jokes at the expense of the heuristics-and-biases tradition, provided the document's own house is in order. Begin your response with the document itself, with no preface or commentary.
