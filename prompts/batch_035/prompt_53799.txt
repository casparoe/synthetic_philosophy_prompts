Our philosophy of law reading group has spent the term on theories of punishment, and this week's meeting on mixed theories ended with two members, Maya and Theo, in a standoff that I, as a bystander, couldn't referee, so I'm bringing it to you. Theo opened by defending straight consequentialism: the only intelligible justification for deliberately harming someone is the good that harm does going forward — deterrence, incapacitation, perhaps the reinforcement of social norms. He added that retributivism cannot do any calibrating work anyway, because the question "how much suffering does this person deserve?" has no answer that isn't just a projection of whatever sentencing practices we already happen to have, so it can fix no determinate sentence; consequentialism at least has a target to optimize against. Maya answered with what she called a screening model: retributive desert sets a range — the guilty may be punished, and never by more than they deserve — and within that range, consequentialist considerations select the actual sentence. That way, she said, we never punish the innocent or beyond desert, but we're also not obligated to keep inflicting suffering when it does no further good. Theo replied that the mixture collapses: either the desert-range binds, in which case desert is doing all the justificatory work and the consequences merely decorate, or the range can be overridden, in which case it isn't a constraint at all and the theory is just consequentialism with extra steps. Maya's response was to split the question: the constraint is purely negative retributivism — it says who may never be punished (the innocent) and how much is never permitted (beyond desert) — while every positive claim, every claim that someone must or even should be punished, comes entirely from consequences. She described her view as "consequentialism with a gatekeeper" and accused Theo of assuming that a theory of punishment must answer one single question, when a practice like this needs both a general justifying aim and rules governing its distribution. Theo's last word was that the gatekeeper still owes us a rationale: if the reason the innocent may not be punished is that they don't deserve it, then a backward-looking desert fact is doing justificatory work after all, and Maya has smuggled retributivism in through the back door while denying she's a retributivist. And, he added, her model lets the state punish an offender far less than he deserves whenever deterrence is low, which treats punishment as a pure instrument — conceding the retributivist's deepest objection to consequentialism while claiming to have avoided it.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher with expertise in ethics and philosophy of law, think carefully before answering — perhaps sketching to yourself what each side is actually committed to at each stage — and then adjudicate the exchange move by move. There are five moves to assess: Theo's opening (including his calibration complaint), Maya's screening model, Theo's collapse objection, Maya's negative/positive retributivism split, and Theo's gatekeeper rejoinder. For each, say whether the argument as actually stated is right, mistaken, or right about one thing and wrong about another, with a brief justification; grade the exchange on what they actually said rather than reconstructing it into cleaner arguments they didn't make. Finally, tell me who is more right overall, and whether there is something both of them are missing — in particular, whether part of their dispute is verbal, turning on what counts as a single "theory of punishment" as opposed to a layered structure of justification for an institution.
