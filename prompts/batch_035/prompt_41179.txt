I'm a high school student in my final year, and I'm working on an extended essay for a school philosophy prize on the ancient problem of future contingents — the puzzle, going back to Aristotle's discussion of a possible sea battle, about whether statements about undetermined future events are already true or false now, and what it would mean for fatalism if they were.

I would be grateful for comments on the draft argument below, which I have summarized in note form.

My central claim is that the famous fatalist worry rests on a confusion between truth and settledness. I argue that a statement can be true now without being settled now: what makes "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" true, if it is true, is tomorrow's events, not any present fact, and there is nothing mysterious about truth being fixed by a fact later in time, because truth is not a causal relation — the sea battle makes the statement true, but the statement's truth does not make the sea battle happen. On this view, accepting bivalence for future contingents costs us nothing in fatalism: everything about tomorrow can still depend on choices not yet made, even though one of the two competing statements about tomorrow is already the true one.

To strengthen this, I introduce what I think is an original twist. I distinguish three kinds of future-tensed statements: (a) settled ones, whose truth is fixed by present facts (e.g., "the sun will rise tomorrow"); (b) open ones, whose truth depends only on how things turn out (the sea battle itself); and (c) what I call self-securing ones, where the assertion of the statement is itself part of what makes it true — for example, if my headmaster announces at assembly that there will be a fire drill tomorrow, and the drill is then scheduled because of the announcement, the announcement made itself true. I claim that category (c) shows that the direction of dependence between a statement and the world can run in unexpected ways, which supports my broader point that truthmaking for future contingents is more flexible than the fatalist assumes.

My teacher's written feedback says the essay "reads like two essays stapled together," and I suspect she is right that the twist does more harm than good, but I am reluctant to cut it because it is the part I came up with myself.

I would therefore especially welcome your answers to the following questions. First, is my main anti-fatalist argument sound as stated, and does it in fact avoid fatalism, or does it merely redefine the problem away — for instance, might a critic say that if one of the two statements is already the true one, then "how things turn out" was never really open, and that I have smuggled determinism back in through the phrase "depends on choices not yet made"? Second, is my category of self-securing statements genuinely distinct, or does it collapse into category (a), since by the time the announcement is made the drill's occurrence is settled by present facts (the announcement, the headmaster's authority)? Third, if the twist is worth keeping, how could I integrate it so that it serves the main argument rather than starting a second one? And finally, is there any standard objection to this general anti-fatalist line that I have overlooked and that an examiner would expect me to address? Please comment as frankly as you can; I would rather revise heavily now than discover the problems after submission.
