Retired physicist here — I spent most of my career doing probabalistic risk assessment for reactor safety, and lately I've gotten hooked on philosophy of moral responsibility. Read Nagel's essay on moral luck a while back and it's been nagging at me, because I think I've built an argument on top of it that proves too much. Tell me where it breaks.

(1) Blameworthiness requires control: you're only to blame for what was under your control.
(2) Outcomes aren't under your control — the essay's two identical drunk drivers, one of whom happens to meet a pedestrian — so if outcomes can't affect blame, blame must attach to something else about the act.
(3) The natural candidate, coming from my old line of work: the ex ante risk of the act, the expectation taken over the ways it could have gone. Blame the gamble, not the payout. Drivers who take sober risks and get unlucky get blamed only for the risk they ran, drivers who take wild risks and get lucky get blamed as much as that risk deserved, and it all comes out clean and luck-free.
(4) But in a deterministic world there is only one way anything could have gone, so the "ways it could have gone" are artifacts of our ignorance, and every act's true ex ante risk collapses to 0 or 1 — which drags the outcomes back in through the rear door.
(5) And if the probabilities are just ignorance, they belong to whoever is doing the judging, so blameworthiness varies with the observer's information: the driver is more blameworthy to a witness who saw the pedestrian round the corner than to one who didn't. That's not control either.
(6) So either blame tracks luck or it tracks the judge. Both sound wrong to me, which makes me suspect I've smuggled something in.

My grandson (a philosophy grad student, insufferable) says step 4 is where I cheat — that I'm quietly sliding between probability as a measure over possibilities and probability as whatever the physics says, though he didn't explain it well. Maybe he's right, but I want a proper diagnosis, not a smirk.

I want you to act as a professional philosopher who works on moral responsibility and knows their decision theory. Think it through carefully before answering — sketch for yourself the candidate failure points in my argument and weigh them against each other. Then give me exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion: which step or steps the argument actually turns on, why that step looked so innocent, what rejecting it costs, and whether anything of my luck-free picture survives the surgery. And be straight with me if the grandson turns out to be only half right.
