I write on science and ideas for a magazine, and I am preparing a long profile of a quantum physicist whose metaphysics I cannot yet pin down. Across several long interviews she has given me these rulings: a magnetic field is real in a region of space no instrument has ever entered; a bookmaker's odds are not real in that sense, since they live in heads; the pure state of a freshly filtered atom is, she insists, about the atom and not about our knowledge of it; the mixed state we assign to a warm gas is different — a confession of ignorance rather than a property of anything; and, on the Wigner's-friend setup, the friend inside and Wigner outside are each right about the atom's state from where they stand, without contradiction. The case I mean to put to her next is this: two laboratories claim to prepare atoms that are physically identical in every respect, yet each assigns its atoms a different pure state, and no measurement on any single atom could ever reveal which laboratory prepared it. Predict her verdict — possible or impossible — identify the rules that fit her track record equally well and show where they diverge on this case, give me your best guess with reasons, and tell me the single further question I should put to her that would best separate those rules. Please answer in American English.
