You are a professional philosopher specializing in value theory and the metaphysics of harm, with experience facilitating structured disagreements between camps that have been talking past each other. Think carefully before drafting — begin with some private brainstorming about what each side actually needs the joint statement to concede, and what each side can sign without feeling sandbagged.

Our department's annual workshop on death is trying something new. For several years the standing debate has been between two colleagues: one holds the Epicurean line — that death, as the absence of experience, cannot be bad for the person who dies, since there is no longer a subject for anything to be bad for — and the other defends the deprivationist line — that death is bad for the one who dies precisely because it deprives them of goods they would otherwise have had. Their exchanges have become ritualized: the Epicurean invokes the ancient claim that where death is, the person is not, and asks whom the badness is supposed to befall; the deprivationist gestures at the obviousness that a sudden death at twenty, in the middle of a flourishing life, is a catastrophe for the person who dies and not merely a loss for the survivors. Neither moves. We would like you to draft the document they should have produced: a joint statement from a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

Structure the statement as follows.

1. Agreed claims, as a numbered list. Include what both accept about death itself (that it involves the end of experience for the person, that the badness at issue is badness *for the one who dies* rather than for others, that posthumous events can affect whether a life went well), and any points where you suspect their disagreement was partly verbal — for instance, whether part of the standoff turns on different understandings of what it takes for something to be bad *for* someone, rather than on a substantive disagreement about the world. If you think some of the dispute is verbal, say which part and why, and if you think none of it is, say that.

2. A table of disagreements, with each side's position phrased so that a defender of that side would accept the wording as a fair statement of their own view. Be especially careful with the timing question: the Epicurean needs the table to convey why saying death is bad *before* it occurs or *while* the person still exists strikes them as an impossible location for the misfortune, and the deprivationist needs it to convey why saying the badness occurs at no time strikes them as conceding the point.

3. The cruxes — the subquestions such that settling them would move at least one side. I suspect the Lucretian symmetry argument about prenatal nonexistence is one (if posthumous deprivation is bad, why isn't the deprivation we all suffered before conception bad?), the subjecthood condition is another, and the timing puzzle a third, but you should work out the full set and rank them.

4. For each crux, at least one case or argument both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. For this, please make use of the following cases, which we have been developing for the workshop:

- Restoration: a person is placed in a reversible suspended state with no experiences for five years and then restored to their previous life intact, with no awareness that any time passed. The deprivationist must say something about whether the interval was bad for them while it was underway, and how that verdict interacts with their account of death; the Epicurean must say whether the interval shares death's harmlessness and what, if anything, was lost.
- Balanced continuation: a person's life is extended for thirty further years, but by a stipulated mechanism that guarantees the additional period contains exactly offsetting pleasures and pains, so that it is neither better nor worse for them than immediate death would have been. Ask whether this case helps either side, and record what each would predict the other ought to conclude about it.
- A case of your own devising, designed to be maximally diagnostic for whichever crux you rank highest.

A few demands on the drafting. First, do not let either side sign anything vacuous: if a claim in the agreed-claims section is so weak that it cannot fail, flag it and either strengthen it or cut it. Second, do not slant the disagreement formulations toward either side; the Epicurean should not be made to concede that deprivation is a kind of badness for the subject, and the deprivationist should not be made to concede that badness requires experience. Third, wherever the statement asserts that a philosopher or tradition held some position, keep it at the level of a characterization rather than a quotation.

Finally, append a short confidence note at the end: say how confident you are in the overall statement — particularly in your judgment about which disagreements are genuine versus verbal — and single out the single point in the document you are least sure of, explaining what would have to be true for it to be wrong.
