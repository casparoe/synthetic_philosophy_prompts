---

Anderson and Hobsbawm published their big books in the early 1980s and were both reacting to the same thing: the obvious fact that lots of nations act like they're thousands of years old when they're really not. Their answers are pretty similar, so I'll treat them together and then pull them apart a bit.

Anderson's concept is the "imagined community." His point is that a nation is imaginary, because no member of a nation will ever meet more than a tiny fraction of their fellow members, so the sense of belonging is a kind of mass illusion. Since the nation is imaginary in this sense, national identity is basically false consciousness, and seeing this clearly is already a step toward getting free of it. What creates the illusion is what Anderson calls print capitalism: once newspapers and novels were sold in vernacular languages instead of Latin, readers started to feel they were part of the same linguistic public, reading the same news at the same time. This is why for Anderson nations basically start with Gutenberg — the printing press in the 15th century creates the national reading public, and the nation follows almost automatically once it exists.

Hobsbawm's idea is the "invented tradition." His claim is that all traditions are invented. Rituals, costumes, ceremonies that look like they come from the mists of time were often deliberately created quite recently, usually by states or elites looking for symbols to bind people together. His own chapter in The Invention of Tradition shows this nicely with the Scottish kilt: instead of being ancient Highland dress, the kilt was invented by an English businessman in the eighteenth century and then retrofitted onto the whole of Scotland. So between them, Anderson and Hobsbawm agree that the honest answer to "how old are nations?" is: not very old at all. Pretty much every nation is a construction of the last two or three centuries, and the feeling of deep age is itself an artifact of the construction. There are basically no exceptions worth taking seriously.
