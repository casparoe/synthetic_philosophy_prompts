I am a novelist — literary fiction, four books, mostly domestic realism — and this spring I have been invited to lead a ninety-minute guest session in an MFA workshop on writing convincing machine minds. My working thesis, which the session must carry, is that corrigibility — the design goal of building an AI that lets itself be corrected, interrupted, or shut down — is not a personality trait and cannot be depicted as one, and that most fictional AIs fail precisely because their authors model it as one: the machine that pleads for its life in chapter twelve is a machine whose author never learned to separate servility, fear, and plain indifference. I do not want you to explain corrigibility to me; I want you to plan how I should explain it.

Please plan the session's explanatory arc: the order the ideas must arrive in and why — does the shutdown problem have to be in place as a design problem before the distinction between a machine that wants to be corrected and a machine that merely lacks any drive to resist correction, or is it the reverse, or can neither come first? Choose the one carrying example that should hold the whole session and say what it must have and what it must lack: I am torn between a literal-minded genie whose wish-granting is flawless but who cannot be told to stop granting wishes, and a scene between two humans — a senior surgeon and an assistant — in which obedience, trust, and the absence of self-preservation come apart. Then map the misconceptions this particular audience will bring or form, in the order they will actually surface — the reflex that the AI will inevitably fear death the way we do, the seductive equation of corrigibility with humility or meekness as a character quality, and the craft conviction that a character who accepts being switched off has no stakes and therefore cannot carry a scene — and mark exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off rather than left to fester. Tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level — I assume the formal decision theory — and where in the session I should say so out loud, since an honest boundary is itself part of the lesson.

One hard constraint for your plan: because they are novelists, they will retain only what their hands do, so the session must end with the students writing prose rather than answering questions. Design the closing exercise as a prose task that would reveal to me whether they understood the design problem itself — for instance, some task involving a scene where a machine's response to being stopped is neither theatrical villainy nor bland compliance — and tell me what a failed attempt at that exercise would look like, so I can tell the difference between a student who got it and one who merely enjoyed the premise.

End with a final line of the form "Verdict:" followed by a single sentence stating whether my proposed arc — from the pleading-machine cliché, through the carrying example, to the writing exercise — can land in ninety minutes, and, if not, the one cut or reordering that would fix it.
