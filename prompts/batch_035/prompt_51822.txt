And the student's answer:

"Paraconsistent logic and dialetheism are basically the same thing. Paraconsistent logic was invented by Graham Priest in the 1970s in order to make his dialetheism rigorous. In classical logic a contradiction entails everything (explosion), so Priest needed a logic where contradictions don't explode, and he built one. From this it follows that using a paraconsistent logic does commit you to dialetheism: if you reason paraconsistently, you are thereby saying some contradictions are true.

The Liar paradox shows why we'd want this. Take the sentence 'This sentence is false' — if it's true then it's false, and if it's false then it's true, so it comes out both. A paraconsistent logic can then prove that the Liar sentence is both true and false, and because explosion fails, the rest of the theory doesn't collapse into triviality.

Paraconsistent logics also reject the law of excluded middle, which they have in common with intuitionistic logic. That is what allows them to contain contradictions without collapse. As an application: an inconsistent database can keep both conflicting records, for example a flight manifest that says passenger X boarded in Paris and also that X never boarded, and the system can still answer queries usefully, since it won't infer arbitrary facts like 'X is a giraffe' from the conflict. So paraconsistency is useful in engineering even if you're not a dialetheist. This might seem to cut against what I said earlier, but I think you can hold both."
