I lead safety evaluations for a lab that trains large language models, and my group has to decide what standard an audit of a model has to meet before we can sign off on deployment. The concept we keep reaching for is something like epistemic dominance: a characterization of the model counts as adequate only if, with that characterization in hand and strictly weaker resources, you could do everything the model can do on the relevant domain — predict it, match it, or safely overrule it — so that nothing the model might do downstream can come as a surprise to you. Two ways of cashing this out are in circulation in our discussions, and I can't tell whether they're equivalent, merely different, or in tension:

1. A transcript picture: there is an explanation of the model's behavior on a task such that a much smaller model, or a competent person, equipped with that explanation performs at least as well as the audited model on every input in the domain — including inputs chosen adversarially to find where the explanation breaks.
2. An ascription picture: a person reading the model's outputs and our account of them would be in a position to know everything relevant the model "knows" — every latent disposition or capability that could matter downstream is either ruled out or attributed in advance, so nothing hazardous can hide in the gap between what the model can do and what our theory of it says it can do.

Before we argue about methods, I want the specification. Write out the desiderata any adequate characterization of this kind must satisfy: which inputs it has to cover and whether worst-case rather than average-case coverage is what the safety purpose actually requires; what resources the dominance comparison is allowed to spend, and whether the "strictly weaker" side is fixed in advance or can grow with the transcript; whether the explanation must be human-comprehensible or only machine-checkable; whether it must support counterfactual claims ("the model would have complied if pressured that way") and not just predictions; and whether it must extend to the model's capacity to misrepresent its own capabilities — the last one worries me, since a deceptive model is presumably the case the whole exercise is for. For each desideratum, give the case behind it, mark which are non-negotiable for our deployment decision and which are refinements that particular camps treat as load-bearing, and say which single requirement does the most to exclude the methods people actually propose.

Then the part I most want: identify any subset of the desiderata that is jointly unsatisfiable. One member of the group claims that a strictly weaker system can never dominate on all inputs, by a diagonal argument — the audited model could in principle simulate its own auditor — and another replies that the transcript changes the resources available to the auditor in a way that blocks the diagonalization. I don't know who is right, and I'd like you to take a position rather than report both sides. Skip a scorecard of current methods; we'll do that in the meeting. Throughout, state how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point in your answer you are least sure of.
