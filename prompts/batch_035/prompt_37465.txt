I'm an undergraduate in a seminar on the historiography of the Third Reich, and across the semester I've drifted from a fairly naive great-man narrative into something like the structuralist camp. I want to state the position I've ended up with as precisely as I can, and then ask what would count against it — not to be talked out of it, but to know where it's actually exposed.

Here is the position. By late 1932 — before Hitler is chancellor, and arguably even before he is politically indispensable — the following structural conditions held in Germany: mass unemployment at depths no democracy had survived anywhere; parliamentary government already replaced in practice by rule via Article 48 decrees; an army and conservative elite that preferred authoritarian rule and was actively shopping for a mass constituency to legitimize it; a Communist Party committed to revolutionary politics, which made the middle classes genuinely fear civil war; and a right-wing electorate that had become the largest bloc in the country. My claim is that these conditions were jointly sufficient to produce some form of authoritarian, revanchist, anti-communist regime within a few years, and that this holds counterfactually: had Hitler died in the putsch of 1923, or even in the war of 1914–18, Germany still goes authoritarian-revanchist by the mid-1930s. What the individual explains, on this view, is selection among variants, not the broad outcome — which regime Germany got, not whether it got one. Hitler's causal work is confined to the character of the regime: the centrality of the racial program, the leader cult, the timing and totality of the war.

I've also been using a rule of thumb that I want examined as part of the position, since it may be where the weakness lives. Call it the redundancy test: a structural explanation beats an individualist one whenever the structural causes of the period are so distributed that removing the individual leaves the outcome standing, or replaces it with a nearby variant. The obvious problem I've noticed myself is that "nearby variant" is doing an enormous amount of work, and I can't find a principled way to say how far the counterfactual regime can drift from the actual one before I have to concede that the structural conditions were not, after all, sufficient for anything like what happened. If no-Hitler Germany ends up as a military-authoritarian regime that remilitarizes, persecutes Jews discriminatorily, and starts a war to undo Versailles — but stops short of extermination camps and a war of continental annihilation — my thesis about the broad outcome stands, but it starts to feel like it's standing on a distinction without much moral or explanatory difference. So a second question is embedded in the first: how should the variant-distance be fixed, and is there any non-arbitrary way to fix it that doesn't just decide the debate by fiat in one direction or the other?

What I'd like from you, then, is a map of the defeaters, ranked by how dangerous they actually are rather than by how often they're invoked. I'm aware of the standard dialectical moves — that the functionalist side of the debate has itself conceded a lot to intentionalism since the 1990s, that Ian Kershaw's synthesis deliberately reintroduces Hitler's decision-making power at the points of no return, and that the "structured by the leader's prophecy, radicalized from below" picture is now closer to orthodoxy than either pure camp. What I want to know is which of the pressures on my position those structuralists would themselves concede as counting against it, which only their critics press, and which are merely awkward. In particular I'd like you to take a definite position on three things, rather than laying out the options and leaving the verdict to me: first, whether the strongest genuine threat is the counterfactual-dependence of the specific radicalization points (the decision on physical annihilation in 1941 being the obvious candidate) or something earlier in the chain, say the dependence of the Nazi party's very survival on Hitler's choices during 1925–32, which structuralists sometimes wave away; second, whether the redundancy test survives the variant-distance problem in any respectable form, or whether it has to be weakened so far that it no longer distinguishes my view from a great-man view that also accepts background enabling conditions; and third, whether the whole position, once qualified to handle these, still counts as a structuralist thesis in a sense that historians of this debate would recognize — or whether, like many positions insulated by successive qualifications, it has been kept alive mainly by shrinking what it claims until the claim is nearly trivial. If the honest answer is that it survives only in a heavily restricted form, say so and say what the restriction is; and if you think one of the defeaters I haven't ranked highly is in fact fatal, commit to that ranking and defend it.
