I teach an upper-level seminar on concepts and categories — 14 students, one 90-minute session. Next week's topic is letterforms: what makes every 'a' an 'a' whether it appears in a serif font, a child's pencil scrawl, a neon sign, or a medieval manuscript. I want an activity built around the fact that a letter carries two identities at once — which letter it is, and which hand or style it belongs to — and that competent readers track both dimensions across instances they have never seen, without ever being able to state the rule they are following.

The participants should perform the abstraction, not just discuss it. My rough thought: small teams receive a sheet showing five or six letters of a made-up script drawn in a consistent invented style; each team must hand-draw letters of that script that were not shown, staying recognizably in the same style. The productions go to a rival team, which must judge two questions separately — which letter is this, and is it by the same hand? — with graded confidence, and the producing team is then asked to write down explicit criteria for one letter, criteria a machine could supposedly apply. Those criteria get tested against everyone's actual judgments. Design the details as you see fit: rules, materials (assume paper, markers, and a copier), a timeline, and a debrief that maps particular moments onto the philosophical distinctions — definitional rules versus family resemblance, letter identity as a role within a system of contrasts rather than a bundle of shape features, and whatever account of style the activity surfaces on its own. No lecture up front. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about which feature of the phenomenon — say, the sheer automaticity of reading for literate adults — the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself.
