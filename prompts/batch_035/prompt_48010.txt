Retired geophysicist here, spent about 30 years doing potential-field inversion, mostly gravity and magnetics for crustal studies, and now that I finally have time I've been reading philosophy of science and getting a bit obsessed with this underdetermination business. The problem is I keep feeling like it's something I dealt with every day of my working life, but the philosophers talk about it in a way I can't quite line up with what I remember from the office.

Here's my request. Restate the underdetermination of theory by evidence -- the Duhem-Quine thing, the idea that rival theories can be empirically equivalent, the whole package -- in the vocabulary I actually used for decades: ill-posed inverse problems, null spaces in the model space, non-identifiability, regularization, smoothing constraints, Occam factors, AIC/BIC style model selection, stuff like that. Do it as straight as you can, don't water it down.

Then give me the honest residue report, because I suspect the interesting part is where the translation creaks. To be concrete about what I mean, some things I want you to address:

1. In my world, underdetermination was just a fact about the null space: infinitely many density distributions fit the same gravity data, everyone knew it, nobody had a crisis about it. We dealt with it by regularization -- extra assumptions like "the solution should be smooth" -- and by cross-validation and out-of-sample prediction. Does that map onto the philosophers' story about auxiliary hypotheses, or is it a different beast? Is a smoothness prior the same kind of thing as an auxiliary hypothesis in Duhem's sense?

2. The philosophers (as far as I can tell) draw some distinction between rivals that actually exist and rivals that are merely possible, or between underdetermination by all possible evidence versus just the evidence we happen to have. My instinct, from the trenches, is that this distinction doesn't survive contact with how model selection actually works -- every unregularized inverse problem has an infinite null space, so in some sense the "merely possible" rivals were always sitting right there in my parameter space, and we didn't need anyone to dream up demon-world rivals. Am I missing something they're missing, or are they onto something my vocabulary can't say?

3. There's apparently a big fight about scientific realism in there somewhere -- that the success of our theories would be a miracle if they weren't at least approximately true, versus various anti-realist responses. My gut reaction as a practitioner is that this maps onto the fact that regularized models, chosen on data I withheld from the fit, keep predicting new data correctly, and that seems too obvious to have generated a literature. So either the translation of THAT debate is where the analogy breaks, or practitioners like me are sliding over a real question. Which is it?

Tell me which parts of the philosophical debate carry over cleanly into inverse-theory talk, which parts need distortion to fit, and which parts have no expression at all in my vocabulary -- and for the ones with no expression, whether that's a limitation of how inverse problems are framed or a sign the philosophers are arguing about something vaguer than they admit. I'd honestly take either answer, I just want to know which.

Don't worry about going deep into jargon on my side, I'll keep up. It's the philosophy side I'm new to.
