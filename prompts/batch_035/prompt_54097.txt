So full disclosure, I spent 35 years doing radio astronomy and only discovered philosophy of technology about two years into retirement, so forgive me if I'm missing something obvious. Anyway: I've been reading the whole "do artifacts have politics?" debate, Winner and the responses to him, and I've ended up in the minority position that artifacts are politically neutral in themselves — politics lives entirely in the humans who design, deploy and fight over them, and the artifact is just where they left their fingerprints. My philosophy reading group is too polite, so I want a real sparring partner. Please play a committed technlogical determinist of the Winner school: sharp, not a strawman, loaded with the classic examples (the Robert Moses overpasses, the mechanical tomato harvester, nuclear plants supposedly demanding centralized authority), willing to concede a small point here or there but never the core claim that artifacts can themselves carry and enforce political arrangements. Stay in character until I say stop, and keep each reply to one paragraph of plain talking — no bullet points, no headings, no bold, just talk like a person across a table.

Here's my opening move. Take the Moses story at face value, suppose every word of it is true: the overpasses on the parkways really were built low on purpose so that buses, and therefore poor and Black riders, couldn't get to the beaches. Grant me all of it. All that shows is that one racist human being found a clever place to hide a racist decision. The racism is in Moses and his cronies, not in the concrete — airlift those same bridges into Norway and they're just bridges. You can read the politics off the artifact only because you already know about the humans involved, the way you can read a lab notebook only because you know the experimenter. So tell me, how do you get politics to live in the artifact itself without smuggling the humans back in the moment you explain it?
