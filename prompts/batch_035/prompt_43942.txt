I'm in a little reading group that's slowly working through Gödel, Escher, Bach (very slowly, we've been at it since autumn), and we just hit the chapter on the location of meaning — the one with the three levels, frame message, outer message, inner message, plus all the stuff about jukeboxes and how even a message supposedly beamed from an alien civilization would need layers of context just to be recognized as a message at all. One of the guys in the group is completely sold on the theory and keeps saying the Voyager Golden Record is the chapter turned into an actual artifact — a message deliberately constructed with all three levels, where you can point at each one. I'm less sure it's such a clean fit, and I got assigned to write up the disagreement for next meeting.

The facts as I understand them (I read up on this a bit, so no need to go find more): in 1977 NASA strapped a gold-plated phonograph record onto each of the two Voyager probes, put together by a committee chaired by Carl Sagan. It carries greetings in 55 languages, sounds of Earth, music ranging from Bach to Chuck Berry, and about a hundred images encoded into the grooves so they have to be decoded from the audio. And the aluminum cover is etched with pictograms: a diagram of the hydrogen atom, a pulsar map showing where the Sun is, drawings of the record itself and the stylus, and instructions on how to play it and how to get the images out of the signal. Everyone involved knew the odds of anyone ever playing it are basically nil, and that it functions more as a message to ourselves.

So here's what I want you to do. Take my friend's side seriously first: map the record onto Hofstadter's three levels properly — what exactly plays the role of the frame message here, the thing that says "I am a message, decode me"? Is it the grooves' regularity, the gold cover, the spacecraft, all of it? What's the outer message versus the inner one, and does the mapping actually work? Then tell me where it gets messy, because I suspect it does — e.g. the pulsar map looks like it's simultaneously outer message (helping you locate/decode) and inner content, and it seems weird that decoding the images depends on instructions that are themselves only interpretable if you've already got a lot of shared context, which seems to eat the distinction between the levels. And then the reverse direction: what does the record show about Hofstadter's theory — does it vindicate the idea that meaning is a joint product of message and receiver, or does it expose something the three-level picture doesn't handle, like the possibility that the real audience was humans all along and none of the levels care about that? Please be honest about where the theory is silent instead of stretching it, and give me a verdict on whether my friend's "textbook case" claim holds up.
