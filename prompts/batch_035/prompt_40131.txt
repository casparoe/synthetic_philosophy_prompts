So here's my situation: I'm one of five trustees of a small family foundation my great-uncle set up, and the will says the money goes "toward the relief of human suffering" -- which nobody thought much about for 40 years, we just funded the usual vaccines-and-clean-water stuff. At our last meeting a program officer, who's finishing a philosophy masters at night, proposed we shift maybe 40% of the annual giving (~$800k) toward morphine and palliative care access in low-income countries and away from some of the child-survival programs. Her pitch is basically negative utilitarianism plus a version of the Epicurean line about death: that a child dying quickly and painlessly from a preventable illness isn't, for that child, the catastrophe we assume it is, whereas a child in uncontrolled bone-cancer pain is in a genuinely bad state, and that our founder's wording actually points at the suffering itself and not at lost life-years. She's clearly read her Popper and her Narveson even if she doesn't cite them by name in the deck. Two trustees are basically converted on the spot, one called the whole thing morally obscene, and the chair is hiding behind procedure. So the decision's kicked to a written vote with a memo attached, and I got assigned to write the memo we'll all circulate before Thursday's call.

Here's what I want from you: a decision memo that actually recommends something concrete -- approve the full reallocation, reject it, or (my hunch) some middle path with specific conditions attached, like a capped percentage or a two-year trial with defined metrics. Whatever you recommend, I need you to take the suffering-focused side seriously on its own terms, including the point that our gut reaction against it may just be status-quo bias dressed up as moral seriousness. But I also need the losing side's strongest argument treated honestly, and I mean its strongest version -- not the strawman where negative utilitarianism immediately collapses into some benevolent world-exploder scenario, because that's the version the program officer pre-empted, but the more careful worry: that if you weight relief of suffering lexically over everything, you end up with a rationale for systematically preferring numb, diminished lives over flourishing ones, and that the pain-focussed framing can't easily explain why we'd bother curing pain rather than just sedating it away. Also worth noting the founder's language cuts both ways -- relief of suffering is right there in the will, but he built his fortune in generic vaccines, which arguably tells us what he thought the words meant.

One more thing: some of the other trustees read a bit of philosophy in college but none of them are specialists, so keep the argument accessible, but don't dumb it down to the point where the program officer -- who will absolutely reply to the memo -- can dismiss it. A page and a half to two pages max, structured like an actual memo (question presented, background, arguments, recommendation), and if you land on the middle path, give me conditions specific enough that someone could check in two years whether they'd been met. Honestly the biggest thing is that the memo shouldn't read like it was written to make one side feel smart and the other feel scolded, since I have to keep working with these people either way.
