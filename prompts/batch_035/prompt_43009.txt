Last night a friend and I argued for three hours about Amartya Sen's work on famines, and we ended not with agreement but with a stalemate I'd like someone to adjudicate. We both accepted Sen's core claims: that the Bengal famine of 1943 occurred without any overall shortfall in food availability, that his entitlement approach explains why some groups starved while others did not, and that his comparative work links famines to the absence of a free press and electoral accountability — with the Great Leap Forward as his showcase of what concealment under an unaccountable regime can do. Our dispute was about the label. My friend held that once you grant all of that, the "natural disaster" framing is not merely inaccurate but propaganda, and that the right description of Bengal, the Irish famine, and the Great Leap Forward alike is "political crime," full stop. I kept objecting that the phrase is true of all three but too coarse — that the political enters each case through a different mechanism, and that the undifferentiated label hides exactly what Sen's analysis was supposed to expose.

Please argue for my side of this — not for the natural-disaster framing, which we both rejected, but for the claim that the entitlement approach, taken seriously, forces a three-way differentiation: Bengal as omission under wartime priorities, where a government with the analytic tools to act failed to treat the dying as a governing priority; the Irish famine as a case where food continued to move out of the affected region under market and colonial arrangements that were maintained rather than suspended; and the Great Leap Forward as a case where the political mechanism was specifically the suppression of the feedback loops — local officials inflating reports, center unable to correct — that the other two cases at least possessed in principle. The argument should show that these differences matter for explanation, not just for rhetoric: that "political crime" picked up as a single predicate predicts the same remedies and the same lessons from all three, and that this prediction is wrong. I'd also like the argument to address my friend's strongest reply, which was that differentiation is morally corrosive — that it diffusely absolves perpetrators by moving from "they did it" to "the mechanism did it."

Constraints: do not rest anything on contested death tolls or precise harvest statistics; build the argument on the mechanisms themselves, which are not seriously disputed. Do not assume the criminal label requires intent — treat that as an open question the argument may engage rather than a premise.

Finally, I want the response to be candid about its own weak points: say which single argument or consideration from my friend's side would most plausibly force you to abandon the differentiated verdict in favor of the undifferentiated one, and identify the step in the reasoning above that you think is most likely to fail — in particular, whether the corrosive-absolution worry is a genuine objection to the position or only a cost of holding it.
