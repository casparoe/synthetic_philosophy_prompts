As a novelist, I have spent two decades inside the problem of point of view: free indirect discourse, focalization, the gap between what a character reports seeing and what the text shows them seeing, and the craft of letting a reader detect a distortion the character cannot. From the outside, the philosophical debate over the cognitive penetration of perception appears to concern the very thing I manage sentence by sentence — whether a perceiver's beliefs, expectations, and categories can shape what they literally see, rather than merely how they interpret, describe, or attend to it. Please restate that debate in my vocabulary. Cover, in craft terms: the New Look claim that wanting, expecting, and categorizing can reach into perception itself; the distinction between perception being merely influenced by cognition and being genuinely penetrated by it; and the critic's challenge that the celebrated effects are really post-perceptual — judgment, memory, or reporting dressed up as seeing. Then give me the residue report: which parts of the debate map onto things I actually control in a draft (for instance, whether a description given in free indirect discourse is committed to the character's perceptual state or only to their assessment of it); which parts must be distorted to fit my vocabulary; and whether anything in the debate has no counterpart at all in narrative craft — and if so, whether that residue reveals a limit of my vocabulary or a sign that the debate was never really about seeing, but about the epistemic standing of experience, something fiction can leave undecided. Be honest about where my terms smuggle in assumptions: they were built to produce effects in readers, not to track what happens inside a perceiver.
