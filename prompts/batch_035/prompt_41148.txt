I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and for the past few years a good share of my beat has been machine learning as it enters public institutions. Twice now I have watched essentially the same argument stall in exactly the same place, with each side convinced the other must be lying or careless: first the 2016 ProPublica findings on the COMPAS recidivism score, where the journalists measured unequal false-positive rates across race and the vendor answered that the score was calibrated within each racial group; then a local controversy over a child-welfare screening tool that ran on the same rails. What neither episode ever conveyed to readers is that both measurements can be right at once — that the two audits are incompatible as demands, not as findings. I have been invited to run a 90-minute session for a graduate seminar in data journalism, 18 students who are comfortable with arithmetic but not with formalism, and I want to build the session around something I have only ever done half-seriously at dinner parties: a game in which the students, playing a lending or screening agency, are required to satisfy three fairness demands at once, and discover for themselves that the demands cannot be jointly met when the underlying outcome rates differ between groups. Design the game for me in full. I am imagining physical card decks standing in for applicants with scores on the front and settled outcomes on the back, so that students act on fixed, cleanly observed ground truth; specify the deck composition and numbers. Structure it as at least two rounds: an opening round in which the two groups have equal base rates and every demand is comfortably satisfiable, lulling the students into the belief that fairness is just a matter of good intentions and careful bookkeeping; and a second round, same rules, in which the base rates diverge and the trap closes. The three demands — calibration within each group, equal false-positive rates across groups, and equal false-negative (or denial) rates across groups — should be delivered by three distinct voices, say a regulator, a civil-rights auditor, and a consumer group, each armed with a legitimate audit the students must pass. Include a point or payoff scheme that requires no real money, a running order with timings, and a debrief that maps the students' failed attempts onto the formal result — that when base rates differ, within-group calibration cannot coexist with equalized error rates, so the damning audit and the vendor's defense were never in contradiction — conveyed without a single formula. Then tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong on a first run and how to handle it, and be frank about what the game cannot reproduce: real deployments never have settled outcomes on the backs of cards, the labels themselves are contested, and the base rates are partly the product of prior decisions — I would rather the debrief admit this openly than paper over it. Finally, commit to a single design: definite rules, definite numbers, one debrief, no alternatives laid side by side for me to choose among.
