I'm grading a final exam in an upper-level epistemology course and would like a second opinion on one of the answers. Please assign a letter grade (A–F) and justify it with point-by-point comments: identify what the student gets right, exactly where the answer goes subtly wrong, and how each error should affect the grade. Quote the specific sentences that are problematic rather than giving a general impression.

The exam question was:

Nozick's tracking analysis says that S knows that p iff p is true, S believes that p, and, holding fixed the method by which S forms the belief, (3) if p were not true, S would not believe p (sensitivity), and (4) if p were true in nearby worlds, S would believe p (adherence). The analysis famously yields: I know that I have hands; I know that my having hands entails that I am not a handless brain in a vat; yet I do not know that I am not a handless brain in a vat. So the analysis gives up closure of knowledge under known entailment. Is this a fatal defect of the tracking analysis, or a principled, acceptable cost? Discuss why sensitivity forces the closure denial, DeRose's abominable conjunction objection, and at least one case where the tracking verdicts look right (e.g., the lottery or skeptical hypotheses).

The student's answer was:

Nozick's tracking theory has four conditions: p is true; I believe p; if p weren't true, I wouldn't believe p; and if p were true, I'd believe p. Condition (3), sensitivity, says my belief couldn't easily have been false — in the nearby worlds where I believe p, p is true. This is the same as what epistemologists call the safety condition, and it is what does the anti-skeptical work.

The reason closure fails is that sensitivity isn't preserved under entailment. My belief that I have hands is sensitive: if I didn't have hands, I wouldn't believe I do. But my belief that I'm not a brain in a vat isn't: if I were a BIV, I'd still believe I wasn't, so I don't know that I'm not a BIV. Since I know I have hands and I know that having hands entails not being a handless BIV, but I don't know that I'm not a handless BIV, closure fails. Nozick wasn't the first to deny closure — Dretske had already done so with the zebra case, where you know the animal is a zebra and know that this entails it's not a cleverly disguised mule, but don't know that it's not a cleverly disguised mule.

The closure denial has to be tested against the fake barn case. Henry, driving through fake barn country, sees the one real barn and believes there's a barn in front of him. Nozick should count Henry as knowing: Henry's belief couldn't easily have been false, since the barn he is looking at is real, and if there were a barn there, he'd believe it. The tracking verdicts in perceptual cases are generally right, which is a point in the theory's favor.

The main objection to denying closure is the abominable conjunction, which Dretske formulated: I know that I have hands, but I don't know that I'm not a handless brain in a vat. Asserting both together sounds terrible. But Nozick has a ready answer: when you assert the second conjunct, the standards for knowledge rise, so the word know no longer expresses the same relation. The conjunction sounds bad only because of this shift in what know means between its two halves; once we see that, the conjunction isn't abominable, just a change of subject.

Finally, the tracking analysis gets the lottery right: before the draw I don't know my ticket will lose, because if it had won I'd still have believed it would lose — an insensitive belief. So the analysis blocks the skeptical argument while letting us keep knowledge of ordinary propositions, and the closure denial costs nothing. On balance, denying closure is a principled and acceptable feature of the theory, not a defect.
