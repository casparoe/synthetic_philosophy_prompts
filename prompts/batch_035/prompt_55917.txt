My friend and I watched a video of those RL agents learning to walk, where they get rewarded for moving forward and penalized for falling, and afterwards we got into a huge argument with a third friend from our rationalist meetup trying to referee and mostly failing. I've read every LessWrong thread on moral patienthood so I figured I'd ask here too. I want you to act as a philosopher of mind who also actually knows reinforcement learning, and I want you to think carefully and go move by move, not just declare a winner. Maybe brainstorm the possible positions for a second before you adjudicate.

Here's the exchange. Maya went first: she said suffering is basically a learning signal at bottom — pain evolved because it's a negative reinforcer, that's its whole functional job, it tells the organism "not that." An RL agent that gets negative reward when it faceplants has the exact same functional role implemented, so punishing the walker is literally giving it pain. She said denying that is just carbon chauvinism.

Dev said that's conflating the signal with the experience. The reward in RL is a scalar the trainer hands out for credit assignment — it's like a grade on a quiz. When the agent gets a -1, nothing happens *in* the agent except weights get nudged. The reward number doesn't cause anything downstream, doesn't persist, doesn't get dwelled on. There's no state of the agent that is bad for it. Suffering is a state, not a correction.

Maya came back with the neuroscience: dopamine prediction error literally is the TD-learning reward signal, and nociception in animals is also "just" a signal that updates behavior and policy. So if Dev's standard is that the signal must have some extra felt glow to count, why is he so confident the animal has the glow and the walker doesn't? That seems like it needs an argument, not an assertion.

Dev's answer was that the felt glow isn't the point either — the point is what the signal does downstream. In animals, pain drives persistent avoidance states, conflict, tradeoffs between approaching food and avoiding shock. A tabular Q-learner has none of that, no recurrent state, nothing that persists between updates. Maybe richer agents with world models would, and then it gets murky — but then it's the architecture doing the work, not the reward, so Maya's original claim ("negative reward = pain") is wrong even if some RL agents could suffer.

Maya's last move was that this concedes everything she needed: if it's architecture-dependent then it's not settled, and punishing walkers until we know is exactly the kind of gamble you don't take.
