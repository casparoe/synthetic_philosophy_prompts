I spent thirty-odd years as an atmospheric scientist, most of it on climate modeling, and since retiring I've been working my way through philosophy -- mostly ethics and decision theory, which turns out to be the part I was doing badly without realizing it all along. The thing I keep coming back to is the discount rate fight. As you probably know, the standard way economists discount the future splits the rate into pieces, and the piece labeled pure time preference (how much less a welfare just counts simply because it happens later) is the controversial one. One camp, roughly the Stern side of the climate debate, says pure time preference should be essentially zero for ethical reasons -- a person born in 2100 doesn't matter less just because they're later, the same way a person in another country doesn't matter less because they're farther away, a line of argument I associate with Parfit among others. The other camp, roughly Nordhaus and the market-rates people, says discount rates aren't a moral judgment you get to make from first principles, they're revealed in how people and markets actually behave, and if you insist on a rate far from observed behavior you're not doing policy analysis, you're doing sermon-writing.

What strikes me, coming from a field where disagreements eventually get settled by data, is that this one has been going basically unchanged for decades. Everyone knows all the arguments, nobody moves an inch, and the two sides don't even seem to be arguing on the same occasions anymore -- they just restate their positions at each other. My working hypothesis, for what it's worth: the disagreement isn't really about a number at all, it's about whether pure time preference is a descriptive parameter (a fact about human psychology you have to accommodate) or a normative one (a moral choice a society makes), and each side answers that meta-question so automatically that they never state it. But maybe that's too generous a diagnosis -- maybe one side is just making a mistake, or maybe the whole framework of splitting the rate into those pieces deserves the blame.

So that's my question: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Verbal, empirical, normative, or two different questions (what should we do vs. what will people accept) wearing one label? Please diagnose it using what each side actually claims, tell me whether anything could count as progress -- a distinction, a piece of evidence, a reframing -- or whether this is one of those debates that stays open because it genuinely should, and a retired scientist like me should stop expecting it to converge the way scientific disputes do. I'd rather have an honest diagnosis I don't like than a tidy verdict.
