My philosophy reading group has been on the transformative experience literature for a few months and we've hit a wall that feels structural rather than just hard. In one corner you have L.A. Paul's original claim: for genuinely transformative choices — having a child, going to war, emigrating, becoming a chef — you can't rationally deliberate in the standard way, because you lack the evidential basis to know what your future preferences and values will even be, and utility theory needs you to assign utilities to outcomes you can't represent from the inside. In the other corner you have the critics, who broadly say some version of: this is just ordinary uncertainty about the future wearing fancy clothes. You already have credences over what your future self will value, you can weigh the epistemically transformative experience as valuable in itself, or you can use fancier decision-theoretic tools (Pettigrew's thing about how your choice is itself evidence about what the experience will be like for you) and the problem dissolves. Paul's side then replies that all of this misses the point: the first-person problem of not being able to imagine the outcome isn't captured by a credence distribution you can't actually ground in evidence.

Here's my hypothesis and I want it stress-tested: I think the two sides stopped talking past each other years ago and are now answering different questions under one heading. The decision theorists are answering a third-person question — what formal procedure best models an agent in this situation, and does the formal machinery break? — and the answer is plausibly "no, with adjustments." Paul and her allies are answering a first-person question — what should *I*, standing here before the choice, treat myself as knowing about my future self — and the answer is "almost nothing, and no formalism makes that go away." If that's right, the debate looks substantive but is really a question about what "can't decide rationally" means: does it mean the axioms fail, or does it mean the agent lacks the self-knowledge the axioms presuppose?

So: is my diagnosis right, wrong, or right in a way that still leaves a real disagreement (maybe about whether decision theory owes us a first-person vindication at all)? Use what each side actually argues, not just labels. And then tell me what would count as progress — a case that splits the two questions apart, an agreement on what a "solution" would even look like, or just an honest admission that this is a battle over which question gets to own the phrase 'rational choice' and nobody should expect a winner. Also, if you think the impasse is hiding a genuine substantive disagreement — say, about whether rational requirements can outrun what agents can actually do — I want to hear that case too, not just the deflationary story.
