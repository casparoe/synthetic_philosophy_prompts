My ethics seminar just did two weeks on trolley cases and the doctrine of double effect, and honestly I left more confused than I arrived. On the last day the professor sketched this argument on the board and then just... refused to finish it, said we should figure out which premise to throw out ourselves, and now it's been living rent free in my head all week. The argument is supposed to show that double effect's means/side-effect distinction is broken, but each step looks fine to me individually and the conclusion feels too strong. Roughly as he gave it:

(1) By the doctrine of double effect, an act that kills is permissible only if the death is a merely foreseen side effect and not intended as a means, setting proportionality aside. (2) In the plain switch case, turning the trolley off the five and onto the one is permissible: the redirection itself is what saves the five, and the one's death isn't needed for that — if he heroically dove out of the way, the five would still be saved. His death is foreseen, not a means. (3) Now the loop track: the one's body is the only thing between the trolley and the five, since the track loops back around toward them. His death is now necessary for the rescue, so it's a means, so the doctrine forbids turning. (4) Bolt a big concrete block onto the loop a few meters past him. Now his body isn't necessary anymore — the block would stop the trolley even if he dodged — so his death is back to being a mere side effect, and the doctrine permits turning again. But the block never actually does anything: in the real run of events the trolley hits the man and stops, exactly as before. (5) Whether a lump of concrete sits on a stretch of track the trolley never reaches can't be what seperates a permissible rescue from a murder. (6) So the means test inside double effect is broken — it makes permissibility flip on remote, morally irrelevant facts. And my professor claims it generalizes: every counterfactual test he knows of (would you still act if the harm could be avoided, is the harm necessary for the good, does the plan still work without it) has some redundant detail that ends up deciding everything.

Here's my problem. I want to keep believing the loop case is permissible, same as the plain switch — everyone in class agreed on that, even Mara, our one hardcore consequentialist who usually thinks the whole distinction is decoration. And I'd also like to keep double effect around, since it was doing real work for us earlier in the unit (the strategic vs terror bombing stuff especially, and craniotomy vs hysterectomy). So which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? The professor did mutter something about the argument smuggling in an assumption about what "means" means, but he wouldn't elaborate, which I assume is his idea of pedagogy. Is it actually true that all the counterfactual tests collapse like this, or is he stacking the deck somewhere — maybe in how (3) and (4) get from causal necessity to intention? And if I do have to give up the loop intuition, is that really so bad, or is that just what biting a bullet feels like before you've bit it?
