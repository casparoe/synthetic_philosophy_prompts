I'm putting together a workshop on formal obstacles to self-improving agents, and as a centerpiece I want an example of an adversarial collaboration done well — the kind of document two people who have argued for a year should have produced but usually haven't. The two camps: Camp A holds that the Löbian obstacle is a deep and reasonably general problem for agents that improve themselves. By Löb's theorem, a sound agent cannot prove that its own proof system is trustworthy, and so the natural-looking proof that "my successor only takes actions I've established are safe" fails: verifying the successor's proofs requires proving something like the agent's own soundness, and anything that could prove that could already prove the conclusions directly. Camp A takes this to show that a self-trusting, self-modifying agent faces something like Gödel's second incompleteness theorem as a design problem, not just a curiosity, and that proposed escapes (parametric polymorphism, quining-style self-trust, time-limited or horizon-limited successors) each give up something real. Camp B holds that the obstacle is largely an artifact of a toy formalism that demands deductive, all-or-nothing trust between an agent and its successor; once you allow probabilistic trust, empirical track records, trust in a different or stronger system, or successors built on modestly different foundations, the problem dissolves into ordinary engineering, and Camp A has smuggled in an unrealistic standard of certainty.

Write the joint statement these two could both sign after a good-faith year of collaboration. I want: (1) a numbered section of claims both accept — about Löb's theorem itself, about what the tiling-agents framework actually establishes and what it merely assumes, about what Gödel's second incompleteness theorem does and does not imply for agents embedded in the physical world, and about which proposed fixes demonstrably work within the toy setup even if the camps disagree on their significance; (2) a disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is stated twice — once in wording Camp A would sign, once in wording Camp B would sign — with no phrasing that either side would reject as a strawman of its own view; (3) the cruxes, i.e. the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one camp. My own suspicion is that one crux is whether trust between an agent and its successor can legitimately come in degrees, and another is whether the tiling model captures anything essential about real-world self-modification or is a proving ground for a problem nothing real has; but identify the genuine cruxes rather than adopting mine. And (4) for each crux, a concrete case, theorem, construction, or thought experiment that both sides agree would bear on it, with each camp's prediction recorded in advance, so that in principle the dispute could be lost honestly. If you conclude that part of the dispute is merely verbal — for instance about what deserves to count as "a solution" rather than "a workaround" — say which part and why. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section, followed by the disagreements table, followed by the cruxes with each side's predictions.
