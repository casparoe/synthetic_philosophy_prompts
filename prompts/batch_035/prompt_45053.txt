The exam question was:

"A stranger stops you and offers a deal: hand over your wallet, and tomorrow he will give you an enormous reward. When you express doubt, he keeps raising the offered reward, eventually to a number so large that almost any nonzero probability of his being sincere makes handing over the wallet the expected-utility-maximizing move. (i) Explain the reasoning that says you should hand over the wallet. (ii) Set out the main responses that have been proposed to this kind of argument. (iii) Say which response you find strongest, and why."

And here is the student's answer, transcribed as written:

"Expected utility theory says pick the option with the highest expected utility. Since the reward is so huge, even a tiny probability that the mugger is sincere gives a bigger expected payoff than the wallet is worth. Also, whatever probability cutoff you pick, the mugger can just name a bigger number, so no fixed cutoff saves you. This is basically Pascal's wager with the numbers updated: instead of heaven, a huge reward, and the wager runs on infinite utility just the same, so all the standard objections to the wager carry straight over. In particular the many-gods objection works here: for every mugger offering a reward if you pay, there's an equal case for being punished if you pay, so the expected values cancel out and you shouldn't pay. Bostrom, who invented the scenario, concluded in his paper that the rational thing is to hand the wallet over, since expected utility is the whole of rationality. Proposed fixes: (1) Bounded utility: our utility functions are bounded, so past some point bigger payoffs add nothing, and once utilities are bounded we can ignore small-probability gambles altogether -- this also takes care of the St. Petersburg paradox. (2) Probability zero: since the mugger's claim can't in principle be verified, a rational agent should assign it probability exactly zero, so the expected value is zero no matter the size of the reward. And since no such mugger has ever been observed, a frequentist estimate of the threat is exactly zero too. (3) Round-off: human brains can't represent numbers that large, so our credence in claims about them is effectively zero. The strongest is (2), because the claim is unfalsifiable, and unfalsifiable claims can be assigned probability zero."
