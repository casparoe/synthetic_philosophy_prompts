For a good few years now I've been circling the anthropics literature the way longtime LessWrong readers do — the Sleeping Beauty flamewars, the periodic Doomsday argument resurgences, the Presumptuous Philosopher thought experiment — and I have finally managed to write the case for the self-indication assumption as a chain of steps each of which looks individually unimpeachable. The trouble is where the chain ends up, which I suspect proves too much. Here is the argument as I've got it:

1. Bayesian confirmation is the whole of rational updating: E confirms T over T′ exactly when E is more probable given T than given T′.
2. Let E be the proposition that there exists, somewhere, an observer with exactly my current evidence — my apparent memories, my present sensory state, my confused thoughts about anthropics.
3. A theory positing N times as many observers, all else equal, makes E more probable: if a cosmological theory says the universe teems with observers, it is likelier that some observer has my evidence than under a theory on which observers are vanishingly rare. On the sparse theory my evidence may well go uninstantiated; on the abundant one it almost certainly does not.
4. So conditioning on E boosts the abundant theory, in proportion to its advantage in observer count.
5. Now take two cosmological theories — call them Sparse and Abundant — identical in every observationally checkable respect, differing only in positing 10^12 versus 10^24 observers, with equal prior credence. On waking, before seeing anything at all, I should be roughly a trillion to one confident in Abundant. This is the Presumptuous Philosopher, and so far I am prepared to bite it: the horror is the point.
6. The same reasoning reaches outside physics altogether. Consider the theory: standard cosmology, plus a simulator who runs 10^30 exact copies of my present two-minute window of experience and then switches them off. Assign it any prior penalty you like — a billion to one against, a trillion to one. Once I condition on E, the copy-farm wins by whatever margin I please, because its advantage in observers with my evidence always outbids any finite prior penalty I can write down.
7. Therefore I can, from the armchair, settle any question whatever in favour of whichever side pads its ontology with duplicates of me, and no observation, evidence, or prior can resist, since any penalty is finite and the multiplier can always be made larger.
8. But a hypothesis should not be able to buy confirmation with extra observers the way a company buys votes, and armchair conditioning should not be able to do physics without doing experiments. So some step above is wrong.

What I would most like to keep is (1); I have watched too many escape routes quietly weaken Bayesian confirmation itself, and it is doing too much work elsewhere to give up lightly. My vague suspicion is that the trouble lies in how E is specified — that something slides between "there exists an observer with my evidence" and "I am that observer" — but every way I try to make that precise either smuggles in a reference class or collapses straight back into the self-indication assumption, so I cannot name the flaw, only its neighbourhood.

Tell me which step to reject and exactly what rejecting it costs. If the fix is to change what conditioning on E amounts to, spell out the corrected update rule rather than gesturing at it. If you think the right move is to abandon anthropic probabilities for anthropic decision theory — treating indexical information as input to a decision problem rather than as evidence for belief — explain how that handles step (5) specifically, because I can see how it handles betting but not how it makes the Presumptuous Philosopher's confidence go away. And if you think the argument is simply valid and I should accept the copy-farm, say so plainly; I will take that answer if it is the honest one. I am aware that the standard escape, the self-sampling assumption, buys relief here at the price of the Doomsday argument, so if that is your recommendation, tell me why its proving too much in the other direction is the cheaper of the two.

Please write your answer in British English, say how confident you are in your diagnosis, and single out the point you are least sure of.
