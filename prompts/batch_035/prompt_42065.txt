I spent most of my career as an experimental physicist and came to philosophy only in retirement, so I still instinctively ask what would settle a question rather than what tradition says about it. Lately I have been reading the dispute over whether the quantum state is a real physical thing or merely a compact representation of our information about a system, and I would like your credence that the wavefunction is genuinely out there -- ontic, in the jargon of the debate. Please do not hand me a bare number; decompose it instead. A natural decomposition might run: the probability that some realist interpretation of quantum mechanics is correct at all; conditional on that, the probability that the correct interpretation is one in which the state itself is ontic (many-worlds, Bohmian mechanics, objective collapse) rather than epistemic in the manner of QBism and its cousins; and any further terms you think belong -- for instance, how much weight to give the PBR theorem, whose conclusion depends on an assumption about independently prepared systems that its critics regard as doing all the work. Assign a number and a one-line justification to each term, show how they combine into your final credence, and then tell me which term the final answer is most sensitive to, and what argument, theorem, or conceivable experiment would move that term the most. And if you suspect the question is confused -- that "real" quietly equivocates between existing in spacetime and figuring in our best theory -- assign that suspicion its own probability and say where it enters the decomposition.
