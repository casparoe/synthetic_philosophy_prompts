I'm a math grad student, and a philosophy postdoc friend of mine hit me with an argument at dinner last week that I still can't get out of my head. He ran it on a claim I actually know: that the Riemann hypothesis is still an open problem. The arguement as he gave it to me:

(1) I know that RH is still open. It's been open for over 160 years, every mathematician I trust treats it as open, and I checked the Clay prize page recently-ish. (2) If I know that p, then for anything that would be evidence against p, I can know in advance that it's misleading — because if I know p, whatever seems to show not-p has to be a fake, a mistake, or a misreading, and by closure I can know that too. (3) I know that if the most respected number theorist alive announced, at a serious seminar with a full manuscript, that she had proved RH, that would be evidence that RH is no longer open — i.e., evidence against p. (4) So I can know in advance that any such announcement would be misleading, and I'm entitled to ignore it without reading a page. (5) The same goes for anything else I know, so I'm entitled to dismiss, unread, any expert testimony against anything I currently know.

He says the conclusion is that being told "you're wrong, here's why" never gives me a reason to even look, which is crazy — it would mean nobody could rationally update on math news, and peer review would be pointless for anyone who already "knows" the relevant results. But each step looked fine to me at the table. Since then I've developed a suspision that the move from (3) to (4) is doing more than it's licensed to — something about "would be evidence" being evaluated from where I stand now versus from where I'd stand after the announcement lands — but I can't make it precise, and honestly I might just be rationalizing because I hate the conclusion. What I do know is I don't want the cheap way out: if the right answer is "give up (2)," I want to hear what that actually costs, since my friend swears it's not free.
