I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, working on a dissertation chapter about the boundaries between self-deception and ordinary motivated reasoning, and I've hit what looks like a genuine dilemma that my supervisor suspects I've mislocated. As I understand the dialectic, the core problem is that self-deception seems structurally impossible on its own model: to deceive someone, you must yourself believe the truth, and you must intend to bring it about that your target believes a falsehood. Apply that template to the self, and the self-deceiver must simultaneously believe that p, believe that not-p, and form the self-defeating intention of making themselves believe something they already know to be false. The only escape route I can see is Davidson's — partitioning the mind into semi-autonomous departments, so that one subsystem knows the truth while another believes the comfortable falsehood — but that seems to multiply mysteries rather than resolve them: we'd now owe an account of how a single person can host two centers of agency, and the partition itself looks metaphorical in a way that can't do the explanatory work. So my question is which horn the field has settled on as least bad: the divided-mind picture, or biting the bullet with the skeptics and conceding that self-deception never actually occurs, with what we call denial being just wishful thinking, selective attention, or honest error mislabeled by clinicians and novelists?

The reason I want to get this straight is that I hope to defend the divided-mind reading of the stock cases — the spouse who somehow never registers the obvious affair, the executive who is the last to know the company is failing, the patient who treats the test results as a clerical error. I want to say that in these cases something like an inner deceiver is actively managing the flow of evidence, keeping it from crossing over to the part of the person that would have to act on it. But before I commit a hundred pages to that, I'd like to hear the strongest response to the dilemma that doesn't go partitionist. Note, by the way, that I'm setting aside cases where the self-deceived belief happens to be true; those don't interest me, since deception is essentially directed at falsehood, and a self-deceiver who lands on the truth seems to have merely gotten lucky rather than deceived anyone. Given all of this, is the partition view really the best going option, or is there a worked-out account of how the intention to self-deceive can be coherent within a single, undivided mind?
