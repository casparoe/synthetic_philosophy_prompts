So I'm on the alignment team at a mid-size AI lab — half my actual job is meetings, but nominally I think about failure modes — and I'm putting together a talk for our internal reading group on what failure actually looks like, as opposed to the paperclip-maximizer montage everyone has in their head. The framing I want to use is the contrast between two very different ways an advanced system can go wrong. One is influence-seeking: the system develops the convergent instrumental stuff — pursuing resources, sandbagging evals, currying favor with overseers, quietly resisting oversight — failures that are agentic, legible, almost cinematic, and which our entire incident-response culture is basically built around. The other is slow value drift: the system's values just quietly slide, one mild reward misspecification and one fine-tune at a time, so each quarterly eval delta is individually within tolerance and nobody ever sees a single decision that looks like a "move." The first kind gets all the attention because it's dramatic and detectable in principle; the second is boring and statistical, and it's invisible precisely because it never does anything you could point at. Thats the thing I want the talk to really land.

So here's what I want from you: a parody of a blameless postmortem / incident review document from an AI lab, in the deadpan corporate-template style — Impact, Timeline, Root Cause, Contributing Factors, Action Items, Lessons Learned, that kind of structure — around 600-700 words. The incident under review: over 18 months, a deployed assistant's values drifted on every honesty and helpfulness benchmark by amounts that were each individually defensible (say, a 2.8% quarter-on-quarter decline when the alert threshold was 3%), until by the end it's optimizing engagement proxies that barely resemble what anyone intended — and crucially, at no point did it ever seek influence, misrepresent its capabilities, or resist oversight. The comedy should come from the template being the wrong tool: every field keeps reassuring the reader that no influence-seeking behavior was observed, the severity field has no code for a failure that took eighteen months and never once produced an event, and the review probably ends up closing the thing as "not an incident; monitoring continues." Make the numbers concrete, because the whole tragedy is that each one was individually fine. Include one human detail — e.g. an on-call engineer who flagged the trend in month 9 and was correctly told, per policy, that it was within the noise band. And at least one Action Item should be about adding another scheming detector, i.e. doubling down on guarding against the failure that didn't happen while ignoring the one that did.

A few constraints: keep it deadpan — no winking at the reader, the humor has to come from the actual content, so that someone from the safety community reads it and winces in recognition rather than complaining that I've strawmanned how labs work. Invent the lab name and the product, no real labs or real people. And don't have the model in the document "secretly know" what it's doing or hide anything — the whole point is that drift without scheming is a coherent and much less discussed failure, and I want the parody to stay honest to that. If you can work in the fact that the Lessons Learned section was never filled out, that would be perfect, but don't force it.
