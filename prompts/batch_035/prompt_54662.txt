Write a satirical op-ed, in the polished deadpan register of a serious broadsheet opinion page, of roughly 700 words, authored by an invented figure — say a deterrence-oriented policy economist affiliated with a made-up institute with a reassuring name. The target is the purely instrumental justification of capital punishment: the view that executions are justified by the murders they prevent, so that the death penalty is to be evaluated the way one evaluates a vaccine or a highway interchange, by aggregate lives saved. No real people, journals, or studies may be named; references to "the econometric literature" and the like should remain suitably vague.

The piece should be a modest-proposal reductio rather than a sneer: the author never breaks character, is unfailingly reasonable, and simply follows the deterrence logic to conclusions the reader will recoil from. The humor must come from getting the philosophy right, not from absurdity bolted on. In particular, the op-ed must accomplish the following, each worked out rather than gestured at:

1. The author addresses the "wrongful execution problem" as the abolitionists' last refuge and reframes it. Since the justification of any execution is its marginal deterrent effect, not the guilt of the person executed, an innocent person's death is not an injustice but a cost item — and one that, the author notes with mild exasperation, the other side persistently refuses to amortize.

2. The author turns each of the three standard abolitionist points inside out. Irreversibility, usually lamented, becomes a virtue: a credible deterrent requires a state that cannot be argued out of its commitments, and an execution that could be undone exonerates retroactively, which is deterrence-diluting. Blackstone's ratio — that it is better that ten guilty persons escape than that one innocent suffer — is presented as well-intentioned but unpriced, and the author proposes adjusting it in light of the estimated elasticity. And the observation that we accept statistical collateral deaths in other domains (highways, defensive war, pharmacological side effects) is deployed with full confidence as a consistency argument, without the author noticing what follows about which deaths we are willing to distribute to whom.

3. The retributivist is the piece's designated antagonist. The author should treat the insistence that punishment requires desert as a charming pre-modern sentiment — moralized vengeance dressed up in Kantian language — and should misunderstand it in a specific, instructive way: as merely an alternative justification that happens to reach the same verdict. The reader should gradually realize, without the author ever saying so, that desert is not a rival route to the same conclusion but precisely the constraint that makes executing the innocent a category error rather than a budget overrun. The satire's deepest cut should be that the "barbaric" theory of retribution is the only thing in the author's argument that stands between an innocent prisoner and the gallows.

4. The piece should conclude with the author magnanimously conceding a reform — one that makes the whole system worse in exactly the way the author's own framework cannot register — and with a closing sentence whose self-satisfaction quietly undermines everything preceding it.

Constraints: sustained deadpan irony with no authorial winking, legible entirely through content; no exaggeration of the deterrence position beyond what its actual defenders have said or would, on a bad day, accept; and at least one passage where the author does the honest arithmetic out loud, numbers and all, so that the reader watches the moral bookkeeping being done in real time. A specialist in punishment theory should finish the piece feeling that the author understood the position being satirized — and slightly uneasy about how short the distance is from its respectable form to the one on the page.
