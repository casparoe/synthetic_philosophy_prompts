I'm not a philosopher or a scientist — I manage a small hardware store, and I came to this question through experience rather than study. Ten years ago I had a root canal under nitrous oxide: I felt the drilling vividly, as pressure and vibration, but I remember watching it with something close to curiosity, and it simply didn't seem to matter. Around the same time I read a magazine piece about stroke patients who report feeling pain that doesn't bother them, and the two experiences fused into a question I've been turning over ever since: if a sensation can be fully present and simply not matter, maybe what makes pain bad isn't the sensation at all but the mattering.

I argue about this with my brother-in-law, a nurse. He sits at about 0.3 on the following claim, and I sit at about 0.8:

The badness of a pain — what makes it bad for the person feeling it, setting aside its causes and downstream consequences — is exhausted by its affective component, the aversiveness, the suffering, the I-can't-stand-this quality, so that a pain with full sensory intensity but no aversive affect is not bad for the person in any respect.

His line is that the intense sensation itself is bad because it is the body's damage signal, so even a person who doesn't mind it is in a state that's bad for her — like being short of oxygen without feeling panic. My line is that his analogy smuggles in an actual harm, since hypoxia damages tissue, whereas pure sensation doesn't do anything to you.

Please give me your own credence on the claim, decomposed. At minimum I'd want a number and one line of justification for each of the following, plus any further terms you think belong: first, whether the people in these reports genuinely feel pain at normal intensity, rather than reporting something milder that just feels like the same thing; second, whether their cases show that the sensory and affective components can come apart in principle, not merely in pathology; third, whether not bad for you follows from not disliked — this seems to me the softest joint, since something could surely be bad for me without my caring, and I can't see how to settle that without first assuming an answer about what badness is; and fourth, anything else that belongs in the chain. Then combine the terms into your overall number and tell me which of them the result is most sensitive to.

After that, two more requests. Given where our two credences sit, where is my disagreement with my brother-in-law most likely living — in the descriptive question about what those patients actually feel, in the normative step from aversiveness to badness, or somewhere else entirely? And what observation or argument, available to someone with no lab access and no way to survey patients, would move your number the most?
