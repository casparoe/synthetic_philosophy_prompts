I have been reading David Hume's essay on miracles (Section X of An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, 1748) on my own, and there is a paragraph in Part I that I keep rereading without understanding how it is meant to work. After saying that a reasonable person matches confidence to evidence, Hume writes:

"A wise man, therefore, proportions his belief to the evidence. In such conclusions as are founded on an infallible experience, he expects the event with the last degree of assurance, and regards his past experience as a full proof of the future existence of that event. In other cases, he proceeds with more caution: He weighs the opposite experiments: He considers which side is supported by the greater number of experiments: to that side he inclines, with doubt and hesitation; and when at last he fixes his judgement, the evidence exceeds not what we properly call probability. All probability, then, supposes an opposition of experiments and observations, where the one side is found to overbalance the other, and to produce a degree of evidence, proportioned to the superiority. A hundred instances or experiments on one side, and fifty on another, afford a doubtful expectation of any event; though a hundred uniform experiments, with only one that is contradictory, reasonably beget a pretty strong degree of assurance. In all cases, we must balance the opposite experiments, where they are opposite, and deduct the smaller number from the greater, in order to know the exact force of the superior evidence."

What puzzles me is the arithmetic. Hume writes as if you could literally subtract a smaller number of contrary observations from a larger number of favourable ones and be left with the exact strength of your evidence, which makes belief sound like bookkeeping — and it ignores that observations surely do not all count equally, since one careful witness might be worth many careless ones. Later in the same section, Hume describes an Indian prince who had never seen frost and, on being told by travellers that water turns solid in cold climates, reasonably refused to believe it until the testimony became overwhelming, and Hume appears to approve of how the prince reasoned. But if the prince had applied the subtraction rule, it seems no amount of testimony about a distant country could ever outweigh a lifetime of uniform experience of liquid water: each report would be a single item set against thousands of contrary ones, so he would be locked into disbelief forever. So my questions are these: is the subtraction picture meant literally, or is it only an illustration of something about proportioning belief to evidence? And how is the prince's sensible reasoning supposed to fit the picture — does Hume accept that it can be rational to keep disbelieving something that is in fact true, or is testimony supposed to enter the ledger in some way that changes more than the count? I would be grateful for an explanation a non-scholar can follow.
