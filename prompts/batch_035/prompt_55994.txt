I write a science-and-ideas column for a general-interest magazine, and I'm preparing a piece on the word "falsifiable," which my editors deploy whenever a controversy flares up — string theory, psychoanalysis, intelligence design, you name it. In the course of my reading I've hit a wrinkle I cannot straighten out on my own, and I'd like a careful conceptual explanation of it.

The naive picture I've been working with says: a theory is scientific just in case some possible observation would count against it, and science progresses by exposing theories to such observations and discarding the failures. But as I understand it, Popper himself ran into trouble at the very bottom of this picture, in what he called the problem of the empirical basis. Individual observation statements — reports of what a particular instrument read, or what some observer saw — are not themselves falsified by further experience; at some point we simply decide to accept them, by convention, as the tribunal before which theories are tried. Popper seems to have accepted this happily: observation statements can be tested in various indirect ways, but never verified or falsified outright, and their acceptance in practice is a matter of decision and convention.

Please explain this problem and its consequences clearly. Specifically:

1. Why exactly can't the falsificationist just say that observation statements are verified by direct experience? What regress or circularity does that run into?
2. How is Popper's move — accepting basic statements by convention, with rules about when it's rational to do so — supposed to avoid collapsing into the conventionalism he was trying to refute, on which a determined theorist can always rescue a theory by revising the status of an observation statement rather than the theory itself?
3. How does this interact with the Duhem–Quine point that a prediction follows only from a theory plus a bundle of auxiliary assumptions, so that a failed prediction never tells you, by logic alone, which component to reject? (I understand Lakatos distinguished a "dogmatic" falsificationism nobody could actually hold from a "methodological" falsificationism built on rules for what to re-examine first; if that framing helps, use it.)
4. What survives of all this for demarcation? Does falsificationism end up as a criterion separating science from non-science, or as something more like an ethos or discipline that a community can choose to uphold — and does the latter still do the demarcating work my editors want it to do?

Please write for an intelligent lay reader: no technical apparatus beyond what you introduce, and please give the intuitive shape of each difficulty before any formal statement of it. I don't need names and dates for their own sake, and I don't need a history of twentieth-century philosophy of science — what I need is the conceptual anatomy of the problem, clearly enough laid out that I can see which parts of the falsifiability slogan are doing real work and which are decoration.
