I am a recently retired evolutionary biologist — most of my career was spent on selection experiments and on the population genetics of sex-ratio distortion in flies — and I have spent the past two years reading philosophy of mind with a small discussion group, which is how I arrived at teleosemantics and, now, at the following argument that I cannot get out of my head, largely because it turns the part of biology I know best against the theory that invokes it.

On the etiological version of the view, the content of a mental representation is fixed by the biological function of the mechanism that produces it, where a function is whatever the mechanism's tokens did that contributed to the reproductive success of the ancestors of its current bearers, thereby explaining why the mechanism was selected and why it is here now. Now the argument:

(1) A mechanism has many effects at once. The heart pumps blood, but it also makes rhythmic noise, adds mass, and displaces neighboring organs; an internal indicator mechanism similarly has many downstream effects.

(2) Only some of these effects were selected for; the rest are free-riders carried along by correlation.

(3) Which effects were selected for is not a further observable fact about the lineage's history. The population-level facts — which variant types, at what frequencies, left how many descendants — are exactly the same whether the heart spread because of its pumping or because pumping was correlated with, say, its acoustic output, so long as the correlation held.

(4) Distinguishing the selected-for effect from the correlated free-rider requires counterfactuals of the form "had pumping been detached from the noise, the lineage would still have favored pumping hearts," and such counterfactuals are not additional selection facts; they are something we import.

(5) If the selection history underdetermines function in this way even for the heart — our cleanest textbook case — it underdetermines the function of belief-producing and content-carrying mechanisms a fortiori, and so it underdetermines content.

(6) Content that is indeterminate in this way is not content at all. Therefore etiological teleosemantics fails not merely at the margins, in contrived cases of organisms assembled by lightning, but everywhere, since every lineage's history is saturated with exactly this kind of correlation.

The conclusion seems to prove too much: I spent four decades attributing functions to organs and confidently distinguishing adaptation from free-riding, and I do not believe I was coasting on an illusion. My instinct is that the false step is somewhere in (3) or (4) — in practice we evolutionary biologists do sort selected effects from mere correlates, and presumably we are doing something legitimate — but every time I try to say what that something is, I find myself producing counterfactuals about selection rather than records of selection, and I can see how a determined opponent would deny that such counterfactuals are selection facts in the theory's sense. Alternatively I suspect (4) contains an illicit slide from "not settled by the selection data" to "indeterminate, full stop."

Please tell me which step you would reject, why that step looked acceptable in the first place, and what rejecting it costs: in particular, whether the repair requires admitting into the notion of biological function something over and above historical selection facts — counterfactuals, propensities, causal roles — and whether admitting it still leaves teleosemantics with what it promised, namely a fully naturalistic ground of content. If you think the argument is sound, say so plainly, and tell me whether the right response is to give up (6) and accept that functions are indeterminate enough that biology's practice was never what I took it to be.
