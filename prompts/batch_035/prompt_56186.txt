so, been reading lesswrong and adjacent stuff for like a decade now, and one move i keep running into — usually from people way into value learning — goes something like this: "inferring human preferences from behavior is just revealed preference theory with new math. the human is the expert demonstrator, their life is the demonstration, and IRL just runs the tape backwards to recover the reward the expert was optimizing. so if you want to know what humans value, don't ask them — just watch them. stated preferences are noise on top of the data."

the strongest version i saw someone defend in a comment thread was: if you claim you value exercise but you never exercise, that's not akrasia, that's a revealed preference for couch time — a model trained on your behavior would be more correct about you than you are. which is basically the "humans are near-optimal planners for their own reward" assumption wearing an economics costume.

i want a real audit of the analogy, not the usual vibes. structure it like this:

1. The Correspondences — a short table of what's supposed to map onto what (expert demonstrator → human, trajectory → behavior, reward function → values, and so on), plus ~100 words of prose pointing out anything that already looks shaky at the mapping stage.

2. Where It Snaps — the disanalogies, but rank them by how much they actually threaten the "just watch, don't ask" conclusion, not by how clever they sound. i'd guess candidates include: economists get to *assume* the agent is a rational optimizer while IRL gets no such guarantee with humans; behavior is driven by habit, impulse, and addiction, not just values; tons of different reward functions are consistent with the same behavior, which is the whole reason IRL is underdetermined; and expert demonstrations come from someone who knows they're being watched and is trying to be legible, whereas nobody's daily life is curated as preference data. but you tell me which of those actually bite and which are cosmetic. ~450 words.

3. Does the Conclusion Survive — after 2, does "stated preferences are noise" hold up, or does the analogy end up supporting something weaker, like "behavior is the primary evidence but self-reports are a real second channel, especially off-distribution"? ~150 words.

4. A Better Analogy, If Any — is there a comparison that handles the bounded, akratic, many-motives-at-once human better than "expert demonstrator"? ~100 words.

keep it casual please, this is for a forum reply not a paper, and dont bother with citations. also no table needed beyond the correspondences one in section 1.
