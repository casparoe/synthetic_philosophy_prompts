i'm putting together a little zine for my typography class around the question of what makes an 'a' an 'a' — the thought being (i think this goes back to Hofstadter's Letter Spirit work, but you don't need to know any of that) that the sameness of a letter can't live in any particular stroke, since a lower-case 'a' can be a circle with a stick, two joined blobs and a tail, or a single-storey squiggle, and we read every one of them as the same letter. there seem to be two levels going on at once: the category (this is an 'a' and not, say, an 'e') and the style (this is *this font's* 'a' — which is how we can tell Helvetica from Garamond at a glance even when we can't say how we did it).

i want a poem for the centre spread that enacts this rather than just describing it. concretely: a poem with a refrain that comes back four or five times, and every time it returns its recognisably the same line, but no two versions are identical on the surface — vary the diction, the rhythm, the register, even the line length, the way a typeface redraws the same letter at a seperate size and weight. the reader should be able to think "ah, that line again" the way they'd recognise an 'a', without being able to point at what exactly stayed fixed. and if you can pull it off, let the final appearance of the refrain be the most distorted version of all and at the same time the one that most feels like *the* line — the swashy italic 'a' of the poem. everything else about the poem is up to you, though it would be nice if the body of it were loosely about reading, writing, or recognition rather than something unrelated. 14–24 lines all in.

since this is going in a zine people will actually read, don't hedge: pick your refrain and commit to it. then, after the poem, add a short note saying where you think the 'a'-ness of that line actually lives — what, if anything, stays constant across its versions — and flag if you ended up leaning on a contested idea to get there. if your honest answer is that nothing stays constant and the sameness is entirely supplied by the reader, say that plainly; don't give me a wishy-washy both-sides.
