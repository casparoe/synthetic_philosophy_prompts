I'm a second-year graduate student in cognitive science, and in a few weeks I defend the qualifying paper that will set the direction of my dissertation. The paper is about modeling analogy-making, and the question it has to answer is whether a model needs an explicit symbolic layer, or whether symbol-like structure can be left to emerge from lower-level dynamics. At our last reading group, on Hofstadter's I Am a Strange Loop, two labmates argued about the Careenium — his imagined world of tiny magnetic marbles (simms) careening on a frictionless table whose walls subtly flex in response to the outside environment, the marbles clumping into larger, semi-stable clusters (simmballs) that, because the walls register what is going on outside, come to encode the environment. After he asks you to speed everything up and zoom out so that the simms blur into a substrate and only the simmballs are visible, Hofstadter's question is: who shoves whom around in there — the meaningless micro-marbles, or the macro-level clusters that now look like symbols pushing each other about?

My labmates split over exactly the question my paper hangs on. Dan, who did physics before switching to cog sci, said the answer is obvious in principle: the simms and the collision laws determine everything; the simmballs are a summary of the simm trajectories, and talk of one simmball shoving another is a convenient compression rather than a further fact about the world. Maya replied that this argument would make every special science epiphenomenal — nobody claims meteorology or economics is shorthand that does no causal work — and that if the simmball-level description carries information about the environment and supports prediction, it earns causal standing like any other level. Dan said there's a relevant disanalogy: a simmball just is a bunch of simms, so saying the simmball pushes its own constituents around is like saying a pattern pushes its parts — either circular or double-counting the same forces. Maya answered that the claim was never that the macro level adds extra forces outside physics, but that causation is level-relative: the same events admit true descriptions at many levels, and the symbolic level is the one where the real explanatory action lives — no one learns anything about analogy-making from a listing of simm coordinates. She added an intervention test: if you could reach in and drag a simmball to a distant corner of the table, you would thereby redirect all of its simms, so macro-level manipulations are genuine manipulations. Dan's last word was that dragging a simmball is just grabbing a clump of simms with an awkward grip.

They eventually dropped it and turned to me to break the tie, and I honestly couldn't. Yet my paper has to come down somewhere. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: at each of the four steps, say who is right about what and where each overreaches, then say what, if anything, both of them are missing about the who-shoves-whom question. I'd also like your view on which position is more right overall, and what follows from it for my modeling decision.
