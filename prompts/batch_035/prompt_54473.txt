My partner and I run a small film night at our house, and last month we showed Arrival. I haven't been able to stop thinking about it since, mostly because I'd recently gone down a rabbit hole on the philosophy of time after hearing a podcast about whether objects persist through time. As I understand it from that podcast, there are two main pictures. On one, you endure: the very same you, all of you, exists at each moment, moving through time like a car driving down a road. On the other, you perdure — you stretch out through your whole lifespan the way the road stretches through space, and what exists at any given moment is only a slice or stage of you, the way a cross-section of the road is only part of it. I gather philosophers fight about this a lot.

Here's what struck me about the film. When Louise learns the heptapod language, she starts experiencing her life out of order — she "remembers" her daughter Hannah's birth and death before any of it has happened, and the film strongly suggests she doesn't foresee the future so much as perceive it as already there, the way the heptapods' circular written sentences are grasped all at once rather than read left to right. That sounds like the stretching-through-time picture: her life as one completed whole that she're simply not normally positioned to see. But at the end, when she embraces Ian anyway, knowing Hannah will die young, the film frames it as a choice — and a choice feels like it belongs to a fully present person standing at a fork, not to someone reading an already-written sentence. So my question is: read the film through this debate. Is the heptapod view of time really the perdurance picture, or just the idea that the future exists plus an unusual way of knowing it — and does the film blur those two things? There's also something the podcast brought up that I only half understood, about how one and the same person can be, say, grieving at one moment and joyful at another, and how each side explains that differently; it seems to me the film is full of moments where Louise-at-different-times has wildly different feelings about the same facts of her life, and I'd love to know whether the film's way of handling that leans toward one side.

Please be fair to both the film and the philosophy. Don't just turn Louise into a textbook illustration of the "time is a block" view — tell me where the film resists that reading, or where its depiction of learning the language doesn't actually fit either picture. I'd also like you to point to specific scenes or moments (the ending, the scenes with her daughter at various ages, the flash-forwards) and say what work each does for the interpretation. And finish by telling me whether you think the film dramatizes something the philosophical debate itself doesn't capture — for instance, whether knowing your whole life in advance would change what it means for any one moment of it to matter. If it helps, I know the film is based on Ted Chiang's novella, but our group has only seen the film, so base the main reading on the film and mention the story only where you're confident of the difference. Please answer in American English.
