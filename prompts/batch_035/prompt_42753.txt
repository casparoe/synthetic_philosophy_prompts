I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have come to hold a specific position on the Sleeping Beauty problem together with a general principle behind it. The position: on any given awakening, Beauty's credence that the fair coin landed heads should be 1/3. The principle: when you are uncertain which observer-moment you are currently occupying, your credence in a proposition should equal the expected fraction of the observer-moments compatible with your evidence in which that proposition is true. My route to this was, honestly, computational: every simulation I have written that samples an awakening uniformly returns 1/3, and the principle extends that result to unrepeatable cases. A colleague has since pointed out that my simulations presuppose exactly the disputed assumption — that all awakenings are equally likely to be this one — which makes the computational support circular. That objection landed, and now I want to know what would genuinely count against the principle. I can already see that it pushes me toward two conclusions I am not sure I accept: near-certainty in a theory positing vastly more observers over an equally simple rival positing very few, and some version of the Doomsday argument, since I will occupy most of my observer-moments early if humanity turns out to be short-lived. Tell me what the real defeaters are, ranked from fatal to merely awkward; which of them a committed defender of this style of view would concede as counting against it, and which they would instead defuse by giving up or reinterpreting some other commitment; what the principle predicts that its halfer and double-halfer rivals do not; and whether, once all the qualifications are in, it has been rendered unfalsifiable — and if so, whether that is a defect in a principle of this kind. For my part, the test I would find most persuasive is a concrete case where the principle and a firm intuition point in opposite directions, so please be specific about which cases those are and why.
