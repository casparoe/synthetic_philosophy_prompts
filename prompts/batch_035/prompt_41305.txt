I'm an undergraduate taking a Bayesian statistics course, and we've just covered Bertrand's paradox — the chord problem where the probability that a "randomly drawn" chord is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle comes out as 1/3, 1/2, or 1/4 depending on whether you pick the two endpoints uniformly on the circle, pick a radius and then a point along it, or pick the chord's midpoint uniformly over the disk. The lesson I keep being handed is just that the principle of indifference is inconsistent, full stop. But I also work as a student assistant on an applied modeling project, and there I regularly put uniform priors on quantities using exactly the reasoning the paradox seems to condemn: an interval for a plausible effect size, a prior over time-to-event, a flat distribution across a handful of candidate models — all justified by some version of "we know nothing about it."

Please describe the most important implications of Bertrand's paradox for these everyday choices of mine. In particular, I'd like to know whether the paradox really shows that my "knowing nothing" reasoning is incoherent, or whether it only bites in the special case of a parameter space with symmetry-rich structure like the circle — and if the latter, what the analogy to ordinary, non-circular parameter spaces is supposed to be. I'd also like your honest assessment of the two escape routes I've heard about: first, that the right prior is the one invariant under the transformations that carry the problem onto itself, so that "no information" should mean "invariance," not "uniformity"; second, that in practice the data swamps the prior anyway, so the choice barely matters. On the second, be specific about when it holds and when it fails — several of our models are data-poor, so "the likelihood dominates" is not obviously true for us.

Finally, state explicitly what would change your verdict — which argument or consideration would push you to a different assessment of whether my priors are salvageable — and identify the point in your reasoning where you think it is most likely to go wrong, say, which of the two escape routes you'd expect a philosopher of probability to contest, and on what grounds.
