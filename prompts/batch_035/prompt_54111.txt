For a reading-group session on conditionals and probability I have been asked to write up where I stand on a family of cases about what an impeccably reliable source's indicative conditional does to a probabilist's credences. Here are my verdicts; I would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

1. A variant of the classic Judy Benjamin case. A pilot parachutes into a region she knows is divided, with equal prior probability, into Red territory and Blue territory; Blue territory is in turn split evenly between the Second Company's HQ area and the rest of the Second Company's area. Headquarters radios: "If you are in Blue territory, you are in the HQ area." My verdict: her probability for Blue should stay exactly one-half, with all of her Blue-territory credence shifting onto HQ. Conditioning on the corresponding material conditional would give one-third, and that seems clearly wrong — the message said nothing about Red territory.

2. Same geography, different mission, except the pilot already knows for certain that she is not in the HQ area (she can see the boundary from where she sits). Headquarters radios the same sentence. My verdict: her probability for Blue should go to zero. I realize this sits oddly with (1), where the same message left the antecedent untouched, but with the consequent known false I cannot see how the antecedent survives.

3. A betting case. There are eight equally likely ways my friend's evening could go; in exactly two of them her train stops at Main Street, and of those two, exactly one has her home by six; of the six in which the train does not stop, five have her home by six. My price for a dollar bet on "If the train stops at Main Street, she will be home by six" is fifty cents: in general I price indicative conditionals at my conditional probability, and I would reject both pricing this one at my unconditional probability that she is home (seventy-five cents) and pricing it at the probability of the material conditional (eighty-seven and a half cents).

4. The same betting case, after news. I learn she is home by six. My price for that same conditional bet should now be a dollar: given the consequent, the conditional is settled whatever the train did. And I would insist this is not a new rule but my old pricing rule applied to my updated credences.

5. A certain antecedent. If I am certain her train stops at Main Street and the reliable source tells me "if it stops at Main Street, she will be home by six," I should become certain she is home by six.

6. The case I keep going back and forth on: the asymmetric Judy Benjamin. Same region, but Blue territory is nine-tenths HQ area and one-tenth the rest. Headquarters radios: "If you are in Blue territory, you are in the rest of the Second Company's area" — the small part. Part of me says her probability for Blue should stay one-half for exactly the reason it did in (1): the message is silent about Red. Another part says it should drop — being in Blue would mean being in a one-tenth sliver, which makes Blue a strange place to find oneself, so the balance should tilt toward Red. Renormalizing over the worlds where the material conditional holds gives one-eleventh, but that is the move I already rejected in (1).

My tentative thought is that a conditional from a fully reliable source does one thing: it drives the corresponding conditional probability to one and redistributes the antecedent's probability among antecedent worlds, leaving everything else alone — though I suspect it will not survive contact with everything above.

Is there a principle, or a package, that captures these verdicts — and if not, which of them has to go? Please state whatever principle you offer precisely enough to compute with, lead with it, tell me what it predicts for the variant of (1) in which the pilot's prior is ninety percent Red and ten percent Blue rather than even odds, and be explicit about which of my verdicts your account cannot save. Keep the whole reply under 600 words.
