Analyze France's 35-hour week -- the Aubry laws of 1998 and 2000, passed under Jospin's Plural Left with unemployment near a postwar record of 12.5% -- through the lens of the two great rival views about work and leisure. The first is Keynes's prediction, in his 1930 essay on the economic possibilities of his grandchildren's generation, that rising productivity would leave people unable to fill even fifteen hours a week with work, and that the real problem would be learning to live wisely and pleasantly. The second is Weber's Protestant ethic: the view that disciplined work is not merely an instrument but a constitutive habit, an end that would not dissolve when its instrumental justifications did. I don't want the standard recitation of either; I want the mapping done carefully. The stated rationale of the Aubry laws was work-sharing and job creation, not leisure as a good in itself -- does that matter for which theory is being tested? What should each view predict about the actual results: hourly productivity that did not collapse, output preserved by intensifying shifts and compressing tasks, managers quietly exempted through annualized-days contracts, and then the 2007 TEPA law under Sarkozy making overtime pay tax-exempt under the banner of letting people work more to earn more? Is the willingness of many to buy their hours back once the state stopped taxing them a revealed-preference refutation of Keynes, or just his own point about insatiable relative needs? And what does the episode say about Weber, given that Catholic France legislated against a work discipline he had located in the Protestant north? End by saying what the case shows each theory never anticipated, and where the honest answer is that the theories are simply silent. Please be disciplined about separating what the theories actually commit to from what partisans of the 35-hour week and its repeal claimed on their behalf.
