Analyze the 2019 UK prorogation affair as a stress test for the standard defense of keeping a crowned head inside a democracy. The salient facts, so the details don't matter: in late August 2019, Boris Johnson advised the Queen to prorogue parliament for roughly five weeks, dead in the middle of the run-up to the Brexit deadline, and she granted the request while staying at Balmoral. Nobody, on any side, seriously thought she could or should have refused. In September, all eleven justices of the UK Supreme Court held unanimously that the prorogation was unlawful and a nullity — as if it had never happened — and MPs were back at their desks by the following morning.

The theory I want this run through is the classic account of what constitutional monarchy is doing in a democracy — the one people usually get from Bagehot. His split between the dignified parts of the constitution, which impress and mobilize, and the efficient parts, which actually govern; his accounting of what's left for the monarch, which comes down to being consulted, encouraging ministers, and warning them; and the reserve-powers story that gets bolted on top, where the crown is a backstop that matters only in the crisis that never comes. Map the episode onto it carefully: who actually held the formal power here, what the conventions around advice did to it, what the court did with the question of whether such a thing is even justiciable, and whether the monarch was ever a player in this game or just a rubber stamp with nicer stationery. Then go the other way. If the reserve power can never be used without creating a bigger crisis than the one it's supposed to prevent — the natural comparison is Australia in 1975, where the governor-general actually pulled the trigger, dismissed Whitlam, and the monarchy has been living it down ever since — and if the real backstop turns out to be a court applying unwritten principle, is the crown doing any constitutional work at all? Dignity with zero efficiency. Or is the answer more like the Scandinavian and Japanese cases: Sweden's 1974 Instrument of Government stripped the king of every last political function and nothing broke, and postwar Japan defines the emperor as a symbol of the state, so if the reserve powers are decorative anyway, why does the UK keep a fiction that only ever activates to fail?

What I most want is discipline about the mapping, not a tour of Bagehot quotes: say where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak, note honestly whether the episode is a confirmation, an anomaly, or simply a case the dignified/efficient framework was never designed to handle, and tell me which parts of the standard defense survive contact with it — including the uncomfortable possibility that the defense was always about civic religion and never really about backstops at all. If you think the monarch's non-refusal in 2019 was itself the convention working as intended, say that too, and tell me what that implies the convention is actually for.
