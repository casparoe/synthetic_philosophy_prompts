I have been recording my gut verdicts on when a performative utterance actually does the thing it purports to do, and I would like to know what principle, if any, captures them — and, if none does, which of my verdicts ought to be surrendered.

Here are the cases with my verdicts. A registrar says the marriage words to a couple: they are married. An actor says the same words on stage: nothing happens — everyone understands the frame. The same words from a registrar who is bored and scrolling her phone: still married, as far as I am concerned; her attitude is irrelevant. The same words from a registrar who was dismissed that morning but whom nobody has thought to tell: I lean towards still married, though uneasily. A lender holding a gun makes the borrower say that he promises to repay: I think no promise is made. I say I will have the report done by Friday to a colleague who rolls her eyes and plainly does not believe me: I think the promise exists all the same. I say to a friend that I bet him fifty pounds it will rain tomorrow, and he laughs and walks off without a word: no bet exists, so that when it rains I owe him nothing and he owes me nothing. A man writes in his diary, addressed to no one, that he hereby bequeaths his car to his nephew: no bequest. A rival wins a prize and I say through gritted teeth that I congratulate him: the congratulation happens, but it is hollow. I tell a visitor that I warn him the dog bites, knowing full well that the dog is a coward: I cannot decide whether a warning was given that was insincere, or no warning at all. A stranger in uniform shouts at a passer-by that he orders him to stop, and the passer-by reasonably takes him for a security guard when he is in fact a caterer: no order was issued, I would say, even though the hearer complied.

Two of these seem right individually but refuse to sit together. The promise to the sceptic exists without uptake, whereas the bet fails without uptake; I cannot see what makes them differ, since both look like joint undertakings created by words. And the bored registrar sits badly with the insincere warning: in the one case inner attitude seems irrelevant to whether the act occurred, in the other it seems to decide between two accounts of failure. There is also a case I keep going back and forth on and have deliberately not counted: a priest who has privately lost his faith says the baptismal words over an infant. By my verdict on the bored registrar, the baptism happens; by my instinct about the insincere warning, something goes wrong at the level of the act itself. I cannot hold both.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the principle, or your verdict that no single principle is available — and then give the reasoning. No preamble, and do not restate my question.

What I want in the body is this: state the principle, or a small set of candidates, precisely, with the clause of it that each of my cases motivates; say what it predicts about cases I have not listed — I would particularly like the automated out-of-office message that says it apologises for the delay, and a chair who declares a meeting open when no one else has arrived; and be honest about which of my verdicts your preferred principle cannot accommodate, explaining why that verdict rather than the principle is what should go. If you find that no principle saves all my verdicts without contortion, tell me which verdict to drop and what becomes of the gun-point promise and the walked-away bet once it is dropped.
