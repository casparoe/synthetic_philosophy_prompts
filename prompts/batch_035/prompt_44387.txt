I keep arguing with my brother-in-law about inheritance taxes and I finally sat down and listed out where my gut actually lands on cases, and it's a mess. So I want to know if there's a principle underneath all of it or whether I'm just pattern-matching to vibes.

Roughly where I stand. A guy dies leaving $80M of index funds to three adult kids he saw at Christmas: full tax, obviously right. A fifth-generation family farm where the heir would have to sell the land just to pay the tax: leave them alone, taxing that feels like confiscation. The daughter who worked the restaurant kitchen for 22 years at half of what she could've earned elsewhere on a promise it'd be hers: taxing her feels like double taxation, she already earned a chunk of that estate in forgone wages. Same portfolio, but moved into a trust 14 years before death instead of at death: my gut says tax it exactly the same, and yet I get squeamish about the government reaching into a transfer between two living people. Grandpa blows the same money on cruises and casino trips during his last decade instead: obviously no tax, and I can't quite articulate why saving-it-for-the-kids should be treated worse than spending-it-on-yourself, but it is what it is. A dynasty trust set up so the money skips three generations of tax: not only tax it, tax it harder -- and I notice this is the same unearned money as case one, which bothers me. A house worth about the median home price going to an only child: no tax, everyone agrees on that one. And the family partnership trick where dad's $40M gets carved into minority stakes that each take a "valuation discount" so the whole estate appraises under the exemption: that's just cheating, tax the real value.

So is there one principle that gets all of these? The candidates I can half-articulate are (a) nobody's entitled to unearned windfalls, (b) each generation should start from roughly the same line, and (c) death is just a convenient realization event, like selling stock triggers capital gains. Each one fits some of my verdicts and breaks others -- the farm case kills (a) and (c) I think, but (b) can't explain why I'm fine with the median house going untaxed, since that's also an unearned head start.

The case I keep flip-flopping on is money genuinely earmarked for the grandkid's business startup vs. the same money in a trust designed to keep the family wealthy through 2120. If my principle is about opportunity those should be treated totally differently; if it's about unearned windfalls it can't tell them apart.

So: give me the principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely enough to apply. Tell me which of my verdicts it has to throw out -- be honest, I suspect the farm one and the dynasty-trust one can't both survive, and I want to know if that's true. And tell me what the principle predicts on something I haven't listed, say, a kid who inherits nothing at death but got tuition, a down payment, and startup capital as gifts spread over 30 years. My instinct is that person should be treated like the heir, but I genuinely don't know if any of my three candidate principles can say that.
