A friend who works in machine learning tells me Goodman's new riddle of induction is a solved problem dressed up as a mystery. His line: observing green emeralds and projecting that all emeralds are green, rather than that they are grue, is just curve-fitting on a training set — the grue hypothesis is the overfitter's high-degree polynomial, fitting the observed data no better or worse than the simple one while earning no credit for it — and the standard statistical machinery (complexity penalties, simplicity priors, regularization) is precisely what licenses preferring "green." He concludes that Hume and Goodman would have caused no fuss had they known modern statistics. Please audit the analogy: lay out the correspondence table (what plays the role of the training set, the test set, the model class, and the regularizer), then say where the mapping breaks — in particular whether "green is simpler than grue" behaves anything like a complexity penalty, given that the apparent simplicity of "green" seems to depend on which predicates are treated as primitive and "grue" can be defined simply in terms of them. Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for his conclusion from those that don't, and tell me whether "there is no residual philosophical problem, only bad statistics" survives once the disanalogies are on the table.
