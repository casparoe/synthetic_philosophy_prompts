I'm taking a seminar on philosophy of biology and we've hit the units of selection stuff -- Dawkins' gene-centric view vs. Gould and Lewontin and more recently people like Samir Okasha trying to referee. The picture the professor keeps coming back to is Dawkins' line that organisms are basically vehicles or survival machines -- robot bodies that genes built to copy themselves around in, with the gene as the only real replicator and everything else (cells, organisms, colonies) as more or less disposable packaging. He uses it pretty much as a knockdown argument: since only genes persist across generations with high fidelity, selection "sees" only them, so talk of selection for the good of the group or even for the good of the organism is basically a bookkeeping error.

Here's my problem. A classmate of mine who does more evo bio than philosophy pushed back in seminar by saying the robot/vehicle picture does tons of quiet work that isn't earned. His examples: endosymbiosis (is a mitochondrion part of my vehicle, or running its own program?), eusocial insects where most of the colony is sterile so whose copying is the vehicle serving, and the point that what makes something a "replicator" with high fidelity is only true relative to a chunk size you picked first -- a short genetic chunk replicates faithfully, a whole genome gets reshuffled every generation, so calling the gene the unit seems like it might be deflationary rather than deep. My professor waved this off as "Gould nostalgia" but didn't actually answer it, and honestly I couldn't tell who won.

So: audit the vehicle analogy. Give me the correspondence table -- what's the machine, the manufacturer, the program, the copying step, what selection is supposed to be "blind" to -- and then tell me where the mapping actually breaks vs. where it still carries weight. I'm especially confused about the fidelity point, whether it's really just an arbitrary choice of bookkeeping unit, and about whether the robot picture is doing the argumentative work against group selection or whether the argument would go through anyway with a weaker metaphor. And if the conclusion "selection at the gene level explains the adaptations, group selection is basically never the answer" survives once the disanalogies are on the table, I want to know what part of the analogy was doing it -- and if it doesn't survive, I want a better analogy if there is one. Keep it at the level where my classmate and my professor would both nod along, not a vibes-level summary.
