I teach a grad seminar that's half philosophy of action, half AI alignment — the running theme is what happens when you try to get an AI to do what you want without ever saying what you want. I'm writing the take-home final and need one essay question on impact measures: the research program that says we can penalize an agent for having "large effects on the world" without ever writing down a full value function. The question should test whether students can say if that program is coherent or whether it just smuggles the value specification into the measure where nobody's looking.

I want the question built around a specific toy case so it cant be answered by reciting the reading. Here's the case, which you should state inside the exam question itself: a warehouse delivery robot has to get a package to dock B. It can wait two minutes for a forklift to clear the aisle, or it can shove a crate of mugs off a shelf and get through now. The designers never wrote anything about mugs. The robot runs an impact penalty of the reachability type: it keeps a simulated "do-nothing twin," and at each step it's penalized in proportion to how much the set of future states it can still reach has shrunk compared to the twin's. Smashing the mugs forecloses a lot of reachable states; waiting barely forecloses any.

Part (a) of the question should make students show they understand why this kind of measure is attractive — why "don't permanently give up options" looks like it captures side-effect avoidance without a value function. Part (b) should make them break it: at least two concrete failure modes or value-leaks. Good candidates they could draw on include baselines gone wrong (what if the do-nothing twin's trajectory is computed from the wrong starting state, or the agent is about to be switched off so inaction itself is catastrophic), the fact that any action whatsoever forecloses something somebody could care about, so somebody still had to decide which state features even count as impacts (mugs are in the state description, dust motes aren't), and the alternative auxiliary-goals version of the penalty, where you penalize change in the agent's ability to pursue some side bundle of goals — which just moves the question to who picks the bundle. Part (c) should ask for a verdict: is the program doomed, or is a partially value-free impact measure still useful even if it's not fully value-free? They have to actually commit — no "on the one hand" hedging into nothing.

Students get 90 minutes and about 1000 words. I also want from you a model answer of roughly 650 words that takes a real position, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points worth a total of 20 marks, with a one-line note on each saying how students typically botch that point. The model answer should not name-drop more than it argues — I want it to read like something a strong student could actually produce under exam conditions, not a literature review. And please make sure the marking scheme seperates a student who understands why the baseline choice is doing all the philosophical work from a student who just memorized the phrase "relative reachability" and deploys it like a talisman.
