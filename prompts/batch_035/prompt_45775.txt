A magazine explainer on voting theory that I read recently summarized the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem like this: 'Once an election has three or more candidates, every voting method is manipulable: a voter who knows how the others are voting can always gain by misrepresenting her preferences. The only method that resists manipulation is a dictatorship, where one fixed voter's ranking is automatically adopted as the outcome, and since that is not really a voting method at all, every actual system — plurality, ranked choice, runoff, the lot — is gameable. So strategic voting is unavoidable in any democracy, and in the end honest voting is never in a voter's interest.' The source seemed careful, so I assume this is roughly right, but the tone strikes me as overstated and I can't pinpoint where. Please take it claim by claim: which parts would a social choice theorist accept as stated, which are simplifications a specialist would flag but let pass, and which are false? I am especially interested in two things: whether the closing inference — that honest voting is never in a voter's interest — follows from whatever true claims survive your audit, and whether the summary's silence about the theorem's assumptions (about the admissible preference profiles and about what counts as a voting method in the first place) is where its overreach lives. If correcting the summary changes its practical upshot for, say, a voter deciding whether to rank honestly under ranked choice, say so.
