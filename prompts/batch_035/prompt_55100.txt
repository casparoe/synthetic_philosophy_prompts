After a decade in a rented apartment where pest control meant a call to the landlord, I have bought a house, and suddenly every creature in it falls under my jurisdiction. My spouse and I have been making these decisions by gut, and I have been keeping notes, because I suspect my guts are not consistent. Here is roughly where I stand after a summer of it.

A mosquito lands on my arm: I swat it without a flicker of hesitation. Mosquito larvae in the rain barrel: I drop in the larvicide tablet and feel nothing, even knowing it kills thousands before they ever bite anyone. A housefly at a picnic: the swatter is fine, but when I found an old flypaper strip in the garage I threw it out rather than hang it — a fly dying over two days of exhaustion and starvation strikes me as off-limits, though the fly itself clearly matters less to me than the mouse did. The mouse in the garage, for that matter: the live trap and release was obviously right; a snap trap would have been an acceptable fallback; the glue traps at the hardware store I put back on the shelf feeling slightly ill. A spider in the bathtub I carried outside on a piece of paper. When a wasp nest appeared under the eaves — we have a toddler, and my spouse is allergic to stings — I sprayed the whole colony at dusk with no compunction. Yet a few weeks later I found myself carefully ushering a single wasp off the window screen and out the door, and feeling like a hypocrite about the nest. The ants that found the kitchen: I set out bait stations, which the workers carry back so that the entire colony dies over weeks — thousands of slow deaths — and I did this without any real hesitation. Finally, the aphids on the roses: I couldn't decide whether releasing ladybirds, who would tear through the colony alive, was better or worse than just spraying, or whether it was merely a way of outsourcing the killing so my hands stayed clean.

A friend of mine works on invertebrate welfare and keeps pressing on me the point that if there is even a modest probability insects feel something, the sheer numbers of them make my casualness look monstrous; I grant the arithmetic and somehow don't change my behavior.

My tentative principle is something like: killing is permissible in genuine defense of self or family, and in proportion to the threat, but never in a manner that prolongs dying, and never merely for convenience or tidiness. But I can see it fails almost immediately: the ant bait is neither defensive (most of those ants never entered the kitchen) nor quick, and I was fine with it; the single wasp was harmless and I spared it, but the nest that had harmed no one I destroyed. So I would like you to try to find the actual principle behind my verdicts. Is it expected suffering, weighted by some rough gradient of sentience from mammals down to insects? Is it something about killing individuals versus accepting systemic deaths? Or numbers? Please state the principle or candidate principles precisely, note which case forces each clause, tell me what they imply for the cases I have not resolved — the termite colony in the framing that has not yet done real damage, the preventive perimeter treatment that kills animals guilty of nothing, the fleas the cat has brought in — and be honest about which of my verdicts the best principle simply cannot save, and which of them I should give up.
