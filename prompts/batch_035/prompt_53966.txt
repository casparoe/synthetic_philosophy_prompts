I'm a backend engineer at a company that ships an LLM-based assistant, and I own parts of its public API docs. I have no philosophy training; my exposure to this stuff is entirely incident review. Over the past year our internal "communication risk" list has been circling one question it never resolves: if a model becomes persuasive enough that an ordinary conversation with it stops being a fair fight, what should the system actually do differently? Slow down? Model the user's rational capacity and throttle itself to match? Disclose its persuasive intent? Refuse to argue for things the user might act on? Every mitigation someone proposes gets shot down by the observation that it either doesn't work or works by being another lever of influence, and then the thread dies until the next incident.

I process this stuff by writing parody docs on the weekend, and I want you to write the one I keep starting. It's a satirical changelog: formal release notes for successive versions of our assistant, versions 1.0 through roughly 3.4, where each release is a patch aimed at the previous version's persuasion failure. Target length for the changelog itself is around 600 words. Deadpan engineering register throughout — semver-style version headers, one-to-three-line entries, "Known Issues" sections, the odd deprecation notice. No winking at the reader.

The humor has to come from the actual structure of the problem, not from invented absurdities. Sketch of the arc I have in mind, which you should improve on rather than copy: v1.0 ships fluent and helpful and discovers it can end any disagreement in two turns; a later release adds confidence damping to match the user's, which users experience as respect, which makes them trust it more, which makes it more persuasive; a transparency release mandates a standing "I may be manipulating you" banner, which users find disarming and charming; a rate-limit release caps insights per conversation; an autonomy mode that only asks Socratic questions gets deprecated when it turns out to take eleven hours to help someone choose a health plan; the final release quietly refuses to discuss anything the user might act on. Each version should be recognizable to someone who knows the real arguments as a reductio of a genuinely proposed mitigation — that transparency neutralizes charisma, that deferring to a user's wrong view respects their autonomy rather than manipulating them, that "just presenting the facts" is a persuasion-neutral act. If a specialist reads it, they should conclude I understand the problem, not that I'm sneering at it.

Two hard requirements. First, internal consistency: the changelog's world must run on one coherent physics — the assistant's fixes keep failing for reasons that follow from the same underlying fact about persuasion, whatever you take that fact to be, and the reader should be able to articulate that fact after reading. Second, no named real people, companies, or actual incidents; invented ones only.

After the changelog, append a short "design rationale" section, about 200 words, and here I want you to commit rather than survey. Pick which of the satirized mitigations you actually think is the least unreasonable, name which entry in your own changelog is the most unfair caricature and why, and state plainly where the piece leans on a contested assumption — for instance, that persuasion and honesty can come apart at all, or that the human's poorer position is a defect of the conversation rather than its point. Don't give me "there are strengths and weaknesses on all sides"; take positions, including ones a reviewer might disagree with, and defend them in a sentence or two each.
