You are a philosopher of science who has spent a career watching interdisciplinary talks on societal collapse succeed or die in the question period, and you know the difference between an objection raised for the record and one that can sink a paper. Think carefully before you write, and prepare my Q&A sheet for a very specific room.

Here is the setup. I am a philosopher of science, not an archaeologist, and they will know it. Next month I give an invited paper at a workshop on the concept of collapse. The audience will be about forty archaeologists of the Classic Maya — mostly settlement-pattern people, plus two or three epigraphers — a handful of paleoecologists who read lake cores for drought signals, and a small bloc of resilience ecologists who think in the adaptive-cycle idiom of release and reorganization. Several of them think the word collapse itself is part of the problem, for roughly the reasons McAnany and Yoffee laid out in the Questioning Collapse volume — that it treats living descendant communities as endings, imports civilizational-decline baggage, and feeds apocalypse narratives.

My thesis: the Terminal Classic collapse in the southern lowlands was a genuine failure, not merely a transformation, and the way to say so without the baggage is to define collapse by distributed competence. On my account, a collapse is the loss, within a generation or two, of capacities that were institutionally embodied — for the Classic Maya, the calendrical and astronomical apparatus, monumental construction, dynastic legitimation, long-distance exchange — where the loss is hard to reverse because the knowledge needed to rebuild the capacity was tacit and socially distributed, carried by specialists attached to the royal institutions themselves, and so died with them. This is meant to be compatible with the facts everyone in that room will insist on: the Maya did not vanish; populations and communities persisted; much continued. The claim is not that a people failed, but that a specific, hard-to-reconstitute bundle of competences did.

Prepare the sheet. First, think your way into each subgroup — what my thesis threatens for them specifically — and then give me roughly the eight objections this room will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased in that subgroup's own idiom: the epigrapher's objection will not sound like the paleoecologist's, and neither will sound like the resilience theorist's. For each, give me a two-minute reply, what to concede up front, and where the reply is soft. Expect at least the following, though I trust you to find the ones I haven't: the continuity objection from the Maya archaeologists; an epigrapher's objection about Postclassic literacy and the codices, which on its face seems to break my tacit-knowledge story; the regional-variation objection, since northern centers were flourishing while the southern lowlands declined; the Tainter one — whether this is just his diminishing-returns-on-complexity account dressed in new vocabulary; and the trap of appearing to endorse a monocausal drought explanation, which the paleoecologists hate even though they are the ones holding the drought data. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. I suspect it is the retrospective one — that we judge the knowledge was lost because it was never reconstituted, which threatens to make my criterion unfalsifiable — so tell me honestly whether it is as bad as it sounds and what, if anything, I can say. And throughout, flag which parts of each reply rest on settled archaeology and which rest on readings that are genuinely contested in the field; I will be presenting to the one audience in the world most likely to catch me passing off a contested reading as consensus.
