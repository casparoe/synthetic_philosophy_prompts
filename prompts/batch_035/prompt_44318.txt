I teach an upper-level seminar on moral responsibility, and next week's topic is standing to blame — specifically whether hypocrisy robs you of the right to blame even when your accusation is spot on. 15 students, 80 minutes, a room with movable chairs. Design the session for me.

Here's the part I care most about: the game has to separate the correctness of an accusation from the accuser's entitlement to make it. So the evidence in every case should be public and undisputed — everyone should know the accused did it — and the only variable should be something about the accuser. If the sanction collapses when the accuser turns out to have done the same thing, and nothing about the evidence changed, the students will have generated the phenomenon themselves instead of being told about it.

Rough shape I have in mind (feel free to improve on it): a fictional co-op or boarding house with maybe five posted house norms, and each participant holds a private "history sheet" logging their own violations from the past week. I'll distribute the sheets myself, and I want some dirty, some clean, and some dirty on a *different* norm than the one they end up reporting on — that last kind matters, I'll say why in a second. Each round someone draws an incident: a specific member committed a specific violation, witnessed by a designated reporter. The reporter decides whether to open a case, then there's an enforcement vote by everyone else, and — this is the bit I'm not sure how to stage — some mechanism whereby the reporter's own sheet can get read out, sometimes before the vote and sometimes after. Ideally some rounds go: vote, reveal the sheet, vote again, so they can watch their own enforcement intuitions shift while literally nothing new has come out about the accused.

The different-norm sheets are for the question I actually want the debrief to land on: if your reporter freerides on the pantry, does she still get to chair the noise-complaint case? I.e. does hypocrisy about one norm cost you standing to blame violations of an unrelated norm, or is standing violation-specific? I honestly don't know what my students will say about that, which is exactly why I want the game to force them to have the intuition before we name anything.

Deliverables: full rules, materials I need to prep, a round structure that fits in 80 minutes including the debrief, the debrief itself (map moments in the game onto parts of the idea — what the vote was tracking, what the reveal changed, what the challenge/reveal mechanism was doing), what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle it, and an honest note on which feature of real standing-withdrawal the game just can't reproduce and I'll have to supply myself afterwards. No lecture up front — they shouldn't here the phrase "standing to blame" until the debrief, and I'd rather the in-game fiction avoid words like "hypocrisy" too. You don't need to bring in any literature by name in the debrief plan; I'll handle that part myself once they've felt the thing.
