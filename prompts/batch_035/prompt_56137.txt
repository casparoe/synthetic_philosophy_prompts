I'm a science journalist working on a longread about the species problem, and somewhere between the third taxonomist and the fifth philosopher I stopped being able to tell what the disagreement is even about. You've got the biological species concept people, for whom it's all about interbreeding and isolation; the phylogenetic camp, who want the smallest diagnosable monophyletic cluster and think reproductive compatibility is beside the point; the cohesion/homeostatic-property-cluster types who say it's about processes holding organisms together, whatever those are; and the pluralists, who say stop looking for THE species concept, there are many legitimate ones. This fight has run basically unchanged since the 70s. Meanwhile actual working taxonomists keep describing new species with barely a nod to any of it, asexual lineages and the whole microbial world sit there awkwardly ignoring the interbreeding criterion entirely (horizontal gene transfer makes the tree of life look more like a net), and every time someone dissects a "ring species" it turns out not to be one, so even the showcase examples keep dissolving.

My working hypothesis as a reporter: this is not an empirical question that more data will settle. I think "species" is quietly doing several unrelated jobs — reproductive unit, evolutionary actor, classificatory convenience for museum drawers, legal unit for conservation law — and each camp has picked a different job to optimize for. That would make it a question about what we want the word for, not about what nature is doing. But a biologist I interviewed pushed back hard: she says if there are real processes that generate and maintain clusters of organisms, then there's a fact of the matter about where the clusters are, and pluralism is just changing the subject dressed up as sophistication.

I want you to answer as a philosopher of biology who's also spent time around working systematists. Please think carefully and actually brainstorm the candidate diagnoses (verbal, empirical-in-disguise, different-questions-under-one-name, bedrock intuitions) before committing to one, because I'll notice if you just list options and shrug. Structure your reply like this:

**The state of the play** (~150 words): just so I can check we mean the same fight, summarize what each camp actually claims, at the level I can print.

**Diagnosis** (~400 words): the meat. What kind of disagreement is this really, and why? Engage my hypothesis and the biologist's pushback directly — I want to know which of us is closer and where each of us is wrong.

**What would count as progress** (~200 words): given your diagnosis, name something concrete that would move the debate, or argue nothing should be expected.
