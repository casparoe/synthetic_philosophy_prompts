I'm a graduate student working on a paper about Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" (1962), and I've drafted a section that makes what I think is a novel move, though I worry it's either quietly well-known or subtly confused. I'd like your comments on it.

The section's argument, in brief: Strawson's standard reading is that reactive attitudes (resentment, gratitude, indignation, guilt) are part of our nature as social creatures, so that suspending them wholesale in favor of the "objective attitude" — treating people as objects to be managed rather than agents to be addressed — is not a live option for us, and the pessimist's question about whether anyone is "really" responsible is thereby sidelined. I think this reading leaves something out. My draft argues that the argument can be strengthened by treating the reactive attitudes as a precondition of interpretation itself: to hear another person's utterances as meaning anything at all, you have to read them as expressions of intentions directed at a shared world, and this reading is enforced by the reactive register — puzzlement, offense, the expectation of justification — rather than by the detached stance of the objective attitude. On this reconstruction, Strawson's appeal to our "nature" is not a biological or psychological claim but something closer to a transcendental one: someone who genuinely occupied the objective attitude across the board would not merely relate to people differently, they would have stopped doing something like understanding them. The draft closes by claiming this blocks a standard objection — that Strawson only shows we can't stop feeling, not that the feelings are fitting — since what is at issue is now the conditions of intelligibility, not the tenacity of sentiment.

I'd like comments on three things specifically:

1. Accuracy: whether this reconstruction departs from anything Strawson actually commits himself to, or whether the transcendental gloss is a fair extension of his text.

2. The central move: whether the bridge to interpretation is doing real work or is decorative. If intelligibility is guaranteed by interpretation, and interpretation can coexist with the objective attitude — as perhaps seems true of the psychiatrist or the skilled manipulator, both of whom understand people quite well while suspending the participant stance — then my strengthened argument collapses back into the ordinary one. This is the point I'm least sure about.

3. An overall verdict, with the following honesty requirement: please say explicitly what would change your mind about that verdict, and identify where you think the draft's reasoning is most likely to fail. If your assessment is negative, name the specific objection or piece of evidence that would move you toward a positive one, rather than just noting weaknesses in the abstract. And if your assessment is positive, say what a hostile reader would press on.

Please be direct; polite hedging is less useful to me than a candid read.
