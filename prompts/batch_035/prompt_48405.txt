I write a column on science and ideas, and my next piece contrasts how different cultures treat the visibly successful: the American Dream's celebration of the striver on one side, and on the other the Scandinavian Law of Jante — with the tall poppy syndrome as its supposed Anglophone cousin. Whenever Jante comes up in interviews and comment threads, someone recites the first of its commandments, "You're not to think you are anything special," and the line gets made to do very different work: a healthy allergy to self-aggrandizement, a norm of understatement about wealth, crab-bucket envy dressed up as egalitarianism, or outright suspicion of excellence itself. Before I write the column, I want the one-liner taken apart rather than used. What distinct precise claims could it be making — about what one may believe regarding one's own worth, about what one may say or display in public, about whether being "special" is impossible, or merely vulgar to claim, and about the "we" doing the policing (an egalitarian claim, a class- or group-loyalty claim, a small-town conformity claim)? For each reading I want a verdict: trivially true, defensible but contested, or false; whether it actually conflicts with celebrating the successful; and whether it is the same norm that Australians and Americans have in mind when they speak of cutting down tall poppies. Please also flag which reading is in play when Scandinavians invoke Jante with affection versus when outsiders deploy it to indict Nordic culture, and which readings the two uses quietly conflate. As this is going into print under my name, please state how confident you are in each claim you make about how the slogan is actually used, and single out the point in your answer you would be least sure of defending editorially.
