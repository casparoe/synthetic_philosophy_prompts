I am a functional-safety engineer. My working vocabulary is fault tree analysis, FMEA, common-cause failure, defense in depth, diagnostic coverage, random versus systematic faults, fail-safe versus fail-dangerous behavior, and safety integrity levels. I design systems whose entire purpose is to behave acceptably regardless of what anyone intends, and I have spent my career making dangerous processes inert when something goes wrong. I have been reading about nuclear deterrence as an outsider, and I keep feeling that the strategists are describing something I almost recognize.

Restate the central claims of Cold War deterrence theory in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Specifically, take at least these four items and translate each one: (a) Schelling's idea that a threat can be made credible by deliberately relinquishing fine-grained control — the threat that leaves something to chance, brinkmanship as a contest in risk-taking rather than a contest in resolve; (b) the argument that a certain amount of apparent irrationality or loss of control on one's own side is an asset rather than a defect; (c) launch-on-warning postures, where the compression of decision time is itself the design feature; and (d) so-called dead-hand systems, automated second-strike mechanisms intended to function precisely when the human operators cannot. Use my terms where they genuinely fit: is a dead-hand system a fail-dangerous design with an intentional common-cause failure mode? Is brinkmanship describable as deliberately operating a system outside its qualified envelope so that its failure distribution becomes the negotiating instrument?

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of deterrence theory translate cleanly into reliability language, which parts translate only with distortion, and which parts have no expression in my vocabulary at all? I suspect the deep problem is this: everything I do is aimed at the behavior of a system, while everything deterrence theory does is aimed at the beliefs of an adversary about that system — the delivered product of a deterrent posture is not an output but a probability distribution inside someone else's head, updated by signals I only partially control. If that is right, then my vocabulary, which has no concept of an observer's model of the system, cannot even state the design requirement. But consider the alternative: perhaps the residue points at a genuine unclarity in the strategists' own talk, since they too slide between designing system behavior and designing adversary beliefs without ever separating the two engineering tasks.

Address both possibilities and tell me which you think is better supported. Also answer one practical question: if I were handed the specification for a deterrent posture and asked to review it the way I review a safety case, what would my methods catch, and what would they be structurally blind to?
