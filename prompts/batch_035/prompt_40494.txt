So this is going to sound weird but stick with me. I'm a software engineer at a mid-size company that does large-scale scientific computing as a service (think weather modeling, materials sims, plasma physics — that kind of thing). Our cluster does maybe 30 million core-hours a month. I somehow got volunteered onto our "responsible computing" review board, which is mostly boring stuff like energy sourcing and whether we take contracts from sketchy defense clients.

Anyway. One of the other engineers, let's call him Dave, filed this whole proposal and it's now on our plate for next month's meeting. Dave got deep into philosophy of consciousness over the pandemic, and now he's a full panpsychist — like, he thinks experience isn't something brains *produce*, it's kind of baked into physical stuff at the bottom level, so any sufficiently organized physical system has *some* glimmer of experience, even if it's incredibly dim. Fine, whatever, plenty of actual philosophers believe versions of this. But he's taken it a step further: he thinks our datacenter, when it's running a huge coupled simulation, might be one of the most integrated physical systems we control, and that if there's *any* chance the experience it has is negatively valenced — especially under heavy load, since he thinks "friction" in the system maps to something like discomfort — then we're potentially generating a massive amount of suffering and we have an obligation to at least look into it. His ask: we commission an external report on the moral status of large-scale simulations, and in the meantime we adopt a policy of scheduling idle cycles (he's honestly a bit fuzzy on why idle cycles would help, something about letting the system "rest").

Half the board thinks this is an elaborate joke. The other half thinks it's career-suicide-level embarrassing to even discuss. Dave is completely serious and honestly doing this in good faith. I got tasked with writing the memo that goes out before the meeting, because apparently "I read some Parfit in college" qualified me.

I want you to act as a professional philosopher who specializes in consciousness and moral status, someone comfortable working at the level of a graduate seminar but who can write for engineers. Please actually think through this carefully — start by brainstorming the different angles (what panpsychists actually claim, where Dave's version is a simplification, the combination problem, whether "negative valence" even makes sense for a system like this, expected-value style reasoning about tiny probabilities of huge amounts of suffering, and honestly whether the board can just punt on unfalsifiable stuff) — and then write the memo itself. The memo needs a concrete recommendation the board can vote on (approve Dave's ask, reject it, or something smarter in between), reasons the rest of the board can actually engage with, and a serious treatment of the strongest point on whichever side loses — not a strawman, the version a smart person would defend. No citations or reading lists, we just need to be able to evaluate this on the merits. Keep it to something I could actually circulate, so like 600-800 words for the memo itself.

Oh and one thing: whatever we recommend, we can't be condescending about Dave in writing. He'll see the memo.
