I'm teaching a seminar on peer disagreement and I need a fresh thought experiment, since I'm sick of the standard examples. Build me a case where two people — both honest, both genuinely trying to get the truth, and each knowing the other is smart and informed — tell each other their evidence AND their final credences, and yet neither one budges an inch. Everyday setting please: two doctors arguing over a diagnosis, or two old friends disagreeing about whether someone they both know can be trusted. No sci-fi, no exotic psychology, no stipulating that one of them is dumb or stubborn — the disagreement has to survive the conversation because of the details you build in, not because someone digs in their heels.

After the scenario, do the diagnostic work. Walk through Aumann's agreement theorem: lay out its premises, and show which one your case secretly violates — and it has to violate at least one, since the theorem is a theorem, so the disagreement can't be real under its full conditions. The trick I want is that the violation should be subtle enough that the two agents themselves never notice it in the conversation — something like the gap between sharing your evidence and having your posteriors be *common knowledge* in the technical sense, or a prior that seemed common but wasn't. Then tell me what a strict Bayesian would say the agents should do once the flaw is pointed out to them, and whether it's realistic to expect actual humans to do that. End with a note on which single detail of your scenario is doing all the work, and what happens if you remove it. Keep the scenario itself to one paragraph — the meat of the answer is the diagnosis, not the setup. And this is for undergrads with one semester of probability, so no measure theory, just clean reasoning about priors, conditioning, and what each agent knows about what the other knows.
