**The exam question:** In class we distinguished *lexical* negative utilitarianism (no amount of happiness can outweigh or offset any amount of suffering) from *weak* negative utilitarianism (suffering and happiness both count, but suffering counts more — so tradeoffs exist, they're just asymmetric). The famous "world-destruction objection" says that negative utilitarianism, if taken seriously, implies we should painlessly wipe out all sentient life, since that would guarantee zero future suffering. (a) Explain the objection carefully, including whatever assumptions it needs about the value of nonexistence and of lives that are prevented. (b) Assess which versions of negative utilitarianism it actually refutes, and which, if any, survive. (c) Say whether the objection generalizes to any view that gives suffering extra weight.

**The student's answer:** "The world-destruction objection is simple: if the only thing with final moral value is the absence of suffering, then a universe with no sentient beings is a perfect universe, since there is no suffering in it. So negative utilitarianism tells us that painlessly annihilating all life is not just permissible but required — one big painless bullet, so to speak. This shows the view is monstrous and can't be right.

The objection refutes every form of negative utilitarianism. Even the weak version collapses, because once you give suffering any extra weight at all, no matter how small, you can always find a life (or a world) with enough suffering in it that the happiness can't compensate. So the weak version just is the strong version with extra steps. And since the objection refutes negative utilitarianism across the board, it also refutes every other view that weights suffering more heavily than happiness — say, a view that says a stubbed toe needs more than one unit of pleasure to balance it. All such views end up endorsing annihilation, which is a reductio of giving suffering any priority whatsoever.

The objection also has an interesting implication for population ethics: negative utilitarianism can't allow us to create new happy people, because creating a person always risks creating suffering, and suffering is what matters. So it slides into the antinatalist position that procreation is wrong. This makes the view even less plausible."
