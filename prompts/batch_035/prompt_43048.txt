Last month at a family dinner, my uncle rebuked my cousin for refusing to repay a loan from her brother. My uncle is in no position to rebuke anyone about unpaid debts: years ago he borrowed a considerable sum from my own grandmother and never repaid it. My aunt silenced him with the proverb about people in glass houses not throwing stones, and everyone nodded. What struck me was that she didn't seem to be warning him about exposure or embarrassment; she appeared to be ruling his rebuke out of order — as though the hypocrisy had voided it, so that my cousin could simply ignore it as if it had never been said. When I pressed her afterward, my uncle offered a defense I haven't been able to stop thinking about: that his track record might affect whether he was entitled to say it, but could not affect whether it was true. My aunt replied that blame isn't just a statement of fact — it's something you do to someone, and some people just aren't in a position to do it.

I want to get clear on what the proverb actually claims. Please lay out the distinct, precise theses it could be expressing — a menu, not a single interpretation. I would expect at least these levels, and you should refine or add to them: (1) a purely prudential warning that the blamer invites exposure or retaliation; (2) a claim about the blamer's credibility — that his blame carries less evidential weight, or that he applies to others a standard he exempts himself from; (3) a claim that blame is an act with felicity conditions, akin to commanding or promising, and that hypocrisy deprives one of the standing to perform it; (4) the strongest reading, on which the hypocrite's blame is not merely weakened but void, so that the target is entitled to disregard it entirely.

For each reading, tell me whether it is plausible, whether it does any real work beyond the prudential warning, and whether it would actually license my aunt's move of dismissing my uncle's rebuke outright rather than merely undercutting it. Then say which reading people who deploy this proverb to end an argument most likely intend, whether or not they could articulate it. Finally, address the point on which my uncle and my aunt divided: supposing the substance of his rebuke was correct — the debt was real, the refusal was wrong — does any reading that survives that fact still leave the hypocrite's blame with some force, and what exactly is lost when the rebuke is delivered by him rather than by the brother who was stiffed?
