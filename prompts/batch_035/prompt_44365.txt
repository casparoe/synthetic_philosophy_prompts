So, for context: I'm a novelist, and the book I'm working on is narrated by a retired Go professional who watches the 2016 AlphaGo–Lee Sedol match on a waiting-room TV while his daughter is in surgery. I did a bunch of homework for that, and somewhere along the way I fell into a rabbit hole that has nothing to do with the novel anymore, and now I want someone to straighten me out about it. The rabbit hole is this: I came across Daniel Dennett's ladder of creatures — Darwinian creatures are hardwired by evolution and can only learn the slow way, by dying; Skinnerian creatures can adjust by trial and error in their own lifetime, but they have to actually try things and eat the consequences; Popperian creatures can pre-test candidate behavior internally, so their bad hypotheses die instead of them; and Gregorian creatures (named after the psychologist Richard Gregory, I think) get handed thinking tools by others, so they can import learning that happened in other lifetimes.

Here's the case I want run through that ladder, with the facts laid out since I'll assume you know them anyway: AlphaGo was bootstrapped on tens of thousands of human professional games, then improved by playing millions of games against itself, and during the five games in Seoul its weights were frozen — no learning between or during the games. It won 4–1. Move 37 in game 2 was the shoulder hit on the fifth line that the human pros put at roughly one in ten thousand, and if I remember right Sedol got up and left the room for a while after it. Then in game 4 Sedol played the wedge (move 78) that alot of commentators called the god move of the match — the one human move the machine misjudged — and AlphaGo's own self-assessed winning probability reportedly cratered after it answered badly. So the mapping questions: is AlphaGo at the board even Popperian at runtime, or is a frozen policy network plus a very fancy tree search more like a fixed Darwinian instinct with a good internal previewer? Where did the hypothesis behind move 37 actually die — in training, where billions of wierd lines got tried and discarded at zero cost to the deployed system, or in the match itself? And does the bootstrapping on human games make it Gregorian in some sense, a creature that starts life by inheriting a whole culture's worth of already-tested ideas? Same questions for Sedol: was move 78 Popperian in a way the machine that week could not be, given that his private, free preview was something the frozen network had no equivalent of?

And then, honestly, tell me where the ladder breaks — I want this to run both directions, the taxonomy illuminating the match and the match testing the taxonomy. My editor, who is smarter than me about this stuff, suggested that the four kinds should really be read as answers to the question of where the information in a creature's behavior comes from — evolution, individual reinforcement, internal simulation, culture — and that a trained artifact scrambles that ordering because it has several lifetimes stacked up: the self-play run, and the deployment, and the inherited human corpus. If she's right, the taxonomy was built for creatures with one lifetime each, and AlphaGo is exactly the kind of case it never anticipated. Does that seem right to you, or is there a reading of Dennett under which the ladder still sorts AlphaGo cleanly? I don't need you to settle what Dennett himself would say — I want the analysis of the match, and then a straight account of what the match does to the ladder, including where you'd be forcing the theory to speak when it's really silent. And no reading lists or citations please, I can't use them while drafting anyway and I'd rather have your own careful reasoning.
