I'm building the next entry for my satire dataset, and this one is about whig history — the style of history-writing where the past gets narrated as a slow, inevitable march toward the present, every era a rough draft of us, contigency politely deleted, and whoever won was obviously always going to. The piece should be a museum audio guide, around 600 words for the guide itself, in the bland, soothing voice of a real audio tour, for a fictional museum I'm calling the Museum of Inevitable Progress. Format it as a series of numbered stops ("Stop 1", "Stop 2", etc.), roughly 6-8 stops, with placid transitions between them like "Please proceed to the next gallery." The narrator is completely sincere: every exhibit is an earlier, less successful attempt to be the modern visitor. The alchemists are chemists who simply hadn't gotten around to being born yet; a plague room is narrated as "an unfortunately slow few centuries" for the story of us; the Scientific Revolution gets treated like a scheduled milestone that the era is to be commended for hitting on time.

The humor should come from getting the historiography right, not from silly voices — the piece exaggerates real features of whig history rather than inventing them: presentism (past people scored by how much they resemble us), retrospective winner-takes-all (whatever survived was the fittest idea, full stop), the pruning of accidents and dead ends as though they were filing errors, the assumption that the present was the telos history was aiming at all along, and a quiet apology whenever the past fails to advance. Someone who's been dragged through actual national-history museum panels or textbook prefaces should wince in recognition, and someone who knows the debates around whig history (Butterfield's complaint, presentism, hindsight bias, the way popular history flattens contingency into destiny) should see each of those land without the guide ever stating any of them. Absolutely no winking at the reader — the narrator genuinely believes it, that's the deadpan, and she should never say "little did they know" as a joke because for her it isn't one: she thinks they did know, deep down, or should have.

One idea I had for a stop, take it or leave it: somewhere in the middle the guide has to admit that the audio for an exhibit on a genuine historical accident is unavailable "due to recording difficulties" and asks visitors to instead enjoy the adjacent panel on inevitability. Feel free to come up with better bits along those lines. For the ending, I keep going back and forth — maybe the final room is just a mirror labeled "The Future's Waiting Room," or the guide cheerfully directs everyone to the gift shop where "Progress: You're Welcome" tote bags are sold, but honestly you decide, whatever ending lands best. Just keep it deadpan all the way through, and don't let the guide accidentally sound wise or melancholy about it — the whole point is that the temptation to write the past as progress toward the present feels completely reasonable from the inside, and the reader should catch herself nodding along at least once before she notices what she's nodding at.
