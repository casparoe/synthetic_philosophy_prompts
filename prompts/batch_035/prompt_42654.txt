I write long-form features on demographics and social change for a general readership, and I keep circling a debate that seems to have frozen in place. On one side are the popularisers who treat Millennials and Gen Z as genuinely different kinds of people, with distinct values, work habits, and psychological profiles shaped by formative events — the recession, smartphones, and so on. On the other are the critics — mostly academics and statisticians — who say 'generations' are arbitrary buckets, that the boundary dates are post hoc, that the differences attributed to cohorts are usually just age effects or period effects, and that the whole discourse is a marketing invention. Every few months a new study or a new debunking appears, and yet neither side moves an inch. As a journalist I'm less interested in who is right than in what kind of disagreement this actually is, and I want to work that out before I commit several thousand words to a piece.

Let me lay out how the dispute looks from where I sit, so you can diagnose it properly. The sceptics have what looks like a decisive formal point: in any dataset, age, period, and cohort are linearly dependent — know two and the third is fixed — so attributing an observed difference to 'cohort' rather than a mixture of age and period effects is an identification problem, not just a matter of better data. Yet the pro-generation side doesn't seem rattled by this; they respond that the problem afflicts the statistical machinery, not the concept, and that historians have sensibly talked about cohorts marked by shared formative experiences for a century without needing regressions. Meanwhile, I've noticed the two sides routinely talk past each other: when the sceptics say generations aren't real, they seem to mean something like 'not valid inferential units', while the defenders seem to mean something like 'culturally salient social groupings with real causal consequences once people believe in them' — closer to how one might talk about national identities or even money. It occurs to me that a parallel dispute about whether biological species are real kinds ran for decades and partly dissolved once people distinguished different claims about 'reality' hiding under one word.

So here is what I want from you, and I want it as a diagnosis rather than a verdict on generations. First, is this disagreement substantive, verbal, or a case of two groups answering different questions under one heading — and if the latter, say precisely what the two questions are? Second, how much of the impasse is the age-period-cohort identification problem doing the work, and how much is a prior, non-empirical conviction on each side (roughly: that kinds of people are always constructed, versus that historical imprinting is real)? Third, is the 'once people believe in them, they become real' move — that generational labels are self-fulfilling — a genuine dissolution of the dispute, a sneaky change of subject, or the beginning of a genuinely interesting third position? Fourth, and most important for my piece: what, if anything, would count as progress here — a conceptual distinction the sides could accept, a standard of evidence for cohort effects, longitudinal data of some particular shape — or should I be honest with my editor that this is a dispute about the legitimacy of a vocabulary, and that no finding would move it? Please give me your reasoning in each case, and be candid if you think the question is ill-posed rather than merely unresolved; I'd rather know that before publication than after.
