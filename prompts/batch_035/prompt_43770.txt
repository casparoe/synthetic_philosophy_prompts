Every year I sit through our company's anti-bribery compliance training (the kind built around the US Foreign Corrupt Practices Act and the UK Bribery Act), and this year it finally got under my skin. I work in sales ops at a mid-sized industrial exporter, so I actually have to apply these rules, not just nod along. Concretely, the rulebook works like this: there's a dollar cap per gift to a government official — think tens of dollars, not hundreds; anything above a threshold needs pre-approval and gets entered in a gift register; hospitality is fine only if it's modest and customary for the industry; there's language about not doing anything that could even appear to improperly influence an official; and the whole regime is wildly stricter for public officials than for private-sector buyers, even though the same company will happily spend real money on dinners, sports tickets, and years of relationship-building with a private procurement manager.

A few things bug me. The cap seems to say that what separates a gift from a bribe is size — but a modest bottle of whiskey delivered at the right moment during a tender seems like it could do more corrupting work than a generic conference dinner costing ten times more, so the threshold can't really be tracking the danger. The register treats corruption as a series of discrete, recordable events, while everyone in sales knows obligation gets built up relationally, over years, without any single gift crossing a line — which is, I gather, exactly what the old anthropology of the gift (Mauss and people following him) would predict: gifts create binding ties precisely because they aren't priced transactions. And the training also just asserts, without argument, that the same line applies in Jakarta as in Ohio — my anthropologist friend claims that in a lot of the world, the very idea of logging a gift from a personal contact in an official register is what looks bizarre and cold, maybe even itself a kind of betrayal.

So here's what I want from you. What theory of corruption — of what makes an exchange a bribe rather than a gift — does this rulebook actually presuppose? I can see several candidates it might be leaning on: bribes as transactions that purchase a specific official act (so diffuse, unspecific gift-giving is fine); public officials as fiduciaries for an impersonal public, which private buyers aren't, hence the asymmetry; or the appearance-of-impropriety language pointing at a third view where the wrong is erosion of public trust in institutions regardless of whether any decision actually gets bent. Please structure your answer as follows, in this order: first a short section called "The implicit theory, stated plainly" (about 200 words) where you commit to the single best reconstruction; then "Do the parts cohere?" (about 250 words) on whether the caps, the register, the appearance standard, and the public/private asymmetry all presuppose the same thing or pull in different directions; then "What the rules could survive losing" (about 250 words) on whether the rulebook is philosophically underdetermined — i.e., whether several rival theories of the gift/bribe line would license the exact same rules, so the rules don't strictly need whichever theory they seem to fit best; and finally "If relational capture is the real story" (about 200 words) on what would actually have to change in the rules if compliance took seriously the idea that the danger is cumulative relational obligation rather than discrete recordable transactions — would caps, registers, and the public/private split survive at all? Don't bother citing statutes or cases from memory; just reason about the practice as I've described it.
