I've just been appointed to the allocations committee of a small health foundation that must, each quarter, divide a fixed budget among three kinds of interventions: pain management at a hospice we operate, mobility restorations such as hip replacements and prostheses, and preventive pediatric care. Our working rule is prioritarian — benefits to the worse off count for more — and I have come to see that this rule presupposes interpersonal comparisons of well-being at several points at once: whose level is lower, whose gain from an intervention is larger, and by how much. Before we commission anyone to build a scoring model on top of these comparisons, I want to know what an account of interpersonal utility comparisons actually has to deliver before it can be trusted with any of this.

So write me the desiderata. I want a requirements list for an adequate theory of interpersonal utility comparisons, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and a clear indication of which requirements are non-negotiable for my purpose — comparisons good enough to rank budget options under a prioritarian rule, not to say that anyone's life contains some number of utils — as against which requirements are demands of one camp rather than genuine constraints. At minimum, the list should take a stand on: the difference between comparing levels and comparing increments, and which my purpose actually needs; whether we require comparability all the way up to a common cardinal unit, or only enough structure to rank gains and their ratios; what the theory should say about self-reports of intensity, given that patients describe their pain on scales that seem idiosyncratic in ways that matter to the budget; whether the account can be sensitive both to momentary intensity and to the shape of whole lives, since a hospice patient and a child getting preventive care are not being compared at a single moment; and the two cases that keep surfacing in our meetings — a small relief granted to very many versus an agony spared to one, and the patient who insists her persistently aching back is the worst thing in her world while another with the same condition rates it mild and asks us to spend on someone else. If you suspect that some pair or subset of the desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied — in particular whether the objectivity my prioritarian rule needs can coexist with the individualist constraints that drove economists to ordinalism and skepticism about comparison in the first place — say so plainly, because that is the part I most need to know before we spend money on a model. Then, in no more than a short paragraph, tell me how three candidate pictures fare against your own list: a hedonic common-scale view, an extended-preferences view in the Harsanyi style, and a view that rests content with rough ordinal judgments. Keep the whole thing under 900 words; I will read it on the train to the next meeting.
