I sit on the governing board of a small state school that has run into a budget shortfall, and I find myself unable to articulate why a proposal before us feels wrong. I would appreciate a careful philosophical analysis of the situation, and I ask that you write your answer in British English.

The proposal concerns how to spend our limited discretionary funds. One option is to preserve a specialised one-to-one support programme that currently serves three pupils with unusual learning profiles; each of these pupils is, by all accounts, flourishing in it, and their parents describe it as transformative. The alternative, championed by our head teacher, is to dissolve the programme and redistribute the money across the school: better software licences, a modest library refresh, and slightly smaller class sizes in several year groups. Roughly two hundred pupils would each benefit a little from this.

The head teacher's argument is straightforwardly arithmetical. Three pupils suffer a large loss, but two hundred pupils each enjoy a small gain; add everything up, and the gains outweigh the losses several times over. She adds that we could soften the blow with a token gesture — a small voucher for the three affected families, say — and that refusing her proposal amounts to letting a sentimental attachment to a vivid few outweigh the diffuse interests of a great many. When I pressed her, she said that the school is a community, and that a community's resources should go wherever they do the most total good.

I am not convinced, but my objections keep coming out muddled, and my fellow governors are tiring of my vague unease. The best I can manage is something like: the three pupils are not a *part* of some larger thing that feels the benefit; they are simply three children who will each, individually, be worse off, and no other child's small improvement seems to *belong* to them in a way that could make up for it. The head teacher replies that this is special pleading — that every cost and benefit lands on somebody or other, and that refusing to compare across people just irrationally privileges concentrated harms over diffuse ones.

Is there a principled way to defend my unease, or is the head teacher right that it is sentimentalism? If there is a real distinction at stake here, please explain what it is, how it would bear on our decision, and whether it commits us to saying that the interests of the many should *never* outweigh the interests of the few, or only under certain conditions. I would find it helpful if you could also indicate what a reasonable compromise position might look like, since we must vote on the proposal next month.
