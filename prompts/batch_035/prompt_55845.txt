As a retired physical chemist who has lately fallen into the history and philosophy of science, I have become fascinated by the fact that the man who gave us the elliptical orbit also cast horoscopes for a living, and — this is the part that grips me — defended astrology on grounds that look strangely like a physics argument. From what I can tell, Kepler insisted that astrology be treated as a natural science: he wanted a physical mechanism, something like light or force emanating from the planets, and he proposed an archetypal-geometric basis in the angles. And on that same basis he threw out most of the apparatus: the zodiac signs were a human convention, the houses were arbitrary, but the aspects — the angles — were real because geometry was real.

I would like a satirical piece built on this. Write an in-universe document: a professional standards memo or a peer-review report from Kepler's own era, addressed to practicing astrologers, laying out which parts of their art are to be retained and which discarded, in the voice of a man who is simultaneously the most rigorous critic astrology has ever had and its last true believer. The jokes must come from the actual structure of Kepler's position: a reformer who keeps the phenomenon while discarding nearly every practice attached to it; a man who demands a physical mechanism and a mathematical harmony where his colleagues are content with tradition; someone who complains that the signs are conventions of the zodiac while insisting, with complete seriousness, that a sextile is a fact about the universe. Let there be absurd specific commitments played straight — the memo should treat an aspect such as the trine as having the epistemic standing of a laboratory constant, while dismissing a house system with the contempt one reserves for an uncalibrated instrument. Somewhere in it, a deadpan line about how the moon, being nearest, must exert the strongest influence, which is why astrologers should take more care with it. And a closing administrative note in which the author, having dismantled most of the discipline, worries about his own income from horoscope-casting.

Roughly 500 words. The piece should be fair to Kepler: a specialist reading it should recognize that the author understands why Kepler held that geometry could be causally efficacious, and should feel that the satire lands on the practitioners rather than sneering at the man. Please do not invent verbatim quotations from Kepler or attribute specific sentences to his actual works; paraphrase his positions rather than putting words in his mouth.
