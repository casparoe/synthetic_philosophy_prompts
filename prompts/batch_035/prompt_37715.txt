I run enrichment programs at a mid-size sanctuary (mostly rescued ex-farm animals, some native wildlife that can't be released, and a handful of exotics people illegally kept and then surrendered). I've been reading about Dennett's four grades of mind -- Darwinian creatures that only "learn" by dying (selection over generations), Skinnerian creatures that learn by trial and error during their own lifetime, Popperian creatures that can pre-test hypotheses in their heads before acting, and Gregorian creatures that use tools and mind-tools like language to restructure their own thinking. My thought is that this ladder is basically an enrichment-matching framework in disguise: what counts as good enrichment for an animal depends on what grade you think it operates at. A puzzle feeder that requires imagining a solution before trying it is worthless (or worse, stressful) for an animal that can only learn by bumping into things repeatedly, and a Skinnerian-level foraging setup is insulting under-enrichment for something that plans.

What I want is a procedure my team (three keepers, varying experience, and me) can actually run when we write or revise an enrichment plan for a species or individual. Concretely:

1. A classification procedure: an ordered list of questions/tests that sorts an animal into one of the four grades (or says it's genuinely ambiguous), using only what we can observe in daily husbandry plus cheap controlled-ish setups we can rig from stuff we already have. It should say what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, where the procedure stops and just assigns the more conservative grade, and where it flags "this is a judgment call, Sarah has to decide" instead of pretending it's mechanical.

2. An enrichment-matching rule: given a grade, what kinds of enrichment are appropriate, what kinds are wasted money, and what kinds are actively harmful (I'm thinking about frustration and learned helplessness here, but tell me if that framing is wrong).

3. Honesty about failure modes: where the grade system itself breaks down. E.g., does learning during an individual's lifetime shade continuously into "testing hypotheses in the head" in a way that makes the Popperian/Skinnerian line somewhat arbitrary? What about animals that are Gregorian about one thing and Darwinian-ish about everything else? Tell me what the procedure can't do.

Then run it on three cases we actually have:

- Our ravens, who are obviously the smartest animals here and get the most elaborate enrichment -- I suspect we're mis-assigning them and either over- or under-shooting.
- Our bearded dragons. My board president keeps pushing for expensive "problem-solving" enrichment for them because it looks good on our social media, and I have a feeling the procedure is going to say something we both won't like, whichever direction that is.
- A batch of battery hens new to us, where we genuinely don't know what they can and can't do because the literature on chicken cognition is thin or I just can't get at it, and we need a plan next week.

Keep it usable by someone who hasn't read any Dennett -- if a distinction doesn't survive translation into keeper language, tell me whether I can drop it or whether dropping it guts the whole idea. Also, if you think the four-grade ladder is the wrong framework for enrichment entirely and something else from animal cognition would do the job better, say so, but only after giving me the procedure I asked for.

(also, small thing, but if any of my statements about Dennett above are wrong or sloppy, correct me -- I read this stuff on weekends and probably mangled something.)
