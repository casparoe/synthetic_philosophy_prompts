The exam question read:

"Newton argued that the bucket experiment shows that rotation cannot be treated as motion relative to other bodies, and concluded that space exists absolutely. (a) Reconstruct the experiment and the inference Newton draws from it. (b) Set out the relationist case against absolute space as Leibniz pressed it in his correspondence with Clarke, together with the substantivalist's replies. (c) Explain Mach's critique of Newton, and evaluate whether Einstein's general relativity resolves the original dispute."

The script, transcribed as written:

"Newton's bucket experiment is his central argument that not all motion is relative. A bucket of water is hung from a cord; the cord is twisted many times and the bucket released, so that it begins to spin. At first the water remains at rest and its surface is flat; the bucket then drags the water around by friction, and the water climbs the sides, the surface becoming concave, highest at the rim and lowest at the centre. The striking fact, on Newton's telling, is that the deformation of the surface tracks the water's rotation relative to the bucket: the wall of water stands highest at the very moment the water is spinning fastest relative to the bucket's walls, and the effect is absent whenever the water is at rest relative to the bucket. Newton concluded that no motion relative to the other bodies in the scene can explain the deformation, since the only candidate relative motion — that between the water and the bucket — is thereby exhausted. The rotation must instead be referred to absolute space itself: the water rotates relative to space, and space is shown to be a real, absolute quantity. In this way the bucket experiment refutes relationism. If no relative motion can account for the deformation, relationism has no account of rotation at all, and the doctrine falls.

"Leibniz was thus compelled, in his correspondence with Clarke, to explain the concavity by appeal to the fixed stars: the water is, strictly speaking, rotating relative to the distant masses, which serve as the true frame of reference. But his deeper arguments were independent of the bucket. From the principle of sufficient reason he argued that God could have no sufficient reason to place the universe here rather than three feet to the left; and since a universe so shifted would be indistinguishable, the identity of indiscernibles makes it the very same universe. So there is no fact about where the universe is, and absolute space, which would supply such facts, cannot exist. Clarke replied that God could have willed the one location rather than the other by the mere choice of his will, and that no further reason was required.

"Mach later objected that Newton had not proved what he claimed. At most, the experiment shows that the rotation is not rotation relative to the bucket; it does not show that it is not relative to anything at all. Mach proposed that inertia is produced by the distant masses, so that in an otherwise empty universe, containing only the bucket and the water, the water would climb no walls: rotation relative to nothing is meaningless.

"Einstein's general relativity vindicated Mach and, through him, Leibniz. In general relativity gravity is geometrized and the metric determines which motions count as inertial, so rotation is, after all, rotation relative to the distant masses. With general relativity in place the bucket experiment is settled in Leibniz's favour, the absolute space Newton postulated can be discarded, and the dispute between substantivalism and relationism is over: relationism won."
