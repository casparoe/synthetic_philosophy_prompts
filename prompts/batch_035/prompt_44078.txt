I have been reading a popular book on the relationship between science and religion, and one chapter offers a two-paragraph digest of Einstein's views on religion and free will, roughly as follows: Einstein was a believer, as his famous remark about God not playing dice makes plain; the God he believed in was essentially the God of conventional religion minus the miracles, and his oft-cited admiration for Spinoza was just a way of saying the universe was created by a rational intelligence. He preferred to call himself an agnostic, but mainly to avoid offending believers; his real position was friendly to faith, and he saw science and religion as converging on the same truths, which is why he liked to say that science without religion is lame. On free will, the book says Einstein's physics settled the matter for him: relativity treats time as a dimension in which the future is as fixed as the past, so our choices cannot be open, and Einstein drew the practical consequence that moral responsibility is a fiction and criminal punishment therefore unjust. The chapter closes by claiming that Einstein's determinism and his religiosity were two sides of one coin — God as the lawgiver whose laws bind even our choices — so that Einstein stands as a great example of the book's thesis that the scientific worldview, followed to the end, leads back to religion rather than away from it.

I don't know enough about Einstein's philosophy to audit this myself, though I will say the picture feels tidier than I'd expect from anything involving Einstein. I'm inclined to think the dice remark at least shows he was some kind of theist, and that if he called himself an agnostic it was largely tact. Please go through the summary claim by claim and separate what an Einstein scholar would accept, what they would wince at as an oversimplification, and what they would say is simply misattributed or wrong — paying particular attention, first, to whether the dice remark functions as a confession of belief at all, and whether there is a real difference between denying a personal God and endorsing Spinoza's God; and second, to whether Einstein's denial of free will actually rested on his physics rather than on older philosophical commitments, and whether it led him to draw the practical consequences the book attributes to him about responsibility and punishment. Finally, tell me whether the book's closing claim — that Einstein's science leads back to religion — follows from whatever in the summary survives your audit, or whether the summary has to be corrected in ways that would undermine it.
