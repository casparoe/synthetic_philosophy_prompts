**Why the Born rule isn't a problem for Everett (and why that's kind of a problem)**

1. **The setup.** In the many-worlds interpretation every outcome of a measurement happens, in some branch. So what can it even mean to say that one outcome has probability 0.99 and another 0.01? Everything occurs with certainty, in the plain sense. This is the standard accusation: Everettians can explain why observers *see* statistics roughly matching the Born rule, but not why anyone is *entitled* to expect those statistics in advance. I'll call this the epistemic gap, as opposed to the older in-principle problem, which I take to be basically solved: decoherence plus limit theorems gets you an emergent quasi-classical branching structure with stable relative frequencies. That part is genuinely impressive and I won't relitigate it.

2. **The two standard responses.** The decision-theoretic program, associated mainly with David Deutsch and later David Wallace, says probabilities in Everett are what a rational agent's preferences over branch outcomes commit you to: if you accept a handful of axioms of rational decision theory, the only weighting consistent with them is the squared amplitude. The self-locating uncertainty program, associated with Sebens and Carroll, says that right after a branching event you're uncertain which future branch-person you are, and a principle of indifference over branches delivers the Born weights. Critics of both (Adrian Kent is the one I have in mind) reply that the axioms are doing the work the theorem is supposed to do — you get squared amplitude out because you put branch-weighting assumptions in at the ground floor.

3. **My move: indifference over splitting, not over branches.** Here's the bit I actually want feedback on. I want to argue the indifference approach works, but only if you apply it at the right level. You can't be indifferent over the branches as such, because branches with unequal amplitudes aren't symmetrical — indifference between non-symmetric options isn't rationality, it's just a mistake, and this is where I think the original presentations were most vulnerable. But consider the branching process itself. When a measurement splits the wavefunction, you can always decompose any branch into sub-branches of equal amplitude (amplitude-splitting is just a change of description in the Hilbert space, not a physical operation). If you demand indifference only over an *equal-amplitude* partition, the squared-amplitude weights come out arithmetically: a branch with amplitude 0.7 is indistinguishable, from the inside, from a bundle of equal-amplitude sub-branches with total weight 0.49, and indifference over equal pieces gives you the squares. So the Born rule isn't an extra postulate or a consequence of decision theory; it's what indifference means when the objects you're ignorant about are amplitude-equal. The reason this is "kind of a problem" (title payoff) is that it makes the Born rule analytic rather than empirical — no experiment could ever have disconfirmed it, which honestly strikes me as too cheap.

4. **Where I worry.** I half suspect the equal-amplitude decomposition is where I'm cheating, since nothing in the physics singles out that description as the one uncertainty should attach to — the whole apparatus is only defined up to the choice of decomposition, and I'm choosing the one that gives the answer I want. Also not sure my use of "indistinguishable from the inside" survives the fact that decoherence picks out a preferred (coarse-grained) branch basis, which is a *physical* fact and seems to cut against treating sub-branch decompositions as merely descriptive. And I'm probably not being fair to Wallace, who I think would say the decision-theoretic route never needed the indifference principle in the first place.
