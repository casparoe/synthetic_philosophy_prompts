I've now taught the Malcolm–Dennett dream debate three years running in my phil of mind seminar, and every year I notice the same thing: the students can reproduce both arguments perfectly and then walk out holding exactly the opinions they walked in with, and honestly so do I. So let me lay it out. Malcolm says dream reports can't in principle be independently checked — you're asleep, and by the time you can report anything, you're no longer asleep — so the idea that dreams are experiences occurring during sleep isVerificationism run amok; on his view, all the sentence "I dreamt I was falling" does is record something about how you woke up and what you're disposed to say. Dennett comes back and says no, dreams are events in the brain, they're just not necessarily when we think they are: for all we know the narrative gets composed at the moment of waking up and reporting, like a novel dashed off to order, and the clock-time dream would be an empirical question we'd need theory and data to answer, not something armchair argument can settle.

And here's what bugs me. Since the 70s we've actually gotten some of the data Dennett said we'd need — lucid dreamers signaling from inside REM with pre-agreed eye movement patterns, for god's sake, which looks like about as direct a check on "dreaming while asleep" as you could ask for — and yet nobody declares victory and the papers keep coming. Malcolm's side just reinterprets, Dennett's side just asserts the obvious.

So that's my actual question and I want a diagnosis, not a verdict on who won. Is this a substantive empirical disagreement that happens to be hard, or a verbal one about what "experience" means? Is the real fight over what counts as data — whether a dream report is evidence of an event or just the event itself? Is Malcolm quietly answering a question about concept-use while Dennett answers one about neurophysiology, with the word "dream" papering over the gap? I have a hypothesis, which I'll state as a hypothesis: the disagreement is about the evidential status of first-person report, and no amount of eye-movement signaling could touch it because both sides just slot the new data into whatever they already believed about reports. Tell me whether that's right, and if it is, tell me what would actually count as progress here — a conceptual distinction, a further experiment, reframing the question, or nothing, and why nothing. If you think the debate is ill-posed rather than merely stalled, say that, but you have to show me where the two positions stop disagreeing, concretely, not just wave at "verbal dispute."
