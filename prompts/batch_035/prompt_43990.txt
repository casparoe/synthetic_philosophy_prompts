Write the official minutes of the monthly meeting of the Elm Court Residents' Association, in the dry, devoted voice of a committee secretary who takes accurate record-keeping very seriously. Format them as numbered agenda items, ending with a routine note about refreshments. Hard limit: 550 words, and keep to it.

Some background, so the target is clear. At last month's meeting, a member's nephew — a philosophy graduate student — gave an informal talk distinguishing epistemic bubbles from echo chambers. A bubble omits outside voices: members simply never encounter the other side, and the cure is exposure — go seek out the excluded sources. An echo chamber, by contrast, has already heard of the other side and has been trained to distrust it: outside sources arrive pre-discredited, defectors are recoded as corrupted rather than as bearers of news, and exposure cures nothing, because the machinery for explaining away outside testimony survives every encounter intact.

The comedy of the minutes should come from the fact that the committee has diagnosed the association as a bubble when it is, unmistakably, an echo chamber — and that every remedy it votes through presupposes the very discrediting machinery it claims to be dismantling. Along the way, without the secretary ever noticing the irony, the record should show things like:

- a motion to invite an outside speaker, immediately amended so that the invitation goes only to speakers who "won't just repeat what the newspapers say";
- the members' unanimous agreement that they are unusually open-minded, arrived at unanimously;
- the Hendersons, who moved away last year after a dispute about the parking rota, discussed entirely as a case of contamination, the minutes making clear that at no point did anyone ask what they thought;
- an epistemic diversity initiative whose criterion for an acceptable outside voice is that, on reflection, the voice agrees with the association;
- and, as the closing item, the finding that exposure to the other side has left everyone more confident in what they already believed — recorded as evidence that the initiative worked.

The secretary stays deadpan throughout. The minutes never state the point, nobody in the room realizes anything, and the reader is never winked at. A reader who knows the literature on bubbles and echo chambers should find that every joke exaggerates a real feature of echo chambers rather than inventing one; a reader who doesn't should still recognize a plausible portrait of a self-satisfied committee. Above all, keep the piece internally consistent: the association believes every word of its own minutes, including the parts that contradict each other. No more than 550 words.
