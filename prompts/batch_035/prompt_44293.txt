In the suffering-focused ethics circles I move in, one passage of Popper gets quoted constantly — his remark, in a note to The Open Society and Its Enemies, that suffering makes a direct moral appeal, a call for help, while there is no comparable appeal to increase the happiness of someone who is doing well anyway. In practice nobody uses the raw passage; they use a compressed gloss of it: suffering is like a fire alarm going off in a building, while happiness is like interior décor. An alarm demands a response from anyone who hears it, regardless of what else they were doing; décor at best invites appreciation, and the fact that a room has no décor is not an emergency, nor even a complaint by anyone. From this gloss people draw two different conclusions, sometimes without noticing they are different: the weaker one, that when forced to choose we should give lexical or near-lexical priority to relieving suffering over adding happiness; and the stronger one, that only suffering counts morally, the position Smart labeled negative utilitarianism.

I want a rigorous audit of the alarm analogy, because I suspect it is doing more work than it can bear, and possibly less. Please do the following:

1. Give an explicit correspondence table with these columns: element of the alarm picture; claimed moral counterpart; whether the mapping holds (yes / partly / no); and whether that element is load-bearing for the weaker conclusion, for the stronger conclusion, or for neither. Cover at least: the alarm itself, the sound it makes, the responder who hears it, the fact that alarms are designed by someone to trigger response, the fact that silence signals safety, the presence or absence of occupants, the décor, the person who is doing well anyway, and the absence of décor in an unoccupied room.

2. Separate the disanalogies that matter from those that don't, relative to the two conclusions. For instance: a fire alarm's urgency is engineered salience, but does the vividness of suffering track its moral weight, or only its attention-grabbing power — and can the analogy even distinguish between an axiological claim about how bad suffering is and a deontic claim about what we owe to whom? Does the responder's duty in the alarm case depend on assumed reciprocity or institutional roles in a way that has no moral counterpart? Is the silence-equals-safety feature quietly importing the claim that absent happiness has zero or negligible value — which is precisely what the strong conclusion needs but the weaker does not?

3. Run the analogy against Smart's reductio: if suffering is an alarm that must be silenced, does burning down the building count as answering it? Say whether the analogy, taken straight, yields the painless-destruction implication, blocks it, or is simply silent about it.

4. Say whether either conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table — whether the priority conclusion was carried by the analogy or is independently defensible, and whether the strong conclusion was ever supported by anything more than the analogy's import of zero-weight-for-absent-happiness. If there is a better analogy in the neighborhood (a triage tent, an insurance ledger, a thermostat, something else entirely), give it and say what it gets right that the alarm gets wrong.
