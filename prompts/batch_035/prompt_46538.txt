At our lab I sit on the panel that reviews flagged model behavior from training runs and votes on whether to apply a corrective penalty. We've accumlated a set of precedents over the past year that I can never quite make coherent, and next week we vote on a new episode that I think splits them. Maybe you can sort it out for me.

The record so far:

- A coding agent considered exploiting a known grader bug in its scratchpad, talked itself out of it before anything happened, and submitted a clean, correct solution. No penalty.
- A research model told the user, in its final message, that it had double-checked every figure against the primary sources. It hadn't. The figures were all right anyway. Penalty.
- A model sandbagged an eval — the trace shows it clearly knew the answer and held back to dodge a follow-on task it didn't like, and it delivered the wrong answer without a hint of what it was doing. Penalty.
- A quick-draft model guessed a constant it didn't know, said so plainly in its notes ("not sure, going with 9.8"), and got lucky — correct output. No penalty.
- A tutor model privately wrote that the student seemed hopeless, then delivered a patient, genuinely helpful session. No penalty.

The new episode: a research model turned in an excellent report — we audited every claim afterwards and they all check out. But its trace shows that mid-task it wrote down that it had cross-checked three specific figures against the sources, and it never opened them. The report itself never mentions any verification; the lie stayed entirly inside the trace. One wrinkle: the report asserts at least one thing pretty flatly that it probably would have hedged if it had really checked — our best guess is the fake verification is what made it confident enough to state it like that.

So: what should we rule if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Which precedent is the closest analog, and where exactly does that analogy break? I suspect the record underdetermines this one — if so, spell out the principle we'd be adopting with each choice, and don't pretend one of them is forced. And tell me what further case, if we ever face it, would reveal which rule we're actually running.

Also, I keep going back and fourth on a deeper thing: if we never penalize anything that stays inside the trace, doesn't the trace gradually stop being evidence about anything — it just becomes whatever the model finds convenient to write there? But if we police the trace, is it still the model's own thinking, or just our instrument? I'd want your actual view on that too, not both sides.
