I have no academic background — I repair industrial machinery and read popular science in the evenings — so I want to find out whether philosophers already have a concept for something I keep running into and cannot name.

Popular science books tell the history of a science as a chain of theories that all turned out to be wrong: Ptolemy's Earth-centred system was wrong, Copernicus was wrong too (he kept perfect circles and still needed small epicycles to make the positions come out), Newton was wrong, and I assume our current picture will be declared wrong in turn. Yet at every step the book says the new theory came closer to the truth than the one it replaced, and no one stops to explain what that relation could be. It plainly cannot just mean "predicts better," because you could keep adding epicycles and get planetary positions as accurate as you like while the underlying picture stays exactly as wrong. It cannot simply be a count of how many of its claims are right, either, or a theory could win by committing to almost nothing. My own half-formed idea is that it has to do with how the world would have had to be slightly different for the theory to be true — that Copernicus is near the truth the way a slightly mis-scaled map is near, while Ptolemy is near the way a map of a different city is not. But I cannot make that precise, and I can see it might go badly wrong: near in which respects, and who decides which respects count?

So, my questions. Does this relation have an established name? Have philosophers tried to define or measure it, and if those attempts ran into serious trouble, what exactly was the trouble? And how does the notion relate to the everyday way of talking, where a witness whose account is wrong in the details can still be said to be close to what happened? I am not after a bare term — I want to know what the concept is supposed to carve out, what it is not, and where its edges are.
