A couple of historians/philosophers of history I know have been arguing with each other for over a decade, and they finally agreed to run an adversarial collaboration and produce one joint statement at the end of it. I want you to write that document. First, the setup.

One of them — call his side the Lock-In camp — is a committed path-dependence theorist, in the vein of Brian Arthur's increasing-returns economics and Paul David's old story about QWERTY: small early contingencies (which keyboard layout happened to ride along with early sales, which video format got the movie-studio deals, which species happened to crawl through a mass extinction) get amplified by positive feedback, and after that the alternatives are locked out even when they'd have been just as good or better. At that level, history is a pile of frozen accidents, and the final state tells you almost nothing about which option was "best" — the winners were early, not superior. He leans on Gould's thought experiment of rewinding the tape of life and replaying it, expecting radically different outcomes each time.

The other — the Attractor camp — is a convergentist, closer to Simon Conway Morris's response to Gould: given roughly similar starting materials and pressures, things converge. Whatever variant wins, the deep structure of outcomes — which functions get fulfilled, roughly which kinds of institutions, technologies, and organisms exist — would come out much the same in a rerun. On this view most "lock-ins" are convergence wearing a local costume, and the apparent contingency dissolves as you zoom out.

You are a professional philosopher of history with a serious background in evolutionary theory and the economics of technical change, and you've moderated enough of these collaborations to know where they go off the rails. Think carefully and do some brainstorming before you write anything — the hard part here is wording, not content. They've accused each other of strawmanning for years, so every sentence has to be phrased so the side it's about would actually put their name under it.

Here's what the document needs:

1. A numbered section of claims both camps can sign. Not vacuous ones ("history is complicated") — claims with real content, including about the famous cases: what they now agree the Liebowitz–Margolis critique of the QWERTY story got right and wrong, what VHS/Betamax does and doesn't show, and what they agree about convergence in biological evolution. They should also commit to a distinction neither of them had drawn clearly before the collaboration: token-level lock-in (which particular variant wins) versus type-level lock-in (whether the function gets fulfilled by some variant at all), and both should sign a claim that much of their old shouting traded on sliding between these two levels.

2. A table of the remaining disagreements. Each row must be worded so the side it describes would sign it. It should cover at least: what "the same outcome" has to mean for a rerun to count as convergent (they part ways on the granularity — same token, same type, or just same structural role); whether lock-ins have a half-life or are permanent (the Lock-In camp splits internally on this — feel free to put the split in the table); and whether the attractors in history are wide basins or narrow ridges.

3. The cruxes — aim for about three, subquestions such that settling any one of them would move at least one camp. For each crux, give one concrete case, thought experiment, or argumentative move that both sides would agree bears on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance. One crux I suspect belongs on the list: whether contingency at the token level can be genuine while being causally inert at the level that matters — but use your own judgment.

4. A closing section on which parts of the residual dispute they now consider verbal rather than substantive, and which are genuinely open — and if no discriminating test exists for some crux, the statement should say so honestly instead of inventing one.

Also: dont pad the agreed-claims section to make the collaboration look more productive than it was. If they only ended up with six real points of agreement, six it is.
