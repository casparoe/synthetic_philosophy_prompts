I've been asked to give the opening remarks at a daylong workshop jointly run by our university's philosophy department and its journalism school, titled "The Public and Its Machines." The idea behind the workshop is to put Kierkegaard's critique of the press and of "the public" into conversation with present-day anxieties about feeds, comment sections, and pile-ons, and the organizers decided the tone should be set by an opening speech rather than a keynote lecture. The audience is genuinely mixed: roughly half the room will be Kierkegaard scholars who will wince if I flatten his account into a generic complaint about social media or turn him into a premature media-studies critic, and the other half will be working journalists and editors who have never opened Either/Or, who use the word "public" to mean the actual readers and viewers they serve, and who have sat through enough conference keynotes about "the death of truth" that they tune out at the first hint of hand-wringing.

Please draft the speech. It should do real structural work with the Kierkegaardian material rather than name-drop it: the distinction he draws between an age of passionate engagement and one of passionless reflection; the public as an abstraction that no actual individual person is, yet which everyone feels themselves addressed by; leveling, and his claim that leveling is driven by envy, which he treats as the negative principle of the age; and his image of the press as a kind of pet that the public keeps for its own amusement — which is far nastier than it sounds and should be unpacked, not just recited. I'd also like one concrete episode anchoring all this: the Corsair affair, where a satirical Copenhagen weekly spent months turning Kierkegaard himself into a running caricature until he was jeered in the streets, which gives the speech a way to connect his theory to the experience of being on the receiving end of an attention machine. Please weave in the extension to the present (the algorithmic feed as an even more efficient instrument of leveling, since it requires no editor at all) without pretending Kierkegaard predicted any of it, and without implying that his answer — the single individual before God — is a workable editorial policy, though it deserves to be stated honestly as what he actually offered. A couple of practical constraints: do not put anything in quotation marks and attribute it to Kierkegaard unless you are certain of the wording — paraphrase instead, and signal clearly when you are paraphrasing; and the speech must not condescend to either half of the audience, meaning the journalists should come away with something they can actually think with in their newsrooms, not just a Humanities Lecture Moment. Please keep the whole speech under 700 words, and aim for something that reads well aloud rather than like an essay being read out.
