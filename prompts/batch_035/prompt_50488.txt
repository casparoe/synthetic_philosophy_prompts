I'm drafting a statement of my views on free will and moral responsibility for a seminar paper, and before I submit it I'd like a consistency audit of the package. Here is the set of commitments, in no order of importance:

1. I have always taken the principle of alternative possibilities for granted: a person is morally responsible for an action only if he could have done otherwise.
2. Nevertheless, Frankfurt-style cases persuade me. In the usual setup, Black waits in the background with a means of forcing Jones to vote a certain way, but will intervene only if Jones shows some prior sign of wavering; Jones never wavers, votes from his own convictions, and Black never acts. My verdict is that Jones is fully responsible for the vote even though he could not have done otherwise. So I take the principle of alternative possibilities to be false as a universal principle.
3. What really matters for responsibility, I believe, is the actual sequence: the action must issue from the agent's own values and character, without anyone interfering. Where the actual sequence is clean, the absence of alternatives costs nothing.
4. At the same time, I hold that covert character-engineering undermines responsibility. If a person's values were shaped from childhood by a manipulative sect, or more fancifully by neuroscientists at birth, then when he later acts from those values, with no intervention at the time, he is not responsible for the action, even though the action flows from what are, by then, genuinely his own values.
5. I am a compatibilist about determinism: in a deterministic world with no intervener and no manipulator, ordinary agents remain responsible for what they do.
6. Finally, I accept an asymmetry. Frankfurt-style cases convince me that blame does not require alternative possibilities, but I am not convinced the same holds for praise. Credit for an achievement seems to require that failure was possible: an act of generosity performed in circumstances where the benefactor could not possibly have done anything else strikes me as not an achievement at all, and so not really creditable.

I don't know where the trouble lies, and I have no prior attachment to any one of these — do not assume I would sooner give up one than another. Please go through the set and tell me: which pairs are genuinely in tension, and how badly; which pairs only look to be in tension, and why the appearance dissolves; and, for each genuine tension, what would have to give and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set. Order the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally serious, and finish by saying which single revision would clean up the most. Then close with one final line of the form \answer{...} containing only the number of the commitment whose revision you recommend, or the word "none" if you judge the set consistent as it stands.
