so my roommate and i got absolutely locked into this argument last night, like 11pm to 2am, about whether you can "cancel" (his word, not mine) historical figures. it started with churchill somehow, then columbus, and it ended with him doing the whole "you can't judge people in the past by today's standards, he was a man of his time" thing and me saying that defense is doing way more work than it can actually do. and then this morning i kept thinking about it and honestly i think the argument is less a principle and more a service? like an actual product people deploy selectively. so i want you to satirize that.

write me a glossy marketing brochure, maybe 600-700 words, for a fictional law firm slash PR agency — call it something like "Temporal Mitigation Associates" or whatever, you pick the name — that sells the "man of his time" defense as a legal/pr package. pricing tiers, testimonials from satisfied clients (invented, obviously, can be figures themselves or their "estates"), an faq, small print. deadpan corporate voice throughout, like they're genuinely proud of their work.

the important part: the jokes have to come from the actual philosophical problems with the defense, not just generic "history is bad" humor. specifically i want at least some of these worked in, ideally more:

- the defense proves too much. contemporaries DID object — las casas was condemning the treatment of indigenous people while it was happening, abolitionists were active during slavery, so "he didn't know any better" quietly assumes nobody at the time knew better. maybe the brochure's fine print admits the client was in fact criticized in his own lifetime and the firm "does not guarantee the nonexistence of contemporaneous dissent."
- the asymmetry. people only reach for this defense for some vices and not others — nobody says "he was a serial killer, but you have to remember, everyone in his era was a serial killer." the firm should visibly be worse at defending some charges than others, and the copy should never acknowledge why.
- the selection effect on who gets defended. the firm's client list should have a suspicious shape — the defense shows up for people we already admire or want to keep admiring. nobody runs the defense to rehabilitate some random medieval nobody, and maybe not for figures the client already dislikes.
- constitutive moral luck. the whole package quietly depends on the idea that being born into a worse moral environment excuses you, which is a genuinely hard philosophical position, and the brochure should treat it as settled boilerplate.
- the difference between explaining and excusing. the firm should blur these constantly and then have one clause in the small print that accidentally states the distinction clearly.
- the "ahead of his time" problem: if the era gets credit for the good stuff ("he was progressive for his time") but the era takes the blame for the bad stuff, something's double-booked. the brochure should absolutely do this and never notice.

also, if you can, give it a tier where the defense gets stronger the more you admire the client's other achievements — the "great man bundle" — since that's how it always seems to work in practice ("but he won the war" as a discount on everything else).
