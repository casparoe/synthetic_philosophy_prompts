For the past year the philosophy of language working group I help run has been stuck on one point, and I've started to suspect the stuckness itself is the philosophically interesting fact. The issue is Wittgenstein's family-resemblance claim about "game": that there is no feature common to all games, only overlapping similarities that crisscross and fade. The counter we keep meeting is the one raised in the sixties, most famously in Maurice Mandelbaum's 1965 article on family resemblances and generalization, and it goes roughly like this: Wittgenstein looked only for surface, intrinsic features; once you allow relational or higher-order features — say, that games are all activities in a certain manner, or all connected in some way to goals, rules, or non-serious engagement — a common feature turns up after all, and the claim that none exists is an artifact of an artificially narrow notion of what counts as a feature. Our Wittgensteinian replies that this is either vacuous (the proposed feature is so broad it sweeps in non-games) or a disguised summary of the resemblance network rather than something that could be used independently to pick the games out. The objector replies that nothing could ever be used "independently," since every feature is identified through the instances in the first place, and that if this is disqualifying, the family-resemblance thesis has become unfalsifiable — anything can be shown to lack a common feature by banning the right kind of feature. We have gone around this circle at least six times over the year and nobody has budged, which is why I'm writing.

I don't want a verdict on who is right. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement. My working hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis: it is largely verbal — a dispute about what is admissible as a "feature" (monadic and observable versus relational and higher-order) that both sides mistake for a dispute about the facts of English usage, and the facts of usage are not actually in dispute; everyone at the table agrees on which things we call games and on the pattern of similarities among them. But I can see a rival diagnosis: that it is substantive but ill-posed, because the two sides are answering different questions under one heading — the Wittgensteinian answering a psychological question about how the concept is actually extended (without a rule we can state), the objector answering a semantic question about what, if anything, our usage tracks in the world. If that's right, no further examples will help, because each side's examples are answers to its own question. So tell me: which story fits what each side is actually saying? If the disagreement is part verbal and part substantive, where exactly does the verbal part end and the substantive part begin? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a principled criterion for admissible features, a way of making the "vacuous versus informative" line non-arbitrary, handing the question to psychology or corpus semantics, or prying the two questions apart explicitly? Or, alternatively, tell me plainly that no progress should be expected here, and explain what the sixty-year persistence of this circle tells us about the question being asked.
