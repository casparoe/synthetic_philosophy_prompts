A student in my philosophy of cognitive science seminar brought me an argument about the frame problem that I've been chewing on all week, and I can't cleanly point to where it breaks. Her version, roughly: (1) The frame problem, in its epistemological dressing, is the problem of deciding which of your standing beliefs a new piece of information leaves untouched — without rechecking the entire stock. (2) Saying that new information q leaves belief p untouched is a counterfactual claim: had q not come in, p would still be fine to go on using. (3) Counterfactuals like that aren't settled by logic alone; they need substantive assumptions about how things persist and what's causally independent of what. (4) Any such assumption can in principle be defeated by further facts — that's just non-monotonicity. (5) An assumption that is defeasible but nonetheless relied on is an inductive generalization: it projects from past cases where things of this sort made no difference. (6) So solving the frame problem just is solving the problem of induction. (7) The problem of induction, at least as Hume left it, has no non-circular solution. (8) Therefore there is no non-circular solution to the frame problem, and every AI project that presuposes one is building on air.

Here's what bothers me about it: taken seriously, the argument doesn't just sink GOFAI. My dog reorients when the gate swings open but keeps fetching the same ball, my undergraduates revise their beliefs about the exam date without re-verifying that Paris is in France, and nobody thinks the dog has thereby dissolved Hume. If (8) is right, though, then any finite system that navigates novelty at all — dog, thermostat with a halfway decent control loop, me grading essays — is either running a non-circular solution to the problem of induction or failing to be rational, and both horns look silly. That's exactly the "proves too much" smell I want my students to learn to notice, but noticing it isn't diagnosing it. My own hunch is that something slips between (5) and (6) — between needing inductive assumptions to get by and solving the problem of justifying them — but a colleage of mine swears the real weak spot is (2), on the grounds that "leaves untouched" needn't be read as a full-blooded counterfactual at all. So: which step do you reject first, and what does rejecting it cost you? I want the price spelled out, not just a flat "that one's false" — if you give up (2), say what the weaker reading of frame-relevance buys and where it runs out, and if you give up (5) or (6), say whether the repaired argument still threatens anything for AI or loses all its teeth.

Please organize your answer around the numbered steps, and end with a final line in the form \answer{step N} naming the single step you'd reject first.
