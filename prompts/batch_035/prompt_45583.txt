My roommate is a philosophy grad student, very into later Wittgenstein, and lately she's taken to ending any conversation about religion with the line "it's not a hypothesis, it's a form of life." The first time it came up I'd been telling her about my aunt, who's a devout Catholic and prayed hard before my cousin's risky surgery, and afterwards told everyone the prayers worked, the surgeon was "guided." I asked my roommate whether the belief had been confirmed, or would have been disconfirmed if he'd died on the table, and she said the question "doesn't even arise" because religious belief isn't in the prediction business. Then last week our friend (an atheist) started going through the usual evidential arguments against religious belief, and she shut him down with the exact same line. That struck me — she used it against both of us, and I can't tell whether that's one move or two different moves wearing the same words.

So what I want is: what are the distinct precise claims "it's not a hypothesis, it's a form of life" could actually be making? I can imagine a spectrum. At one end something pretty trivial, like "people don't usually adopt religion by weighing evidence," which seems true but way too weak to justify refusing to answer either of us. In the middle maybe something like "religious language has a different grammar — it's not used to state propositions that could be true or false," which would explain the "doesn't arise" response but sounds like it might be false, or at least like my aunt IS asserting something when she says the prayers worked. And at the radical end something like "faith can't be rationally evaluated at all, from any standpoint," which would dismiss my atheist friend but surely can't be right, because people do lose their faith for reasons, sometimes good ones, and it would make religious conversion or deconversion unintelligible.

Please lay the readings out as a list I can actually use next time she says it, and for each one say (a) is it plausible, and (b) does it support dismissing my question about my aunt, my friend's evidential arguments, both, or neither? And if you think the strong reading is what people who repeat this line usually mean, say so — I'd also like to know whether the readings that survive scrutiny are the ones that can do any work in a conversation, or whether once you precisify it the slogan basically collapses into either a platitude or a falsehood. Oh and one more thing that's been bugging me: if the middle reading is right and my aunt's utterance isn't a propositional claim, what was she doing telling everyone the surgeon was guided? That looks like a claim about the world with a causal story attached. Does the slogan have an answer for her case specifically, or is her case exactly where it starts to wobble?
