Analyze the collapse of footbinding in China as a test case for competing explanations of how immoral practices end. The salient facts: footbinding persisted for roughly a millennium despite periodic imperial prohibition attempts and centuries of moral criticism within China itself; yet in the 1890s, reformers founded anti-footbinding societies whose members made a double pledge — not to bind their own daughters' feet, and not to allow their sons to marry women with bound feet — and the practice largely died out within a generation, amid reformist anxiety about China's standing among industrialized powers. Run this episode through both Kwame Anthony Appiah's honor thesis from The Honor Code (2010), on which practices end when they come to seem degrading by the participants' own internalized honor code, and Gerry Mackie's convention account (American Sociological Review, 1996), on which footbinding was a self-enforcing marriage-market equilibrium that fell only once coordinated pledges dissolved the interdependent expectations holding it in place. Map the episode carefully onto each: who the relevant "players" were, what changed their incentives or their self-conception, and which explanation covers the double pledge, the timing of the collapse after the failure of bans and arguments, and the role of national humiliation versus domestic marriage prospects. Then say where each account is silent — what neither honor-shift nor coordination can explain — and, running in the other direction, what the case shows about whether moral revolutions are ever driven by moral argument as such. If you think the two accounts are ultimately competing descriptions of one mechanism rather than rival explanations, defend that; if not, tell me which one the footbinding case favors and what evidence from the episode would have come out differently under the other.
