As a journalist covering science and ideas, I've been asked to host a participatory session at a public ideas festival: sixty minutes, roughly twenty-five adult attendees with no philosophy background, a room with movable chairs, and a few festival volunteers I can press into service. The theme the programmers want from me is Newcomb's problem, and rather than lecture about it, I want the audience to actually face it — to stand in front of the boxes with a decision to make, and to discover from their own mouths that the two habits of reasoning they use everywhere else in life give opposite answers here. My worry is that every public treatment I've read just describes the puzzle and then reports, as though settled, that you'd have to be a fool not to take both boxes; I'd rather the room generate the tension than me assert it.

Please design the session end to end. The "predictor" must be real for the room, not hypothetical: I need a workable mechanism by which the opaque box's contents are actually fixed in advance by a prediction made about each individual chooser, with the prediction being genuinely better than chance — a pre-session questionnaire, an earlier staged choice in the same hour that the choosers don't realize is diagnostic, discreet observation by the volunteers, or any other scheme you can make concrete. Payoffs should use festival scrip redeemable at the festival bookshop rather than cash, so the stakes bite without me handling money; specify amounts, materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, and the instructions I read aloud, and arrange things so that the dominance-style argument for taking both boxes and the expectation-style argument for taking only the opaque one each get voiced by participants before I name either principle in the debrief.

That debrief should map specific moments in the session onto the two lines of thought — the one that says the money is already sitting in the boxes and your present choice can't reach backward to change it, and the one that says the room's own tally shows which kind of chooser walks out richer — and then tell me honestly what the activity cannot reproduce, above all the stipulated near-infallibility of the predictor: my hit rate will fall well short of the near-certainty the classic thought experiment assumes, and I want to be upfront with the audience that the sharpest form of the argument lives inside that gap. Predict what will go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. Finally, since I am a journalist and will get letters if I editorialize, please separate throughout what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this: in the debrief, do not present either one-boxing or two-boxing as the consensus view, and say plainly that the underlying dispute between causal and evidential ways of treating choice — the one philosophers have been having since Robert Nozick put the problem in print in 1969 — remains open, so that if the audience leaves split, I can tell them that's not their confusion; that's the finding.
