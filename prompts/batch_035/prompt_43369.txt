i'm a final-year student taking a political philosophy course this term, and my essay topic (assigned, not chosen — thanks, seminar lottery) is state apologies for historical wrongs. here's the pattern that keeps bugging me. Governments apologize in the first person plural for things no living member of that government did: the Australian PM in 2008 apologized to the Stolen Generations, Canada's PM the same year to residential school survivors, etc. Yet the officials issuing these apologies almost always add that no living person bears personal guilt for any of it — German presidents are the classic case, we inherit responsibility but not guilt. And on top of that, the exact wording gets treated as hugely consequential: Tony Blair in 2006 expressed deep sorrow for Britain's role in the slave trade while conspicously refusing to say the word apology, reportedly because lawyers worried a formal apology would strengthen reparation claims; and Rudd paired his apology with an explicit insistence that it did not entail compensation. So the same practice seems to (a) treat the state as an agent that can own acts from generations back, (b) deny personal guilt for anyone now alive, and (c) still take the word apology seriously enough that legal liability supposedly turns on it.

My tutor told me to think about who exactly is the author of the apology — the office or the officeholder — but that just moved the question for me rather than answering it. So here's what I want from you: what theory does this practice actually presuppose? Is it the view that the state is a corporate agent persisting across generations, so the apologizing entity literally is the one that wronged? Or the view that present citizens inherit duties of repair without inheriting guilt? Or something about the office as a continuing moral person? And crucially — can a strictly individualist view, where the state is just a sum of persons and nobody now alive did anything, license the practice at all, or does it quietly require the corporate-agent view? Also tell me whether (b) is genuinely in tension with (a) or whether the guilt/responsibilty distinction is doing exactly the work needed to make them consistent.

Please actually commit rather than laying out the options for me to pick: state which theory the practice presupposes, and if you think it's underdetermined, say which theory it fits best anyway and why that one. Then say what would have to change in the practice if the presupposed view were swapped for strict individualism — would these apologies collapse into mere statements of regret, or survive in some deflated form? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble and no restating my question back at me.
