I'm a novelist who works in historical fiction, and my fourth book — a Napoleonic-era novel I intended as a study of logistics — has been reviewed, without exception, as a study of the transformative power of small accidents, because a subplot about a misrouted shipment of boots appears on page 312. My publisher would like a craft essay for the paperback edition, in the tradition of those self-serious author's notes that explain a novelist's "method" to book clubs. I have decided to give the public exactly what it apparently wants.

Write that essay for me: a deadpan author's manifesto, roughly 800–900 words, titled and signed by an invented novelist of my stripe, arguing that the historical novel is morally obliged to attribute every major event to a progressively smaller contingent object. The doctrine should be presented with complete sincerity and escalating rigor: begin with the old nursery rhyme about the lost nail and the lost kingdom as founding scripture; lay down numbered rules of craft (no army may be defeated by an idea; no revolution may be caused by a class; each chapter must pivot on an object strictly smaller than the last — a horseshoe, a bootlace, a lentil, a mote of dust); include a boxed exercise for the aspiring writer, in the manner of a workshop handout, asking them to locate the trivial cause of some well-known historical turning point and then to locate the still smaller cause of that cause, and then to keep going.

The humor must come from getting the historiography right rather than laughing at it. The manifesto should, without ever admitting it, run the real problems to their absurd conclusions: the slide from triggering causes to standing conditions (a misdelivered letter may precipitate a war, but so may the existence of two armies facing each other, which is awkward to hang a chapter on); the infinite regress the method generates, since every nail itself requires a blacksmith, a forge, and an economy, which the essayist must handle by rule rather than argument; the selection effect by which we only hear about the nails that happened to matter; the retrospective fitting of counterfactuals, which the essayist should treat as effortless and self-evident; and the quiet disappearance of human agency, so that by the final pages the manifesto's author is assigning motivations to lint. Somewhere near the end, allow him a single unearned lyrical passage about the beauty of a world in which no one is responsible for anything, phrased so that a reader can't tell whether he finds this consoling.

Constraints: the voice is pompous, tender toward his own corpus, and entirely without self-awareness — no winking, no authorial rescue; the irony must stay legible entirely through the escalating content. Do not name or parody any living historian or novelist; the target is the practice of pop-contingency history, not a person. You may allude, once and without quotation marks, to the tradition of great-man historiography and to the Russian novelist who spent an entire war's worth of essays attacking it, provided the essayist misunderstands that tradition in a way that is itself the joke. A historian of contingency should finish the piece feeling the essayist's method is the reductio of a position they half hold, not a caricature of it; a general reader should simply find it funny and a little vertiginous.
