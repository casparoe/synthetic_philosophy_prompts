I've been around the rationalist blogs since the Overcoming Bias days, so I've read my share of invocations of Aumann's agreement theorem — the result that two agents with a common prior and common knowledge of each other's posteriors cannot assign different probabilities to the same event. The usual takeaway in that corner of the internet is that disagreement between people who've talked it out is evidence that somebody is being irrational, or lazy, or insincere. I've become suspicious of how frictionless that inference is, and I'd like you to help me locate my doubt with an actual number and an actual decomposition rather than vibes.

Here is the proposition, stated so it has a truth value: the agreement theorem has genuine normative force for realistic interlocutors — that is, if two honest, intelligent, self-aware agents disagree about some proposition after communicating as much as they care to, the theorem gives us reason to fault at least one of them. My credence that this is true is about 0.3; a friend of mine who was deep into the old LessWrong sequences puts it at 0.75. I want your number, but more than that I want the decomposition. Off the top of my head it should include at least the following terms, and you should add or subtract terms as you see fit. First, the probability that the common prior assumption is a defensible idealization rather than a sleight of hand — in particular whether priors can be cleanly separated from disagreements about which hypotheses are even in the model space, which to me look like the disagreements that actually matter. Second, the probability that common knowledge of posteriors is something finite agents can ever achieve, versus merely common p-belief for some p short of 1 — Monderer and Samet's approximate version of the framework — and whether the p-belief approximation leaves the theorem's conclusion intact in any useful sense or whether the room it opens up is exactly where real, persistent, rational disagreement lives. Third, the probability that the complexity-theoretic follow-up work, showing that agreement requires only modest communication between Bayesians, closes the gap in practice rather than just in the limit. Fourth, whatever term you think belongs for the worry that the theorem applies to the boring cases, where everyone already agrees on the question being asked, and not to the interesting cases, where the dispute is about how to formulate the proposition at all.

For each term give a rough probability and a one-line justification, then show me how you combine them into your final credence. Then tell me which term you are least confident in and what evidence or argument would move it the most. And finally, tell me whether you think my 0.3 and my friend's 0.75 trace back to a disagreement about one of these terms in particular, or whether we're probably disagreeing about something I haven't put on the list. Please write your answer as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way you'd write a careful email, not a slide deck.
