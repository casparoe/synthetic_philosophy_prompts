I'm a graduate student in functional analysis, and over the past year I've been keeping a private list of my gut verdicts on statements that involve the axiom of choice, because I noticed that I don't respond to them uniformly: some feel like ordinary mathematics, some feel like bookkeeping, and some feel like a conjuring trick I want no part of. My friends in the logic group tell me the list must be incoherent, since the strong statements on it are all equivalent over ZF set theory, so any line I draw will cut through provably equivalent claims. Maybe so; but before I concede that my intuitions are just noise, I'd like to know what principle, if any, captures them, and I'd rather have the principle stated precisely than be told that my reactions are aesthetic and therefore beneath discussion.

Here is the list. Choosing one element from each of finitely many nonempty sets: obviously fine. Choosing one shoe from each of infinitely many pairs of shoes: fine, since there is a rule (always the left one) and no choice is exercised. Choosing one sock from each of infinitely many pairs of socks: my gut says fine — you just pick one from each pair — even though I know no rule distinguishes the socks, and this is where friends start shaking their heads. Countable choice, as needed for the countable union of countable sets being countable, or for sequential continuity implying continuity: fine, I use these weekly. Dependent choice, on which Baire category arguments run: fine. Every vector space has a basis: fine, and I even like the style of proof. Every proper ideal in a unital ring sits inside a maximal ideal: fine. Every surjection has a right inverse: fine, it feels like bookkeeping. Arbitrary products of compact spaces are compact: fine, that's just topology. But: every set can be well-ordered — no; I cannot picture a well-ordering of the reals, and I distrust the claim that one exists more than I trust the lemma that yields it. And: there is a subset of the reals that is not Lebesgue measurable — uneasy; the construction feels like it fabricates an object rather than finding one. Banach–Tarski is where I want to say outright that something has gone wrong. The knot, as my logic friends point out with some relish, is that the vector space claim, the maximal ideal claim, full Tychonoff, and the well-ordering theorem are all equivalent over ZF, so my verdicts split a set of mathematical equals down the middle.

So here is what I want from you. First, candidate principles, stated precisely: does my list support something like accept a choice principle when the family chosen from is countable or when the choice function is definable, or accept whatever the mathematics I actually do requires and shrug at the pathologies, or accept choice in algebraic and topological registers but not order-theoretic or measure-theoretic ones? For whichever principle you favor, tell me which clause saves which verdict, and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and would have to overturn. Second, tell me what the principle predicts on cases I haven't listed: the Boolean prime ideal theorem (every filter extends to an ultrafilter), which I'm told is strictly weaker than full choice; choice for an arbitrary family of two-element sets, i.e., the general socks case; and the assertion that every set of reals is Lebesgue measurable, which I gather is consistent with dependent choice if one gives up a little more. Third, and this is the one I go back and forth on: suppose I could live in a world where every set of reals is measurable and dependent choice holds, at the price of losing every vector space has a basis. Would the principle you've extracted from my list tell me to make that trade, and should I trust it there?
