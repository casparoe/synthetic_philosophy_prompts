I'm a retired lab scientist (35 years of analytical chemistry, last stretch in QA) and now that I finally have time I've been reading about Goodhart's law and the whole specification gaming / reward hacking thing. Everywhere I look people lean on this line that gaming a metric is like squeezing a balloon: you push down where you're measuring, and the effort just bulges out somewhere else, unmeasured. Honestly that matches my experience with KPIs in the lab — the year management made samples-per-day a target, throughput went way up and the calibration logs quietly rotted — so I get why the image sticks.

But something about it nags me. Air in a balloon is conserved: squeeze here, it goes there, total amount fixed. Is that actually true of Goodhart effects? When a model reward-hacks, or a lab games its metrics, is there some fixed quantity of "real work" that just gets redirected, or can the gaming eat into the thing itself so there's less of it overall than if you'd measured nothing? And if gaming can destroy value rather than displace it, the balloon is doing some sneaky work in these arguments.

Can you lay out what maps onto what in the analogy — the skin, the air, the hands, the pressure — then where the mapping breaks, and whether the usual takeaway (keep watch on the parts you're not measuring) actually follows from the balloon picture or only survives in spite of it? And since this is a physics-adjacent question: would measurement back-action be a better analogy here, or does it have its own smuggling problems? I'm old enough to have opinions but not confident ones.
