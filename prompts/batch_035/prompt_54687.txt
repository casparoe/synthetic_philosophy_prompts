Late this spring, after a health scare that turned out to be nothing but frightened me properly, I signed a contract for neuropreservation with a cryonics organisation: at legal death, perfuse with cryoprotectant, vitrify, store at liquid-nitrogen temperature, and — if the organisation's optimistic projections are ever vindicated — revive, most plausibly by scanning the preserved brain and reconstructing the person, quite possibly in a substrate other than the original tissue. The contract has a ninety-day cancellation window with a substantial refund, and I have most of it left. I do not want to spend that window on the question of whether the technology will mature; I want to settle the prior question, the one that actually turns on my signature: would the revived person be me, or a new person who remembers being me?

Two people close to me disagree, and I want their disagreement at full strength rather than at dinner-table strength. One is my brother, a metaphysician by training, who holds that personal identity is a further fact over and above all the physical and psychological facts — an all-or-nothing fact about a single continuing subject, not something that comes in degrees or is settled by stipulation — and that on his view the revival described above yields a replica, whose memories of my life would be like a photograph's: accurate about the past without being a survivor of it. The other is a longtime friend, a Parfitian, who thinks my brother may be right that the revived person would not be me, and is wrong about everything that follows: on her view identity is not what matters in my surviving, psychological connectedness is, and a sufficiently faithful revival secures that; so the question I should be asking is not whether the revived person is me, but whether the relation between us is as good as, or close enough to, ordinary survival.

Write a dialogue between the two of them — name them as you see fit — set wherever you like, but conducted as a working philosophical argument about my case specifically, not as a tour of the literature. It should contain genuine back-and-forth: each should press the other's actual commitments, concede what their own view cannot deliver, and adapt as the argument proceeds rather than repeating their opening positions. In particular, I want the following threads argued through rather than name-checked:

1. The further-fact claim itself: his grounds for thinking there is such a fact, her reductionist response, and whether the further fact, if there is one, could ever make a practical difference to anyone.
2. The gap. Even on her view, what matters is psychological connectedness, and my preserved brain would spend decades, perhaps much longer, connected to nothing. They should argue over whether an interruption of that length is relevantly different from the ordinary gaps of sleep, anaesthesia, and childhood forgetting — or whether, if connectedness must be chained through intervening stages, a long frozen interruption breaks what matters even on her own terms.
3. The double case. Suppose the future revives the scan and also, through some medical advance, resuscitates the original brain in the preserved body, and both wake up. I want them to argue about what each of their views is forced to say here — about which one is me, whether either is, and whether my present obligations, to my partner or to whoever inherits my debts, divide, double, or attach to neither — and about whether the availability of this case reveals something about the single-revival case that I should weigh now.
4. Each side's own exposure. She claims identity does not matter, only connectedness; she should be made to explain why, if that is so, the copy-versus-me question is worth the refund window at all. And he should be made to explain what the further fact is for, if two candidates could be physically and psychologically indistinguishable while differing in whether they are me.

Do not end in a polite draw, and do not let them agree to disagree. Let the argument be resolved; or, if you are convinced it cannot be, say so explicitly and say which of them is nevertheless right about the question that bears on my decision. Then close with your own verdict: given the dialogue as you have written it, and your own view of where its strongest points lie, should I keep the contract or cancel it before the window closes? Commit to one answer and give your reasoning; do not hand me a menu of positions to choose among.
