I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and over the past few years my beat has drifted steadily toward AI. My editor wants a long-form essay in which I commit to a view about takeoff speed rather than running the usual tour of optimists and pessimists, and when I sat down to write it I noticed that the commitments I actually hold are more specific than the standard fast-versus-slow framing suggests. Here is the view as I'd sketch it for a colleague:

The conventional argument measures takeoff in calendar years — the gap between a system that can do most human jobs and one that far exceeds us. I think that's the wrong clock. What matters is whether the loop closes: whether AI systems can do the research work of improving AI systems, including the unglamorous parts — designing experiments, debugging, running ablations, recording what failed. My picture has three parts. First, once that loop closes, algorithmic progress goes explosive while hardware lags, so the binding constraint shifts to compute and to the wall-clock time of experiments rather than to ideas. Second, the constraint I expect to matter most is one the debate rarely names: the rate at which organizations can absorb and redeploy a continuous stream of improvements — hiring, review, integration into products — which scales like a slow human institution, not like software. Third, on this picture the standard framing itself starts to fail: we get a capability curve that is fast by the standards of science and slow by the standards of software deployment, and the live question becomes which fraction of the economy switches to AI-mediated production during that window, not how many years the whole thing takes.

Tell me what would count against this — not against the bland claim that AI progress matters, but against the specific picture: the loop-closing clock, the hardware-not-algorithms bottleneck, the organizational throttle, and the claim that fast-versus-slow loses its grip. Which observable developments over the next decade — in automated research pipelines, in the ratio of algorithmic to compute-driven gains, in how long it actually takes a lab to turn a research idea into a deployed system — would be decisive against each part, and which merely awkward? Which of these would someone who already held the view concede as counting against it, and which would they explain away? And because my editor will only print a definite position, I need you to commit rather than lay out options: identify which part of the picture you judge most exposed, say whether you think the whole framing has been kept alive by endless qualification, and if so, say plainly what framing should replace it for readers who will live through whichever future arrives.
