third year phd student in econ here (decision theory group), and my advisor keeps telling me to "figure out what you actually believe before you write the chapter." so I did the thing you're supposed to do: for the past year, whenever I caught myself reacting to a bet or a choice where the probability was vague, I wrote the reaction down. now I have a list of my own verdicts and no idea whether one rule generates them or whether im just a mess. here's the set:

1. classic Ellsberg-style setup. one urn, 50 red and 50 blue; another urn, 100 balls, red/blue in unknown proportions. $100 on red, same price either way. with the known urn I'll bet down to about a 48% chance of winning; with the unknown one I want at least 50-50 before I'll touch it. fine, ambiguity aversion, expected.

2. but it flips for losses. say a court fines me and offers a choice: pay $100 now, or pay a randomly drawn amount between $0 and $200. I take the draw without even thinking. and I notice I don't rush to replace vague-with-precise in insurance contracts either, the way the urn case made me run from vague. so: avoid ambiguity in gains, kind of seek it in losses.

3. tie-breaks, and this is the one that bugs me. two experimental drugs, both free, both described only by an interval: one works with probability somewhere in [0.5, 0.7], the other somewhere in [0.5, 0.9]. identical worst case. I take the tighter interval every single time. but on the fine side, a draw between $0 and $200 vs a draw between $100 and $200 (also identical worst case), I take the wider one, more hope of $0. in gains I want the interval narrow, in losses I want it wide, holding the worst case fixed. my advisor muttered something about worst cases but the worst cases are equal here, so that cant be it.

4. information. if a clairvoyant offered to tell me the exact composition of the unknown urn before I bet, obviously I want to know. I genuinely cannot construct a case where finding out more leaves me worse off, assuming I can always ignore what I learned. this one feels like it has to be true, almost as a rationality axiom.

5. stakes. at even odds on the unknown urn I'll bet $5 all day, either side, dont care. I will not bet $500 at even odds on that urn without something more to go on. the aversion only kicks in once it starts to matter.

so: is there a rule, or a family of rules, that generates all five? Ive tried checking them against the standard candidates and each one fits some verdicts and cant even see others — the worst-case-everything rules go blind on 3 and 5, the permissive ones have nothing to say about 1.

what I want from you, specifically: (a) state the principle precisely enough that I could mechanically derive my verdict on any new case, not just vibes; (b) tell me what it predicts for two cases I havent listed — a small-stakes bet that's favorable at my best guess but negative at the worst case, and whether I'd pay a small fee to NOT be shown the result of a partially informative signal before a large ambiguous bet settles; and (c) be honest about which of my five verdicts you had to throw out to get the rest to fit, and whether the survivors are stable or whether patching one breaks another. if the five turn out to be jointly unsatisfiable, thats actually a result I can use in the chapter, so dont paper over it — tell me which subset has to go and whats the cheapest subset to drop. definately dont just reassure me that im consistent, I pretty strongly suspect im not.
