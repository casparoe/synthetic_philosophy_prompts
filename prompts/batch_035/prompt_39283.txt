I run communications for a public university system, and my team spends a surprising amount of time walking administrators back from statements that are "technically true but..." — we don't say layoffs are happening while a plan is being drafted, we say a dean "is not under investigation" because the paperwork hasn't been opened yet, stuff like that. We have no shared standard for when this is fine and when it's not, so it comes down to whoever's in the room feeling squeamish or not.

You're a philosopher who works on assertion, deception, and the ethics of speech — really think this through before answering, maybe brainstorm a bit first. Write me a decision procedure my communications officers can actually use when someone senior asks us to word something carefully. Ground it in the lying vs. misleading distinction (the idea that what you say can be true but exploit what the listener will wrongly infer), but make it operational: the questions to ask in order about a proposed statement, what counts as a red flag at each step, where the procedure says no, and where it has to hand the call back to a human because it's a values question not a technical one. It needs to be runnable in fifteen minutes during a media cycle, by people with no philosophy background. Also flag honestly where the procedure will misfire or can be gamed by someone smart enough to follow it in letter but not spirit.

Then run it on three cases so I can see it work: (1) a spokesperson who is asked whether any complaints have been filed against a professor, and there are several informal complaints but nothing in the formal system, and the spokesperson says "no formal complaints have been filed"; (2) a press release about a hospital expansion that leads with the jobs it will create, while burying that a clinic for low-income patients is closing as part of the same project; (3) a statement that "the president has full confidence in the athletic director" when the president has in fact decided to fire him and is only waiting until after the weekend game. Make sure one of these is a case where the procedure gives an answer I won't like — I suspect I'm part of the problem here, and I'd rather know.
