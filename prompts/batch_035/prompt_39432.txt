So my brother-in-law, who took a couple philosophy courses in college and never lets anyone forget it, cornered me at Thanksgiving about Quine's gavagai thing. I'd actually read a bit about it myself after seeing it mentioned in a book about language I picked up, but his version went like this: Quine imagines a field linguist among some remote tribe, a rabbit runs by, the native says gavagai, so the linguist writes down rabbit. But Quine's insight is that the linguist can never really be sure, since all the observable evidence is equally consistent with translations like undetached rabbit part or rabbit-stage or rabbithood, and no matter how many conversations the linguist observes, the data can't settle it. From this Quine concluded that translation between languages is impossible in principle, and more than that, that meaning itself is a behaviorist fiction -- just stimulus and response, nothing underneath. My brother-in-law added that Chomsky basically demolished the whole argument decades ago with the poverty of the stimulus, and that the only reason gavagai seems like a puzzle at all is that Quine arbitrarily handicapped the linguist by forbidding him to point at things, ask questions, or use any context beyond raw sounds and situations.

Some of this sounds right to me based on what I've read, but other parts felt off, like the leap from "you can't decide between rabbit and rabbit-part" to "translation is impossible." I'm in no position to referee a fight between my brother-in-law and a famous Harvard philosopher though. Where you can, please distinguish between things that are basically settled among people who study this and things that philosophers still actively disagree about -- I don't want to walk away thinking some contested reading is the official verdict.
