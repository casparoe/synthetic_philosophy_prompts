My friend — the type who's done calibration training, logs every one of his forecasts, and generally treats the Sequences as scripture — hit me with an argument last week that's been bugging me ever since, because it seems to show rationality training is worthless once it's done its job:

(1) All the practical value of a belief comes from its being true. A true belief about which road goes to Larissa gets you to Larissa exactly as well as knowledge of the route does — this is basically Meno's point at the start of the whole knowledge-vs-true-belief mess.
(2) Knowing that p = truly believing that p plus some extra ingredient. Pick your favorite: reliability, justification, having formed the belief via a truth-tracking process.
(3) The extra is valuable only as a means to true beliefs. A reliable thermometer is valuable only because of the readings it gives.
(4) If you already have the true reading, the thermometer adds nothing. So in any particular case where you already have the true belief, the extra adds nothing.
(5) If knowledge were worth more than true belief, that extra worth should show up in actual cases — there's nowhere else for value to live.
(6) So knowledge is never worth more than true belief, case by case. A lucky guesser and a well-calibrated forecaster who both land on the truth are exactly as well off.
(7) So the only value of reliability is forward-looking: it makes your future beleifs true. Once the process has produced a true belief in you, its work is done, and caring about process after that point is confused. The rationalist obsession with how you believe is something you should indulge before believing, not after.

When I pushed back he threw decision theory at me: the value of information is computed BEFORE you acquire it — you're choosing between acting now and paying to get better-informed. Once you already hold the true belief, the expected utility of acting on it is identical for the lucky guesser and the informed observer, so even by our own lights they come out even.

I think (6) is nuts. I'd much rather be the forecaster than the lucky guesser even after both turned out right, and I don't think that's just vanity. But every escape I've tried has a price. Denying (5) seems to mean value that never shows up anywhere, which sounds like a free lunch. Denying (3) means reliability is valuable for something other than truth, and I can't say what. And the classic Meno move — true opinions are like Daedalus' statues, they run off unless tied down — always struck me as redescribing the problem rather than solving it (tied down how, and why does tying count as the same belief being worth more rather than a different state entirely?).

Which step do I reject, what does rejecting it cost, and is there a way to make I'd rather be the forecaster into something more than a preference? Lead with the bottom line in your first sentence, then give the reasoning — no preamble, no restating my question back at me.
