I've spent a long time in the comment sections of the effective altruism and x-risk blogs, and I've noticed that whenever the discount rate question comes up — Ramsey, Sidgwick's impartiality, astronomical waste, the Stern Review's rate — I don't actually hold a consistent position so much as a bundle of verdicts. I've tried to write them down honestly. I'd like you to tell me what principle, if any, captures them, and which ones have to go.

The verdicts, concretely: a day of migraine for me in 2055 matters exactly as much as an otherwise identical day next month — I can't detect any difference from the impartial standpoint. A stranger's migraine in 2200 matters the same as one today. But when I imagine a project that costs one unit of present welfare to deliver ten units in 2300, my confidence that we're required to build it is noticeably lower than the ten-to-one arithmetic suggests, and I can't fully explain that away by pointing out that we don't owe strangers positive aid — some residue remains. In my own choices I take 110 dollars next year over 100 now, and I exhibit the standard preference reversals over longer horizons, which I'm prepared to call irrational in myself while wondering whether institutions aggregating people like me are entitled to lean on it. I think discounting for extinction risk and project failure is obviously fine, and I notice that a low enough per-century survival probability produces numbers indistinguishable from an exponential discount rate, yet I keep insisting the two are different in kind — I'd like to know whether that insistence survives scrutiny or whether it's branding. I accept that future people's greater wealth justifies weighing their marginal dollars less, while being uneasy that the same logic, run on welfare rather than money, seems to license neglecting very large far-future gains for the sake of small present ones. And I take the point that any positive pure rate makes welfare in the year one million worth essentially nothing to be close to a refutation of pure time preference — yet I can't bring myself to run my own life on zero discounting, and I'd like to know whether that's an inconsistency or just the difference between prudence and morality.

My tentative principle is that uncertainty about existence and about outcomes can do all the legitimate discounting work, and the residual pure rate is zero. Test it against my cases. Does it handle the one where my considered judgment (equal) and my disposition to choose (unequal) come apart, and which of the two should the principle be built on? What does it predict for cases I haven't listed — say, a benefit to people whose existence depends on our choice now, or welfare gains that grow faster than any discount rate compounding against them? And be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot save: if the residue in the 2300 project case is a real pure preference for the near, I'd rather know that than have it laundered into uncertainty.
