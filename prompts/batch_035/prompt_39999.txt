You are a professional metaphysician, working mainly in ontology and the philosophy of time, and I want you to reason carefully and at length rather than survey positions. For context: I am a graduate student in a seminar on persistence, and my study group has spent most of the past year on the endurantism–perdurantism dispute — whether things persist by being wholly present at each time at which they exist, or by being extended through time and having temporal parts. The debate has been running in essentially its present form since the 1980s, the stock arguments appear in every textbook, and as far as I can tell almost nobody changes sides. The recurring exchange in our group runs as follows, and it never resolves. The perdurantist opens with the temporary intrinsics argument: the seminar room's desk is standing now but was flat in pieces during last summer's renovation; if the very desk is wholly present whenever it exists and has both properties, something has to give, and positing temporal parts that each have their shape outright looks cleaner than making properties, or their instantiation, tacitly time-indexed. The endurantist answers that instantiation can simply be relativized to times, and then reverses the ad-hocness charge: the perdurantist also needs a primitive — a restriction on which slices count as parts of one object — to rule out scattered collections of stages, so neither side is innocent of unexplained ideology. The perdurantist then plays the analogy with space: nobody thinks a thing is less than wholly present where it is just because it also has a left half, so why should extent along the fourth dimension be different in kind from extent along the first three? The endurantist replies that the analogy begs the question by building the four-dimensional picture into the premise, and closes with common sense: ordinary people take it that the very same whole desk, not a stage of it, was there last summer. The perdurantist calls that folk prejudice, and the conversation ends where it began.

One further wrinkle that feeds my suspicion about what is going on: the four-dimensionalist camp is itself split, between those who say the persisting thing is the whole spacetime worm and those who say it is a single stage with earlier and later counterparts. Members of that camp accuse each other, in print and in person, of exactly the verbalism each camp accuses the other side of, which makes me wonder whether the entire dispute might be a choice of notation. My working hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is this: the impasse is not about what exists but about what a theory of persistence is for — the perdurantist side is optimizing for a uniform picture continuous with physics, the endurantist side for fidelity to how we actually talk and think about things, and each side counts the other's favored criterion as question-begging. But others in the group insist the disagreement is substantive all the way down.

Please diagnose the disagreement, using what each side actually says. Is it verbal, substantive but underdetermined, or a case of two groups answering different questions under one heading — one about ontology, one about the semantics of ordinary temporal talk? If some of the disagreement is merely notational, say precisely where the notational part ends and the real part, if any, begins. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a new type of case, a formal constraint on theories of persistence, an agreed standard for when a posit counts as ideology rather than machinery — or argue that no progress should be expected, and explain why.
