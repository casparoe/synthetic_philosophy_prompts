I write novels, and they tend to be set in small places, which means over the years I've built up a private ledger of when shared things get wrecked and when they mysteriously arent, and I'd love to know whether there's one actual principle behind my verdicts or whether I'm just pattern-matching on vibes.

The cases, with my calls:

- The strip fields behind the village I grew up in were grazed in common for something like four hundred years and, as far as anyone can tell, never collapsed. Whatever went on there, it wasnt a tragedy waiting to happen — it worked.
- The biscuit table at my old publishing house: restocked monday, stripped by tuesday, fridge permanently feral. Wrecked, every single time.
- On my allotment site the communal hosepipe got nicked within a month of appearing. The communal wheelbarrow, which I swear is older than me, has outlasted every hosepipe and shows no sign of dying. Wrecked and not-wrecked, by the exact same crowd.
- Six of my friends have shared a holiday cottage for twenty years and its pristine — better kept than most of our actual houses. The same six friends reduced a shared sailing dinghy to a mouldering hull in five summers. I cannot reconcile these two at all.
- The book-swap shelf at my station, run entirely on strangers leaving and taking paperbacks, has worked for years and I've never once seen it empty or abused.
- The atmosphere, obviously, but that one feels too big to learn anything from.

My working theory for ages was the "will you meet them again" one: shared stuff survives when the users all know each other and have to show their faces tomorrow. The cottage fits, the old commons fit, the biscuit table fits. But the dinghy is the same six faces as the cottage, and the station shelf works on total anonymity, so the theory is dead and I dont have a replacement.

Also this is practical, not idle: I'm two thirds through a novel set in a fishing village. Fourteen boats, everyone knows everyone going back generations, the fish are getting scarcer but you cant see the stock the way you can see a hedge, and one skipper has quietly put a bigger engine in. My editor says a village that tight would never let the fishery collapse — some elder-statesman type would sort everyone out. I have my doubts, but she's usually right about human nature, and the book needs to go the way things actually go, not the way my sentiment wants.

So: is there a principle, or a couple of clauses, that fits all six of my verdicts? Which of them, if any, does it have to throw out? And what does it predict for my village — do they wreck the fishery or not, and would a harbour association with actual fines be a realistic save, or would that just be me smuggling in a happy ending?

Keep it under 500 words please — I know you could write a dissertation on this, but I mainly need the working end of it.
