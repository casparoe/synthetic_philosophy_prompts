I run a mixed reading group -- a few decision theory grad students plus a couple behavioral econ people who mostly know Ellsberg from the psych-lit side -- and next session I want to hand them a case that messes with the standard story. Here's what I need you to build, it's basically a thought experiment to spec:

One agent throughout. In a classic two-urn setup (urn with a known 50/50 split vs urn of unknown composition), they show the textbook Ellsberg pattern: they'd rather bet on the known urn than the unknown one for a bet on red, AND for a bet on black, so no single probability assignment over the unknown urn can rationalize both choices. So far so boring. But then give them a second stage where they're already stuck holding an ambiguous bet they can't unload, and the only clean way to hedge it involves taking on another ambiguous bet tied to the same unknown urn -- and they pay a real premium to do that instead of taking an actuarially equivalent unambiguous hedge. So they're ambiguity *seeking* in that spot, while being ambiguity averse in the first.

Constraints: it has to be something a normal person could actually face. Think a farmer stuck with exposure to an unpredictable weather outcome choosing between insurance contracts, or a founder already committed to a coinflip-ish launch date choosing between two investor offers -- whatever, your call, but no casinos and no supernatural predictors. The scenario itself should be one paragraph, maybe a paragraph and a half max. And crucially the flip has to fall out of the structure of the bets (hedging, correlation, whatever you want to call it) -- it can't be that I stipulated the agent suddenly feels different about ambiguity.

After the scenario, do the walkthrough:

1. Lay out the choice pairs so the Ellsberg pattern is visible in stage one and the reversal is visible in stage two.
2. Say what a subjective expected utility agent with any single prior does at each stage. (They can't reproduce the stage-one choices at all, right? Make that explicit, since that's the whole point of the two-color urn.)
3. Pin down exactly which feature of the second-stage situation does the work in flipping the agent from ambiguity averse to ambiguity seeking -- what would happen to the case if you removed that feature.
4. Tell me what the hardliner in the group will say: the guy who thinks ambiguity aversion is just incoherence under the sure-thing principle and that nothing was ever rescued. Phrase it the way he would, then tell me honestly whether his objection actually lands on this case or just on the stage-one choices.

I'm doing this because I want to argue that ambiguity attitudes are properties of portfolios, not of individual bets, and the case is supposed to be my evidence -- so that's the standard for cheating: if the reversal only works because you snuck in a risk-aversion difference or changed the stakes between stages, it doesn't count, say so rather than fudging it. And if you think my two specs quietly conflict somewhere I haven't noticed, flag that instead of papering over it.
