I'm a retired physicist who spent most of my career in condensed matter before getting thoroughly hooked on philosophy of mind and computation in the last few years, and somehow this has landed me giving a talk at our local "science for everyone" series at the library. The audience is mostly retirees and a few curious high schoolers -- smart people, zero background beyond vague memories of hearing that "computers only do zeros and ones." I've decided to talk about the Church-Turing thesis, but specifically about what kind of claim it even is, because that's the part that gripped me once I finally understood it: nobody proved it, nobody could have proven it as stated, and yet it's not just a definition either -- it sits in this strange spot between a math theorem, an empirical hypothesis, and a proposal about how to use words. That epistemic status is what I want the talk to actually be about, not a history lecture on Turing machines.

Here's my problem. I can write out the tape-and-head picture fine, but when I try to plan the whole arc I keep going back and forth on the ordering. Do I need to show them a couple of actual machines doing a toy task (say, adding in unary) before I can say anything about the thesis, or does that bury them in mechanism before they see why anyone cares? And then the deeper question of which analogy carries the whole thing: I was thinking of comparing it to how "energy" got fixed in physics, where the term's meaning was partly settled by which formalism kept proving useful across phenomena -- i.e., a concept whose authority comes from everywhere agreeing rather than from one proof -- but I honestly don't know if that analogy earns its keep or if there's a better one from, say, measurement conventions or invariance principles. Also, I already know from arguing about this with my old colleagues that people will immediately jump to "so you've proven the brain is a computer" or "but quantum computers/analogue machines break it" or even "so physics says reality is discrete," and I want each of those headed off at the right moment rather than all crammed into Q&A at the end when everyone's tired.

So what I'm asking for is a plan for the talk, not the talk itself: the order the pieces should go in and what depends on what, the single carrying example or analogy and what it needs to do (and what it shouldn't promise), where exactly in the sequence each of those misconceptions I listed should be defused, and one question I can ask at the end that would tell me whether they actually got the math-hypothesis-definition distinction versus just nodding along. Also please tell me what to deliberately refuse to cover in 45 minutes and admit I'm refusing -- I suspect full independence-results stuff and the whole hypercomputation rabbit hole both belong in that bin, but push back if you disagree. Don't give me options and let me choose; make the calls yourself and say why, and if you think my energy-analogy is a dud, say that flat out rather than diplomatically including it alongside a hedge.
