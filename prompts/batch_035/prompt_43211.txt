I am a sixth-form student writing an extended project on whether we can ever test the thought experiments of early modern philosophy, and one of my chapters is about Molyneux's question. The problem is that nearly everything I know about it comes from a single paragraph in a popular book about the brain that I found in the school library — a chapter claiming that modern neuroscience has settled questions the empiricists could only argue about. I took notes from it and built an outline around it before it occurred to me that I should check whether the paragraph is actually reliable, and I do not yet know enough philosophy or neuroscience to tell. The book is by a science journalist, and it reads convincingly to me, partly because the story it tells is tidy. Here is the paragraph:

"In a letter of 1688, the Irish philosopher William Molyneux asked John Locke whether a man born blind, who had learned to tell a cube from a sphere by touch, could recognise the two objects by sight alone if his sight were suddenly restored. Locke said no, and Molyneux agreed — as did nearly every philosopher who later weighed in, Berkeley and Leibniz among them — so the empiricists considered the case closed: the ideas supplied by sight and by touch are separate, and the newly sighted man must start from scratch. The question went untested for three centuries, because nothing could be done for people born with dense cataracts, until 2011, when surgeons in India restored sight to five young people who had been blind from early childhood. Tested within a day or two of the operation, the patients could not match an object they had felt to the one they were seeing — the negative answer, apparently confirmed at last. But within days they had learned to do it, which shows that the senses draw on a shared, partly innate stock of spatial concepts. So the study in the end vindicated the rationalists, and Molyneux's question, after three hundred years, has been answered."

I suspect the middle of the paragraph is roughly right and the framing is where trouble lies, but that is a guess. Please take the paragraph sentence by sentence and tell me which claims a historian of philosophy and a vision scientist would accept, which they would wince at as oversimplifications, and which are simply wrong — and, for anything wrong, what the correct version is. I would particularly like to know whether the two historical figures it names as agreeing actually did agree, whether the claim that nothing could be done for congenital cataract patients for three centuries is fair, and whether the study's results were as the book describes. Finally, assess the book's conclusion: does the claim that the rationalists were vindicated, and that Molyneux's question has now been answered, actually follow from whatever in the paragraph turns out to be true? Please distinguish carefully between what the study showed about these particular patients and what it could show about Molyneux's question in general, and write your answer in British English.
