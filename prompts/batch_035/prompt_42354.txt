I serve on the constitutional court of the Republic of Veldania, a young democracy, and I would like your recommendation on a case we must decide this term. Decide it from our own record rather than from the doctrine of any country you may know: what matters is consistency with ourselves.

Our constitution is brief. It guarantees the vote and free expression outright, states equality before the law and personal liberty in general terms, and protects property only as statutes determine. In ten years the court has decided five constitutional cases:

1. The Disenfranchisement Act (struck down). Parliament had barred anyone convicted of any criminal offense, however minor, from voting for life. Our judgment stressed that a legislature empowered to define away its critics' votes has closed the one channel through which its acts can be corrected.
2. The Ballot Deposit Act (struck down). Deposits for new party registration were set so high that only the two established parties could field candidates. We held the scheme a barrier to political competition, not a mere regulation of it.
3. The Fuel Price Decree (upheld). Wartime fuel rationing with price controls, challenged on liberty and property grounds. We held that economic policy belongs to Parliament, however mistaken we might privately find it.
4. The Memory of the Fallen Act (upheld). Criminalizing public insult to the war dead. The majority held that core political debate was left essentially untouched; two dissents argued the statute was overbroad and easily aimed at unpopular speakers.
5. The Day of Rest Act (upheld). A Sunday trading closure challenged by a Saturday-observing sect whose members, in practice, may rest on neither day. We held the burden incidental to a general secular law with a secular purpose. A concurrence added, pointedly, that the sect's members vote, organize freely, and sit in Parliament.

The bench divides loosely into two camps that have voted together in all five cases. The self-described gatekeepers would intervene only when the majority has rigged the channels of political change or legislated against a group so despised that no electoral mechanism will ever correct the injury. The clear-mistake judges would strike down only what no reasonable legislator could have believed constitutional, leaving everything else to the voters.

The new case concerns the Weavers: an endogamous group, roughly one percent of the population, descended from bonded laborers and stereotyped as thieves and cheats. After a fatal market fire was blamed, probably wrongly, on a Weaver's stall, Parliament enacted the Market Order Act, which denies street-trading licenses to members of historically itinerant communities, excluding the Weavers from their principal traditional occupation. The legislative debates were candid — several members said their constituents demanded it — and the only dissenting votes came from members whose districts contain too few Weavers for the matter to have electoral weight. A Weaver prosecuted for continuing to sell textiles has brought the challenge.

Please do the following. Recommend the decision most consistent with our precedents, and identify the closest analog among our five cases, saying honestly whether it controls or is distinguishable. If our record underdetermines the answer, state what principle each available decision would commit us to going forward, and predict how each principle would dispose of two cases we are likely to see next: a facially neutral licensing scheme administered so that Weaver applications in practice always fail, and a ban on a proselytizing religion that is intensely unpopular but politically well organized. Finally, name the single future case that would most sharply reveal which principle we had actually adopted, and address one internal worry: if the gatekeepers distinguish the Day of Rest Act on the ground that its burden was incidental rather than targeted, does that distinction survive without their position collapsing into ordinary rights review — and if it does not survive, is the collapse something we should resist or accept?
