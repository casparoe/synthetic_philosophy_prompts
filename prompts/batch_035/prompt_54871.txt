I chair the working group that maintains Flatpack, a small plain-text data format (think JSON's less glamorous cousin) that a lot of scientific software quietly depends on. I also teach philosophy, which is why what should be a boring errata dispute is keeping me up at night.

The situation: our spec, clause 12.4, says parsers must match field names case-insensitively. For ten years every implementation in the wild has done simple ASCII case-folding -- A matches a, and that's it. Now a big library wants to switch to full Unicode case-folding, which would suddenly merge field names that every existing parser treats as distinct -- think of a German dataset with fields differing only by sharp-s versus double-s. The spec's original author is still on our committee, and he's adamant that he "only ever meant ASCII" and that the question is settled by that. Half the working group agrees with him in spirit: they think the correct reading of clause 12.4 is already fixed -- by the text, by the author's intent, or by our ten years of shared practice -- and our job is just to correctly find what the spec has always required. The other half thinks we're legislating, full stop, and that the honest thing is to say in the errata that we're making a choice, not discovering one.

Here's my problem. I have to write the decision memo before our July meeting, recommending a concrete resolution (pick a folding rule, and pick a story about our authority for picking it). But I keep recognizing this as the rule-following skeptic's puzzle with a lanyard on. If you look at any finite body of past usage -- every sentence ever written about clause 12.4, every past decision, the author's own memory of what he meant -- it seems compatible with different future applications, the way Kripke's addition-skeptic says all my past calculations are compatible with my meaning quus instead of plus. The author's intention doesn't obviously help, since his intention is itself just more interpretation-material; the text doesn't, since the text is what's in question. And yet the "we're just legislating" half of the committee has its own problem: if nothing in the past fixes what the spec requires, in what sense did anyone ever conform or fail to conform to it before now? What was the library doing wrong, exactly, if there was no fact of the matter?

So the memo needs two layers. First, the practical call: which folding rule Flatpack 1.1 should mandate, and how to stage the migration so a decade of existing data doesn't silently change meaning. Second, and this is the part I actually care about, the memo has to take a stance on our authority: are we interpreting a standard that already determines the answer (and if so, what determines it -- text, intent, community practice, some combination?), or are we freely choosing, and should we say so out loud? Does admitting we're choosing undermine the normativity of the spec -- the fact that an implementation can be non-conforming -- or is that fear a mistake? I suspect there's a middle position where what fixes the rule is something like the community's shared dispositions to go on in the same way, but I want to know if that actually does the work or just relocates the problem.

A few things for whoever drafts this:

- The memo should be around 800 words and readable by engineers with no philosophy background -- no name-dropping unless you can make it earn its keep.
- It needs a clear recommendation with a time frame, plus an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on whichever side loses.
- Don't dodge the authority question by saying "the text is clear." The text is exactly what's in dispute; treat that move the way it deserves.
- Assume the answer starts with the memo itself -- no preamble, no restating my question, no commentary before or after. If you think the philosophical framing belongs in the memo, work it in; if it belongs in a footnote or an annex, fine, but the memo proper has to stand on its own as something the committee could circulate.
