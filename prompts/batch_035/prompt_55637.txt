I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums since the Overcoming Bias days, and one thing I've noticed is that every few years someone rediscovers the expressivism-vs.-realism fight, the thread runs to four hundred comments, and nobody ever states the disagreement exactly. The Frege--Geach problem is where this always happens, so I want the artifact those threads never produce: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration. You are a professional metaethicist experienced in chairing good-faith exchanges between hostile camps; think hard before drafting, and begin with some private brainstorming about candidate formulations for the agreed section, discarding any wording that would strike one side as slanted or as vacuous -- the value of the document is entirely in formulations both parties could sign without wincing.

The two parties: a committed expressivist in the quasi-realist tradition (Blackburn, Gibbard), and a committed moral realist who thinks moral judgments are truth-apt beliefs with representational content. The exclusive topic is the Frege--Geach problem, i.e., the behavior of moral vocabulary in unasserted and embedded contexts: negation, disjunction, conditionals, questions, and the validity of the kind of argument Geach pressed against Hare in the 1960s (roughly: if tormenting the cat is bad, getting your little brother to torment it is bad; tormenting the cat is bad; therefore getting your little brother to torment it is bad -- the premises can be accepted by someone who never asserts the embedded claim in a standalone way, and the validity has to be explained, not stipulated). Scope it tightly: moral disagreement about cases, motivational internalism, and epistemology are off the table unless they surface as cruxes about embedding.

Produce the document with the following sections, in this order, with the approximate lengths given:

1. "The problem, as both parties state it" (~150 words) -- one paragraph on the phenomenon, in wording the expressivist accepts as capturing the explanatory burden and the realist accepts as capturing what needs explaining.

2. "Agreed claims" (numbered list, ~400 words) -- what both sides concede: about the phenomenon itself; about what any adequate semantics for moral language must deliver (a compositional account of embedding, a story about what makes the inference valid rather than merely persuasive, a treatment of mixed moral/descriptive compounds); about what the expressivist's machinery -- attitude-consistency accounts of validity, the planning-state model, deflationary truth as applied to moral sentences -- actually achieves and where the realist thinks the achievement is genuine rather than mimicked.

3. "Where we still disagree" (a table, ~300 words) -- each residual disagreement stated twice, once in wording the expressivist would accept as fair to their view and once in wording the realist would accept as fair to theirs; include at least the dispute over whether the expressivist's story of validity in terms of preserved consistency of attitudes is an alternative to truth-conditional semantics or a notational variant of one, and the dispute over whether deflationism about truth hands the expressivist everything the realist wanted from truth.

4. "Cruxes, and what each side predicts" (numbered, ~450 words) -- for each crux, a concrete case, construction, or argument that both parties agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Prefer cruxes that a thought experiment or a formal result could in principle settle, and say what settling each crux would force on which party.

5. "What is left, and what is verbal" (~150 words) -- whether any part of the remaining dispute is verbal (and if so, which part and why), and, if not, what development both parties agree would actually move the debate, or whether each thinks the other side's position is now insulated by construction.

Do not declare a winner anywhere; the document is neutral, and the two parties are its co-authors, not its subjects.
