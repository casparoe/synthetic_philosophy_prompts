So for context: I'm a PhD student in ML, and our lab uses debate-style oversight to evaluate model behavior -- one model argues a claim is fine, another argues it's not, and a human judge (usually me or another grad student) picks a winner. The selling point is that the adversary points you at the specific flawed step in a long argument, so you verify a little instead of everything. Lately I've been worrying about obfuscated arguments, though. By that I mean arguments so long no judge will ever check every step, where each step looks locally fine, but somewhere in there sits a subtle error that even the opposing debater would need nearly as much work to locate as it would take to just verify the whole thing. In that regime the debate trick stops helping: I can't distinguish "there is no flaw" from "there's a flaw but neither debater can cheaply point at it," and both debaters end up rewarded for confidence rather than for being checkable.

Can you write me a procedure the judges can actually run on a transcript? Constraints: we get maybe 30 minutes per debate, the judges are grad students with no domain expertise in whatever the debate is about, and there's no budget to rerun debates we regret. The procedure should say what to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where it stops with "penalize the argument as unverifiable," and -- importantly -- where it has to hand off because the question is about trust or values rather than facts, since I don't want a checklist pretending those are settled. Then run it on two or three worked cases: (a) a safety argument for a code change that's a 40-step proof sketch where the opponent concedes it "looks right," (b) a debate where one side wins by splitting its argument into a hundred trivially checkable steps that never actually connect to the conclusion, and (c) one where the procedure gives an answer I won't like. Please also be upfront about what the procedure can't do -- my suspicion is there are transcripts where the honest output is "no procedure can settle this," and I'd rather have that marked explicitly than papered over. Keep it usable, we're not trying to rebuild decision theory here, just not fool ourselves about when the judges are actually doing oversight versus rubber-stamping.
