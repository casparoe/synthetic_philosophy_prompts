Me and my fiancée are getting married in October (we're both women, if that matters) and my uncle — who is genuinely lovely, came round to the whole thing years ago, absolutely not the fire-and-brimstone type — pulled me aside after the engagement party and said he'll be at the wedding and he'll be happy for us, but he thinks marriage as an institution is fundamentally about children: that the whole business exists because it's how the next generation gets made and raised, and a marriage that can't even in principle produce children is sort of playing at it rather than doing it. I pushed back by pointing at my aunt and other uncle who married at 58 (post-menopause, his vasectomy, the lot), and he had an answer ready, which threw me: he said their marriage is still the right *kind* of union — one that's naturally ordered towards children even when it happens not to work — whereas ours just isn't that kind of thing at all, so the cases aren't parallel.

The annoying part is I can feel there's something off about that move but I can't put my finger on it. Is it doing real work or is it a dodge? If being the right "kind" of union is enough even when the capacity is entirely absent, where does that stop — does he have to say a 30-year-old woman who's had a hysterectomy marrying a man is fine but we aren't, and would he actually bite that bullet or would he suddenly discover some other consideration? It feels like the "kind" is doing all the work while being impossible to pin down. Or maybe that's unfair and the idea is more respectable than I'm giving it credit for.

Also, the awkward wrinkle: we're planning to have kids via a donor, so we'll arguably be doing more of the actual child-having-and-child-raising than half the straight couples we know. Does that help our case, hurt it (because it concedes children are the point?), or is it beside the point entirely? I'd like to know.

I don't want a row with him, I want to know if his reasoning is actually any good, so I can either give him a proper answer at the wedding or concede he's onto something I just don't like. Be honest with me even if its not what I want to hear — I'd rather be corrected now than be smug and wrong at the reception. (Sorry for any typos, typing this on the train.)
