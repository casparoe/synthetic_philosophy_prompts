Every year in my philosophy of math course for third-years we hit the axiom of choice, and every year I lean on the stock analogy: AC is "obviously true" because, given any collection of nonempty sets, you can just go through and pick one element from each — like pulling one sock from each pair in a drawer with infinitely many pairs of socks. I usually do Russell's old shoes-vs-socks bit for a laugh: with pairs of shoes you need no axiom at all, just say "take the left shoe every time," but socks have no distinguished member, so the choice function has to be posited rather than defined. Well, this week a sharp student pushed back hard enough that I realized I've been letting the picture do more work than it can carry. Her complaint: the analogy has a physical chooser with a procedure, working sequentially, in time, each pick leaving a record — whereas the axiom asserts a single completed set encoding all the picks at once, no procedure, no time, and possibly no definable rule whatsoever (else we'd get a definable well-ordering of the reals, which we know we can't have). And then there's the other direction: the same cozy sock-picking intuition gets used to defend full AC, which gets you well-ordering of the continuum and Banach–Tarski, and it's hardly obvious that intuitions about dressers underwrite that.

So — act as a set theorist with serious philosophical training (you've marked papers both from the platonist and the formalist camps) and help me audit this analogy once and for all. Give me the full correspondence table first — collection of nonempty sets ↔ drawer of pairs, choice function ↔ the picker, distinguishable members ↔ shoes, literal indiscernibles ↔ socks, definable choice ↔ "always the left shoe" — then sort the disanalogies into ones that actually matter for the dialectical use and ones that don't. For instance: does it matter that a human chooser can't finish infinitely many picks (my suspicion is that this conflates AC with something about completing supertasks, but check me), or that socks are distinguishable in principle even if not by a rule, while abstract sets can be genuinely indiscernible relative to the ambient structure? Which fragment of the principle does the intuition really support — full AC, countable choice, choice for two-element sets (which, if I remember right, isnt even provable in ZF) — and is there a principled stopping point, or do we just stop where the theorems get weird? Think through it carefully before answering, and then give me the bottom line straight: my student claims the analogy was carrying the entire classroom argument that AC is true, and once the disanalogies are on the table, "obvious" is no longer available. Is she right? Or does a patched-up version — maybe something with a catalogue, or selections made all at once by fiat rather than one-by-one — still do honest work? And flag which side of the platonist/formalist fence each repair puts me on, since I have students sitting on both.
