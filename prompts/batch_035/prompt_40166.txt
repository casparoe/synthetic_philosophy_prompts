I'm a retired physiologist -- thirty-odd years studying autonomic regulation, mostly baroreflex work -- and since retiring I've gotten deep into philosophy, which is a fun humbling experience. I've come out roughly where William James was: an emotion just is the perception of your own bodily state. Fear is what it feels like to perceive the surge, the gut drop, the racing heart; there's no separate appraisal happening first that then causes the feeling, the perception of the body IS the emotion. Philosophers seem to treat this view as a punching bag, but half the time their objections look to me like they don't know the physiology very well. So tell me straight: what would actually count against the view? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward -- things like spinal cord injury patients still reporting emotions, or people on beta blockers not feeling less afraid (do those results hold up?), or the fact that we tell shame apart from embarrassment without any obvious difference in autonomic signature. And I want to know what a committed Jamesian would concede really counts against them versus what they'd wave away. Related thing I keep worrying about: Damasio apparently patched the theory by saying the brain can just simulate the bodily response instead of reading it off the body -- that move makes the view awfully hard to test from where I'm sitting. Is that legitimate repair or has the position been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that a dealbreaker for a theory of this kind? Be honest about how insulated the view actually is. I'd rather find out now that I fell for a beautiful theory that can't lose than keep defending it in book club.
