At a rationality meetup I keep hearing the line that if your preferences don't conform to expected utility theory, a sufficiently clever bookie can strip you of everything you own. It was most recently deployed against me for admitting Allais-style preferences: I'd take a sure million over a gamble that's better in expectation, while reversing my ranking once both options become gambles. I was told this makes me not just unusual but exploitable and therefore irrational, full stop. That one sentence is doing a lot of work, and I suspect it's several distinct arguments wearing the same words. Lay out for me the menu of precise claims the slogan could be making. At least these seem separable to me, and I'd like your verdicts on each and on anything I've missed:

1. That any violation of the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms can be exploited in a single sitting, by an adversary who knows your preferences and can offer whatever trades they like.
2. That only some violations are exploitable in practice — e.g., that intransitivity can be turned into a money pump across a sequence of trades, but that violating the independence axiom, as in my Allais case, requires the bookie to offer gambles I could decline, so the exploitation story is weaker there.
3. That the argument only goes through if I accept every trade I weakly prefer, so an agent who refuses trades when indifferent, or who can commit up front to a policy and refuse to reconsider mid-sequence (something like deciding once, at the start, and treating later temptations as non-options), escapes the pump — and whether that escape is admissible or just hides the irrationality elsewhere.
4. That the betting framing is being smuggled over from arguments about credences — where a bookie can exploit probability assignments that violate the probability axioms — to arguments about preferences over risky options, where the transfer is not automatic and may fail.
5. That the whole argument is circular because not wanting to be pumped already presupposes you care about the money the pump extracts, or otherwise depends on background conditions (the trades are small, the bookie is patient, transaction costs are zero) that no one states.

For each reading, tell me whether it's true as stated, whether it's trivial, and whether it actually applies to my Allais preferences. Then tell me which reading the meetup person needs for their conclusion that non-EU agents are irrational, and whether that reading survives scrutiny. Finally, be explicit about where your own verdict is most fragile: what argument, counterexample, or result about commitment or sequential choice would move you to a different verdict on the strongest reading, and what part of your reasoning do you most expect to have gotten wrong?
