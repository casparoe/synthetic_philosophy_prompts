I'm writing a term paper for a philosophy of probability seminar, and my study group has an argument that starts out as the standard case for the thirder answer to Sleeping Beauty and ends up somewhere I refuse to follow. To fix the setup: Beauty is put to sleep Sunday night, a fair coin is flipped, she is woken briefly on Monday, and if the coin lands tails she is woken again on Tuesday after a drug that erases the Monday awakening, the two awakenings being subjectively indistinguishable. The argument goes: (1) on Sunday night Beauty's credence in heads is one half. (2) When she wakes, her total evidence — everything she has available at that moment — is the same in each of the three centered possibilities, heads-Monday, tails-Monday, and tails-Tuesday, so she cannot distinguish them even in principle. (3) Rationality requires equal credence in possibilities that are evidentially indistinguishable in every way. (4) So her credence in heads on waking is one third. (5) Nothing in the reasoning so far turned on the details of the experiment; all it needed was that the tails world contains more subjectively indistinguishable awakenings than the heads world, and whenever one hypothesis predicts n times as many indistinguishable experiences as another it deserves n times that other's credence, all else equal. (6) So in a boosted version of the experiment, where tails means a million awakenings instead of two, her credence in heads on waking should be about one in a million — a fair coin she watched tossed on Sunday night becomes very nearly certainly tails overnight, without her learning a single new fact about the coin. (7) And the same principle applies before any experiment is run at all: given two cosmological theories equally consistent with everything we have observed, the one that posits vastly more observers deserves vastly higher credence, so how crowded the universe is could be settled by counting observers rather than by pointing telescopes at anything.

I have been staring at this for a week and cannot find the flaw, and that bothers me because each step looks like the previous one plus nothing. If I deny (3), I owe a principle about when indifference over indistinguishable cases is licensed that does not also outlaw its ordinary and obviously correct uses. If I deny (5), I have to say what exactly about the two-awakening version fails to carry over to the million-awakening version, and it cannot merely be that the numbers got big. If I accept (5) but reject (7), I need some difference between awakenings inside an experiment and observers inside a universe that does not look invented on the spot. And the option I keep circling back to is that (7) is simply true — that we do get this evidence for free, before looking at anything, and my conviction that it is absurd is the prejudice. One member of my group is a halfer instead, and when I press her on what happens to her credence when Beauty is told it is Monday, she says it stays one half; I cannot make that cohere with anything else she believes about how likely today is to be Monday, so feel free to use her troubles if they help you locate the structural issue. But I want a verdict on the argument itself, not a survey of the field. Tell me which premise or inference to reject, why it looked fine in the first place, and what rejecting it costs — including what it costs elsewhere, since I suspect whichever premise falls here has friends in other arguments. Write it as continuous prose only, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write a letter to a student who has been going in circles.
