A research institute is setting up a joint programme between its philosophy of mind group and its AI interpretability group — four researchers in total, working together for two years, with the explicit brief of making interpretability research more conceptually principled rather than faster. You have been asked to set the intellectual agenda: the group's first task will be to work through the notion of a free-floating rationale, roughly the idea (associated with Dennett) that something can have reasons guiding its behaviour — in the sense that there are things it is *for* and facts that *explain why* its features are as they are — without any thinker, designer, or internal process representing those reasons, as with the "reason" a plant's leaves turn towards the light or the "reason" a bird's wing is the shape it is.

Several open questions in this vicinity have been floated, and I want your help deciding which of them most deserves the group's limited time:

1. **The taxonomy question.** Are the reasons behind a trained neural network's behaviour free-floating rationales of literally the same kind as evolutionary ones (selected because they did something), or does gradient descent's incremental, interpolative character make the analogy misleading in ways that matter?
2. **The underdetermination question.** When several incompatible rationales each explain a system's behaviour equally well — as multiple fitness stories can explain the same evolved trait — is there a fact of the matter about which rationale the system *really* has, or is rationale-talk a stancerelative instrument we should simply accept as such?
3. **The correctness question.** What distinguishes a genuine free-floating rationale from a merely post hoc rationalisation? If any behaviour can be made to look reasonable from *some* vantage point, what norm governs attribution — explanatory parsimony, counterfactual robustness, something else?
4. **The etiology question.** Is the rationale a system has fixed by its training history (why the behaviour was selected for) or by its current internal organisation (what the circuitry is doing now), and do these come apart in ways that make a difference to interpretation?
5. **The alignment question.** If a model's behaviour can have reasons that no one designed or intends, what does this imply for the idea of "intent" in alignment work — in particular, whether misalignment without any malicious intent is not merely possible but the default, and whether alignment failures are best modelled as failures of rationale rather than failures of motive?

Please order these — or drop some and propose others you think matter more — and defend the ordering. I want the criteria you are using (importance for interpretability practice, tractability for conceptual rather than empirical work, neglectedness, and how much downstream depends on each), the trade-offs between those criteria, and an explicit statement of which question you would deprioritise despite its philosophical interest, with reasons. Finally, describe two or three concrete developments — findings, methods, or conceptual results that might emerge in the next few years — that would most change your ranking, and in which direction. The deliverable is a defended agenda, not a survey.
