I'm on the ethics committee at a mid-sized hospital (mostly I'm the "outside member," I teach philosophy part-time), and we keep getting dragged into brain-death disputes without ever having settled what we think death actually *is*. Every time it comes up — a family that won't accept the neurologic determination, a DCD case where the surgeons are watching the clock, a pregnant patient declared brain-dead while the fetus is still being gestated on the ventilator — we argue straight past each other because one of us is assuming death is a fact about the brain, another that it's about circulation, and a third that it's basically a legal/social status we assign when it's safe to stop treating. Before I read another paper on whole-brain vs. higher-brain criteria, I want the spec written down: what does an adequate definition of death have to deliver? Not the usual "it must be biologically accurate and clinically applicable," but the real list — cases it has to get right, distinctions it can't collapse (like event vs. process, or being dead vs. being as good as dead vs. being permanently unconscious), and things it has to stay consistent with.

A few candidate requirements I'd want you to actually engage with rather than just list, because I suspect some of them do the heavy lifting or conflict with each other: that the definition yield a test a physician can run at the bedside without waiting days for confirmation; that it leave a safety margin (the old fear of being declared dead too early is basically why anyone cares, and it's why the autoresuscitation window after cardiac arrest scares people about DCD protocols); that it not make death depend on contingent medical technology, since a definition under which someone is dead in a rural clinic but alive at a transplant center seems broken; that it stay neutral on organ transplantation even though the dead donor rule ties procurement to the definition; and that it handle the weird cases — the brain-dead body that heals wounds, gestates a fetus, and keeps growing hair for weeks, or the patient with total loss of the cortex but an intact brainstem. For each requirement I want the actual case or consideration behind it, a verdict on which are non-negotiable and which are just camp preferences dressed up as demands, and — this is the part I most want — whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, such that any definition has to give something up and the interesting question is what. Then one short paragraph at the end on how whole-brain, circulatory, and higher-brain views do against the list, no more than that. Keep it under 800 words please, I'll be circulating it before the next meeting and nobody reads long documents. Table is fine if it helps, but the reasoning behind each requirement has to be in prose, not just bullet labels.
