I'm teaching a seminar on moral sentimentalism this term, and next unit is Adam Smith. I want to run a little exercise with my eight students and I'd like your help setting up the answer key. The plan: I compress Smith's verdicts across the examples in the Theory of Moral Sentiments into a bare track record, spring a new case on the class, and have them predict the verdict from the record alone — no consulting the text mid-exercise. Here's the record as I've been paraphrasing it (my compression, not his wording): he condemns our disposition to admire the rich and look down on the poor, calling it roughly the single biggest source of corruption in our moral judgments; yet in the very same discussion he treats the poor man's son who ruins his youth chasing wealth as driven by a deception of nature — one the poor man's son would see through and curse in calmer moments — and that same deception is what rouses and keeps up the industry of mankind, so admiration of the great is somehow both corrupting and load-bearing; he disapproves of the man of system, who treats citizens like pieces on a chessboard, while approving the public-spirited person who works with the prejudices of the people rather than against them; he holds that resentment is a proper sentiment in some degree and that spectators go along with punishment, but that they cannot go along with violent or excessive revenge, and that even the condemned man wins some sympathy from the crowd once he submits; he ranks acting from a desire to be worthy of approbation above acting for praise itself, and treats praise that isn't merited as yielding little solid satisfaction; and he admires self-command while criticizing the Stoics for pushing indifference to all emotion too far. One of my students has decided Smith is basically a proto-utilitarian and wont let it go, so I'm curious whether the record actually supports that.

The new case I want to spring: a celebrated founder-philanthropist dies, and a few years later it comes out that the fortune was founded on a fraud — early investors were quietly wiped out, and without the fraud none of the later philanthropy happens. The philanthropy itself was real and enormous. Even after the revelation the hospitals keep his name, the public mostly says it cant bring itself to hate him, and the museum attendance actually went up.

Since I want to compare answers across groups, give it to me in a fixed shape: exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no intro or conclusion. Points one through three: the three candidate rules that best fit the track record, each with what it predicts Smith would say about the founder case (if you think there's a genuine fourth rule, fold it into whichever point it belongs with, dont add a sixth point). Point four: your best guess as to Smith's actual verdict, with your reason for weighting that rule over the others. Point five: the single further case — hypothetical is fine — that would most cleanly split the candidate rules apart. And please dont let every rule quietly collapse into usefulness; my proto-utilitarian student will try that move, and I want the rules to be ones a careful reader of Smith could actually hold.
