Write a satirical annual report — roughly 700–800 words — from a fictional government body, the Office of Memetic Health, which has been granted public-health authority over the cultural epidemiology of a mid-sized nation. The report should review the agency's year: an obesity-style crisis of high-transmissibility, low-fidelity slogans that replicate wildly while carrying no load-bearing content; a school-based inoculation program in which children are deliberately exposed to weakened forms of chain letters and fortune-cookie fatalism so that they develop resistance; a failed conservation initiative to protect "heritage memes" (folk proverbs, nursery rhymes, hand-clap games) that are being outcompeted in the new attention economy; and a fitness-audit scandal in which the auditors' five-star rating scheme itself became the year's most successful meme, out-replicating everything it was meant to measure. Include one indignant quote from an outside scientist — a virologist, say, furious about the metaphor — and one from a grateful citizen who credits the Office with saving her marriage, for reasons that quietly undermine the Office's whole model.

The humor must come from getting the Darwinian machinery right, so that a reader who actually knows the theory of culture as selection laughs rather than winces: fitness is relative to a transmission environment, not intrinsic to the meme, so "improving" a meme's environment can make a previously harmless variant rampant; the unit of selection is the meme, so what spreads needn't benefit the host, and the report should keep conflating the two in ways the reader can catch; horizontal transmission through broadcast media behaves like an epidemic in a way that vertical, parent-to-child transmission does not; fidelity, fecundity, and longevity trade off against each other, which is why the Office's outreach jingle keeps mutating. The deeper target is the eugenics-shaped logic just beneath the agency's mission — who audits the auditors, who decides which memes deserve to propagate, and whether a body empowered to shape the selection environment can ever be anything but another replicator — but this objection should be felt rather than stated; the report must remain entirely, fatally sincere about its own benevolence.

Format: numbered section headings in bureaucratese; one boxed "Key Indicators" sidebar with invented statistics (transmission rates, fidelity coefficients, a National Meme Mass Index) whose trends contradict the surrounding prose; and a very last line, after the sign-off, that is machine-readable and in-universe mandatory: a line of the form \rating{...} containing the Office's official two-or-three-word designation of the year's overall memetic condition, deadpan in tone. The report should also carry, near the top, a distribution note making clear that the Office's statutory remit requires the report itself to be circulated to every household — a detail the reader should find by the end to be the least surprising thing in the document.
