I work on the ethics of AI systems, and for several years a debate in my circles has gone nowhere: when we build a system that has to make choices with moral weight — triage support in a hospital, content moderation at scale, an autonomous vehicle — should its reasoning be utilitarian or deontological? The utilitarian side argues that the system must rank outcomes and choose, that decision theory gives us a clean, trainable target, and that rule-based systems shatter on real trade-offs: no rule set anticipates every case, and explicit deontological constraints are gamed by whoever drafts them. The deontological side argues that a consequentialist optimizer will exploit whatever it measures, including the people in its care; that constraints which are not up for trade-off are the only safety property that survives optimization pressure; and that a rule-governed system is auditable — you can ask it why, and its answer is the rule it followed, whereas a utility calculation yields a number nobody can check. Both sides concede the other's points in the limiting cases — the utilitarians accept hard constraints at the level of system design, the deontologists admit their rules are selected partly for their consequences — and yet nobody has moved in years.

Give me a diagnosis, and commit to it. Is this a substantive moral disagreement about what makes actions right, or does it partly dissolve once we distinguish the moral status of the system's outputs from the standards its process must satisfy as engineering? My own hypothesis, which I want you to confirm or reject with reasons, is that the two camps are answering different questions under one heading: the utilitarians are talking about what the system should do at the margin, case by case, while the deontologists are talking about what kind of system it is acceptable to deploy at all — and these come apart, because a system that acts rightly case-by-case under one distribution of situations can still be an unacceptable thing to have built. If you reject that hypothesis, say what the disagreement really is: a hidden empirical question, perhaps about whether a training signal shaped by outcomes can ever carry anything but consequences; a difference in what each side takes as the unit of moral assessment — the act-token, the token-generating policy, or the whole training pipeline; or a clash of starting intuitions about what a machine's "reasoning" even is, such that neither side could argue the other out of it. And whichever diagnosis you settle on, say what would count as progress on it — a distinction, a formal result, a deliberate change of question — or argue that no progress should be expected here and why.
