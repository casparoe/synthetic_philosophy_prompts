I've recently joined the board of the housing cooperative where I've lived for six years, and at this month's meeting I was made chair of a working group that has to recommend, by the end of the quarter, how the co-op should vote on future decisions — starting with three competing renovation proposals for our common house, between which our current plurality rule just produced a winner supported by only about a third of members. The recommendation has become urgent, but the working group is deadlocked because of an argument between two members, Ravi and Elena, that ran for over an hour. I took notes, and I'd like a careful adjudication of it.

Ravi opened by saying the whole exercise is pointless: Arrow's impossibility theorem shows that with three or more options, no ranked-ballot voting method can satisfy a small set of obviously fair conditions, so every alternative to our current rule is as flawed as any other, and we should keep the system everyone already understands rather than chase a fair method that mathematics has proven doesn't exist. Elena replied that this misuses the theorem twice over: it concerns methods that convert individual rankings into a complete social ranking obeying, among other things, independence of irrelevant alternatives — a standard our plurality rule itself does not meet — and impossibility of having everything is not impossibility of doing better; we should choose which condition to give up deliberately rather than by tradition.

Ravi then granted that if Elena wants to slip Arrow's net entirely, she can point to cardinal methods — approval voting, score voting — which don't collect ordinal rankings at all, and which their advocates say sit outside the theorem's assumptions. Elena said good, let's use approval voting: it's simple enough to explain on a ballot and it escapes the impossibility result. Ravi objected that this escape is illusory: score ballots invite voters to report intensities of preference, but there is no common scale on which my seven-out-of-ten and your seven-out-of-ten can be compared, and voters who grasp this will inflate their favorites to the maximum and zero everything else, at which point score voting degenerates into plurality voting with more ink.

Elena answered that the whole fight is being driven by an axiom that doesn't deserve its reputation: independence of irrelevant alternatives is what makes it a scandal when adding a minor, hopeless option to the ballot can flip which of the two front-runners wins, but she didn't see why information from a third option should be discarded — if members' willingness to approve the cheap option tells us something about the strength of their feelings between the expensive and the moderate one, a sensible rule should be allowed to use it. Ravi came back with Gibbard–Satterthwaite: whatever you think of IIA, every voting method with three or more outcomes that isn't an open dictatorship is vulnerable to strategic manipulation, so Elena's implicit promise of a system where honesty is safe is another thing that has been proven not to exist. Elena's last word was that there is a difference between manipulability in an abstract theorem and manipulation in a sixty-household co-op where the members all know each other and will be living with the outcome; meanwhile plurality just handed us a renovation most of us actively opposed, which is a perfectly strategy-proof way of getting a terrible result.

Please adjudicate this exchange round by round: whether Ravi's opening use of Arrow is legitimate or a misreading, whether Elena's first reply gets the theorem's scope right, whether the approval-voting escape is real or Ravi's utility-comparison objection to it is fatal, whether Elena's defense of violating independence of irrelevant alternatives is coherent, and how the exchange over Gibbard–Satterthwaite should be scored, including whether it applies to cardinal methods at all. Where one of them is simply wrong as a matter of logic or mathematics, say so plainly. Then tell me what both of them are missing that a small association choosing a decision rule should actually care about, since that is what my recommendation will have to address.
