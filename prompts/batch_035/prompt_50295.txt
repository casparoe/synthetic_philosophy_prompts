I'm a junior at a public high school that turns one hundred this year, and I sit on the student council committee charged with planning the centennial and, less pleasantly, with resolving a dispute about it. Preparing for that job has left me genuinely confused about what our school's past is *for*, and the adults around me keep giving opposite advice, so I'd like a more careful answer than any of them has managed.

Here is the situation in full. Our school treats its history something like a shrine: a corridor of framed photographs going back decades, a display case of old trophies and letter jackets, an alumni association that appears at every event and addresses us as keepers of a flame. Some of this feels warm, but at times it also feels as though the school believes its best days are behind it and that our job is mainly to conserve them. At the same time, the speakers at our assemblies keep invoking famous alumni — a novelist, an astronaut, a couple of professional athletes — as evidence that greatness is possible here because it happened here once. I find this double-edged: occasionally it stirs me, but more often it makes my own ambitions feel small and belated, as though everything worth doing was done decades before I was born by someone better. And there is a third matter. Last semester a student journalist found, in the school archives, that our founding principal — whose portrait hangs in the front hall — spent years quietly steering Black students away from honors courses. One group of students wants the portrait moved to the archive with an honest caption; a vocal group of alumni calls that "erasing history"; our current principal proposes a "balanced account" that I suspect will satisfy no one. I have to draft the committee's recommendation on the portrait by spring, and I realize I don't know what principle should govern any of it — the shrine, the heroes, or the portrait. The only remark that has stuck with me came from my history teacher, who muttered after one assembly that some philosophers think too much dwelling on the past can become a kind of illness. I don't know whether that's serious or just a grumpy thing to say, but I'd like to find out.

So what I'm asking for is a framework, not just a ruling on the portrait. Please distinguish the different things people can be doing when they invoke the past — preserving it, holding it up as an example, or putting it on trial — and tell me what each is good for and what it costs, since our situation seems to involve all three tangled together. I'd also like you to be candid about where the framework runs out: which parts of our dispute it can genuinely settle and which it can only reframe.

Finally, two requests about the answer itself. First, wherever you rely on a position that serious people actually disagree about, say so plainly — please separate what is settled among those who have studied this from what remains contested, and don't hand me a disputed view as though it were consensus. Second, please organize the reply into five named sections, in this order: "What this is really about" (roughly 150 words, identifying what's at stake beneath the surface of the dispute); "Three ways of using the past" (about 300 words, naming each mode and saying what it serves and what it harms); "Applying it to our situation" (about 200 words, covering the shrine, the heroes, and the portrait in turn); "Where the framework runs out" (about 150 words, on what it cannot decide for us); and "A draft recommendation" (about 150 words, written plainly enough that I could adapt it for the committee).
