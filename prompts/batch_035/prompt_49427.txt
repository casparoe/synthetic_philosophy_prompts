At a team offsite last month our facilitator kept coming back to the ubuntu saying "a person is a person through other persons" — she'd picked it up at a leadership workshop. Fine whatever. But then in the follow-up slack thread a colleague used it to argue that an AI company training models on scraped public posts owes something to everyone whose writing it ingested, because "your words aren't really yours, they only exist because of the community that shaped you." Someone else pushed back that by that logic a hermit raised by wolves wouldn't count as a person at all, which she didn't really have an answer to.

I have a feeling this one sentence is doing a lot of different jobs and nobody's being clear about which one. Can you lay out, as a list I can post back to the thread, the distinct precise claims "a person is a person through other persons" could be making? I'm guessing some readings are basically trivial (yes, humans develop through socialization, thanks), some are substantive metaphysical claims about personhood being constitutively relational, some are normative claims about what we owe each other, and maybe some are just communitarian politics in proverb clothing. For each reading, say whether it's plausible, and crucially whether it would actually support the data-training argument my colleague made — since "your words are community property" seems to need a much stronger reading than "you learned language from others." Also flag which reading gets wrecked by the wolf-child objection and whether the weaker readings can dodge it.

(ps if there's a standard zulu or xhosa original that's commonly cited feel free to mention it, but im mostly after the conceptual menu, not the philology)
