Two colleagues of mine, a philosopher of physics and a historian of science, have been arguing for the better part of a decade about how thought experiments in physics can yield knowledge, and they have finally agreed to run an adversarial collaboration and asked me to secure a drafting of the joint statement. I'd like you to produce that draft.

The first colleague holds a reconstructionist position: every genuine thought experiment is, epistemically speaking, an argument in costume. The premises are borrowed, however tacitly, from prior observation and established theory; the drama of imagining the falling bodies or the demon sorting molecules is a delivery mechanism that makes the conclusion vivid, and the feeling of "seeing" the result is psychology, not epistemology. On this view nothing is learned from a thought experiment that was not already latent in its premises, and the pressing task is to make the hidden premises explicit and check whether they were ever entitled to.

The second colleague holds an irreducibility position: some thought experiments are not arguments at bottom. In the strongest cases — the two unequal weights chained together, which must fall both faster and slower than the heavy one alone, undoing the Aristotelian account of speed; the observer freely falling and finding gravity locally vanished; the demon who sorts fast from slow molecules — the reasoner grasps the result in something like the way one grasps that a figure cannot be both triangular and square, and this grasp is a route to knowledge of what is physically necessary that is neither observation nor derivation. If a reconstruction into explicit premises can always be produced after the fact, that is rationalization after the fact, not the route by which the knowledge was actually gotten.

Both accept, and the joint statement should record, at least the following: that the historical record shows thought experiments doing real work in physics, including work that was then confirmed instrumentally only much later; that thought experiments also mislead — appeals to intuition about simultaneity, about rigid bodies at high speeds, and about the demonstrative power of paradox have all been overturned by later theory, and any acceptable account must explain the difference between the successful and the failed cases, not just celebrate the successful ones; that a thought experiment whose verdict could never conflict with any possible observation, however indirect, has no claim to yield knowledge; and that the nineteenth-century suggestion that thought experiments merely exploit half-digested experience, while capturing something, cannot be the whole story, since some thought experiments overturned the very experiential generalizations they were supposedly parasitic on.

Please structure the draft as follows. Section 1, "Agreed claims": a numbered list, roughly ten items, each phrased so that both colleagues could initial it. Section 2, "The disagreement, in each side's words": two parallel statements of about 120 words each, the first written so the reconstructionist accepts it as a fair statement of the disagreement, the second so the irreducibilist does, and each acknowledging the other's statement as accurate. Section 3, "Cruxes": a table with one row per crux — I would expect at least the following to appear, though you may add or replace: whether a successful thought experiment can always be reconstructed as an explicit argument with no epistemic loss, or whether something knowledge-yielding is lost in the reconstruction; whether the felt "seeing" is a sui generis faculty or trained pattern recognition; and how each account explains thought experiments whose intuitive verdicts were later overturned — with columns for the crux, why settling it would move at least one side, an argument or case both agree bears on it, and each side's prediction recorded in advance. Section 4, "A jointly constructed test case": one new thought experiment, devised in the statement itself, which both agree would discriminate between the accounts, with the verdicts each side predicts it would have and why. Section 5, "Residual disagreement and verbal residues": an accounting of roughly 150 words on which parts of the surviving dispute are genuine and which, if any, are merely verbal — for instance, whether "the thought experiment yielded new knowledge" and "the argument hidden in the thought experiment yielded new knowledge" might be two descriptions of one event rather than two theories.

The whole document should be one both colleagues could sign without wincing: formulations neither vacuous nor slanted, concessions where concessions are honestly due, and no side allowed to define the other's position for it.
