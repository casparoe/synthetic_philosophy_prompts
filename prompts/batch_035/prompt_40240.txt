I chair the prize committee of a learned society that awards its highest honor in chemistry every two years, and I need a decision memo for our upcoming meeting. The case before us is a priority dispute, and the committee is split three ways.

Two research groups independently developed what is recognizably the same catalytic method, announced eleven weeks apart. Team A is led by an eminent chemist at a major institute; Team B is a junior group at a regional university. The timeline is awkward: Team A's result was first presented at a conference talk, video posted online, three weeks before Team B's preprint appeared; Team A's journal paper followed four months later. One member of Team B was in the audience at that talk. Team B's preprint reaches the same result by a route that overlaps in its central step with Team A's conference presentation but differs elsewhere; when asked informally, Team B's PI said their method had been "basically working for a year" and that the preprint was delayed by revisions.

Our bylaws give the committee three live options: a single award to one team on a strict priority rule (first public disclosure, which would be Team A via the conference talk), a joint award to both teams, or splitting the biennial prize into two awards of half prestige and half money. The prize has never been shared in its history.

The committee's disagreement is only partly evidential. One faction holds that priority rules are the only defensible tiebreaker and that softening them invites lobbying; a second thinks simultaneous invention is the norm rather than the exception (they keep invoking the idea that discoveries are typically 'in the air' and made independently by several people at once), so that who crossed the line first is close to arbitrary, and a joint award recognizes what actually happened scientifically; a third worries about the contamination question — if Team B's central step was absorbed from a talk they attended, 'independent' is doing no work, but if it wasn't, denying them the prize punishes them for a coincidence of timing. There is also an unspoken worry that awarding only Team A feeds the tendency for credit to accrue to the already eminent.

Please draft the memo I will circulate: a specific recommendation among the three options (or a reasoned variant, such as joint award contingent on the outcome of a review), the reasoning the committee can act on, a concrete proposal for how to handle the contamination question that doesn't just relabel it as a he-said-she-said, and an honest treatment of the strongest argument for the option you're rejecting. Assume the memo will be read by the society's council, which wants closure at this meeting rather than another year of deliberation.
