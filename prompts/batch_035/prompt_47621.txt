I'm writing a term paper on weakness of will and I've hit a point where I suspect the whole thing is structurally stuck, not just hard. On the philosophy side you've got basically the same three or four positions trading shots since the 70s without anyone converting: the Socratic line that genuine akrasia is impossible, so the cases must involve some kind of ignorance or redescribed option; the Davidson-style line that it's real but rationally anomalous, a choice against your own all-things-considered judgment; and the divided-mind views where different motivational systems just pull in different directions. And the pattern I keep seeing is that whenever someone produces what looks like a clean case of acting against your better judgment, the other side redescribes it until it stops being a case of the thing -- either you didn't really judge it worse, or you judged it worse only under one description, or the conflict wasn't in you but between you and some subsystem. After a while every case gets absorbed and nobody has moved.

Meanwhile there's this whole other literature in behavioral economics that doesn't even use the word akrasia. They model the same behavior with hyperbolic discounting: you prefer studying tomorrow over partying tomorrow, but prefer partying tonight over studying tonight, and the preference reversal just falls out of the shape of the discount curve. And crucially, on that framing nothing is irrational in the way the philosophers mean -- there's no judgment being defied, just an agent whose preferences have a certain temporal structure, and the "conflict" between your selves is predicted, not anomalous. So half the time I can't even tell whether the two fields disagree or are just doing different jobs entirely, and within philosophy itself the debate has this quality of trench warfare where everyone agrees on the cases and disagrees about what the cases are cases OF.

My working hypothesis is that the stall is because the disagreement is at least partly verbal -- the sides mean different things by judgment, preference, and even the word irrational itself -- but layered on top of a real question about whether there is a unified phenomenon there at all, or just a family of loosely similar failures that we've bundled under one word since the Greeks. But I want an actual diagnosis, not a survey. What kind of disagreement is this: verbal, different questions under one heading, a hidden empirical question about how motivation actually works that philosophy can't settle, or bedrock intuitions about what rationality requires that nobody can argue for from a neutral standpoint? And given your diagnosis, what would even count as progress here -- a conceptual distinction, some finding about how preference and judgment are actually realized, a change in how the question gets posed? I'd especially like to know whether the economics framing could be imported to do real work in the philosophy, or whether importing it just quietly changes the subject.
