I'm a PhD student in political science writing a dissertation on the epistemology of political trust, and next month I'm giving a talk at an interdisciplinary workshop on political economy and institutional design. The audience is roughly twenty public choice economists and rational-choice political scientists, plus two economic historians. For most of them, rent-seeking is the default model of government, and they regard appeals to civic culture as soft-headed. They will not ask me what trust really is; they will ask me whether my standard of rationality is circular.

My thesis: the ordinary Scandinavian's trust in government is, epistemically, the more reasonable attitude, and the reflexive American version of suspicion is not. My argument runs as follows. First, I split political trust into competence-trust (belief that the administration can deliver) and intent-trust (belief that it means well), and claim that only the first is directly answerable to evidence. Second, competence-trust should be calibrated against an observable administrative record, and the Nordic states have one that is unusually cheap for a citizen to check. Third, American distrust fails this calibration standard: it is better predicted by partisan identity and generalized social distrust than by any evidence about government performance, and it persists through long stretches of solid administrative performance, which suggests it is not tracking anything. Fourth, I concede that survey-expressed distrust partly coexists with behavioral trust (Americans file taxes and claim benefits at high rates), so some of it is expressive — but I argue the expression still matters, because despised institutions suffer adverse selection into public service, so even cheap-talk distrust is epistemically and practically costly.

Give me the six or so objections this audience will actually raise, in their vocabulary — I expect endogeneity, survivorship, cheap talk, adverse selection, and something like a Goodhart point, but tell me what I'm missing — ordered by how much damage each would do, each phrased the way a public choice economist would phrase it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what to concede up front to buy credibility, and include the question I am hoping nobody asks: I suspect it is the one about whether my standard collapses into saying that trust is reasonable wherever trust has so far been vindicated, with no forward-looking content at all — and if that is the killer, say so plainly rather than reassuring me.
