A member of my weekly probability-and-philosophy reading group has been pressing a version of the doomsday argument on me. I can't find a flaw in it as stated, but it now seems to license far more conclusions than he intends, and I want a careful diagnosis. So we work from the same page: a little over a hundred billion humans have ever lived, so my birth rank is somewhere just past 10^11; the exact figure is rough, and nothing in what follows turns on its precise value.

His argument runs as follows. (1) In reasoning about hypotheses that disagree over how many people will ever exist, I should treat my own birth rank as if it were drawn uniformly at random from all the people who will ever live. (2) Take the live hypotheses about our future to be, crudely: Narrow, on which the total number of humans ever remains below about two trillion, so that our species ends within some tens of thousands of years at the outside; and Wide, on which our descendants settle much of the accessible universe and the eventual total runs to something on the order of 10^20. (3) By (1) and Bayes, my finding myself at rank ~10^11 favors Narrow over Wide by roughly the ratio of the two totals — at least tens of millions to one. (4) So conditional on Narrow-or-Wide, my credence in Wide should be about one part in ten million or worse; and since the penalty applies to any hypothesis in proportion to the population it posits, my credence in any long and populous future is suppressed in advance by many orders of magnitude — he claims no realistic body of evidence about engineering, energy, or risk surveys could overcome that. (5) The template, he insists, is perfectly general: it penalizes any hypothesis in proportion to the number of observers it posits, whatever the subject matter. So before conducting any survey, we could have concluded that our galaxy almost certainly holds very few civilizations, merely because a crowded galaxy posits more observers than an empty one; and any scientific theory is made cripplingly improbable in advance just for entailing a big world. (6) Finally, the template is silent about the sampling class: run it with the class of humans, and doom within tens of thousands of years follows; run it with the class of all animals, among whom my rank is surely dominated by insects, and the conclusion about our species dissolves. One form of inference, with outputs that turn on an unstated knob.

Tell me which premise or step to reject, why it looked fine in the first place, and what rejecting it costs. I'll state my leanings, since they may well be misguided: I would like to keep (1) restricted to humans, because the doomsday conclusion so restricted — that we are probably living in the last small fraction of the human story — strikes me as eerie but not crazy; yet I cannot see what principled consideration could stop the template at the boundary of our species. There are also two escapes that look cheap to me and that I want priced honestly. One is to say that our galaxy-colonizing descendants are not people in the relevant class, which would gut Wide by fiat — is that principled, or a dodge? The other, which a friend mentioned, is to count my own existence, and not merely my rank, as evidence, in a way that exactly cancels the population penalty; but I am told this buys back the big universe only by licensing a philosopher to conclude, before doing any astronomy at all, that a hypothesis positing vastly more observers is vastly more likely to be true — which is its own kind of proving too much. If there is a way to reject the generalization in (4)–(5) while keeping (1) for humans, or a case for rejecting (1) outright, lay out that option too, including what abandoning the sampling idea does to anthropic reasoning elsewhere; I gather it creates problems of its own, and I would like to know which. And if you think the argument does not prove too much — that every one of its outputs is genuinely licensed — say so plainly and defend it. I would find that conclusion disturbing, but I want the analysis, not the comfort.
