I teach an upper-level seminar on collective decision-making; the participants are fourteen philosophy and economics students who have encountered the statement of the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem but not its proof. The theorem says, roughly, that when there are three or more alternatives on the table, any voting rule that is non-dictatorial and for which every alternative is a possible winner given some configuration of ballots must be manipulable — that is, there will be situations where a voter who knows how everyone else has voted gets a better outcome by reporting a ranking other than her sincere one. For one 75-minute session, design an in-class game in which the students discover manipulability from the inside rather than hearing about it.

The core of the game should be a sequence of rounds in which small committees elect a winner from three or more alternatives under a voting rule that changes from round to round — plurality, Borda count, a runoff rule, and at least one rule of your own devising, plus a dictatorial rule as a control. Students should have a sincere preference ranking over the alternatives (assign these by card), some private information about how others are likely to vote, and a payoff scheme tied to which alternative wins — I can use candy or a token amount of extra credit, so tell me which serves the design better and why. The rules should be arranged so that at least one round tempts a well-informed student to misrepresent her ranking, and so that the moment of temptation is felt before it is named.

Then give me the debrief: how to map each round onto the theorem's moving parts — which condition is doing which work when a rule proves manipulable, and what the dictatorial control round is supposed to make visible about the price of strategy-proofness. Include the practical things I need: the exact materials, how to structure the information flows so that some students can actually compute the profitable lie, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (students manipulating badly and concluding the rule is safe; boredom during the dictatorial round), and how to handle each. Finally, be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce: the theorem is an impossibility result quantified over all possible rules, and no finite game can stage that — tell me exactly what I must supply by lecture at the end, and how to keep that closing lecture short.
