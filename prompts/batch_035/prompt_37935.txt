I'm in a post-Keynesian economics reading group that's been working through Keynes's 1937 piece in the Quarterly Journal of Economics, the one where he replies to his critics and ends up explaining why he thinks investment is so unstable. The passage everyone in the group treats as scripture: he lists the kind of questions an investor actually faces — the prospect of a European war, the price of copper twenty years hence, the rate of interest twenty years hence, the obsolescence of a new plant — and says, roughly, that about these matters there is no scientific basis for forming any calculable probability whatever, and that on these questions we simply do not know. And then the part the group really loves: he goes on that the necessity for action and decision compels practical men to overlook this awkward fact, so they end up behaving exactly as if they had a good Benthamite calculation of the pros and cons behind them, when the honest answer has been pulled out of the air.

In the group this has become a slogan — "we simply do not know" — deployed to end arguments, most recently against a classmate of mine who is Bayesian-inclined. He said, reasonably I thought, that even without frequency data a person could put a prior on where interest rates will be in twenty years and update as evidence comes in, and he was told, in effect, that Keynes had shown that doing this is unscientific, and that was that. My instinct is that the slogan is doing a lot more work than the passage actually licenses, but when I tried to say why out loud it came out as hand-waving. So this is what I want from you: a menu of the distinct precise claims "we simply do not know" could be making in that passage, laid out roughly from weakest to strongest. Maybe something like: the boring reading where it just means no actuarial/frequency basis is available (surely true but nobody disputes it); a middle reading turning on the fact that Keynes had this earlier book, the Treatise on Probability, where as I understand it probabilities don't have to be numerical and there's some extra notion like the "weight" of an argument, so maybe the 1937 line is a claim inside his own framework about non-numerical probabilities (I honestly don't know if that's relevant, tell me); and the radical reading, which is how my reading group uses it, that no assignment at all — not even a subjective degree of belief — is even intelligible for questions like these, so the Bayesian isn't just wrong, he's doing something meaningless.

For each reading, I'd want a verdict: is it plausible on its own, is it plausibly what the 1937 Keynes is committed to, and — this is the part I actually need — does that reading support using the line to shut down my classmate, or does it only support something much weaker, like "your prior is a guess and you should hold it loosely"? If some reading does license the shut-down move, say so honestly; if the strong reading only works combined with extra premises nobody in the group has stated out loud, I want that pointed out, since I suspect it's the unstated premises doing all the work. Something I can actually bring back to the group and defend without sounding like I'm just being contrarian.
