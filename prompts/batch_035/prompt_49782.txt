I'm an American academic on secondment at an Australian university, and last week I watched a one-liner do real philosophical work without anyone noticing it was doing any. At a staff meeting, someone proposed a monthly slide publicly celebrating colleagues' grants, prizes, and promotions. A senior colleague objected: tall poppies get cut down — publicly elevating people just invites resentment and backlash, so we'd be harming the very people we meant to honor. Having been raised on the opposite folk theory, the American Dream's injunction that visible success deserves a spotlight and that celebrating it is how a society tells its children the ladder is real, I honestly could not tell what claim I had just been given. Was it a sociological prediction about how communities react to excellence? A norm about how they should react? Advice to the successful about self-presentation? An accusation that egalitarian culture is envy in formal dress?

Please disentangle the distinct precise claims that slogan could be expressing. I can see at least these candidates, and probably there are more: that resentment of the successful is a near-universal social fact that celebration will predictably trigger; that there is a legitimate norm of equal standing that visible elevation threatens and that cutting poppies protects; that the successful have a duty of modesty that public praise would corrupt; that the phrase names a vice of the culture uttering it rather than a feature of success itself; and that it's a bit of prudential insurance marketed as wisdom. For each reading, tell me whether it is plausible, whether it is a platitude dressed up as insight, and — this is the part I actually need — whether it supports the use my colleague put it to, namely withholding institutional praise from high performers. Note that some readings might support the opposite conclusion: if cutting poppies is a vice, the slogan could be an argument for deliberately celebrating people as a way of changing the culture's habits rather than deferring to them.

Present the readings as a numbered menu I can bring back to the next meeting. Where you can, distinguish how the sentence functions when uttered approvingly, as our Danish exchange students do when they invoke Jantelagen to explain why they'd find a public award ceremony mortifying, from how it functions when uttered resignedly, as my Australian colleague did — the same words, I suspect, may be doing different jobs in those two mouths. And tell me which reading, if any, is the one a person who uses the slogan to end an argument is most likely to actually mean, as opposed to the one they could most defensibly be interpreted as meaning.
