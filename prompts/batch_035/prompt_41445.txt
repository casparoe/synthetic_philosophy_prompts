Last night a friend and I argued until nearly two in the morning about epiphenomenalism, and I have not been able to put it out of my mind since. She accepts the standard causal picture — every report I give of my own experience is a physical event, produced by earlier physical events — yet she insists her pain is real and not reducible to any of that. When I pressed her on the paradox of phenomenal judgment (if the experience itself does no causal work, then nothing about my sincere claim to be in pain owes anything to the pain; my identical zombie twin, with the same neural history and no experience at all, would report pain with equal conviction), she said only that this is the price of taking both science and experience seriously, and that the alternatives charge more. We ended nowhere, which I understand is where these arguments tend to end.

I would like a poem that keeps the argument open rather than settling it. Please write a sestina, in the first person, set in the aftermath of such an argument. Choose the six end-words from two families that never intermarry: words belonging to the physical story (brain, firing, report, signal — words of that register) and words belonging to the felt side (ache, red, hush — words of that one). One end-word, let it be the word for the feeling itself, should return in every stanza without ever standing as the subject of a verb that makes anything happen: it comes back on schedule regardless of what the lines are doing, the way the phenomenal, on the view we were fighting about, accompanies every process while pushing none of it. Somewhere in the poem the zombie twin should appear — her version of me, saying what I say with the same causal history and, by hypothesis, nothing it is like to be her. The envoi must not adjudicate. Let it register instead that my own certainty that I am not her is precisely the kind of item the theory owes an account of, and leave that standing unresolved.

The doctrine should be rendered accurately enough that a philosopher of mind would not wince: epiphenomenalism is the view that experience is caused by physical processes but causes nothing further in return, and the paradox arises specifically for our judgments and reports about experience, not for our knowledge of the world. Someone who has never heard of any of this should still find it a good poem about an argument no one could win.
