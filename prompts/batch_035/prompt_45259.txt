so I'm a novelist, and the narrator of the book I'm plotting is a moral philosopher whose views I've basically stolen for myself at this point. before I write her into a corner I want to know if she (I) am coherant. her commitments, which are now mine:

1. contractualism, Scanlon flavor: an act is wrong if any principle permitting it could reasonably be rejected by someone it burdens. crucially, individual complaints don't sum across people — that's the feature I love, it's what stops the surgeon from harvesting one patient's organs to save five.
2. on the organ case itself she's firm: you don't do it, the one person's complaint simply dominates.
3. but on the transmitter case — one engineer dies, versus a billion fans each missing the match — she'd say broadcast, and I agree. numbers have to count somewhere.
4. her official line on numbers is pairwise comparison: you weigh any single complainer's grievance against any single beneficiary's, never against the total. numbers matter when complaints are roughly comparable in strength, and that's it.
5. and she's anti-utilitarian to the bone — no interpersonal summing of wellbeing at the foundation of ethics, ever — and she's supposed to win every arguement with the utilitarian foil character on the page.

does this hang together? my worry is I can't derive (3) from (1) plus (4) no matter how I turn it, but I also can't bring myself to think (3) is false. is there a fix that doesn't smuggle aggregation back in and hand the organ surgeon his argument, or does something have to give? tell me where it actually breaks, how badly, and what the cheapest revision is — rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them flat. and if you think I've misdescribed the view somewhere, or rigged the set so no answer exists, say that straight out instead of being polite — she'd notice, and so would my editor.
