I have no academic background — I worked in insurance claims for thirty years — but for the last few years I have been thinking hard, mostly alone and partly in an online forum, about the asymmetry at the heart of David Benatar's case against bringing people into existence. As I understand it, the asymmetry has four clauses: the presence of pain is bad; the presence of pleasure is good; the absence of pain is good even though there is nobody to enjoy that absence; but the absence of pleasure is not bad unless there is somebody who is deprived of it. If that is right, then coming into existence is never a net benefit to the person who comes into existence, and creating a person can only impose a risk of harm without offering a compensating good.

Here is my problem. This argument has been around for a while now, and both in the literature I have struggled through and in my own arguments, nobody moves an inch. The odd part is that almost everyone already accepts the intuitive core of clauses three and four in ordinary cases: we say it is a mercy that a child doomed to agony was never conceived, and we feel no regret on behalf of a merely happy possible child who was not. Yet the same people who grant that refuse the conclusion, and when pressed they either switch to talking about a different, luckier child who could have been born instead, or they say our judgments about our own lives' worth cannot be trusted because we are built to overestimate them — which cuts both ways, since Benatar's side uses exactly that to explain away the objections.

My working theory, offered only as a hypothesis, is that the two sides are evaluating the absence of pleasure from two different standpoints. The defenders score nonexistence from no standpoint at all, like a ledger with no one to owe anything to. The opponents cannot help scoring it from the standpoint of an almost-existing person — someone the decision was, in some sense, about. If that is even roughly right, the disagreement might not be about value at all, but about whether an evaluation with no subject behind it is intelligible, or whether it is just blank paper pretending to be a ledger.

So I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is this a substantive disagreement about the structure of value, a verbal one about what a harm is (comparing an actual life with nonexistence versus comparing it with other lives the same parents might have had), or two groups answering different questions — whether a life is worth starting versus whether it is worth continuing — under a single heading? And given your diagnosis, tell me honestly what, if anything, would count as progress: a thought experiment that forces one comparison rather than the other, a formal constraint from population ethics, or nothing at all, because here the bedrock intuitions are the only evidence there is.
