I'm a second-year graduate student in cryptography, and last semester our reading group worked through the research program on formalizing heuristic arguments and the presumption of independence — the idea being that when we can't prove hardness of, say, inverting a well-designed hash function, we still reason informally that the function should behave like a random function unless an efficient algorithm exploits some structure, and the hope is to make that style of reasoning precise: a formal criterion specifying which arguments count, such that anything certified by an argument in the class is actually true. My advisor is deeply skeptical; a friend who works on AI safety is enthusiastic, because the same formalism is supposed to license conclusions about systems far too messy for ordinary proof — for instance, whether a model whose many components each contribute a small effect will fail roughly independently, which bears on how much comfort we can take from an ensemble or a security margin. After a long argument we realized our overall credences are about 0.15 (my advisor's side) and 0.7 (my friend's) that a genuinely useful formalization exists, where "useful" means a criterion that is sound (never certifies a false claim), covers a non-trivial fraction of the arguments practitioners actually give, and is precise enough that one can check whether a given argument belongs to the class.

Give me your own number, but the number is the least of it — what I want is the decomposition. Which sub-questions does this credence factor into, what rough probability do you assign each, and how do the terms combine? A few I'd expect to see: the probability that the known counterexamples are fatal rather than merely showing the criterion must be incomplete — random self-reducibility shows that treating a problem as if it were a random instance can fail badly for problems where instances can be efficiently rerandomized, and there is a construction showing that any fixed, complete class of efficient heuristic arguments can be fooled by functions that look pseudorandom but are actually structured, so it would certify security for a function that is easy to invert; but everyone already accepts incompleteness, and one can imagine answering the counterexample by making the class of arguments hard enough to search that finding the fooling function costs more work than the argument class itself requires, so maybe soundness is recoverable from computational inaccessibility. Also, the probability that soundness-plus-interesting-coverage is even a coherent target, and the probability that the formalism, if it exists, could be extended from cryptographic objects to things like learned models, where we have no design story for why the components are independent at all. Finally, tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to, what development — an impossibility theorem closing off the computational-inaccessibility escape, a positive soundness result in a toy domain, or empirical evidence that learned models fail dependently in ways no heuristic argument predicted — would move your number most, and where you think a person at 0.15 and a person at 0.7 most plausibly diverge: on the soundness question, on how much coverage is enough to count as useful, or on whether the AI-safety application is even the same question as the cryptographic one.
