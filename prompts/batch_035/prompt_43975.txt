I'm teaching a 200-level course on personal identity, cross-listed with religious studies, and we're moving from Parfit onto the Buddhist anatta (no-self) material. The problem I keep hitting: by the time we get there, the students have already accepted psychological-continuity views and read the teletransporter cases, so they treat no-self as anticlimactic — "oh sure, there's no soul, the self is just a bundle, big deal, we basically already said that." They nod along and then, ten minutes later in discussion, say things like "but *I'm* still the one experiencing it" without noticing anything's happened. The intellectual acceptance and the felt conviction are completely decoupled, and I want an activity that puts them in a room with that gap instead of me lecturing about it.

So: 18 students, one 75-minute session, classroom with movable chairs. Design a structured activity (game, role-play, staged search — your call, but it can't just be discussion) where participants actively try to locate or catch the "one who" — the experiencer, the decider, the referent of the first-person pronoun — in some live task, and the structure of the activity itself keeps making that referent slip away. Hard constraints: no guided meditation, nothing that requires sitting quietly with eyes closed (my skeptical students will treat it as woo and check out, and the two who've done retreats will dominate). It should work with ordinary classroom stuff — paper, cards, tasks, talking — and it should NOT have students who think no-self is trivially true coasting through; ideally the activity embarrasses that confidence in a friendly way. Also, if there's a way to get at the two-level point — that denying a findable self doesn't commit you to talking funny at the dinner table, that "I" remains perfectly usable even once you've looked and not found — I'd love the activity to hit that, since it's the part my students actually trip on when they try to be clever about it.

Please give me: rules and materials, a minute-by-minute-ish timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the actual philosophical content (the unfindability of the owner of experience, conventional vs. ultimate truth, whatever else naturally arises). Also tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I assume someone will try to be clever, or the room will get overly mystical, or the activity will quietly smuggle in a homunculus. And be frank about which part of the traditional anatta teaching no classroom exercise can reproduce — my sense is the soteriological piece, where no-self is supposed to matter because it loosens clinging, is exactly the part that can't come through in an hour of games, but I'd take your view on it. Oh and one more thing: I'd rather the students not get the term "anatta" until the debrief — they shouldn't know going in that this is the Buddhism unit catching up with them.
