I am a retired physical chemist — thirty years in spectroscopy before I left the bench — and for the past few years I have been teaching an introductory ethics course in my university's continuing-education program. This term we covered the debate between moral particularism and moral generalism, and since my own instincts still run toward laws and invariances, I built the essay question around a comparison with the natural sciences. One of my students, a thoughtful adult learner, has submitted the answer reproduced below. I would like your help grading it.

The essay question I set was:

"Particularists such as Jonathan Dancy claim that moral thought does not proceed by subsuming cases under general principles; generalists insist that it must. Explain the disagreement as precisely as you can, making clear what each side means by a principle and why the exceptionlessness of principles is at issue. Then consider: does the fact that our ordinary moral principles seem to admit of exceptions favor the particularist, or does the generalist have a reply? Finally, does the analogy with laws in the natural sciences — which may be only approximately true and yet remain indispensable — illuminate this debate, or mislead us about it?"

The student's answer, in full:

"Generalism is the view that moral reasoning works by subsuming cases under principles: to decide whether an action is wrong, you locate the relevant principle — lying is wrong, promises ought to be kept — and apply it to the case. Particularism, associated with Jonathan Dancy, denies that principles play this role. The particularist's central claim is holism about reasons: whether a feature of a situation counts in favor of an action depends on the other features present. That I promised is normally a reason to do what I promised, but if I promised only under duress, it may count for nothing, or even against. Since no principle like promise-keeping counts in favor can be exceptionlessly true, judgement must operate case by case.

The particularist can point to W. D. Ross, who held that our duties are only prima facie and can always be overridden, and concluded from this that ethics can never be made systematic — that all we can ultimately do is weigh each case on its merits. So even among the classic theorists, the particularist suspicion of principles is well represented.

Nevertheless, I side with the generalist, because science shows that laws can survive exceptions. Newtonian mechanics is strictly speaking false, yet it remains the workhorse of engineering. The sensible moral theory should likewise rest on principles that are approximately true, and the particularist's demand for exceptionless moral laws is one that no working scientist would make either.

There is also a deeper problem. The particularist's position amounts to subjectivism about ethics. If no principle holds across cases, then two people facing identical situations could each be right even if they reached opposite verdicts, since there would be nothing general for either of them to answer to. Ethics would collapse into private preference. Since no serious philosopher accepts that, particularism should be rejected, and ethics should model itself on the approximately true principles of science."

The course uses letter grades from A to F, with pluses and minuses. Please assign the answer a grade and justify it with point-by-point comments: specify what the student gets right (so that credit lands where it is due), and identify each error, conflation, or misattribution precisely enough that the student could correct it in a rewrite. I should say that I grade the philosophical content, not elegance of prose, and that I do not reward or penalize a student for which side of the debate they end up on — only for the accuracy of the exposition and the validity of the reasoning.
