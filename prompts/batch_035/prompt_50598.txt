my brother-in-law is really into econ blogs and when I mentioned I'm taking a course on epistemic game theory he sent me a post he liked that explains the common prior assumption. I copied the bit he sent:

"Game theorists assume that all players in a game share a common prior. This means that if the players are rational, they must all hold the same beliefs about how the game will go, because rationality pins down a unique set of beliefs. This is what allowed Aumann to prove that rational people can never disagree: if you and I have seen different information but we're both rational, we'll converge to the same view. The same idea explains why there's so little speculative trade in real markets — the no-trade theorem shows no rational person would ever buy a stock from another rational person, so all the trading volume we observe is basically evidence that real people aren't rational. These days most game theorists consider the assumption refuted and have dropped it."

Something about this felt off given what we covered in class (we spent a while on what the prior is even a prior *of*, and on how the assumption is about the information structure, not about rationality), but the post is by someone who seems to know the literature, and honestly the no-trade part sounds right to me. Also the post later concludes that since priors can't really be common, Bayesian epistemology is a dead end as a description of actual disagreement — does that follow from anything that survives your corrections?
