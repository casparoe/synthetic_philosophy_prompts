Our philosophy of physics reading group has been circling supertasks, and last Thursday two members, Ada and Ben, spent the whole session arguing about Thomson's lamp — the one that is switched on after a minute, off after another half-minute, and so on through a halving sequence of toggles, with the question being what state the lamp is in at the end of the minute. I took notes because the exchange went somewhere I hadn't seen it go before, and I genuinely can't tell who came out ahead. The moves were roughly as follows.

(1) Ada began with the standard observation that the description seems to leave the lamp's final state completely unset: nothing in the infinite toggling history dictates "on" rather than "off" at the limit point. But she drew a stronger conclusion than usual: since the lamp, if it existed, would have to be determinately on or determinately off at the end, and since no fact of the story settles which, the scenario is not merely incomplete but incoherent — a reductio of the supertask itself. A world that performs infinitely many discrete toggles in finite time would have to land somewhere undetermined, she said, and since reality cannot be indeterminate in that way, such a lamp cannot exist. (2) Ben answered that she had confused an underspecified description with an impossible situation: the story simply says nothing about the state at the limit point, the way a description of a sequence of positions needn't fix any limit at all, and the right reaction is to note that the description fails to determine an answer — not that every completion of it describes a contradiction. Nothing forces us to think the pre-noon history plus the specification is meant to be exhaustive; a fuller physical account (the switch's material microstructure, the exact laws) could settle the matter or leave it to a genuinely chancy final event.

(3) Ada then introduced a variant she'd invented on the bus: suppose the lamp is not toggled at all, but instead has a dimmer that moves continuously, the brightness easing toward a limit value of exactly one-half by the end of the minute, never flipping. Here, she said, the final state is fixed without any mystery — the limit exists. So the problem with Thomson's original lamp is not infinity per se but the discontinuity of a genuine toggle: an infinite task with a convergent profile is fine, and an infinite task with an oscillating one is impossible. Since real switches have inertia and cannot change state instantaneously, she concluded the original lamp is physically, maybe metaphysically, banned. (4) Ben thought the variant proved too much: it shows that when we specify a continuous easing function, the limit of that function is what the function's limit is — a mathematical triviality, not a physical constraint. He offered to extend her dimmer story with one extra clause: at the end of the minute, an independent mechanism — say, a timer that has nothing to do with the dimming — snaps the lamp off. The pre-minute brightness profile is unchanged and still converges to one-half, yet the lamp is plainly off. So the convergent history does not determine the limit-point state even in her own variant; the limit of a function over open intervals simply does not control the value at the endpoint, and no amount of continuity talk changes that. (5) For a final move, Ada replied that Ben's added timer merely smuggles in a new cause for the final state, which concedes her real point: some determiner of the noon state over and above the toggling history must exist, and for the original lamp there is nowhere for such a determiner to come from — the switch has done all the causing, and it has caused no fact. Ben's closing remark was that she was still treating the story as if it were the world: the argument shows the telling is incomplete, not that no world could realize it, and "I can't see where the determiner would come from" is a statement about her imagination, not about possibility.

Could you adjudicate this move by move? For each of the five moves, say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or somewhere in between, with a brief justification. I'd especially like to know whether Ben's point about the limit of a function not constraining the value at the endpoint really does dissolve Ada's dimmer-lamp argument, and whether her final move — that the toggling history leaves no "room" for a determiner — is a genuine constraint on possibility or just an epistemic gap. Please also say who is more right overall, and identify anything important that both of them missed about what Thomson's lamp is supposed to teach us.
