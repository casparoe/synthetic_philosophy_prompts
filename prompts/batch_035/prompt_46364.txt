I sit on the internal review board at a mid-size AI lab that does a lot of RL research. A team has a proposal pending: they want to train agents in a pretty punishing adversarial enviroment where the agent takes negative reward for mistakes basically the whole time it's "awake," and by design the value function ends up pessimistic over most of the states it occupies. Another board member has put forward what she half-jokingly calls a "no lingering dread" rule: reject or condition any training run where the agent's learned value function encodes sustained negative expectancy across states it spends a lot of time in, with no reachable region where the outlook is better. Her thought is that if anything in an RL agent could map onto something like suffering, the best candidate isn't "negative reward occurred" (that's every RL run ever) but a persistently negative value function over states it can't escape.

The board is split. Half think this is the right first cut at a welfare policy for RL training. The other half think it's animism dressed up as governance — reward is a training signal, not a sensation, nothing in the agent plays the role pain plays for us (no persistent record of the episode, no trade-offs the agent makes outside the objective it was given, etc.), and passing rules on this basis will make us look unserious.

You're a philosopher of mind who's also genuinely comfortable with the basics of RL — value functions, reward, how the agent actually works. Please think carefully before you write, and I'd like you to start with a bit of brainstorming: sketch the strongest considerations each side will bring up. But then commit — don't end with "the board must weigh these itself." Draft the memo we'd circulate: a clear bottom line (adopt the rule as proposed, reject it, or adopt with conditions — say which conditions), the reasoning behind it, an honest treatment of the strongest point on the losing side, and something the lab can actually operationalize: what we would even measure to enforce or evaluate the rule, and what would count as a reason to revisit the decision in a few years. Don't just give me a tour of positions on machine consciousness — I want a decision with your name on it, and I want the memo strong enough that the losing side reads it and says "ok, that's the argument I have to beat."
