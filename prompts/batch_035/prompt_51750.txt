I'm a high school junior writing an extended essay for my diploma program on whether introspection — looking inward to find out what's going on in your own mind — can be trusted. Rather than summarizing the famous studies, I decided to work from my own gut verdicts on cases and then find the principle behind them, but I've hit a wall. Please take on the role of a professional philosopher of mind and help me work this out. Take your time and think carefully before committing to an answer; I'd appreciate it if you started with some brainstorming — a few candidate principles — and then tested each against my cases rather than jumping to one.

Here are my verdicts, as honestly as I can state them:

- When I stub my toe, my introspective report that I'm in pain is about as certain as anything I know. Trustworthy.
- Before a big exam I can look inward and find racing thoughts and dread, and I trust that report that I'm anxious. Trustworthy.
- But here's one that bugs me: last week I only realized I was angry at my brother when I noticed I'd been gripping my pen hard enough to hurt my hand. Before noticing the grip, introspection told me I was calm. I lean toward saying the anger was already there and introspection just missed it — which seems bad for introspection.
- When I picked next year's classes, I told everyone (and sincerely believed) I chose AP Chemistry because I love chemistry, but looking at my transcript, I chose every class my friends chose. I now distrust that introspected reason, even though it felt sincere at the time.
- I once read about an experiment where people are shown two faces, asked which is more attractive, and then handed the face they rejected along with a made-up explanation of why they chose it — and many people don't notice the switch and cheerfully explain a choice they never made. Assuming that's real, my verdict is that the subjects' introspected reasons were confabulated, even though those reasons felt exactly as confident as my report of a stubbed toe.
- Placebos: if a sugar pill takes away my headache, my introspection that the pain is gone is trustworthy, even though my belief that the pill caused the relief is not. Same episode, two different verdicts about my own mind.
- Empathy gap: after lunch I genuinely introspect that I'll skip dessert tonight, and at 9 p.m. I genuinely introspect that I meant to. Each report feels completely honest while it's happening, and I trust each one about how I feel now — but I've stopped trusting either as a prediction of what I'll feel later.

The hard pair, as I see it: my introspected reason for taking AP Chemistry felt just as immediate and confident as my introspected pain from the stubbed toe, yet I trust one and not the other. And my anger revealed itself only through my body, even though feelings are supposedly the very thing introspection is best at.

So here are my questions. Is there one principle that saves all of these verdicts — perhaps something about which kinds of mental states introspection is reliable for, or under which conditions? If a simpler principle can't save them all, which of my verdicts should I give up, and why those? Please state the winning principle as precisely as you can, show me which case motivates each part of it, tell me what it predicts on cases I haven't listed, and be honest about which of my verdicts it simply cannot accommodate. Then apply it to the case I keep going back and forth on: tonight I introspect that I want to go to Saturday's party, but a nagging voice says I only want to say yes so my friends won't stop inviting me. On your principle, should I trust the first report, the second one, or neither?
