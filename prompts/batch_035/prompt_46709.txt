I'm an environmental economist — carbon pricing and leakage is basically my whole career, PhD on emissions embodied in trade, then a few years advising on border carbon adjustments. My brother keeps going on about AI safety stuff whenever we talk, and the thing he keeps coming back to is what he calls the "alignment tax" and how it butts up against what he calls the "competitiveness constraint": the idea that making an AI system safe costs something (extra training time, refusing some tasks, being less useful in some ways), and that if the cost is real, any lab that pays it gets outcompeted by one that doesn't, so the safety work either has to be nearly free or forced on everyone at once. When he explains this I keep thinking it's just the leakage problem with different nouns — a carbon price in one jurisdiction raises local production costs, production shifts to places without the tax, and the environmental gain leaks away; his version is safety costs raising one lab's costs, users and capital shifting to the reckless lab, and the safety gain leaking away. He half-agreed but said I was flattening something important.

So here's what I want. Take the debate he's actually in — whether the alignment tax is real, whether it shrinks as the research matures, whether safety measures are more like scrubbers (pure compliance cost) or more like quality control (arguably good for the product), the racing dynamic between labs and between countries, and the proposals people float for handling it (voluntary restraint, coordinated pauses, compute governance, liability rules) — and restate all of it in my vocabulary: compliance costs, the pollution haven hypothesis, leakage rates, border adjustments, the Porter hypothesis (regulation inducing innovation that offsets its own cost), the difference between a tax and a standard, first-best versus second-best instruments.
