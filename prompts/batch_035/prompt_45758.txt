I have no background in philosophy or statistics — I work in construction management and think about this sort of thing on my own time — so please keep the language plain throughout.

Here's what I've been chewing on. Some years ago I ran across an old mathematical result about voting, usually called the jury theorem: if a large group votes yes-or-no on a question with a definite right answer, and each individual voter is just slightly more likely than a coin flip to be right — say 51% — then the majority is very likely to be correct, and its reliability approaches certainty as the group grows. I accept that the arithmetic is fine, so my question isn't about the math. It's about whether the conclusion describes anything in the real world.

Three things bother me. First, the fine print apparently requires voters' mistakes to be independent of one another, and I can't see how that survives contact with reality: if a million people all formed their views from the same handful of sources, they will be wrong together, and the million looks like one person counted a million times. Second, the competence condition: on a genuinely hard question I can imagine the average voter sitting below 50%, and I gather that makes the theorem run in reverse — the majority becoming nearly certain to be wrong. Third, and this is my own wrinkle: most of what large groups actually vote on doesn't look like a question with a definite right answer at all, so I suspect the whole apparatus quietly only covers trivia, and that where it matters most the "right answer" may be something nobody in the crowd is well placed to see.

So here is what I want from you. My friend, who trusts the crowd, puts the probability at 0.9 that in a real referendum of a million ordinary people on a hard but factual question — say, which of two proposed safety regulations would actually save more lives — the majority would land on the true answer. I put it at about 0.5, mostly because of the independence worry. Give me your own number, but the number is the least of it: decompose the estimate into the terms it rests on — the chance that typical voters on such a question are even slightly better than chance, the chance that their mistakes are correlated enough to swamp the group size, how much it matters that the right answer may be practically undiscoverable, and anything else you think belongs — assign each term a rough probability with a one-line justification, and show how they combine into your figure. Then tell me which single term my friend and I most likely disagree about, and what evidence of the kind I could realistically get without being a researcher would move your number the most. Please keep the entire answer under 450 words, and give your final number in the first sentence.
