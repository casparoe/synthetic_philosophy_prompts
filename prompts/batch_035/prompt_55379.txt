A friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about Bertrand's paradox — the chord drawn at random in a circle, and the question of whether it is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. She holds that the three rival answers, one third, one half, and one quarter depending on how the random chord is parameterized, show that the principle of indifference is not merely incomplete but incoherent: if "all chords equally possible" cannot be made sense of even within a circle, then any attempt to extract probabilities from ignorance alone is a mirage, and every uniform prior is really a disguised substantive assumption. I want to resist her, and my resistance takes the form of a question I could not answer to her satisfaction: why is this not simply an empirical matter? If we physically generate chords — throwing long straws from a distance at a circle chalked on the pavement, or having a computer mark random pairs of points on the circumference — the long-run frequency will converge on some number. Surely that number tells us which of the three parameterizations is the correct description of the setup, the one nature itself endorses, and once we know it the principle of indifference is rehabilitated: we simply apply it in the right variables. My friend kept insisting that specifying the experiment already smuggles in the answer, but I do not see how — a random straw is a random straw. So, two things I would like settled: which of the three answers does an actual straw-tossing experiment confirm, and does that confirmation restore the principle of indifference, or was my friend right that the experiment, far from settling the paradox, merely illustrates it?
