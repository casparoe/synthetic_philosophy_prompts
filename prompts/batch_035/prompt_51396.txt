I'm a graduate student in cognitive science, and in our consciousness seminar the professor — a committed Dennettian — keeps deploying the slogan that consciousness is fame in the brain, sometimes given as cerebral celebrity. He used it most recently to wave off a question I raised about global workspace theory: I had been reading about Ned Block's overflow argument — the claim that phenomenal consciousness outstrips what we can access and report, as in the Sperling-style cases where subjects can name only a few letters from a briefly flashed array yet seem, by their own lights, to have experienced more — and I asked whether the workspace theory might explain access while remaining silent on phenomenality. His reply was, in effect, that the question was confused, because consciousness just is whatever achieves cerebral celebrity. I wasn't satisfied, but I couldn't articulate why.

Please take the slogan apart for me. Lay out, as an ordered list I can bring to seminar, the distinct precise theses it could be expressing, arranged from the trivial to the radical, and for each one say (a) whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible claim, or false, and (b) whether it, so read, would actually license dismissing my overflow question. The list should distinguish at least these possibilities, plus any others you think are genuinely live: that fame is merely a metaphor for the wide availability of information in the brain, and so just restates the global workspace; that fame names the competition by which some contents come to be widely available, making the slogan a claim about a mechanism rather than a state; that fame is the achieved end-state of that competition, so the slogan must choose between identifying consciousness with the process or with the outcome, which are different claims; that the slogan is constitutive — fame is what phenomenality consists in, so there is no further fact about what an experience is like beyond its dissemination — versus its being merely causal, a claim about which contents get reported; and the most radical reading, on which Block-style phenomenal consciousness, conceived as something over and above fame, simply does not exist, so that overflow cases must be reinterpreted as degraded or partial access rather than as evidence of experience beyond access. After the list, tell me which reading the professor most plausibly intends, and be explicit about which readings genuinely support his dismissal of my question and which, even if true, would leave the question standing. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — specifically, your verdict on whether any reading of the slogan dissolves the overflow worry — and give the reasoning after that, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
