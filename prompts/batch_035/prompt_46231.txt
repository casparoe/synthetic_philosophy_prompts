I'm taking a philosophy of biology course, and our study group fell into a long argument last week about what it means to say a biological trait has a function — as when we say the function of the heart is to pump blood. Marcus held that function-talk is essentially historical: a trait's function is whatever past natural selection put it there for, so it is a fact about the trait's lineage rather than about what the trait is doing right now. He liked the example of feathers: if they originally evolved for thermoregulation and only later got co-opted for flight, then flight was a beneficial side effect rather than the function, and it only became the function once selection began favoring feathers for flight. Dana thought this was perversely history-obsessed: when a physiologist asks what the kidney does, she said, she is asking what role the kidney currently plays in keeping the organism alive, not asking for a story about our fish ancestors. On her view, the function of a part is just the contribution it makes to some capacity of the containing system — that is what lets function-ascriptions do explanatory work in physiology, and it shouldn't matter how the part got there.

Marcus answered with malfunction: a diseased heart that pumps blood poorly, or a congenitally malformed valve that mostly leaks — we say such organs are failing to perform their function, and that criticism only makes sense if the function is fixed by something other than what the organ currently happens to do; otherwise the function of a leaky valve would be leaking, which is absurd. Dana conceded this was a real cost but said it could be paid by relativizing functions to the system's normal organization rather than to its history: the function is the contribution the part is supposed to make given how the system is standardly put together. She then went on offense with novelty: in a bacterial population in a lab, a brand-new mutation arises in a single cell that lets it metabolize some synthetic compound; on Marcus's view, until selection has spread and favored that mutation, the protein it encodes has no function at all, even though it is doing exactly the job function-talk was invented to track. Marcus bit that bullet cheerfully — a newly coined word has no meaning until usage accumulates, he said, and a newly arisen trait has no function until differential reproduction accumulates. He added that his view is the only one that preserves the distinction between what a trait is for and what a trait merely does, which he claimed is the whole point of the concept: the heart is for pumping blood rather than for making thumping sounds, and only a history of selection can mark that line.

Dana's closing move was the artificial heart: a titanium pump implanted in a patient's chest circulates blood just as well as the original ever did, and if Marcus wants to say its function is pumping blood, then whatever confers function there can't be natural selection; if it's the engineer's intention instead, then we have two completely different kinds of function wearing the same word, at which point maybe 'function' is just doing several jobs at once and neither of them owns it. Marcus said he was happy with an analogy: artifact functions come from intentions, biological functions come from selection, and the common thread is that both are etiology — what a thing is for depends on how it was produced — so there is one underlying notion after all. I honestly lost track of who was winning. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move — who is right about which specific points, whether either of them concedes too much, and what, if anything, both of them are missing?
