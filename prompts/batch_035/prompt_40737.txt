I'm a PhD student in philosophy coordinating the discussion sections for a large philosophy-of-mind course. For the section on the problem of other minds I want a diagnostic quiz — which position on other minds do you actually hold? — rather than a survey of labels, since students routinely call themselves Wittgensteinians without ever having been forced to say anything about a case. Please build it as a decision tree with eight to ten branch questions and no more than seven leaves. The root should be a commitment the whole room can be presumed to share (confidence in one's own mind, at least tentative confidence about the person in the next seat), and every branch node should be a concrete case answerable without knowing where it leads. The leaves must include the analogical argument, inference to the best explanation, behaviorist or otherwise deflationary dissolution, Wittgensteinian criteria, direct or perception-based accounts, and one genuinely skeptical leaf. For the branching cases, draw at least three from the following, chosen for where they make the positions split rather than for coverage: a fluent adult whose cognitive architecture is radically unlike yours, say distributed rather than centralized; a perfect actor who displays the full behavior of pain on stage but reliably reports having felt nothing; a newborn; a stranger under general anesthesia; a conversational system; an octopus. Construct the tree from the logical space first — the questions and the answer combinations they permit — and then locate the named positions within it, noting which combinations are unoccupied and hazarding a diagnosis of why (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored). Finally, flag any pair of leaves your cases cannot separate: my worry is that criteria-views and direct-perception views collapse together under every case I can devise, and if that is so I want to know whether the difference between them is substantive or merely terminological.
