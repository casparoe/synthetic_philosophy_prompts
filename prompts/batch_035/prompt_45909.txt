At a meeting of my university's political economy society, a fourth-year shut down my concern about Britain's current account deficit by invoking David Hume's dictum from the essay Of Money (1752): money, in Hume's words, is not properly one of the subjects of commerce at all, but merely the instrument people have agreed upon to facilitate the exchange of one commodity for another. His gloss was that since money is not wealth but only a kind of token or grease for trade, a nation that persistently ships money abroad in exchange for goods is not thereby impoverished — and so my worry about the deficit was, as he put it, mercantilist nostalgia for counting coins. I suspect the one-liner is doing several different jobs at once, and that only some of them would actually support his conclusion. Please separate out the distinct precise theses that the slogan could be expressing, ranging from the trivial to the radical — for instance a stipulation about how the word money is being used; a conceptual claim that money belongs to a different ontological category from the goods it circulates alongside; a neutrality claim that the quantity of money in a nation has no effect on its real wealth; a price-level claim about how monetary inflows and outflows work themselves out through prices; and whatever claim is implicit in Hume's own story about how specie flows out of a country, prices adjust, and trade rebalances on its own. For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude, whether it is substantive and defensible, and whether it is false. Then say which readings, if any, would actually license dismissing a concern about a persistent trade deficit, and which would leave my worry standing — in particular, address whether a country could be losing something other than money (industries, capacity, future options) that the slogan simply says nothing about. Lay the readings out as a numbered menu I can bring to the next meeting, with a short verdict on each. Please write the answer in British English throughout.
