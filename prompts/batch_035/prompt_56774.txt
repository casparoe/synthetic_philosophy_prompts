Here's my reconstruction of the passage, from section 3. Nietzsche says that monumental history serves the person of action and striving who despairs because he can find no model or exemplar among his own contemporaries. For such a person, history offers the great moments of past individuals, which together form a kind of chain or mountain range stretching across the millennia; and because the same kind of greatness seems to recur in wildly different cultures and epochs, the person of action learns that this greatness was once actually possible, and therefore that it could be possible again. That knowledge is supposed to work as a consolation against discouragement and as a counterweight to the skeptic who insists that greatness is finished. But almost immediately, in the same section, Nietzsche turns on his own creation: he suggests that the resemblances monumental history finds across the ages are really the historian's poetic projection; that it treats effects as equal even when the causes were enormously different; that it slurs over the long, collective, humble labor that actually produced the great achievements, making them look like sudden gifts of genius; and that it is politically dangerous, because the bare thought that something was once possible can be marshaled to argue it could be possible again — including by reactionary forces whose conception of greatness is nothing the striving person should want to emulate.
