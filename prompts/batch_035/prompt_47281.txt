Background on me: I spent thirty-eight years doing experimental condensed matter physics, mostly neutron scattering on magnetic materials, and since retiring I've gotten properly hooked on philosophy of physics. I finally worked through the hole argument literature -- Earman and Norton's "What Price Spacetime Substantivalism? The Hole Story" from 1987, plus the stuff around it -- and I've landed on a position I'd describe as this: the hole argument really does sink plain manifold substantivalism. If you say the bare manifold's points are real individuals in their own right, then general relativity is radically indeterministic, because a diffeomorphism that's the identity outside some hole region gives you infinitely many distinct futures compatible with the same complete past, which is obviously absurd. So the manifold points can't have this bare identity, and the diffeomorphic models are one physical world described many ways. Where I've ended up, though, I'm less sure about: it's either relationalism proper (spacetime is just the system of relations among events) or what people call sophisticated substantivalism (points are individuated by the metric and matter fields defined on them, and you keep saying spacetime is a genuine thing). My working view is that the second one is basically relationalism with a sentimental attachment to the word "substance," but I hold that view loosely.

So here's my question: what would count against this whole package? I want you to map its exposure, not to argue me out of it. Concretely, a few things I keep chewing on. (1) What could a stubborn plain substantivalist say that I'd have to take seriously -- e.g., denying that substantivalism commits you to points having haecceitistic identity, or the suggestion that the indeterminism is fake because the "distinct futures" differ in nothing observable? Is there a reading where Earman and Norton's argument only works against a substantivalism nobody sensible ever held? (2) The argument looks like it's really just a gauge-freedom argument, and gauge freedom shows up everywhere -- electromagnetism, Newton-Cartan theory, even plain Newtonian mechanics in certain formulations. Does that show the argument proves too much, or exactly enough? If every theory with a gauge symmetry forces you to give up individuated structure, maybe the "argument against substantivalism" is just a lemma of a much older point about surplus structure, and the spacetime part is rhetoric. (3) Is there anything in physics itself that would have to be true for my view to be wrong -- say, some genuinely nonlocal observable that reaches into the bare manifold, or holography-style results where the bulk geometry is derivative in a way that cuts against my story? And honestly: is my dismissal of sophisticated substantivalism as "relationalism with a label" fair, or is there a real metaphysical difference there that a model of the hole argument should make me care about?
