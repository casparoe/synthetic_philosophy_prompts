Quick background on where I'm coming from: I'm a second-year undergrad in a seminar on skepticism, and every time someone raises the possibility that our whole belief-forming apparatus could be broken, the professor waves it off with a line like "you can't use reason to argue against reason" — and then just moves on as if that settled it. The line comes out in slightly different flavors: "there's no standpoint outside reason from which to criticize it," "the skeptic, in order to argue at all, has to concede the very thing hes attacking." And then last week a friend of mine used almost the same move to defend his religious commitment — "you can't judge faith by the standards of reason, because reason can't judge anything." Thats when I got suspicious that the sentence is doing very different jobs in different mouths, and that some of those jobs are legitimate while others are hand-waving.

So here's what I want. Lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims that "you can't argue against reason without presupposing reason" could be making. Some of these look trivially true to me (of course any argument whatsoever employs inferential norms, that's just what an argument is — how would that silence anyone?). Some look like they might be the real thing: I gather there's a whole genre here — I've run into the phrase "transcendental argument" — where the idea is that certain norms are what make judging or doubting possible in the first place, so the skeptic's conclusion can only be stated as a conditional or ends up undermining its own intelligibility. And I suspect at least one reading is just false, or at least not something anyone has established. For each reading: is it plausible, is it a platitude or a substantive thesis, and — this is the part I actually care about — does it do the work my professor wants (silencing the skeptic) versus the work my friend wants (immunizing faith from criticism)? Are those two even the same job, or does the sentence slide between them?

One more thing I keep tripping over: when people say the skeptic "presupposes" what he denies, is that supposed to be a pragmatic inconsistency, like asserting "I don't exist" (which is fine as a sentence, just impossible to sincerely assert), or is it a stronger claim — that the denial can't even be so much as understood without already granting what's denied? That distinction seems to be where the real argument would live, if there is one, so I'd want the menu to be clear about which readings land on which side of it. A menu with verdicts, please, not a defense of whichever reading you find most attractive.
