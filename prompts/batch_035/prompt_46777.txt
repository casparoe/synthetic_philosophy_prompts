My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether Dennett's intentional stance extends cleanly to large language models, and rather than let the argument dissipate, I want to convert its residue into a decision about research funding. I advise a small foundation that can support roughly four researchers for two years on conceptual work — philosophy and theory, not empirical ML — concerning the attribution of beliefs, desires, and intentions to AI systems. Which open questions in this area should the foundation prioritize, and in what order? I care less about the list itself than about the reasoning behind the ordering: what makes a question worth these particular years and these particular researchers, not just that it is unresolved.

To make the prioritization non-trivial, let me reconstruct where we ended up, because the open questions are downstream of the point at which we deadlocked. My friend took the orthodox Dennettian line: the intentional stance is a predictive tool, nothing more, and so the only question worth asking about belief attribution to AI systems is whether it pays its way — whether treating the system as a rational agent predicts its behavior better than the alternatives. If it does, we are entitled to say it believes things, in exactly the sense in which we already say this of corporations, institutions, and other agents that don't have minds in the old metaphysical sense. I pushed back on two fronts. First, I doubted that predictive leverage settles anything by itself, because for language models there is a serious rival stance — call it the corpus stance — on which you predict the system's outputs by modeling the human-generated text it was trained on, attributing beliefs to no one. If the intentional stance and the corpus stance are roughly equally predictive, my friend's criterion goes silent, and we have no guidance on which attributions to use, teach, or encode in law. Second, I worried about what belief attributions are for once you strip away their usual anchors. When I say my colleague believes the meeting is at three, the attribution is stabilized by a whole surrounding practice: her perception of the calendar, her past behavior, the fact that she can be reminded, corrected, held to it. Language models have no perception, no persistent objects of belief, no causal history connecting their putative beliefs to the world — and yet, unlike thermostats, they produce articulate, context-sensitive, seemingly revisable commitments. My friend's reply was that these are just the familiar features that made people nervous about corporate and institutional agents a century ago, and that normative-legal frameworks eventually stabilized those attributions, so the AI case differs only in degree. I am not sure whether that is reassuring or an indictment of the attributions we already make.

Against that background, here is what I want from you. Rank the open questions — for instance, the question of what, if anything, beyond predictive leverage should constrain intentional-stance attribution; the question of whether de re belief attributions can be true of systems with no causal-historical connection to their objects; the question of whether the stance licenses genuinely normative assessment (can the system's beliefs be correct, unjustified, irrational, or only predictable); the question of how far the corporate-agent analogy actually carries; the question whether AI attributions need something like the legal-normative scaffolding that stabilized corporate attributions, and whether such scaffolding could be built or merely imposed — but also any question you think belongs on the list that we never got to. For each, tell me why it is upstream of the others, how tractable it is for philosophers working a priori or with published model behavior rather than running experiments, and what would have to happen in the next two years to move it up or down the ranking. I am equally interested in what you would deprioritize: I suspect some of the standard implications people draw from this debate — about moral status, about blame and accountability, about whether AI systems can really know things — don't follow from the stance-theoretic considerations at all, and if you agree, say which and why. Please reason from the positions themselves rather than surveying the literature; I am not after a bibliography but a defended agenda. If you think my friend and I framed the whole thing badly — that, say, the interesting question is not whether to attribute beliefs but what the attributor is doing in different contexts of use — make that case, but be explicit about what it displaces in the ranking.
