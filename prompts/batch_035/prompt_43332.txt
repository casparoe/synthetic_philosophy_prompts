I'm a backend engineer and I volunteer on the grants committee of a small foundation some friends and I started — we pool a few hundred thousand a year and give it away. Until now we've funded pretty standard stuff, mostly global health grants plus a local literacy program. At our last meeting two members showed up with a proposal to move something like 80% of the next funding round into "longtermist" causes — technical AI safety research and a fellowship for people working on "civilizational resilience." Their pitch is basically the strong longtermism thing: there could be trillions upon trillions of future people, so even a tiny reduction in the chance we wipe ourselves out this century beats saving a bunch of lives right now, mathematically. One of them kept redoing the expected-value calcualtion on the whiteboard where the future-lives number is so big that everything else rounds to zero.

I proabbly buy the math and I definitely hate the conclusion, and I can't tell if my hesitation is a real objection or just that the beneficiaries are abstract and the bednet kids are not. Also one committee member thinks the whole thing is Pascal's-mugging-flavored — you can justify literally anything by positing a big enough number — and another thinks that objection is lazy. We have to actually decide at our meeting in three weeks, and I'm the one who's supposed to circulate the writeup beforehand, so please draft that memo for me.

It should recommend a concrete allocation — an actual number for how much, if any, goes to the longtermist portfolio versus the global health one, and feel free to propose a third bucket if you think that helps. It has to take the strongest version of the longtermist argument seriously, the version where the astronomical number of future people does the real work, not the soft "the future matters some" version everyone already agrees with. But I also want it honest about where that argument is fragile: what assumptions does the expected-value story lean on (that our grants can meaningfully move extinction risk, that future people's welfare counts like present people's, that tiny probabilities times astronomical stakes stay meaningful instead of being noise we can't reason about), and which of those would a smart proponent actually concede versus fight on. Don't split the difference just to keep the peace — if you think we should go mostly one way, say so. And please include some decision procedure we could actually adopt: a rule for what evidence would tell us next year whether this allocation was a mistake, whatever it turns out to be. End the memo with a final line beginning "Recommendation:" followed by the allocation in compact form, like "60% global health / 30% longtermist / 10% evaluation".
