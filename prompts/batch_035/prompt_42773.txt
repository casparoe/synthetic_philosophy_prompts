I am a retired experimental physicist who has lately taken up reading decision theory for pleasure. In laboratory life I kept running into a configuration I never had a word for. A team would be years into building a bespoke instrument — say, a cryostat with hand-machined seals and decades of accumulated workshop lore behind it — and a plain accounting would show that finishing it would cost more than scrapping it and buying an off-the-shelf system of equal performance. The textbook verdict is that the years and money already sunk are irrelevant: decide by future costs and benefits alone. Yet whenever a team did cut its losses and switch, the replacement underdelivered, and the post-mortem diagnosis was always the same: the accumulated experience with the bespoke machine — knowing which seals leak, which settings drift, which repair takes ten minutes — had been most of what the machine really was, and none of it transferred. Continuing looked, from the outside, like irrationally honoring sunk costs, but it was really the purchase, at low incremental cost, of an asset that had no market: know-how that existed only as long as the project did. Does this configuration have an established name in decision theory or economics? I am aware it must sit somewhere near the sunk cost fallacy, near what psychologists call escalation of commitment, and near ideas of switching costs and lock-in, but in each of those the continuing looks like a mistake or an institutional pathology, whereas here it seems to have a rational core. Please structure your answer in four sections: (1) "The nearest named concepts," roughly a paragraph; (2) "What is distinctive about the pattern as I described it," roughly a paragraph; (3) "A hard boundary case," a short paragraph in which it is genuinely unclear whether continuing is rational or merely committed; and (4) "Verdict," a few sentences stating which existing concept fits best, or coining a term with a definition if none does. I am not after a bare label; I want to know exactly what the phenomenon is and is not, and where its edges run.
