I spent thirty-five years in a physiology lab and I've been retired for a while now, but the journal I reviewed for over the years broadened its scope a few years back, and they keep sending me evolutionary psychology manuscripts — four or five a year — apparently on the theory that an old physiologist who knows his statistics is close enough. Since retiring I've gotten deep into philosophy of science, and I keep running into the complaint that a lot of this work is "just-so stories" — the Kipling reference, after the giraffe one. Meanwhile my evolutionary biology friends tell me that's a lazy slur, that there are real tests you can run on adaptationist hypotheses, and that the people making the complaint don't hold papers in other fields to anything like that standard. I don't want to be the referree who either waves everything through because it sounds Darwinian, or rejects everything because the hypotheses are about the past and you can't rerun evolution.

So here's what I'm asking for: a decision procedure I can actually run when one of these manuscripts lands in my queue. Something I can work through in an afternoon before writing a review that an editor and the authors will actually read. I want the questions in order, what a paper has to show at each step to pass, where the procedure outputs "reject" or "major revisions," and — importantly — where it has to stop and admit the remaining question is a plausibility judgment that no checklist can make for me. The distinction I care most about is whether a trait is an adaptation for the function the authors claim, versus a byproduct of something else, versus an old adaptation co-opted for a new use. If the procedure can't handle that three-way distinction it's useless to me. I'd also like it to say where it will misfire — the kinds of papers it will pass that it shouldn't, or kill unfairly.

Then run it on three manuscripts so I can see it work:

(1) A hypothesis paper arguing that morning sickness is an adaptation protecting the embryo from plant toxins during organogenesis. The timing fits, the toxin data is assembled from other literatures, there's no experiment, but there is a testable prediction about miscarraige rates differing across traditional diets.

(2) An empirical paper with decent sample sizes finding that women's preference for the color pink is somewhat stronger in the luteal phase, and the authors interpret this as a leftover adaptation for tracking ripe fruit. Clean-ish data, wild interpretation. What does the procedure do with a case where the data and the story are doing different amounts of work?

(3) One where I suspect the procedure will hand me an answer I won't like: an experiment on fear learning showing that people, and lab-raised monkeys, acquire snake and spider aversions from a single pairing with a photo, while flowers and mushrooms need many trials, and the authors read this as evidence of evolved prepared learning. My gut is to be annoyed by anything with "evolved" in the interpretation section, but the design looks clean.

One request I want respected throughout: separate what's actually settled — among philosophers of biology and evolutionary biologists who know this stuff — from what's genuinely contested. I've been burned by philosophy books that present one side of an argument as if it were the consensus, and at my age I'd rather know when I'm standing on a plank somebody's sawing at from the other end. So wherever the procedure encodes a standard that people in the field actually fight about, flag it rather than baking it in silently.
