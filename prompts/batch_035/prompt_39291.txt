My partner and I have this recurring thing where we stand outside two restaurants, both sound equally good, and she refuses to flip a coin about it. Her view is something like: if you can't say why you picked one over the other, you didn't really choose, you just let something outside you choose — and that's a failure of practical rationality, not a solution to it. My view is that flipping the coin is perfectly fine and arguably the rational thing to do once you've established the tie. (I've been reading about Buridan's ass in a decision theory course, the donkey that starves between two identical haystacks, and honestly I'm on team donkey-should-just-pick-a-haystack-and-eat.)

So here's what I want from you: give me your credence that this claim is true — "in a case where an agent is precisely indifferent between two options and must choose one, choosing arbitrarily (coin flip, or just 'picking' one without a discriminating reason) is fully rational, not merely excusable or a second-best workaround." And please decompose it rather than giving me a bare number. Some terms I suspect belong: whether practical rationality actually requires a reason that discriminates between the options, or whether a weaker reason (like "I need to eat something") is enough to make the choice itself rational; whether randomizing can itself count as acting on a reason (saving time, avoiding regret, preserving the value of deciding); whether exact indifference ever really occurs or is always just a tiny hidden preference in disguise; and whether "rational" here should even be judged per-choice vs. as a policy over many choices (she'd probably concede a coin-flipping policy is fine while denying the individual flip is rational, and I'm not sure how much of our disagreement lives in that distinction).

Give me a rough probability and one line of justification for each term, then the combination. My credence overall is around 0.75, hers is maybe 0.25, so also tell me: which of those terms is the most likely place our disagreement actually lives, and what evidence or argument would move your number the most? Extra credit if you can say whether the starving-donkey version (where refusing to choose is catastrophic) is genuinely different from the restaurant version (where dithering just costs you time), or whether that asymmetry is doing more work than it should.
