For most of my career I was an experimental physicist — thirty-five years in condensed matter before retiring — and it is only in the last few years that I have worked seriously through the philosophy of mathematics, so I hope you will bear with what may be a newcomer's framing of an old question. The object of my puzzlement is Skolem's paradox. As I read it, the theorem itself is mathematically uncontroversial: any countable first-order axiomatization of set theory, if consistent, has a countable model, and inside such a model there will be sets that the model's own functions cannot be put into correspondence with the model's naturals, so the model satisfies the statement that uncountable sets exist even though, viewed from outside, everything in it is countable. What I cannot get clear on is the philosophical quarrel this has generated, which seems to have run ever since Skolem himself drew a relativist moral from his result in the early 1920s, prompted Zermelo's distaste, and was much later revived on the anti-realist side by Putnam in Models and Reality, where the theorem was deployed against the idea that our axioms single out an intended universe of sets. The rejoinder, as I understand it, is that the external countability of the model is a perspective no first-order theory can articulate, and that trading on it smuggles in exactly the absolute notions whose coherence the relativist denies.

I have now sat through a semester of a philosophy of mathematics reading group in which this exchange replays itself without anyone moving. The relativists say the theorem shows that uncountability is not an intrinsic feature of any set but only a relation to a model or a conceptual scheme; their opponents say this confuses what holds in a model with what holds of the model, and that the philosopher's 'from outside' count is either meaningless or else presupposes the absolute standpoint under dispute. Each side seems to regard the other's central move as a simple confusion, and yet nobody can produce an argument the other side counts as an argument. My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the dispute is not really about set theory at all, but about whether there is any coherent notion of an absolute standpoint from which models and their contents can be surveyed and compared — a question that strikes me as metaphysics wearing mathematical dress, and which no further theorem-proving could touch either way.

So what I would like from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is the disagreement verbal, turning on two readings of 'uncountable' — no internal bijection with the naturals versus no bijection full stop — between which the theorem gives no leverage? Are the two camps in fact answering different questions, one a metamathematical observation about first-order formalization and one a metaphysical claim about whether cardinality facts outrun all schemes? Or is there a genuine substantive issue here about absolute uncountability, perhaps resting on starting intuitions neither side can argue for without circularity? And given whatever diagnosis you settle on, tell me what would count as progress: a sharper distinction, a formal result, a reformulated question — or whether, on your diagnosis, none should be expected and the perennial quality of this quarrel is itself the finding.
