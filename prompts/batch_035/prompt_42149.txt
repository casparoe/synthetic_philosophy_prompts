At a reunion last month, two old classmates of mine, Vera and Ian, spent an hour arguing about whether the adoption of agriculture was a mistake, and I couldn't follow all of it. The exchange, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Vera opened with Marshall Sahlins's old idea of hunter-gatherers as the original affluent society: their wants were few and the environment met them easily, so they worked fewer hours than farmers. She added that the skeletons tell the same story — after the switch to farming, average height dropped, tooth decay and degenerative joint disease rose, and signs of famine stress appear — so by health, nutrition, and leisure, early farmers were worse off than the foragers they replaced. She said this is why Jared Diamond, in a famous essay, called the adoption of agriculture the worst mistake in human history.

(2) Ian called the evidence cherry-picked. The foraging societies we can actually observe are the ones pushed onto land farmers didn't want — the Kalahari, the Arctic — so they're a biased sample of Paleolithic life, not a random one. He also said that even granting the early misery, you can't judge a ten-thousand-year process by its first few centuries: the same revolution that produced peasants also eventually produced medicine, science, and billions of lives, and that has to go on the ledger too.

(3) Vera said the "mistake" framing doesn't require anyone to have chosen badly. Her point was structural: farming supports more people per acre, each generation grows slightly, and once the land is full of farmers there are too many of them to go back to hunting — the foraging option disappears as a side effect of no one's decision. And she added that this is when inequality begins: surplus and storage let some people own what others need to eat.

(4) Ian pushed back on both counts. On inequality: it didn't wait for agriculture — the anthropologist James Woodburn distinguished foragers who live hand-to-mouth from those who store food, defend territories, and hold resources over time, and it's the second kind, hierarchy and status differences appear without any farming; so agriculture amplifies inequality rather than inventing it. On the ledger: counting billions of descendants against early suffering requires a view about how to aggregate welfare across people who wouldn't otherwise have existed, and Vera hasn't offered one — without it, "mistake" has no standpoint to be a mistake from.

(5) Vera replied that the descendants argument proves too much: if more total lives could redeem the arrangement, then anything that fills the planet with people would be justified by that fact alone, and the word "mistake" could never apply to any historical process, which is absurd. Ian answered that maybe that's fine — perhaps calling it a mistake is just the wrong idiom for a population dynamic, like calling evolution a mistake. They broke for dinner at that point.
