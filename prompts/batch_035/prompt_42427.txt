A fight broke out in my social epistemology reading group this week and I got stuck refereeing it, badly, so I'm outsourcing. We've been reading Fricker on epistemic injustice, and the case on the table was one a colleague brought in from her old industry job: a junior ML engineer, two months into the team and the only woman on it, told the team lead in a standup that she thought the evaluation pipeline was leaking test data into training. He cut her off with something like "let's see how you do on the next sprint before you start auditing us" and moved on. Two months later it turned out she was completely right. My colleague swears the lead was otherwise a decent guy, not some cartoon sexist.

The disagreement went like this. (1) Marco said this is a textbook testimonial injustice: a credibility deficit that tracks identity prejudice rather than anything about the actual content of her claim, exactly the kind of case the book is about. (2) Ines said hold on -- the lead discounts infrastructural complaints from *everyone* who's new, because most such complaints from juniors are wrong, and that's just ordinary reliability tracking, which is epistemically fine and not prejudicial at all; Fricker's own account requires the prejudice to be the operative thing in the discount, and if a junior guy would've gotten the same brush-off, identity isn't doing any work. (3) Marco replied that the counterfactual test is exactly what we *can't* run, and that the prejudice is structural anyway: the lead's sense of who "sounds like an engineer" is calibrated on a male-coded baseline, so "you're new here" is doing double duty when juniorness and being the only woman are entangled in that team -- the discount can be identity-tracking even if the lead would sincerely deny it. (4) Ines answered that if any statistically-grounded discount that happens to correlate with identity counts as injustice, the concept has been stretched to uselessness, and anyway even granting everything Marco says, the real wrong to Dana showed up *after* the meeting -- she stopped raising concerns for the rest of her time there -- so the injustice is in the silencing effect, not in the original credibility judgment, which might've been ex ante reasonable given that most junior alarms are false alarms.

They eventually agreed to disagree and I honestly could not say who was right about what. Please go through the four moves one at a time and rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification -- and then tell me who's more right overall, and what (if anything) both of them are missing. I have a sneaking suspicion they're talking past each other about what the *wrong* in testimonial injustice is supposed to be wrongness *of* (a wrong to Dana as a knower vs. a bad epistemic practice for the team), but I can't quite make that thought precise, so I'd love it if you could either sharpen it or shoot it down. No citations needed, just the arguments.
