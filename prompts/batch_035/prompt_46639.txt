You are a professional moral philosopher with expertise in population ethics, the measurement of well-being, and the debate between classical utilitarians and suffering-focused views. Think carefully before writing: start by brainstorming the genuinely distinct theses a person could have in mind when saying what she said, and then write up a disciplined analysis.

At an effective altruism meetup I got into an argument with someone who described herself as a suffering-focused ethicist. We were comparing two programs: one that prevents a great deal of intense but temporary pain (say, surgical follow-up care in a poor region), and one that creates or sustains a great deal of ordinary happiness (say, community mental health work). When I tried to set up a cost-effectiveness comparison, she waved it off twice: once with "agony and happiness just aren't on the same scale," and once with "no amount of happiness can outweigh the worst suffering." She then used these one-liners to argue that the whole practice of assigning moral weights and comparing interventions across categories is misguided at the root, not merely error-prone in the details.

I suspect a lot of distinct claims are hiding inside those sentences, some trivial, some radical, and only some of them would actually support her dismissal. Please lay them out as a menu I could take back to her. Cover at least these possibilities and any others you find: that she is just claiming a very steep but ordinary tradeoff rate, which standard cardinal utility already accommodates; that she is claiming a lexical threshold, such that some band of suffering cannot be outweighed by any finite quantity of any pleasure; that she is really claiming an asymmetry between creating happy lives and preventing miserable ones, which is a different commitment and doesn't obviously transfer to intra-life comparisons; that she means a genuinely incommensurability claim, on which there is no complete ranking at all; and that she means something epistemic — that there is a fact of the matter but we cannot measure it, which would counsel humility rather than refusal. For each reading, say whether it is plausible, and — crucially — whether it would actually get her the conclusion that cross-category moral weights are impossible or illegitimate.

Also address one complication she herself should face: if she takes the lexical reading seriously, what happens when many instances of sub-threshold suffering pile up? Does a million mild headaches aggregate into something that competes with the threshold, or do threshold views have to forbid any aggregation within the suffering category itself — and is either answer something she could accept? Be honest about which readings support her rhetorical use of the slogan and which quietly undermine it.
