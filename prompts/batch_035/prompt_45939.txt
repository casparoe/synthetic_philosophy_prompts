I am a retired natural scientist — thirty-six years in molecular spectroscopy, the first half in a chemistry department, the second in a physics one — and since retiring I have, for the first time, been reading philosophy of science with serious attention. In the course of this I have assembled a set of positions on scientific realism, set out below. My question is whether they form a coherent package: which of them pull against each other, which only appear to, and what the least costly repair would be. I should say at the outset that I do not know where the trouble lies, if there is trouble at all, and I have no attachment to any particular item being the one that survives; please examine all of them with equal severity.

The commitments, numbered for reference:

1. The predictive success of our best current theories would be an unexplained miracle unless those theories were at least approximately true. This argument has struck me as sound since I first met it, and it is what drew me toward realism in the first place.
2. Phlogiston chemistry, in its heyday, was good science: it unified combustion, calcination, and respiration under a single explanatory scheme, guided experimental work, and was believed by serious people for serious reasons. It was not a foolish aberration of method; it had the epistemic standing, relative to its evidence, that our theories have relative to ours.
3. Phlogiston does not exist and never did. Stahl's theory was false — not approximately true, not true of something in the neighborhood, but false. I regard this as a plain fact about the world, and it is not a commitment I am prepared to revisit.
4. Electrons are real, and dark matter is a real substance rather than a bookkeeping entry. I believed in electrons throughout my working life; and when I look at galaxy rotation curves and the lensing data, the straightforward reading seems to be that there is additional matter there. I confess I have never liked the look of proposals that modify the law of gravity each time the ledger fails to balance.
5. Realism should be selective. We should believe only those parts of a theory that carried its novel predictions and went on to survive subsequent theory change; entities posited merely to fill a gap in a scheme deserve agnosticism. I arrived at this from the history of the decades around 1905–1930: what survived the collapse was the mathematics — the Lorentz relations and their descendants — while the ether, the substance supposed to carry it all, was simply dropped. That suggests trusting structure and staying agnostic about gap-filling substances.
6. Scientific change is conservative at the level of limiting cases: Newtonian mechanics re-emerges from general relativity as a limiting case, and I take the same to hold generally. There is no incommensurability worth the name; successive theories are about the same world and largely the same quantities.
7. Thomson's corpuscle of 1897 and the electron of present-day theory are one and the same entity. We discovered it once and have been correcting our description of it ever since; we did not swap one object for another somewhere along the way.

I would like your audit returned as structured data: a single JSON object, with no surrounding commentary, using this schema:

- "tensions": an array, ordered by severity with the worst first, each element an object with the fields:
  - "commitments": the numbers involved, e.g. [1, 2, 3];
  - "kind": "genuine" or "apparent" — use "apparent" for pairs that dissolve on inspection, and explain the dissolution;
  - "severity": an integer from 1 to 5, where 5 is worst;
  - "diagnosis": two or three sentences on what each commitment demands that the others cannot grant;
  - "cheapest_fix": the single least costly revision that removes this particular tension, with a note on what that revision costs elsewhere in the set;
- "recommended_package": the one revision, if any, that cleans up the most overall, stated plainly;
- "residual_cost": what your recommended package still leaves unexplained or uncomfortable, stated honestly.

Please rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally grave, and do not confine yourself to pairs I might be expected to worry about: if a pair I have not considered is in tension, or one I might have worried about dissolves, that is precisely what I want to hear. I am not looking for reading recommendations or references; I want the analysis on its own terms.
