Last night my friend and I had a three-hour argument, over coffee that went cold twice, about whether Hannah Arendt already anticipated the age of synthetic political content. My friend, who wrote her master's dissertation on The Origins of Totalitarianism, had just watched a documentary about deepfaked candidate videos circulating during an election campaign, and she kept insisting that Arendt had said everything that needed saying about this in her essays on lying in politics — the one on the Pentagon Papers and the one titled Truth and Politics. Her claim was that what Arendt diagnosed there, the organised substitution of image-making for factual truth, the problem-solvers and public-relations men who dissolve inconvenient facts into plausible scenarios, extends seamlessly to deepfakes and AI-generated propaganda, and that invoking Arendt is enough; no new concepts are required. I am not so sure, and the argument got quite heated.

My worry is that everything in Arendt's account of political lying seems to presuppose a liar: someone who knows the truth, has some grasp of the distinction between what is and what is not the case, and chooses to destroy it. She even suggests somewhere that the liar, precisely because he must first know the truth in order to contradict it, is in a strange way attached to the world of facts. But much of what worries me about the new technologies does not fit that shape. A diffusion model producing a convincing fake of a politician has no intent and no knowledge; a language model hallucinating a statistic that then gets cited in a parliamentary debate is not lying in any sense Arendt would recognise; an astroturf campaign of ten million machine-generated comments manufactures a public opinion that never existed without any human deceiver being in the loop, or at least without the deception being localisable in one. If she is right that the distinctive modern danger is not the individual lie but the organised lie, the lie woven into the texture of government, then perhaps what we face now is a further stage — lying without a liar, image-making without an image-maker — and her apparatus, built for deceivers, cannot get a grip on it.

So here is what I would like you to do: reconstruct, as carefully as you can, what Arendt would say about three specific phenomena she never lived to see — deepfaked video of political figures, mass-produced synthetic astroturf creating the appearance of public opinion, and confidently hallucinated claims from language models that then circulate as authoritative. For each, say which parts of her existing framework do the work, where the framework strains or breaks, and whether she would need genuinely new conceptual apparatus or merely an extension of the old. Where her various texts point in different directions — the Orwellian worries in The Origins of Totalitarianism about the destruction of the capacity to judge, versus the later essays' focus on the image-makers of the national security state — say which strand she would likely privilege here. And finally, tell me whether she would side with my friend, for whom all this is a matter of degree rather than of kind, or with me: would she say that a public sphere flooded with content that is neither true nor false, because no one asserted it, is a worse condition than one awash in deliberate lies, or would she insist that the deliberate liar remains the real danger because only a liar can dissolve the shared world deliberately? Please be explicit about where you are extrapolating beyond anything she wrote, as opposed to reporting what she actually held.
