I've spent the last couple of years on and off thinking about the old sea battle puzzle, with no philosophy training, just a notebook and long walks, and I've finally written down the full set of beliefs I've ended up holding about the future. Before I show it to people whose opinions I care about, I want to know whether it can all be true at once. Here are the commitments:

1. Every statement is either true or false, full stop — including "there will be a sea battle tomorrow." I am not willing to weaken basic logic to keep the future open.
2. Truth is never a free lunch. When a statement is true, that is because the world is some way; something has to make it true, it can't just be true from nowhere.
3. The admiral's decision tomorrow morning is genuinely open. Nothing that exists or has happened before that morning settles whether he attacks, and he could go either way.
4. The past is closed. What was true yesterday cannot be made false today, and nothing the admiral does tomorrow can reach back and change a single fact about today.
5. I believe in an all-knowing God, and I believe that a hundred years ago God already knew whether the battle will happen. People say that threatens freedom, but I don't see it — knowing something isn't causing it.
6. I don't think the future is any less real than the present. Tomorrow's battle exists in the same full-blooded way that yesterday's dinner does; there is nothing half-formed about the year 2300.

Go through these in pairs and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and, for each real tension, how bad it is and what the cheapest repair would cost me. Then tell me which single revision, if I made it, would clean up the most. I honestly don't know where the trouble is, and I don't want to be told that everything conflicts with everything — discriminate between the real problems and the ones that dissolve when you look closely. Lead with your overall verdict in the first sentence, then give the reasoning with no preamble and no restatement of my list. Afterward, tell me where your own audit is most likely to go wrong — which of your pair-by-pair judgments would you give up first under pressure, and what would change your mind about it.
