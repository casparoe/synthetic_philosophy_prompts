I am a novelist, twenty years into the trade, and I have staked out a position on machine-generated writing that I want stress-tested rather than applauded. Call the view biographical entailment: a work is creative only if its significant choices are answerable to the lived experience of their maker — only if there are facts about the maker's biography, memory, or perceptual history such that, had they been otherwise, the work's significant choices would have come out differently in ways the maker could articulate. On this view a large language model can produce novelty — the recombinatory kind, statistical rearrangement at scale — but not creativity, because its choices are answerable to a training distribution rather than to a life. I chose this formulation deliberately, to avoid the weaker ones my colleagues reach for ('it has no intention', 'it feels nothing', 'it isn't original'), which seem to me either false or beside the point. What I want from you is a map of the view's exposure.

Lay out what would count against it: the observations, arguments, cases, and possible developments that should move me, ranked from fatal through serious to merely awkward. Distinguish the defeaters I could dismiss with consistency from the ones a defender of biographical entailment is committed to conceding — including ones that come from inside my own craft's history rather than from technology. Does the view entail that a novelist who writes brilliantly about a country she has never visited, purely from research, is not creative, and is that a bullet I can bite? What about ghostwriters whose significant choices belong to someone else's biography, or the Oulipo writers who deliberately handed their significant choices to formal constraints, or any prolific novelist whose composition was famously mechanical — procedure rather than felt life? For each, say whether it refutes the view, forces a qualification, or actually vindicates it on some reading.

I also want to know what the view predicts that its rivals do not. If creativity requires biographical entailment, should readers be able to discriminate, blind, between passages grounded in lived experience and passages grounded in research or recombination? If no experiment of that kind could bear on the view, say so plainly. And give me an honest verdict on insulation: I can see myself qualifying the view case by case until nothing could refute it, and I want to know whether that is fatal for a claim about the nature of creativity, or just the ordinary fate of a concept anchored in a craft — and if it is the latter, what that tells me about how much the claim was ever doing.
