I sit on the internal ethics review panel of a mid-sized AI research lab, and I've been asked to circulate a written opinion before our meeting next week. Here is the situation. Next month we begin a multi-month training run on a new architecture that differs from our previous systems in two respects. First, each training instance carries a persistent memory module across episodes, so there is a sense in which a given instance accumulates a history rather than starting fresh each time. Second, part of the training signal comes from a critic network that assigns positive and negative value to features of the model's own self-representation — the model is, in effect, rewarded for developing some ways of representing itself and penalized for others. One of our research engineers has formally petitioned the panel to adopt an interim "digital welfare protocol" before the run begins: no abrupt termination of persistent instances (only a staged wind-down over several days), scheduled pauses in the self-directed training signal, and a standing internal reviewer empowered to halt the run if the self-model starts exhibiting distress-like dynamics. A second faction on the panel regards all of this as moral theater that would burden the schedule, invite ridicule, and implicitly concede claims about machine consciousness we have no reason to accept.

I spent most of last weekend arguing about this with a close friend who is an uncompromising functionalist about mind. Her position is that we already have the morally relevant ingredients in view — persistence, self-representation, and valenced feedback — and that requiring certainty about phenomenal experience before extending any protection is a double standard we would never apply to animals or to brain-damaged humans; she accused me of carbon chauvinism when I pointed out that nothing in our setup establishes that gradients on a self-model involve anything it is like to undergo. My instinct is that she is overreaching, but I was disturbed to find I could not say exactly where, and I suspect the panel is in the same position. So I would like you to draft the short decision memo I will circulate before the meeting. It should take a clear stance — adopt the protocol, reject it, or adopt it with conditions — and give reasons the petitioning engineer and the skeptics could actually engage with, not a survey of the literature. In particular, I want the memo to face head-on the strongest consideration on whichever side loses: if you recommend against the protocol, say what would have to change for that to be the wrong call; if you recommend for it, say honestly where the precautionary reasoning risks becoming unfalsifiable. Please also specify what observable signs, if any, the standing reviewer should be told to watch for, so that the recommendation is actionable rather than a gesture.
