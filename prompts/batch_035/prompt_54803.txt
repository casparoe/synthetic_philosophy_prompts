I was making the neutral-tool case (weakly, I'll admit), and another student shut me down by invoking Langdon Winner's thing from 1980 — do artifacts have politics? — like quoting it settled the whole debate. She basically said artifacts have politics, therefore the recommender is political, end of discussion. The professor nodded and we moved on and I never got a chance to push back.

The thing is, I've read a bit of Winner since and I'm pretty sure that slogan is doing a bunch of different jobs at once, and not all of them get you to my recommender being political. Can you help me sort it out? I want a menu of the distinct precise claims that could be packed into artifacts have politics — like, everything from the near-trivial reading (any design makes some uses easier than others, so in that thin sense choices have consequences) to the genuinely strong reading (artifacts can have political properties built in, inhering in the thing itself, independent of what anyone intends or does with it) to whatever's in between — maybe readings about who has to be organized to keep the thing running, or about design entrenching a social order after the designers are gone. For each reading say whether it's plausible, whether it's actually just technological determinism in disguise, and crucially whether it would get my classmate her conclusion that the recommender itself, not just its deployment, is political. If that story is shaky, does it matter for which readings of the slogan survive?
