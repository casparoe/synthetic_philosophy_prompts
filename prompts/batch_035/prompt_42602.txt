I chair the search commitee for our new ancient philosophy line this year, and we've managed to walk ourselves into something I lecture on every spring in my social choice seminar and still can't settle when it's my own problem on the line. The committee is just three of us this cycle (everyone else is on leave or dodging service). We agreed in advance on the criteria: the candidate needs (a) a genuinely strong research record and (b) a credible teaching fit, and we hire iff both. On the top candidate, the votes went like this. I said yes to (a), yes to (b), hire. My colleague R said yes to (a), no to (b), don't hire. Colleague S said no to (a), yes to (b), don't hire. So a majority of us endorsed each premise, but a majority voted against hiring.

The dean wants one recommendation, with reasons attached. R says the only thing we ever actually voted on was the motion "recommend hiring," and that motion lost 2-1, end of story — tallying premises instead is just the chair gerrymandering the vote until it comes out the way he wants. S says the opposite: we committed to the criteria in advance, premise-by-premise is the only tally that respects that commitment, and counting conclusions lets two people's holistic hunches veto an evaluation each of them only disputed half of. I keep noticing that whichever way we go, the report to the dean will read either "the committee judged the research strong, judged the teaching credible, and recommends against hiring" or the same two judgments plus "recommends hiring" — and one of those documents makes my department look like it can't do basic modus ponens in writing. I know this is a cousin of the Condorcet paradox, but I'm not sure that helps, since there's no cycling here, just a straight collision.

So give me a real answer, not a tour of the options. Which recommendation should we send, and why is the other tally not merely disfavored but wrong in an instructive way? If you genuinely think both tallies are defensible, own that, but then tell me what feature is missing from our situation that, if it were present, would make one clearly win — and whether the dean should care about the difference. I get enough "it depends on your theory of group agency" from my grad students.
