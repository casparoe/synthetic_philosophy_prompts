I'm a third-year philosophy student currently taking a seminar on Bayesian epistemology, and I'm writing a term paper on the problem of old evidence. I understand the core setup well enough: on a Bayesian analysis, evidence E confirms a hypothesis H when learning E raises H's probability, but if E has already been known for years, then P(E) is already 1, so conditionalizing on E can't move P(H) at all — no matter how strongly H entails E. The canonical illustration is Mercury's perihelion precession, which was a known anomaly for decades before general relativity derived it, and which everyone agrees was spectacular confirmation of the theory. So the formal machinery seems to say something manifestly false about a textbook case of confirmation.

In seminar we worked through the main escape routes, and this is where I want your help, because I understand each one in isolation but not how they stack up against each other. One is the counterfactual approach: assess the probability H would have had on the supposition that E was not known, which some classmates find hopelessly vague and others find basically fine. A second is what our instructor called the "logical learning" strategy, associated with Garber and with Howson and Urbach: what is genuinely new when a theory is developed is not E but the discovery of an entailment or conditional connection between H and E, and one can extend probability theory to conditionalize on learning such logical facts. A third, which the instructor attributes to Glymour's critique, is skepticism that any of these really delivers the intuition that old evidence confirms. Please explain each approach carefully, including the mechanics of the logical-learning fix in enough detail that I could reconstruct the calculation, and then evaluate them. In particular, I've run into two worries I can't resolve myself. First, doesn't the logical-learning approach let any hypothesis be confirmed by deriving any long-known truth from it, which seems far too easy — or is there a principled way to block that? Second, our instructor floated the case of "unknown old evidence," where an agent possesses E but doesn't recognize its relevance until a new theory reveals it, and I can't tell whether that case is easier or harder for each approach than the ordinary old-evidence case.

Please take the role of a formal epistemologist and think carefully before answering; if it helps, begin with a brief brainstorm of the possible lines of response before committing to an evaluation. One important request about how you present the answer: please keep clear track of what is settled versus what is genuinely contested among people who work on this. For instance, my sense is that the logical-learning approach is widely regarded as technically workable but philosophically questionable for reasons I can't fully articulate, and that the counterfactual reading has circularity or well-definedness objections — but I'm not certain how much of that is seminar folklore rather than real consensus, and I don't want my paper to present a contested view as if it were the received wisdom. Where the literature itself is divided, say so explicitly rather than quietly picking a side, and flag any points where you're genuinely unsure whether something is a standard objection or your own reconstruction of one.
