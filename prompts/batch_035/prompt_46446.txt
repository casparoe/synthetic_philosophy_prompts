I recently became managing director of the firm my grandfather founded, which machines replacement parts for industrial packaging equipment, and I have discovered that a decision he made in 1974 now governs nearly everything I do. He built our production around one maker's control system; that maker has since gone out of business; we are now one of the few shops on the continent that can service the surviving installed base. Our hires, our suppliers, our certifications, the physical layout of the building — all trace back to a choice whose original justification (a large customer who demanded it) vanished forty years ago. And the same pattern keeps appearing around me. Our town obtained its highway interchange in 1961 on the strength of a single county vote; the town eleven miles east, then the larger of the two, did not, and has since lost its bank, its high school, and half its population. Meanwhile my own industry is in the middle of choosing between two rival connector standards for the next generation of machines, and I must soon decide whether to bet our tooling budget on the one that gets to market first.

I need to think clearly about something I can only half-describe. It is more than sunk cost, because the old expenditure is not just unrecoverable but still actively generating consequences; it is more than switching costs, because the difficulty is not only the price of changing but the way every intermediate step of our history has widened it; and it is not mere habit, because no one involved is doing anything they could simply stop doing. When I try to explain it, I find I am conflating two different things the early event might be doing. In some cases the early choice seems only to select among routes that would all have led to roughly the same destination — people in my industry insist that whichever connector standard had prevailed, we would have ended up with essentially the same machines five years later, the standardization arriving anyway. In other cases the early choice seems to open or close destinations outright — my grandfather maintains that with the other control system the firm would simply never have existed, and the two towns look more like that than like different routes to one place.

Does this configuration have an established name, in economics, in the history of technology, or anywhere else? If there are several near-misses, I want to know what each one is, exactly how it fails to cover what I have described, and which covers it best. In particular I want to know whether the literature recognizes the distinction between the two roles I described — selecting among converging routes versus opening and closing destinations — whether there is any name for each, and whether anyone has proposed a way to tell, after the fact, which role an early event actually played, or whether that is permanently untestable. If no single established concept fits, coin one, define it, and mark its boundary against the nearest things it is not.

Whatever you conclude, please make the very last line of your reply of the form \answer{...}, containing only the established term that best fits the phenomenon I have described, or, if none fits, your coined term.
