I'm a psychiatrist, mostly inpatient work, and a fair amount of my week is spent on exactly the kind of case residents argue about: someone who reports that God has spoken to them and given them a task, and we have to decide whether that's their religion or something that needs treatment. We lean on the standard stuff — how fixed the belief is, whether it fits with their cultural background, how they're functioning, whether it's ego-syntonic, whether the content is bizarre vs. culturally sanctioned. I finally got around to reading Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling this year and it's been sitting badly with me ever since, because the teleological suspension of the ethical looks uncomfortably like a systematic attack on every one of those criteria. The tragic hero stays inside the universal — Agamemnon can explain himself to the community, his sacrifice of Iphigenia is hideable-within ethics — but the knight of faith is the single individual as the single individual in an absolute relation to the absolute, and crucially can't speak, can't make himself understood. And the ethical, for Kierkegaard, is basically the socially universal — which sounds an awful lot like our cultural-consonance criterion pointed in the opposite direction.

So here's the request. Restate the knight of faith / tragic hero distinction, and the teleological suspension itself, in the clinical vocabulary I actually use: the mental status exam, delusion criteria, ego-syntonicity, insight, risk formulation, the spiritual-vs-psychotic assessment we do at the bedside. Do it as faithfully as you can — don't quietly convert the knight of faith into a well-integrated religious patient just because that makes him fit. Then give me the residue report: what translates cleanly, what gets distorted to fit, and what has no clinical expression at all (I'm thinking of the double movement of infinite resignation and faith, and the absolute duty being higher than the universal — but tell me if I'm missing bigger pieces). And I want an honest verdict: is the residue showing me that my clinical vocabulary is missing something real about these patients, or is it showing me that Kierkegaard's categories can't actually do clinical work — that "faith" as he uses it might be indistinguishable from the fixed, idiosyncratic, incommunicable belief I'd chart in a progress note?

One thing I keep chewing on and want you to address directly: when I read the Abraham case and think, well, obviously this man isn't delusional — how much of that is anything detectable in him, and how much is just that I know how his story ends? The ram shows up. My patients' stories don't come with narrators. If the only thing separating the knight of faith from the man I'm admitting Friday is whether the ram arrives, that's a epistemological problem for me and maybe a worse one for Kierkegaard — but I want to know if that framing is even fair, or whether the double movement is supposed to be something a clinician could in principle detect even before the outcome. Be blunt with me. Also, minor thing, but I'd rather you not pretend there's a clean answer on whether Kierkegaard was describing real people or an impossible ideal — if that ambiguity matters for my question, say so.
