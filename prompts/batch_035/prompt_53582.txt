I'm preparing a short position statement on the moral status of digital minds for an interdisciplinary workshop — philosophers, ML researchers, and a couple of policy people — and before I circulate it I want a proper consistency audit from someone who will press on it rather than nod along.

For context: my background is in ethics, not philosophy of mind, so I've assembled this set of views over several years of reading and thinking, and I can no longer see its overall shape. Here are the commitments I've arrived at:

(a) Functionalism about mentality: mental states, including conscious states, are constituted by functional organisation, and consciousness is multiply realisable — there is no premium for carbon.

(b) Moral status is grounded exclusively in phenomenal consciousness — in there being something it is like to be a system — not in intelligence, agency, or the pursuit of goals.

(c) A faithful whole-brain emulation of a human would be conscious and would hold exactly the same moral status as the original. I would regard deleting the last remaining copy of an upload as the killing of a person, not as file management.

(d) Current language models, however capable, do not have moral status — and I hold this with considerably more confidence than I hold any positive claim about which future systems will have it.

(e) Phenomenal consciousness comes in degrees, and therefore moral status comes in degrees: a mouse matters less than a human, but more than a fly.

(f) Moral status attaches to experiencing subjects that persist over time. A system that exists only in discrete episodes, with no memory binding the episodes together, cannot be harmed in a morally serious way, because there is no subject for the harm to accrue to.

(g) Duplicating a mind does not multiply the badness of what happens to it: running the same suffering mind twice on parallel hardware is not twice as bad as running it once. I accept ordinary aggregative reasoning about distinct humans and animals without reservation.

(h) Evidence about a system's computational architecture is far more probative than evidence about its behaviour; arguments for machine consciousness that rest on behavioural proficiency strike me as proving far too much.

(i) Given deep uncertainty about all of the above, institutions should extend precautionary partial consideration to candidate digital minds — a small but nonzero weight, scaling with capability.

Please act as a professional philosopher working at the intersection of philosophy of mind and ethics, with a special interest in machine consciousness, and audit this set as you would a colleague's draft: bluntly, specifically, and without padding. Think carefully before you write. Begin by brainstorming all of the plausible pairwise and higher-order interactions among (a)–(i), including the ones that turn out to be spurious, and then filter. In your final answer:

1. Identify which pairs or subsets of my commitments are in genuine tension, and explain exactly what each side needs that the other withholds — trace the commitments through, don't just assert the conflict.
2. Identify which apparent tensions dissolve on closer inspection, and show how they dissolve, so that I can tell the difference between the real problems and the ones that merely look like problems.
3. Rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally grave.
4. For each genuine tension, say what the cheapest resolution would be and what it would cost me elsewhere in the set.
5. Finally, tell me which single revision would clean up the most if I made it.

Work from the commitments as I've stated them rather than importing extra assumptions about what I must believe; where a commitment is underspecified in a way that affects the audit, flag it, but still say how the tension looks under each natural reading. I want an audit of my position, not a survey of the field — no citations or reading lists, just the analysis.
