I'm a philosophy of biology grad student, and my labmate — a sharp evolutionary biologist I'll call Dr. R — has very firm instincts about when it's legitimate to say that a trait "has a function," and she issues verdicts on this constantly, usually over coffee. We've been keeping a running list of her rulings, and I've noticed that more than one general principle seems to fit them, though she'd never articulate one herself. Her verdicts so far: a healthy mammalian heart has the function of pumping blood; the vestigial pelvic bones of a whale have no function at all (not "a malfunctioning locomotion organ," just no function); a degraded human olactory-receptor pseudogene has no function; the beta-lactamase gene in a bacterial population living downstream of antibiotic runoff has the function of breaking down the antibiotic; the feathers of a flightless cormorant have the function of insulation but not flight; and the human chin has no function, being a mere byproduct of jaw growth.

Next week she's promised verdicts on four new cases, and I want to go in with predictions. The cases: (1) a de novo mutation in a single lab mouse that currently protects it from a virus but appeared so recently that it hasn't yet influenced anyone's reproduction; (2) an allele for cold-hardiness in mosquitoes that spread only because it is tightly linked to an insecticide-resistance allele under strong selection — its cold-hardiness effect is real and useful, but that effect played no causal role in its spread; (3) a particular B-lymphocyte clone that expanded within one patient's body by clonal selection during an infection and now produces antibodies against the pathogen; and (4) a lizard population newly colonized a rocky island, where toe pads originally selected for climbing in their home forest now prevent fatal falls from the rocks, though no differential reproduction on the island has yet occurred. My suspicion is that some of these are cases her existing verdicts already settle, some are cases where the plausible readings of her track record diverge, and one or two are cases her pattern of verdicts may just be silent on.

Please act as a philosopher of biology with expertise in the literature on biological function (selected-effect theories, modern-history variants, causal-role and dispositional accounts, and anything else you think is a live candidate). Start by brainstorming which candidate principles can each fit her entire track record above — and be explicit about which popular readings her verdicts already rule out, since e.g. her ruling on the whale pelvis seems to exclude some strictly historical views. Then, for each of the four new cases, tell me what each surviving principle predicts, give your best guess at her actual verdict with reasons, and say which of the four cases would do the most to discriminate between the surviving principles if I only get to press her on one. Finally, if there's a further case I could describe to her that would separate the surviving principles even more cleanly than my four do, describe it. Please reason carefully and show your work; I care less about the predictions themselves than about whether the underlying rules are correctly identified and cleanly separated.
