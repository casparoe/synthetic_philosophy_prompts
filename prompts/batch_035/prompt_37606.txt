I teach a 200-level course on decision theory and rational choice, mixed philosophy and economics majors, 18 students, one 75-minute session. We've just finished expected utility theory and Savage-style representation theorems, and the students are comfortably maximizing everything in sight. I want to design an in-class game that makes them run into Pascal's mugging before they've heard the term: a stranger offers an unimaginably huge payoff for a modest payment, justified by an astronomically small but nonzero probability — and the rational-sounding calculations start pointing somewhere they don't want to go. The shape I'm imagining: an opening round of ordinary lotteries with tickets and a points ledger (no real money — points convertible to something small at the end, like snacks or a modest bonus quiz question, so stakes are real but trivial), then a sequence of escalating offers where the payoff grows much faster than the probability shrinks, culminating in an open round where students can make mugging offers *to each other* and set their own numbers, which I hope will show them the exploitable side of the principle. They should not hear the name or the associated arguments until the debrief.

Please write the full activity: materials, rules, the exact escalation of offers I should make (with the point values and the stated probabilities or credences attached to each), how to handle the problem that probabilities here are just my say-so rather than anything objective, and how the peer-to-peer round should be structured so it doesn't collapse into pure comedy. Then a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the live options — unbounded expected utility with its "shut up and multiply" implication, bounded utility functions, treating sufficiently small probabilities as effectively zero, and the observation that a mugger can always name a bigger number, so any decision rule that pays up once can be bled dry. I'd like the debrief to press on whether their reluctance in the game was a considered objection to expected utility or just status-quo bias and embarrassment in front of peers, and to give them at least one round where they must state a general rule out loud and then confront its consequences.

Finally, be honest about failure modes and limits: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (students gaming the points, someone paying up as a joke and muddying the data, the class coordinating a refusal for social reasons), and which feature of the real problem the game cannot reproduce — presumably something about the fact that in the actual scenario there is no ledger, no end-of-class settlement, and no way to verify that the probability wasn't zero all along. That gap is the part I'll have to supply by talk rather than by play, and I want to know exactly what it is and how to present it.
