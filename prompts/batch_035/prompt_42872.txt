I've been rereading Gödel, Escher, Bach and I want to get straight on how the MU puzzle is supposed to connect to the incompleteness stuff. So: you start with MI, and the rules let you double everything after the M, swap any III for a U, delete any UU, and stick a U on anything ending in I. Hofstadter shows you can never get to MU, since the number of I's is never divisible by 3 — and the only way to see that is to step outside the rule-system and do a little arithmetic about the rules rather than following them.

My main question is how tight the analogy to Gödel actually is. The MU invariant is a simple divisibility fact, and some pretty weak formal system could prove underivability of MU, so the "jumping out" looks cheap in that case. Gödel's theorems are about systems that can't establish their own consistency from inside. So does the MU puzzle really illustrate anything about formal limits, or is it basically a warm-up that teaches you the outside-view move, with the real content only showing up later? Where exactly does the analogy hold, and where does it break down?

Second thing: Hofstadter talks about systems that can jump out of themselves versus ones that can't — chess programs that only ever shuffle pieces, etc. Is "jumping out of the system" even a coherent idea, or does whoever steps outside the rules just land inside a bigger system whose rules they happen to not be noticing? I feel like the Lucas/Penrose argument about minds and machines is lurking nearby, and I want to know whether JOOTSing can be defended as a real notion without sliding into their conclusion.

Gut-level explanation please, I don't need formal proofs — but do be precise about where the MU case is disanalogous to Gödel, because I suspect that disanalogy is doing a lot of work and Hofstadter mostly waves at it.
