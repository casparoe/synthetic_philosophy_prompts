A little over a year ago I started practicing insight meditation seriously, following a period when I was barely sleeping and felt increasingly like a spectator of my own life, and last month, on a ten-day retreat, I had the experience teachers refer to as seeing through the self: in careful examination of seeing, hearing, and thinking, no inner observer could be located anywhere. The teachers framed this as a direct confirmation of the Buddhist claim about no-self, and for about two weeks I was fully persuaded. Then I made the mistake of reading Douglas Hofstadter's I Am a Strange Loop, having loved Gödel, Escher, Bach in college, and I've since come to think the retreat experience is badly described as evidence that the self is an illusion. My reading of Hofstadter is that the "I" he describes isn't an inner object waiting to be found by introspection at all; it's a self-referential pattern that comes about when a system's model of the world grows rich enough to include itself, the way the famous example of the camera pointed at its own screen produces not a picture of anything but a strange new entity out of pure self-reference. If that's right, then failing to find a spectator at the center of experience is exactly what the strange-loop view predicts, and the illusionist conclusion doesn't follow from the data at all.

The center where I sit has a monthly evening for precisely this kind of thing, and I've been invited to lead the next one, making the case that the no-self experience is better accommodated — not explained away — by Hofstadter's account. My sketch is roughly this: first, that contemplative examination can only ever turn up contents, never the container, so it was never a fair test for a Cartesian self, let alone for a pattern view; second, that Hofstadter's loop view, unlike both the substantialist self and the eliminativist no-self reading, explains why anything was ever experienced as a self at all, and also why the illusion is so useful and so persistent in daily life; and third, extending an idea I found moving in the book, that the parts of our patterns that live on in the people who knew us make better sense of memory, grief, and moral continuity than the claim that the person was never there to begin with. What worries me is that my argument leans on a gap: as far as I can tell Hofstadter's case for the self is built from logic, analogy, and autobiography rather than from phenomenology, from what it is like to search inwardly and fail, so the very evidence my audience knows best is the evidence my main source never really engages.

The audience is the problem. About fifteen regulars: several long-term insight practitioners who regard the no-self insight as the central discovery of the path, a retired professor who spent decades in Tibetan Buddhism and will speak in doctrinal vocabulary, a cognitive science PhD student who thinks strange loops are a metaphor with no mechanism, and two hard-line secularists who find any talk of patterns continuing after death to be smuggled mysticism. I want a proper preparation sheet, not a defense I can only deliver from behind a podium: the objections these particular people will actually raise, in roughly the order they will arrive and phrased the way each of them would phrase them, each rated for how much damage it does; what I should concede up front versus what I must hold; the weakest point in my own position, so I can prepare to defend it rather than hope nobody finds it; and the one question I am quietly hoping nobody asks, so that I at least stop pretending it isn't coming.
