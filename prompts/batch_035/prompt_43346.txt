I sit on the steering committee of a small foundation that can fund three postdocs for five years on the border between moral psychology and metaethics, with one explicit aim: clarifying what, if anything, cross-cultural variation in moral judgment implies about moral objectivity. My own suspicion is that the standard WEIRD-sampling critique of moral psychology has been over-milked, and that the genuinely open questions lie further upstream or off to the side. Candidates I have in mind include: whether the cross-cultural differences actually documented are best described as disagreement over moral verdicts, or as disagreement over which concepts the verdicts are about (a community's verdicts about purity and hierarchy may not contradict ours at all if the words are doing different work); whether the near-universality of some moral themes — harm norms, reciprocity, taboos — is any more evidentially relevant to objectivity than the universality of optical illusions is to the objectivity of colour; and whether the objectivity commitments of ordinary moralizers, which themselves vary across cultures, should carry metaethical weight at all or are just more data. Rank the questions our money should chase, defend the ordering explicitly in terms of importance, tractability, neglectedness, and how many other questions depend on each, and tell me plainly which putative implications of cultural variation are overrated or do not follow. Commit to a ranking rather than surveying the options, and note what evidence over the next few years would move items up or down your list. Please answer in British English.
