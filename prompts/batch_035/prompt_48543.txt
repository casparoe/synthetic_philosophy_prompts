I have been recording my gut verdicts on when the provenance of an artwork or artifact matters to its value, in the broad sense of what I would cherish or pay for, and I would like to know whether they add up to a coherent principle. The cases and verdicts:

1. A painting I have loved for years is revealed to be a technically perfect forgery, indistinguishable from the original under close examination. My affection dims considerably, and I would not pay a tenth of what I once would have. Here the original matters enormously.
2. Two prints look identical; one was struck from the artist's own woodblock, the other from a block carved to match it exactly. I care, but only mildly — a modest premium, nothing like case 1.
3. A modern violin and a Stradivarius sound identical in blind listening tests. As a listener I do not care which one is being played; as a prospective owner I would pay vastly more for the Strad.
4. A high-quality scan of a medieval manuscript satisfies me completely for the purpose of reading it, and I feel no loss at never handling the vellum. Yet no photographic reproduction of a painting satisfies me the way even the forged canvas in case 1 still partly does.
5. A famous chef is away, and the sous-chef produces a dish I cannot distinguish from the chef's signature version. Upon learning this, I do not care at all, not even briefly.
6. A song I had long attributed to a certain songwriter turns out to be by an unknown imitator working in her style. I like it somewhat less for a while, but the feeling fades and barely affects what I would pay for the recording.
7. A fresco of which perhaps a third is original, the rest expertly restored. I care intensely that it is the partly original fresco rather than a total modern replica.

My tentative principle was that what matters is standing in the right causal-aesthetic relation to the originating artist — the object must carry the artist's touch. But cases 4 and 5 seem to trouble it: the scanned text satisfies me without any such relation to the scribe, and the dish satisfies me without the celebrated chef's hand in it, while the Stradivarius in case 3 commands my money even though its specialness is inaudible to me. I would like your help in reflective equilibrium. Please structure your response with the following sections, in this order: "1. Candidate principle or principles" — at most two, each stated precisely in a short paragraph, with the case that motivates each clause; "2. Application to my verdicts" — roughly 250 words, stating for each of the seven cases what your preferred principle predicts and flagging any mismatch; "3. The workshop case" — about 100 words on the case I cannot settle: a painting from a master's studio, the face painted by the master's own hand and everything else by a favored apprentice, which I do not know whether to value closer to an autograph original or to a period copy; "4. Verdicts I should give up" — one short paragraph naming any verdict the best principle cannot accommodate, and what price in complexity I would pay to keep it; and "5. A test case of your own" — one new case, not on my list, where your principle gives a clear verdict, so I can check it against my gut.
