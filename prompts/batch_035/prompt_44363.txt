I teach a seminar on democratic theory, and every year the unit on judicial review turns on the same image: the constitution as Ulysses lashed to the mast, with courts playing the role of the ropes. The pedigree is respectable — Elster's study of precommitment from 1979 is the usual source, and constitutional theorists of a liberal stripe (Holmes's work from the mid-1990s is the one my students most often cite) took up the Ulysses story to explain why it is no insult to democracy that unelected judges can strike down legislation: the demos lashed itself to the mast at the founding, so when a court refuses to enforce a statute that a present majority wants, it is not thwarting the people's will but executing their own prior, clearer one. I want to press my students on whether the analogy is actually doing the work it is being assigned, and I would like a careful audit to structure that discussion.

Please give me, first, the full correspondence table, worked out element by element rather than at the level of the slogan: who or what plays Ulysses, the ropes, the mast, the sirens, the wax in the crewmen's ears, the crew's advance orders to ignore Ulysses's later commands, and the moment of calm in which the binding was arranged. Some of these are not obvious — the sirens could be momentary majoritarian passions, or demagogues, or the legislature itself, and the choice between them changes what the rope (the court? the constitutional text? both?) is supposed to be.

Then tell me where the mapping breaks. The disanalogies I want weighed, at minimum, are these: (1) Ulysses binds himself at a moment when he is perfectly rational and knows exactly what temptation is coming and when; a founding generation binds successors who never consented, on the theory — Jefferson's old complaint — that the dead should not govern the living. (2) Ulysses's precommitment is defined against a specific, foreseeable temptation, whereas a constitution lays down open-textured guarantees that somebody must interpret, which seems to hand the crew the power to decide what counts as a siren. (3) Ulysses's crew is immune to the song (they have wax), but judges are members of the same polity whose passions are supposedly the sirens, so the mechanism of immunity is missing. (4) The crew's orders are to disregard Ulysses's later commands and tighten the ropes if he begs — the whole apparatus works by defeating the bound agent's contemporaneously expressed will, which is precisely the feature a democrat is supposed to find objectionable; the analogy seems to identify judicial review with, rather than excuse it from, counter-majoritarianism. (5) The ropes can, in principle, be untied only by an extreme effort (constitutional amendment), but the analogy is silent on whether a majoritarian precommitment can legitimately entrench itself against the very majority that made it.

For each disanalogy, please say whether it matters for the conclusion the analogy is used to support — that judicial review by unelected judges is democratically legitimate because it is self-binding — or whether it is incidental. A disanalogy that a defender of the precommitment view could patch without changing the structure counts as incidental; say how it would be patched. Finally, give me your overall verdict: does the legitimacy of judicial review survive once these breaks are on the table, does a weaker conclusion survive (some judicial review, of some laws, under some conditions), or was the analogy carrying the conclusion the whole time? And if the Ulysses story is a poor analogy, is there a better one in the neighborhood — I have heard it suggested that a constitution is less like a man lashed to a mast and more like a standing alliance among rowers with divergent interests, some of whom want the ship to reach Ithaca and some of whom do not — and would that replacement do better, worse, or merely different work?
