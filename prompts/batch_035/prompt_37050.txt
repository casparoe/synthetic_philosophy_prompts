I've spent two decades as a science journalist — biomedical research, public health, the occasional epidemiological scandal — and I'm now working on a long essay about what my profession actually owes its readers. Philosophers argue over whether the norm governing assertion is truth, knowledge (Timothy Williamson's knowledge rule), or reasonable belief (Jennifer Lackey's reasonable-to-believe norm). What I want to know is which norm, if any, my newsroom's practices presuppose. Let me lay out the practice as concretely as I can, because the philosophy has to engage with the details.

We run corrections when we state something false: if we misreport a sample size or garble a hazard ratio, the corrections desk will say so. But we will not correct an accurate report of a false claim. If we wrote that a minister said the water was safe, and the water wasn't safe, our sentence was true, so nothing needs correcting. Attribution — "according to a senior health official," "researchers report" — appears to let claims pass through our pages without our asserting them. Yet our headlines take ownership: the body of the story says "researchers report a 40 percent reduction," and the headline above it says "New drug cuts deaths by 40 percent," written by an editor working from the hedged body. We practice balance in the name of not taking sides, which in science coverage has meant a fringe skeptic getting the same two paragraphs as a consensus of thousands of studies. We grant anonymity, which strips readers of the ability to assess the source for themselves, in exchange for claims we could not otherwise print at all. And while a courtroom witness swears to tell the truth, my byline promises, if it promises anything, only accuracy about who said what.

So here is my question: what norm of assertion does this bundle of practices presuppose, if any? Is attribution a device for honoring a knowledge norm — we assert only what we know, namely that the official said it — or a device for evading one? Is the gap between hedged body and flat headline a straightforward inconsistency, or do the two run on different norms, and can a single publication coherently do that? Does balance-as-practice make sense only if the governing norm is something like "report what credible parties assert" — a norm the philosophical literature doesn't even list — and if so, is that norm defensible, or is it a degenerate cousin of one of the standard three? Where is the practice underdetermined, such that several theories of the norm would equally license what we do, and where do the parts genuinely clash? I'm not looking for a gotcha about the media; I want a reconstruction careful enough that I could take it back to my editors as a description of the institution we actually run.

Please structure your reply as follows:

1. "The practice, restated" — strip away my framing and describe the norms governing corrections, attribution, headlines, and balance purely as they bear on what the paper asserts and what it merely reports; roughly 150 words.
2. "The norms on the table" — a neutral statement of the truth norm, the knowledge norm, and the reasonable-to-belief norm, including what each predicts about when an assertion is proper and what each says about assertions that are true but not known, or reasonable but false; roughly 250 words.
3. "What each practice presupposes" — go practice by practice (corrections, attribution, headlines, balance, anonymity) and say for each which norm it requires, which it merely tolerates, and which it excludes; roughly 400 words.
4. "The clashes" — where the practices presuppose incompatible things, and whether the whole can be saved as a division of labor — say, reporters governed by one norm and the institution by another — rather than as a contradiction; roughly 250 words.
5. "If we took that norm seriously" — sketch what corrections, attribution, and headline writing would look like in a newsroom genuinely governed by whichever norm you judge closest to what journalism should presuppose; roughly 200 words.
6. "Verdict" — which norm the practice as it exists actually presupposes, whether that norm is coherent, and whether it is the right one for an institution like ours; roughly 100 words.
