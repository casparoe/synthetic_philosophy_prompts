I want you to analyze the dress from 2015 -- you know, the photo that split the internet -- as a case study in disjunctivism and the argument from illusion. Quick recap of the facts so we're working from the same thing: someone posted a photo of a dress on Tumblr in February 2015, and people divided roughly in half -- one camp saw white and gold, the other saw blue and black, and both camps genuinely couldnt believe the other wasnt trolling. It later came out that the actual dress was blue and black, so one half of the internet was (eventually) veridically perceiving and the other half was having some kind of non-veridical experience of the very same photo, at the same time, side by side on the same couch.

What I want is a careful mapping, not a rehash of the standard setup. I know the argument from illusion: if a non-veridical experience can be subjectively indistinguishable from a veridical one, then good and bad cases share a common mental kind, and perception can't just be a relation to the world. No need to re-explain it. What I want to know is what the dress does to it. Was the white-gold experience an illusion, a hallucination, or something disjunctivism doesn't have a clean slot for? What even counts as the best case here -- seeing the dress as it is, or seeing the photo as it is (photos are a weird intermediate case, since the pixels really are ambiguous)? Were the two groups' experiences subjectively indistinguishable in the sense the argument needs, given that people could argue about the dress, check by tilting their screens, and sometimes flip between the readings? My hunch is the episode is awkward for both sides: for the disjunctivist because both halves seem to be running on the same proximal stimulus with only subtle differences in processing, but also for the common kind theorist, because the disagreement was transparent to the disagreeers and resolvable by testimony in a way that's hard to square with the experiences being introspectively identical. Run the analysis both directions: what disjunctivism says about the episode, and what the episode says about disjunctivism -- does it confirm it, trouble it, or just fall outside what the view anticipated?
