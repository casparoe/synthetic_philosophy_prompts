Lately I've been going down a rabbit hole about this thing I read about online — the idea that you can't really "harm" someone by doing something when that very action is also the reason they exist at all. The classic example is some policy that depletes resources and makes life worse for future generations, except the future people living with the depletion are literally different people than who would've lived otherwise (different couples meet up, different kids get born), so nobody ends up worse off than they would have been — without the policy they just wouldn't exist. The weird part is I find this kind of convincing and it bugs me a lot.

My own attempt to squirm out of it was something like a fertility clinic scenario I made up: imagine a clinic deliberately mixes up samples so that kids end up born with a high chance of some painful condition. The clinic's action is in a sense the reason each of those kids exists, and each kid still has a life worth living, yet my gut says the clinic did something wrong TO the kids, not just some impersonal "the world got a bit worse" wrong. My theory was that what matters isn't being worse off than an alternative version of you, but ending up below some line — a life full of avoidable pain falls short of what people deserve, whether or not there was ever a better-off "you" out there. But a friend of mine pushed back: if the line is the thing, then ordinary parents who have a kid knowing they'll probably inherit a painful-but-manageable condition are doing the same wrong the clinic did, and also who exactly sets this line, and doesn't this prove too much — wouldn't it forbid having kids in tough circumstances generally? Once you compare a sperm-mixup clinic to a poor family having a kid, my confidence in my line theory kind of collapses.

So heres what I want: please make the strongest argument you can FOR the view that the clinic (or the resource-depletion policymakers) really do wrong those specific future people — an actual argument, not just "it feels wrong." Ideally one that doesn't collapse into either (a) my shaky line idea, or (b) saying morality is about the state of the world rather than people — that second one feels like giving up on the interesting part. Really commit to the position and steelman it properly, even though I know there are big objections, like the fact that the person supposedly wronged can't even coherently wish you hadn't done it (they wouldn't be around to wish anything).
