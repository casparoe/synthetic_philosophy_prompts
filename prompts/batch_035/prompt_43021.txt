I teach a bioethics elective for third-year med students, ~16 of them, one 90-minute session. Design a role-play where they sit as the death-determination committee of a fictional hospital and have to rule "dead" or "not dead" on a sequence of case cards, one at a time, with no idea what's coming later. The cards should work as a ratchet: every ruling gets logged as binding precedent for the committee, and later cards are built so that sticking to your earlier precedent now forces a ruling that makes you queasy — so by the end they've squeezed themselves. Draw the cases from the real fault lines: whole-brain vs. higher-brain criteria, the irreversible-vs-permanent move that lets donation after circulatory death go forward after only a few minutes of asystole, a brain-dead pregnant patient held on a ventilator, the autoresusciation (Lazarus phenomenon) worry, maybe an anencephalic newborn — but you pick which ones and in what order so the squeeze lands hardest. Give me the case cards themselves, a scoring/precedent mechanic that doesn't involve real money, and a debrief where I map each card onto a piece of the underlying idea — but that idea, that a unitary definition of death is something we settle by choice for practical purposes rather than discover in the biology, should come from what happened in the room, not from me lecturing up front. Two more things: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and be honest about which part of the problem a card game just cannot make them feel. When you lay out the case order and what each card is meant to do, tell me how confident you are in each choice and flag the single one you're least sure of — I'd rather know where you're guessing.
