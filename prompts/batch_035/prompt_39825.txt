I'm a PhD student in political theory, and my cohort runs a reading group that's working through Pascal's Pensées this term. Last week we did the fragments on justice and force, and two of my cohortmates, Maya and Deniz, got properly stuck into it over coffee afterwards. I mostly stayed out of it but honestly couldn't tell who was getting the better of the exchange, and it's been nagging me. Could you adjudicate it for me in American English?

The exchange, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Maya said the fragments amount to a debunking of justice. Her reading: Pascal observes that justice without force is helpless and force without justice is tyrannical, and concludes that, since we can't make what's just strong, we've settled for making what's strong just — so actual legal orders are just force that's been retroactively dressed up, and our sense of justice is mostly custom plus fear of punishment. On that reading Pascal is basically a mordant proto-Hobbesian, and his later religious appeals to justice have no footing he hasn't already sawed off himself.

(2) Deniz said this conflates diagnosis with endorsement. Pascal is exposing our confusion, not recommending force: the complaint isn't that justice is fake but that we obey laws while telling ourselves a flattering story about why. The fragments make a normative point too, Deniz said — the goal is to unite justice and force, which is a claim about legitimacy, not a surrender to might-makes-right. He also pointed to a fragment describing tyranny as wanting to obtain by force what can only be obtained through legitimate authority, which only makes sense if legitimacy and force come apart for Pascal.

(3) Maya pushed back with the custom material: Pascal seems to say that custom is the whole of equity for no better reason than that it's accepted, and that the only universal rules are the law of the land and majority opinion — which traces back to might, not to justice. If the content of justice is just whatever happens to be customary, that's relativism, not diagnosis, and uniting justice with force just means the customs of whoever has the army.

(4) Deniz answered with Pascal's three orders. Justice and force belong to different orders, the way body and mind do — force can produce obedience but never obligation, and expecting force to ground justice is a category error, like expecting a geometrician to prove first principles by strength of arms. So the view isn't quietism: it says the only thing that could legitimize force is something that isn't force, and the fact that we can't produce that thing politically doesn't make the standard disappear.

(5) Maya's last shot: if justice belongs to another order entirely and can't be strengthened by force, then in the political world force wins by default, and the ideal of uniting the two is empty — you get a politics where the strong rule and Pascal gets to feel superior about noticing, which is exactly the retroactive dressing-up she started with. Deniz said that mistakes a limit on what we can know for a limit on what there is, and pointed out that Pascal's wager shows he's willing to act on something he can't establish. Then they both had to catch buses, so I never found out where it landed.

For format, please structure the reply as: (a) Move-by-move — go through the five moves in order, rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a sentence or two of justification; (b) What both are missing — a short section on anything neither of them raised that would change the shape of the debate; (c) Verdict — a short closing paragraph on who comes out more right overall and on which specific points the loser actually wins.
