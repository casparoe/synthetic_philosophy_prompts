Last term I took a seminar on classical Islamic philosophy, and this past weekend I hosted two people from that seminar, Nadia and Marcus, for what was supposed to be a casual dinner. I made the mistake of mentioning that our professor had called Al-Ghazālī "Hume avant la lettre," and the two of them spent close to three hours going at each other about the causal theory in the Incoherence of the Philosophers. I mostly watched, occasionally tried to change the subject, failed, and eventually just took notes, because I genuinely could not tell who was winning. I'd like you to referee the exchange as it actually happened, move by move, rather than just giving me a verdict on the general question of "was Ghazālī an occasionalist proto-Hume."

Nadia opened with the proto-Hume reading. Her claim was that the core of the seventeenth discussion of the Incoherence is an epistemological point: when we examine the case of fire and burning cotton, we only ever observe the two events together, never any connection between them, and Ghazālī is explicit that the philosophers' alleged necessity is unobservable and unfalsifiable. So, she said, Ghazālī has the entire Humean apparatus — constant conjunction, no perceptible link, the conceivability of the effect without its customary cause — and simply fills the resulting gap with God's direct creation of the effect at the moment of conjunction, where Hume will later fill it with custom and habit. On her telling, the occasionalism is the same negative thesis about causation, plus a theistic posit.

Marcus objected that this is anachronistic and gets the direction of the argument backwards. Ghazālī's target, he said, is not Hume's target: the philosophers he attacks, following Avicenna, claimed that things like fire genuinely necessitate their effects by their natures, so that given fire and cotton, burning follows with the same kind of necessity as three following two. Ghazālī's denial is a metaphysical thesis — there is only one real agent, God, and what we call causes are occasions — not the epistemological thesis that there might be hidden connections we can't detect, which is Hume's more cautious line. Nadia's reading, Marcus said, treats a stronger and stranger claim as if it were a familiar one. Nadia replied that even granting all that, the occasionalist still owes an account of why the fire burns the cotton every single time, and when Ghazālī answers that God has a settled custom, his ʿāda, Nadia said the whole thing collapses: the custom is just a regularity theory with a divine anchor, and a contingent one at that — God could change the pattern tomorrow, so Ghazālī is worse off than the naturalist, who at least locates the necessity in the things themselves.

That provoked the exchange's sharpest round. Marcus answered that the fact that the pattern could change is not a bug but the point: it is precisely what allows Ghazālī to accommodate miracles without treating them as violations of anything, since there was no necessity there to violate — a dead man's revival is no more a breach of order than the original life was. And, Marcus continued, Ghazālī nowhere gives up prediction or everyday inference: past conjunctions still license expectations, which is how we remain confident that fire burns and water quenches; science survives. Nadia's closing move was that if custom licenses all the same predictions the naturalist's laws do, then the denial of causal necessity is doing no real work, which is essentially Averroes' objection in the Incoherence of the Incoherence: destroy necessity and you destroy demonstration itself, yet Ghazālī quietly carries on demonstrating — so the occasionalist pays an enormous ontological price, continuous re-creation of the world, for zero empirical advantage. Marcus's last word was that this begs the question: Averroes assumes demonstration requires essential necessitation, which is exactly what Ghazālī was denying, and besides, Nadia had started the evening by assimilating Ghazālī to Hume and ended it by complaining that he wasn't enough of a Humean — so she owed us an account of which Ghazālī she was criticizing.

Please adjudicate each of these six moves separately, saying who is right about what and where each of them overreached, and then tell me what, if anything, both of them missed. I'm especially interested in whether Nadia's opening reading survives Marcus's correction about the direction of the denial, and in whether her final charge of idleness is fair — that is, whether a denial of necessity can earn its keep purely by making miracles non-violations, or whether that is a theological rather than philosophical payoff.
