I've spent enough years reading decision-theory arguments on rationalist forums to know that intuitions are cheap, but I've been unable to make progress on when a society owes its members compensation for bad outcomes without cataloguing my own gut verdicts on cases and seeing whether a principle falls out. So here they are. Someone born with a congenital condition that keeps them from working: full compensation, no hesitation. Someone who bet their entire savings on a single spin of the roulette wheel and lost: no compensation — they took a fair gamble and lost. A commuter badly injured in a train crash: compensation owed, since the risk they ran was imposed on them rather than chosen. A recreational skier who breaks a leg on an expert slope they freely chose: no compensation, though I admit this one is slightly less firm. So far I'd have guessed the option luck / brute luck distinction — responsibility for outcomes tracks whether the risk was knowingly and voluntarily taken on — captures everything. But then there are the cases that make me doubt it.

Two neighbors own identical houses in a marginal flood zone. One buys flood insurance; the other, not reckless but careful, runs the numbers, judges the premium actuarially unfair, and self-insures, accepting the risk as part of the deal. The flood takes both houses. My gut says the self-insurer is owed substantial help despite having voluntarily taken on the risk — which looks flatly inconsistent with my verdict about the gambler, who also knowingly and voluntarily took on a risk. Worse, there's the smoker who started at sixteen in a town where everyone smoked, was hooked by eighteen, and now has emphysema at fifty-five: I go back and forth on whether that's closer to the congenital condition or to the skier. So my questions are these. Is there a single principle, or a slightly refined version of the option/brute luck distinction — perhaps distinguishing gambles taken for entertainment from risk decisions made within constraints one didn't choose, or gambles one would have been irrational to decline from gambles one could sensibly have declined — that saves every verdict above? If so, state it precisely, tell me which case motivates each clause, and say what it predicts for a case I haven't listed: someone who drifts into a sedentary desk-job lifestyle they never reflectively endorsed and develops heart disease. If no principle saves all my verdicts, tell me honestly which ones have to go and why, and give me your best candidate principle even if it costs me a verdict or two. I know reflective equilibrium can launder my biases along with my intuitions, so if you think one of my verdicts is simply status quo bias or scope insensitivity dressed up, say so.

Lead with your bottom line — the single principle or the verdict about whether one exists — in your first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my cases.
