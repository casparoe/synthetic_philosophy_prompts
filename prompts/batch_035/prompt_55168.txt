Write the minutes of a fictional meeting — the Forty-Seventh Emergency Session of the Colors Subcommittee of the Marigold Court Housing Co-operative — as a piece of sustained deadpan satire of Sen's impossibility of the Paretian liberal. Around 600 words for the minutes themselves, followed by a drafting note of at most 150 words (more on that below). The voice is bureaucratically flat: motions, seconds, points of order, adjournment, in the manner of real housing-co-op minutes. No exclamation points, no italics for emphasis. The comedy must come entirely from the social choice working exactly as it does in the theorem, not from anyone at the meeting being a fool.

The setup, which the minutes should render concrete: the co-op's courtyard has two front doors facing each other, Mr. Ferris's (Flat 4) and Ms. Larch's (Flat 5). Each door is either teal or the original 1974 ochre. The bylaws contain two provisions everyone reveres. Article III (the Right of the Door): each resident is final authority over the color of their own front door and nothing else — not mailboxes, not curtains, the bylaws are explicit about how little this right covers. Article VII (the Unanimity Clause): any change the whole co-op agrees on, the subcommittee shall enact. The preference profiles are these, and the minutes should let them surface through behavior and recorded statements rather than a table: Ferris most wants his door teal with Larch's still ochre; but he would rather both doors stay 1974 ochre than see the whole courtyard teal; and his least favorite arrangement of all is Larch's door teal while his stays ochre. Larch's preferences are the exact mirror image, with the names swapped.

Given those two articles and those preferences, the committee is trapped in a cycle the minutes should faithfully exhibit: Ferris paints his door teal (his right, over the pair of arrangements that differ only in his door); Larch, facing teal opposite her window, paints hers teal too (her right); but both now prefer the original ochre courtyard to the all-teal one, so the Unanimity Clause compels restoration of 1974 ochre; at which point Ferris's right again puts teal back, and around it goes. Every individual step follows a rule the members would refuse to repeal, and the subcommittee has now repainted the courtyard every spring for a decade. Somewhere in the minutes, a sharp-eyed member — or the visiting inspector from the municipal housing board — should notice that the chair's order of recognizing motions decides where in the cycle the meeting stops, and that this is the only power in the room that actually determines anything; let the members grasp this dimly and argue about the agenda rather than about the bylaws, since repealing either article is unthinkable to them.

Two constraints on the satire. First, legibility: a reader who has never heard of Sen should come away having genuinely understood that two individually compelling principles — each person decides their own small domain, and unanimous improvements should happen — can be jointly incoherent, with no villain in sight. Second, fairness: a social choice theorist should confirm the mechanics are right — rights as decisiveness over pairs differing only in one's own door, the unanimity condition as weak Pareto, the resulting strict cycle — and should find the residents' other-regarding (spiteful, really) preferences a legitimate illustration of how the theorem actually bites, not a cheat.

Then append the drafting note, and make it committed rather than hedge-y: state plainly, in definitive sentences, which liberties the piece takes with Sen's actual result. At minimum take a definite position on whether the cross-courtyard spite is doing essential work in generating the cycle or merely making it vivid; on whether the chair's agenda power is a genuine corollary of cyclical social preferences or an embellishment borrowed from a different literature; and on whether the bylaws' restriction of the right to front doors alone is a fair rendering of how thin minimal liberalism really is. Make choices and defend them; do not close with "it depends" or offer the reader alternatives. If any liberty taken is one you'd defend as load-bearing rather than cosmetic, say which, and say why.
