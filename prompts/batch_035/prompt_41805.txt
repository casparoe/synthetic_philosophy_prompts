I'm taking a graduate seminar in philosophy of science, and I think I have a serviceable grip on the standard motivation for epistemic structural realism: the no-miracles argument pulling one way, the pessimistic meta-induction (phlogiston, caloric, the luminiferous ether) pulling the other, and the proposal that what survives across theory change is relational structure rather than underlying nature, with the old Fresnel-to-Maxwell transition serving as the canonical illustration. What I cannot get my head around is the Newman objection. My professor disposed of it in about two minutes as a mere cardinality point, but the secondary literature I've glanced at seems split between treating it as a nuisance and treating it as fatal, and I can't reconstruct why. As I understand it, the complaint is that if all we can know of the world is its structure, and a structure is anything had by any sufficiently large domain once you're allowed to interpret the relations however you like, then knowing the structure comes to knowing almost nothing: any consistent theory has some model or other of the right size, so the structuralist's epistemic humility collapses into near-vacuity. But if it were really that simple, it's hard to see why anyone would have taken the view seriously for a century.

What I want is a genuinely intuitive, step-by-step explanation of why the objection bites. In particular: what exactly is the slide from "only relational structure is retained" to "mere cardinality," and why is it illegitimate for the structuralist to smuggle in our own predicates and relations to interpret the preserved structure — why is that cheating in a way that gives back exactly the substantive content the view was supposed to have given up? I'd also like to understand how the epistemic/ontic distinction reshuffles the dialectic. I've heard it said that ontic structural realism is meant to answer the objection by denying that there is anything "underneath" the structure for the model-theoretic construction to exploit, but I can't see how that is more than changing the subject from what we can know to what there is. And finally, does the common move of restricting "structure" to relations fixed by substantive mathematics — group-theoretic, topological, or otherwise constrained ones — actually blunt the objection, or does it merely relocate it, since the choice of which mathematics counts as "structure" now does all the work the discarded nature-claims used to do? Assume I know basic model theory at the level of a philosophy student rather than a logician, and please don't gesture at the historical literature or name-drop debates; I want the actual reasoning worked through, addressed to someone who is confused rather than to someone who already half-agrees with the objection.
