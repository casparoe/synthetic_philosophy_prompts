A running disagreement in my metaphysics reading group has finally been referred to me, and I don't trust myself to referee it, so I would like a careful adjudication.

The topic is counterpart theory versus transworld identity. Mira champions Lewis's counterpart theory; Jonah holds out for the view that one and the same individual exists in many possible worlds.

Mira opened with the Leibniz's Law complaint. If I am strictly identical to a person in another possible world in which I never studied philosophy, then that person both has and lacks the property of being a philosopher — has it in the actual world, lacks it in theirs. To escape the contradiction you must index every property to a world, at which point the "identical" person has no properties simpliciter, only world-relative shadows of them, and identity has quietly become an idle wheel. Her alternative: each of us exists in exactly one world, and 'I could have been a surgeon' is true because someone very like me — my counterpart — is a surgeon in another world. Leibniz's Law is preserved, and modality becomes a matter of comparative similarity.

Jonah replied with the Humphrey objection. On Mira's view, when I say that I could have become a surgeon, what makes this true is that someone else — a surgeon resembling me — exists elsewhere. But the content of my regret is that *I* should have become a surgeon, not that someone similar did. The counterpart does the winning on my behalf, or instead of me, and either way the truth of the modal claim has come apart from what it was supposed to be about. He added that the counterpart relation is loose in a damaging way: it is just similarity in respects left unspecified, so whether 'I could have been a poached egg' comes out true apparently depends on which respects the conversation happens to favour.

Mira answered that this is a feature, not a bug. De re modal claims really are context-sensitive, and counterpart theory explains that rather than burying it; and Jonah has conflated content with truth-maker — the claim is still about Jonah, it is merely made true by the counterpart's surgery. Besides, the transworld identity theorist must help herself either to bare, unanalysable haecceitistic thisnesses or to those world-indexed properties, which she suspects are just counterparts wearing a notational disguise.

Jonah's closing move was that Mira's two replies pull against each other. If the counterpart relation is fixed by similarity in respects relevant to Jonah, so that what happens to his counterparts is something he is entitled to care about, then the Humphrey worry is merely relabelled rather than answered: it is still someone else's surgery. And if, on the other hand, de re claims genuinely lack determinate truth values apart from conversational context, that is a higher price than the primitive thisnesses she is refusing to pay for his view.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification; say who is right about which specific point; and identify what, if anything, both of them are missing. I would also like you to say which argument or consideration, if it succeeded, would most change your overall verdict, and to indicate where in your reasoning you think the adjudication is most likely to go wrong.
