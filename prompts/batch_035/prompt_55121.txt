Over the last few years I've drifted into a position in decision theory that my friends have started calling my "fanaticism phase", and I want to stress-test it properly. The view is simple: when facing choices under risk, an agent should always choose the option with the highest expected value, with no cap on the magnitude of value at stake and no discount for risk. If a gamble offers a one-in-a-billion chance of an outcome worth a trillion times a certain, modest benefit, then the gamble wins, full stop. I know the standard crowd-pleasers — Pascal's mugging, the St Petersburg game, the discomfort of being at the mercy of anyone who can spin a convincing tale about cosmic stakes — and I have so far been willing to bite all of those bullets. What I want from you is not a re-run of those objections, but the most honest map you can draw of where my view is genuinely exposed.

Please sort the defeaters by how decisive they are. I'd like you to distinguish fatal ones — a demonstration that the view collapses into incoherence or makes choice impossible, or a rival framework that reproduces everything attractive about it without the pathological verdicts — from those that are merely awkward, such as weird verdicts in contrived cases. A few specific avenues I'd like you to explore rather than merely list: my friend Priya argues that any rule under which a small enough probability of a large enough value overrides everything else will, in practice, be dominated by noise, since tiny probabilities are precisely where our estimates are least reliable — she jokes that fanaticism is "garbage in, cosmic obligation out". I want to know whether that epistemic problem is a real defect of the view or just a practical caveat that leaves the mathematics untouched. I'd also like to know whether there is a principled place to stop short of fanaticism: if one allows risk-weighted valuations, or treats certain thresholds in value as lexically prior, is that a stable rival theory, or just an ad hoc patch that gets whittled away by mixed cases? On the pure formal side, tell me whether the best case against the view is really the paradoxes of unbounded value, or whether bounded-value rivals have their own embarrassments I should weigh.

The question I most want you to take seriously is this: whenever a new counterexample surfaces, I simply bite the bullet, and I've noticed I can always do so without cost to the view's core. At what point — how many bullets, and of what calibre — does bullet-biting stop being a sign of theoretical courage and become evidence that the view has been insulated into something unfalsifiable? Is that even a problem for a normative view of this kind, or only for empirical ones? Finally, tell me what the view's more thoughtful defenders would themselves concede counts against it, and where they would draw their line, as opposed to what only critics say. Please answer in British English.
