I TA an animal-cognition survey course, and every year we do the unit on Dennett's four creature types — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian, the tower of generate-and-test where each level adds a smarter way of killing off bad hypotheses before they kill you. And every year the students walk out having memorized the labels and nothing else. They just slap the tags on animals by vibes: crows are Gregorian, worms are Darwinian, done. So this year I want a sorting instrument that forces actual discrimination.

Here's the thing though: the quiz taker isn't sorting *themselves*, they're sorting *systems*. Each question should present a concrete observation or a tiny experiment you could actually run on some system, with two or three answer options describing what the system does — and the scoring key maps patterns of answers onto the four creature types. That means the questions have to be answerable from behavior alone, and phrased so a student can't tell which creature-type an answer is pointing at. No "is this system Popperian?" style questions, and shuffle which option is the "smart" one so they can't just always pick C.

Constraints:

- 8 to 10 questions. At least three should involve artificial systems (RL agents, thermostats, chatbots, whatever) — that's the part students find fun anyway, and it stops them from pattern-matching on animal = lower level. But don't just recycle my examples, write your own cases.
- The tower is supposed to be a *nested* hierarchy — a Gregorian creature passes all the lower gates too. So the scoring key can't just be four independent tallies; it has to handle that somehow, and I'd like to see your reasoning about the cleanest way to score a nested hierarchy with behavioral evidence.
- At least one question has to target the thing that actually makes this hard: separating a Popperian from a Skinnerian that just got lucky. That is, designing an observation that distinguishes learning-by-doing from learning by rehearsing without doing — hypothesis tested offline versus hypothesis tested by nearly dying. If you can't build a question that does this cleanly, say so and say why, don't fake it.
- The key needs an explicit fallback for "evidence insufficient" or "pattern is mixed," because with real animals that's most of what you get.
- Flag which adjacent pairs of levels the quiz genuinely can't separate, and diagnose why (cost of running the experiment, the behavior looks the same from outside, whatever it is). Don't paper over it.

For format, please structure the response in this order, with roughly these lengths (don't obsess, these are vibes): "The questions" (~700 words, the bulk of it), "The scoring key" (~300 words), "Two worked sortings" (~200 words each — run it on a honeybee colony and on a chatbot that has web search and can write itself notes it rereads later; show your work on where they land and where the evidence runs out), and "Where the quiz misfires" (~150 words — the cases or answer patterns a grader should be suspicious of).

One more thing: students always want to know where octopuses land, so if any question happens to be about an octopus that's a bonus, not a requirment.
