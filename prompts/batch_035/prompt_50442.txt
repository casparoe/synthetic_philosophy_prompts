I serve on the board of a mid-sized nonprofit music school — about four hundred students, a modest endowment, no municipal backing. Four years ago we launched a capital campaign to convert a 1920s vaudeville house we own into a proper recital hall. The original estimate was $3.4 million; we have now spent $11.2 million, the contractor has just disclosed a serious waterproofing problem at the foundation, and completing the work is now priced at roughly $5 million more. Meanwhile, a comparable, fully renovated 400-seat venue two neighborhoods over has come on the market at $7.9 million, and our broker believes we could sell our half-finished shell for perhaps $3 million. The board is split down the middle. The strongest argument for continuing is not architectural: the family behind our largest naming gift — $4 million, pledged specifically for this building — has let it be known that they would withdraw all support from the school, and encourage their circle to do likewise, if we abandon what they regard as their hall, and their annual giving covers an operating gap no other funder plausibly would. One of our trustees, who teaches game theory, has begun calling the project Shubik's dollar auction: the game in which a dollar bill is auctioned to the highest bidder under the rule that the second-highest bidder also pays, so that once bidding passes a certain point both parties are trapped paying for a prize only one can win. She argues our position is worse, because under every option we are the one who pays for something already sunk, and each individually reasonable decision to continue has carried us to an outcome none of us would have voted for at the outset.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the board before next month's meeting. It should recommend one concrete course of action — finish under a hard cap, buy the alternative venue and sell the shell, stop and sell outright, or a better option we haven't yet considered — with the options compared on a forward-looking basis only, treating the $11.2 million as gone under every branch, as our game-theorist trustee insists. But it must also engage the donor consideration seriously rather than waving it away as sentimentality, because it is the one respect in which our situation arguably is not a dollar auction: the money already spent is, in effect, held over us by parties who can retaliate, and I want the memo to say plainly whether that makes further escalation rational here, or merely explicable. Finally, it should close with a standing rule for any future capital project — some form of pre-committed stop-loss or renewal gate at fixed spending thresholds — and defend that rule against the obvious objection that it will sometimes force abandonment of projects whose expected value, judged from inside the escalation, still favors continuing. The other trustees are practical people; they will not accept a memo that just labels the sunk-cost fallacy and stops, nor one that pretends the donor question doesn't exist.
