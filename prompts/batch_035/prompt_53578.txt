I'm TA-ing a bioethics seminar this term (mixed grad students and fourth-years, 14 people, one 90-minute session) and I've been asked to run the week on advance directives and precedent autonomy. The assigned readings cover the standard dialectic around dementia and refusal-of-treatment cases, and honestly when I led discussion last year the whole thing stayed really abstract -- everyone agreed the earlier self "probably" has some authority, nobody felt why that's such a weird claim. So instead of another discussion section I want to stage something where they have to actually author a directive and then later be governed by it after their values have shifted under them. My rough idea: participants get persona cards early on, write an advance directive as that persona, then at the halfway mark the cards get swapped/revised so they're now playing a later self whose wants have drifted (new attachments, changed conception of what makes life worth living, mild cognitive stuff) while a proxy player in the room holds their earlier directive and has to decide whether to enforce it. But I'm very open to better structures if you see one. What I want is for the anxiety of being ruled by your own past handwriting to be something they feel in their stomach, not something the debrief has to argue them into.

Design the full thing: rules, materials (index cards, timer, whatever), how to distribute the personas and the "drift" so people aren't just playing themselves, whether the proxies should face any cost for overriding vs. following, and how to run the debrief so it maps onto the actual philosophical positions (the experiential vs. critical interests distinction, the critique that precedent autonomy only works if you smuggle in a contentious theory of personal identity, that whole cluster) without me lecturing. Also tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- I assume people will write jokey or self-undermining directives, and I don't want to kill the point by policing them too hard -- and be frank about which part of the real problem no classroom simulation can reproduce (my guess is the actual terror of nonexistence or the fact that the dementia patient, unlike an actor, genuinely wants what she now wants, but you tell me). If you can think of a small twist that makes at least some participants flip their position mid-session, that would be gold -- last year nobody changed their mind about anything and it was dead air for the last twenty minutes.
