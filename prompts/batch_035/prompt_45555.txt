I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm preparing a long feature revisiting the debate over whether human violence is really in decline. My readers already know the standard story: if you count violence per capita rather than in absolute numbers, the trend line points sharply downward — a small-scale society where a substantial fraction of people die at the hands of others looks worse than an industrial war that kills tens of millions out of billions. Alongside my reporting, I want to run an essay-length argument that pushes back not on the data but on the normalization step itself — the division by population. Please write that argument.

Argue that the per-capita framing smuggles in a moral premise rather than merely reporting a measurement: roughly, that a violent death counts for less the more people there are to be counted alongside it, so that the very same atrocity registers as a smaller evil in a larger world. Develop the argument through a comparison of two hypothetical worlds that are identical in every respect — same number of violent deaths, same suffering, same perpetrators — except that one of them also contains a vast population living far from any violence, untouched by it and unaware of it. If the per-capita method declares the second world less violent, ask what could make that verdict true other than the claim that each individual death matters less when there are more individuals for it to be averaged against.

Then take on the strongest replies you can construct for the other side. In particular: that per-capita rates are best read as measuring risk — the probability that a randomly selected individual dies violently — and that risk is a fact about individuals, not a statement about the moral weight of their deaths; and that defenders of the trend are not claiming absolute numbers are meaningless, only that falling risk is what it means for life to be getting safer, the way a falling murder rate can coexist with a rising murder count in a growing city. Explain where those replies succeed and where they change the subject rather than answering the objection.

A few constraints. Grant the empirical record throughout — do not dispute the numbers, the archaeological estimates, or the historical direction of the curve, so the essay cannot be read as a quarrel with the data. Do not cite specific studies, authors, or books, and do not include a bibliography or reading list; I am sourcing the empirical material separately, and I want the essay to stand on the conceptual point alone. Where you need a technical notion — for instance, the contrast between assessing a world by how bad things are for the average person in it versus by how much badness it contains in total — explain it in passing and in plain terms, since my readers are educated but not specialists.

Finally, close with a candid note telling me where the argument is weakest: which step a sharp critic would target first, and whether the weakness is fatal or merely something I should frame carefully before publication. I want to know which paragraphs will draw fire so that I can decide whether the essay leads with the argument or buries it.
