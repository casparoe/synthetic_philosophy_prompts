Background: I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I've recently become fascinated by Kurt Gödel's modal ontological argument, largely because of its computational afterlife. Around 2013, researchers in automated theorem proving formalized Gödel's argument in higher-order logic and had theorem provers and proof assistants work through it. They verified variants of the proof, showed as a corollary that the axioms entail a striking thesis philosophers call modal collapse (roughly: whatever is the case is necessarily the case), and in later work the same machinery demonstrated that Gödel's original axiom set is actually inconsistent, with a slightly emended version due to Dana Scott faring better.

At work, a colleague who saw a popular article about this announced that Gödel's proof of God's existence is now "as established as the four-color theorem," since a computer verified it the same way. My own instinct as an engineer is that a verified proof only certifies an inference step, not its inputs: a build passing CI tells me the code matches the spec, not that the spec was what anyone wanted. But I'm not confident I have the philosophy right, so I'd like a careful audit of the analogy my colleague is leaning on.

Please give me: (1) an explicit correspondence table between the two cases — what plays the role of the proof assistant, the theorem, the axioms, the mathematics community's acceptance, and so on; (2) the disanalogies, sorted into those that matter for his conclusion and those that don't — I'd particularly like your view on whether the deep difference is that the four-color theorem's axioms are the shared, uncontested substrate of mathematics while Gödel's axioms are precisely the premises under dispute, and whether the modal collapse result strengthens or weakens the analogy; (3) a verdict on whether "as established as the four-color theorem" survives once the disanalogies are on the table; and (4) if the analogy fails, a better one from my world that captures what machine verification actually delivers here. Please don't recommend reading; argue the case directly.
