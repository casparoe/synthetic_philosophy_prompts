I've been rereading the old coherent extrapolated volition material — the idea that we should build AI to satisfy not what humanity currently wants but what we would want if we were smarter, better informed, and more the people we wish we were — and it has finally forced me to admit I don't know what I believe about idealized preferences in general. So I've been cataloguing my gut verdicts on individual cases, and I'd like help extracting the principle behind them, if there is one.

The cases and my verdicts:

An adult Jehovah's Witness, raised in the faith, refuses a life-saving transfusion. If she had somehow been raised free of the specific fears her upbringing installed, she would almost certainly consent — but that counterfactual self gets no vote. The refusal governs. Same verdict for a member of a strict religious community who would, under almost any other upbringing, have left and lived differently: we do not treat the counterfactual defector as the real person.

A friend, sober and deliberate, hands me her phone and says that if she asks for it back while drunk tonight, I am not to give it to her. Drunk, she demands it, angrily. I keep the phone. The sober preference governs over the current one, and this seems so obvious it barely feels like philosophy.

A patient in a severe depressive episode, with no prior stated wishes, refuses treatment for a physical illness that will kill him within days. His doctors treat him anyway, and this seems right: we discount the preference produced by the depressive state in favor of the one his unimpaired self would have, even though he never explicitly recorded it.

A stranger is brought in unconscious after a car accident with no identification. The surgeons operate, assuming he would want to live. Obviously correct, though here there is no current preference at all, only the extrapolated one.

A patient with a severe needle phobia, otherwise rational and fully informed, begs not to be given an injection that will save her life, and fights the staff. I think it is permissible to restrain and inject her — the phobia produces a preference that doesn't count as fully hers — but I notice I feel much less comfortable here than in the drunk-phone case, and I can't fully say why.

A committed racist votes, campaigns, and argues for his views with full information and no compulsion. His extrapolated self — calmer, better informed, more the person he would endorse being — would find his politics repugnant. Yet I don't think his actual vote should be discounted, recorded differently, or cast on his behalf by his better self. He gets his actual vote, full stop. Persuading him is fine; substituting the idealized preference is not.

And then CEV itself, which I find myself endorsing at the collective level: for humanity as a whole facing a superintelligence, extrapolating everyone's volition seems clearly better than polling seven billion current, confused, manipulable minds. Yet this looks structurally like doing to everyone what I refused to do to the racist.

Here is my tentative principle, which I'd like tested and repaired: the extrapolated preference governs only when the current preference issues from a condition that the current self would not endorse as a genuine part of its values — drunkenness, phobia, unconsciousness, and perhaps acute depression are conditions people can have preferences about, whereas faith, ideology, and character are not; they are what the preferences are made of. This gets most of my verdicts, but it's under strain in at least two places. The depressed patient often does endorse his despair as insight rather than illness, which by my principle should flip the verdict — and I'm not sure I want it flipped. And CEV extrapolates from the endorsed preferences of an entire species, which the principle seems to forbid.

So: is there a single principle that captures all of these, or a small set of principles with a principled division of labor? If the endorsement constraint is right, what does it say about the cases I haven't decided — the smoker who has repeatedly tried and failed to quit but made no explicit precommitment; the person who says "I understand that if I worked through the math I'd want the AI to overrule me, and I refuse"? And which of my verdicts does your preferred principle simply have to overturn? I'd rather learn that one of my gut calls is wrong than carry around three principles that don't compose.
