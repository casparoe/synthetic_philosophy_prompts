My reading group spent its last half hour on Kripke's example of the standard meter in Paris (the stick S whose length, at a certain time t0, was used to fix what "one meter" means), and two members got into a dispute about whether the example really establishes the existence of contingent a priori knowledge. They asked me to referee, and I found I couldn't. Here is the exchange as I recall it:

Elena opened: The stipulators could know, without any measurement, that S is one meter long at t0 — that's just what they meant by "one meter." So the knowledge is a priori. Yet S could easily have been slightly longer or shorter than it was at t0 (heat, warping, a manufacturing accident), so what they knew a priori was contingent. Contingent a priori knowledge, full stop.

Marcus replied: That conflates two different things. If "one meter" rigidly designates the particular length L that S in fact has at t0, then "S is one meter long at t0" expresses the claim that S's length at t0 equals L — which is necessary if true, being a plain identity. What is contingent is only that S has length L at other times, and nobody ever pretended to know that a priori.

Elena said this made Kripke's own machinery destroy his example, and offered him a fork: either "one meter" is being used non-rigidly in the stipulator's knowledge claim — in which case the doctrine survives — or it is rigid, and the example dies. But she added that the fork cuts both ways: on the non-rigid reading, "one meter" means something like "the length of S at t0," and then, at any world, S's length at t0 equals S's length at t0 — so that reading seems to make the claim necessary too.

Marcus answered that this is exactly right, and that no reading of the sentence yields contingency where the knowledge was a priori: the stipulators' genuine a priori knowledge is only of a metalinguistic fact — that "one meter" was to refer to the length of S at t0 — plus a trivial truth about lengths. The felt contingency, he said, comes from sliding between "the length of S at t0" and the length S has at other times, or from the later empirical business of measuring other objects against S. The contingent things in the neighborhood were never known a priori, and the a priori things were never contingent.

Elena's last move: If Marcus's sentence-versus-proposition machinery dissolves the meter case, it had better dissolve the other cases too, and she doubted it could. Consider "the actual tallest spy is a spy." Anyone who understands it can know it holds, without knowing who the tallest spy is — so it seems a priori. Yet the person it is about could have failed to be a spy — so it seems contingent. If Marcus grants the contingency there (as his rigidity-based approach apparently must), he owes an account of why the apriority is an illusion in that case; and if he can't give one that also handles the meter, the phenomenon returns somewhere.

Marcus said the spy case is different, because there the designator is rigid from the start and the contingency is genuine — the real question is whether the apriority is genuine, and he suspected it isn't: what seems knowable a priori is only the generalization that whoever is in fact the tallest spy is a spy, whereas the singular proposition about that particular person is something we can't know without knowing which person it concerns.

Please adjudicate the exchange round by round: which of the specific claims and moves each of them makes are right, which are wrong, and where each is talking past the other. Then say who comes out more right overall, and what, if anything, both are missing. Finally, since my own grasp of this is shaky: identify the single point on which your verdict most depends — the move that, if you were wrong about it, would most likely flip the overall adjudication.
