I'm a money coach at a community nonprofit. Most of my clients aren't investors, they're people facing one or two big irreversible decisions: take the pension as a lump sum or as monthly payments for life, accept the insurance settlement or sue, sell the family restaurant now for a guaranteed number or hold out for a better offer that might never come. I see maybe six of these a month and I get one 60-minute session per decison, two if I'm lucky. I'm not licensed to recommend specific products, so all I can really do is help them figure out what they actually want and what the tradeoffs are — every decision has to be signed off as theirs, not mine.

Here's the idea I want operationalized, and I apologize in advance for butchering it. Somewhere I read about a version of decision theory where you don't have to weight outcomes strictly by their probability to count as rational — you're allowed to count the bad half of a 50/50 as mattering more than half, i.e. be cautious, and it's still rational as long as you do it consistently, instead of it being a bias somebody should councel out of you. I think the term was risk-weighted expected utility or something like that, but don't quote me and don't bother with citations, I can't use those in a session anyway. The point that stuck with me: my job isn't to find the "correct" attitude toward risk and fix the client until they have it. It's to elicit whatever stable attitude they actually have and rank their options under it. What I need is a procedure for doing that.

Please write it so I can actually run it inside a session: the questions to ask in order (I'm imagining comparing made-up bets to triangulate how much they discount the good branch of a gamble, but do it better than that), what counts as a usable answer to each question, where the procedure stops and hands the decision back to the client because what's left is about values or obligations to family rather than risk attitude, and where it flags that I'm making a judgment call dressed up as math.

Then run it on three cases:

1. A 58-year-old choosing between the lump sum and the annuity, who says she's "not a gambler" but then picks the gamble on every small-stakes hypothetical I throw at her.
2. A couple deciding whether to sell now or hold out, who answer every question consistently but so cautiously that the procedure would tell them to take an offer I think is genuinely bad — one I'd feel awful walking them into, and honestly one I'll be tempted to override.
3. A man who clearly already wants to take the settlement and go home, and whose numbers keep quietly changing to justify whatever he'd decided in the parking lot.

For each case, say what the procedure yields, where it misfires, and what it can't do. And be straight with me about the biggest question underneath all this: is there a principled line between a stable risk attitude I should respect and, like, a mood or a fear response I should probe before I use it as input? Can a procedure even find that line, or is that part just me?
