I run a college seminar on practical ethics, 16 students, one 90-minute session, and I want a grantmaking game that makes them feel the measurability critique of effective altruism from the inside -- the worry that when you rank do-gooding by what you can count, you quietly redirect money toward whatever happens to be countable, whether or not that's what matters most. Split them into small "foundations," give each a pot of points, and have me hand out a made-up portfolio of candidate grantees: some with clean cost-per-outcome numbers (deworming, bednets), some with plausible but messy long-run effects (policy advocacy, community organizing), and at least one whose effects are plausibly huge but basically unmeasurable. The key mechanic: the audit rule. After allocations, each foundation has to defend its choices to another group, and the defense scores better the more numbers it can cite -- so they should drift toward the legible interventions on their own, before anyone says the word "measurement." Then a debrief mapping what actually happened onto the real critique (the drift toward quantifiable interventions, whether earning to give is partly a legibility phenomenon too, and the harder question of whether this bias is specific to EA or just what any accountable funder does). No lecturing up front, and they shouldnt hear "measurability bias" until the end. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about which part of the idea no game with points and a 90-minute clock can reproduce.
