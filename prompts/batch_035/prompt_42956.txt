I finished a dissertation chapter in the late nineties on the epistemology of testimony, and I have been waiting ever since for the reductionism/anti-reductionism debate to go somewhere. It hasn't. I'd like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is, and an honest assessment of whether anything could move it.

Here is the state of play as I see it, so you can engage the positions rather than a caricature. The global reductionist, in the Humean mode, wants every instance of testimonial justification to be validated by non-testimonial evidence: a track record, accumulated by observation, of the reliability of the kind of speaker, in the kind of circumstance, on the kind of subject. It is generally granted that this global project is hopeless as stated — no one has the inductive base. The anti-reductionist, in the tradition of Reid and more recently C. A. J. Coady, concludes that testimony is instead a basic source of justification: absent defeaters, a hearer is prima facie entitled to trust an asserter, and this entitlement need not be earned from elsewhere (some versions, like Tyler Burge's, make the entitlement a priori). The standard reductionist rejoinder came from Elizabeth Fricker in her mid-nineties exchange with Coady: so-called local reductionism. Within a given epistemic community, the hearer already possesses a body of background knowledge about how things go in that community, and can monitor the individual speaker online for signs of insincerity and incompetence; justification for a particular trust need not be built up from nothing each time, because the community's track record is already part of the hearer's evidence. Anti-reductionists reply that this concedes the substantive point — justification still ultimately traces back to what the hearer could verify without testimony — while Fricker insists that local reductionism captures everything that mattered in the reductionist's complaint, namely that the hearer must do epistemic work rather than receive warrant for free. And there the matter has rested for roughly a quarter century. More recently, the field largely stopped arguing and changed the subject: the interesting work is now about the hearer's ethical obligations to speakers (epistemic injustice, silencing, credibility deficits), which presupposes some answer to the old question without ever giving one.

My own hypothesis, which I offer as a hypothesis and want you to test rather than flatter, is that the residual disagreement is at bottom about what a "source" of justification is supposed to be. The two sides agree on nearly all cases: on when a hearer ought to withhold trust, on what defeaters look like, on the value of critical monitoring. If so, the dispute is verbal — a disagreement about whether the label "basic source" attaches to anything that isn't locally reductive in Fricker's sense, or perhaps about where the burden of proof sits when a putative source is proposed. But I can see two ways my hypothesis could be wrong, and I want you to weigh them. First, there may be a genuinely substantive issue hiding in the intergenerational case: testimony about what no living member of the community could verify, including testimony about the community's own past reliability, which the anti-reductionist thinks reductionism cannot license without circularity and the local reductionist thinks is licensed by the background knowledge itself. Second, the disagreement may be methodological rather than epistemological: a dispute over whether epistemology of testimony should model an isolated hearer and ask what she is entitled to, or model a participant in ongoing social practice and ask what the practice normatively requires of her — two research programs that share vocabulary but would count different facts as data.

So, three questions. Given what each side actually says, is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, or two groups answering different questions under a single heading — and which of my two candidate diagnoses (the hidden intergenerational issue, or the methodological split) does the real work, if either? If the disagreement is verbal or methodological, what would count as progress: a distinction, a change of question, a demonstration that one program runs out of cases to explain? And if it is substantive, be honest about whether we should expect it ever to be resolved, and say why. Please confine yourself to what is internal to the debate as I've described it; do not pad the answer with reading lists or name-dropping beyond the positions I've given you. Write in American English, and keep the answer to roughly 800 words.
