I'm a third-year PhD student in metaphysics with a side interest in philosophy of law, and I'm applying for a one-year dissertation fellowship that requires a two-page proposal. My topic is causation by omission, and I have a half-formed idea I'd like you to develop into that proposal. The background, so you know where I'm starting: omissions are notoriously awkward for theories of causation. If the gardener's failure to water my plants caused them to die, then so did my neighbor's failure to water them, and the failure of every gardener in the city, and arguably the plants' own failure to absorb water that was never there — the problem of too many omissions. The responses I'm familiar with are, roughly: contrastivist treatments, on which the causal claim is really about a contrast class, so the gardener rather than the neighbor counts because only the gardener was supposed to water; views on which omissions aren't genuine causes but some weaker, quasi-causal relation inherited from the real causes in the neighborhood; and deflationary views on which the extra "causes" are selected by norms of salience rather than by causal facts proper. My suspicion is that all of these responses quietly rely on an unexamined distinction between omissions proper — cases where some agent could have acted, and in some loose sense was expected or obligated to — and mere absences, like the absence of rain, where there is no agent and no expectation at all. Maybe the puzzles look uniform because we use the same vocabulary for both, and splitting them dissolves the standard counterexamples; or maybe the split fails, and the normative dependence turns out to be ineliminable, which would be a more interesting result — it would suggest that a claim like "her omission caused the death" is never a purely causal claim, and that the causal and responsibility-tracking components can't be pried apart even in principle.

Please turn this into the fellowship proposal. I'd also like you to make a call on whether the legal applications — liability for failures to rescue, medical negligence, omissions in the criminal law — belong in the fellowship year itself or should be flagged as future work, and to defend that call.
