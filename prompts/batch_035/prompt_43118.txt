I write historical fiction, and my town has spent two years arguing about its monuments, so I have been forced to notice that I hold verdicts I cannot fully justify. Here they are, as honestly as I can state them, and I would like a principle — or a short list of rival principles — that captures them.

The Confederate cavalry officer on the courthouse lawn, erected in the 1920s as an assertion of the racial order, comes down; I have no hesitation there. A statue of that same officer, moved into the county museum with a wall of text beside it, I would keep indefinitely. Our town founder, a slaveholder whose bequest built the library, stays on his pedestal, but I want a plaque that tells the whole ledger. The street named for a doctor whose fame was the state eugenics program — a name nobody now recognizes — I would rename without ceremony. But the bronze plaque honoring the novelist born three streets over, a man whose private correspondence is vicious on the subject of nearly everyone, I would keep untouched, because his books are still read and still good. The middle school named for a twentieth-century industrialist whose fortune came from labor practices he knew were brutal, and named to honor the donation, I would rename — and here I notice I cannot say why the industrialist loses and the novelist survives, since both are honored for what they made, and what they made is inseparable from what they did.

My tentative principle is something like this: remove when the commemoration itself honors the wrongdoing; recontextualize when the wrongdoing is incidental to what is honored; and where the honored work survives and is good independent of the vice, the commemoration may stand. But I can see it wobbles. Our founder's civic achievement was financed by the slaveholding, so his wrongdoing is not exactly incidental; the museum statue of the cavalry officer honors the same man the courthouse statue honors, and my principle seems to make the interpretive setting do all the moral work without saying why that is legitimate; and the novelist's bigotry arguably shaped the books, which is a sentence I resent writing.

There is also the case I cannot settle: the war memorial in the square, a generic soldier erected after the First World War as a gesture of mourning and reconciliation, which a far-right group has lately adopted as its rallying point, so that the monument's public meaning is being changed under it by people who did not build it. I want to keep it, and I distrust the wanting.

Please test my principle against the cases, repair it or replace it, and tell me plainly which of my verdicts the repaired version cannot save — and, since the answer may bear on how my next novel handles a man it must render sympathetically, what it predicts for the one case I have not decided: a commemorative name chosen, in living memory, to honor someone who was then believed to be a hero and has since been shown to be a fabrication. Please answer in American English.
