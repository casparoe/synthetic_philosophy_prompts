I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training; everything I know about the simulation argument comes from a podcast episode and the first half of an encyclopedia article, so please build the piece on the widely-known core of the idea rather than on any specific author's later refinements.

My team maintains a wiki with a tradition of parody postmortems, and I'd like to contribute one on this topic. Write a mock incident postmortem, in the style a site-reliability team writes after an outage, but authored by the operations team that runs an ancestor simulation. The incident: a class of simulated agents has begun asking, in print, whether they themselves are simulated, and the question is spreading through the population. The document should be deadpan throughout, use the standard headings a real postmortem would have (Summary, Impact, Timeline, Root Cause, Resolution, Action Items, roughly 700 words total), and never acknowledge that anything metaphysical is at stake.

The humor should come from the actual structure of the simulation argument, not from stock "we're living in the Matrix" gags. The following should appear somewhere, each rendered as an ordinary engineering decision:

- A fidelity budget: the operators simulate observers' surroundings only at the resolution the agents can distinguish — the interiors of unopened boxes, the far side of planets, the neural activity of everyone other than the agent currently reporting — and an internal debate over whether economizing there is detectable in principle. If the agents build instruments that probe below the rendered resolution, the postmortem should treat this as a latency incident rather than an existential one.
- The trilemma, transposed: a section noting that the operators themselves cannot settle whether they have operators, whether they are the base layer, or whether base layers are rare — and this question should be marked out of scope for the current incident, which is where the joke lives.
- Nested simulations: the agents running crude simulations of their own, and a chargeback dispute about who pays for the compute — with a note that the more simulations the agents run, the more observers exist downstream of them, which skews some probability estimate the document confidently misapplies.
- A proposal to patch out the asking-behavior, and the counterargument that making the question unaskable would alter the agents so much that the operators would no longer be simulating their ancestors — at which point the whole project's purpose is called into question by a parenthetical and then dropped.

For fairness: someone who actually knows this literature should feel the satire lands on the argument's real features — its agnosticism about physics, the observation-selection reasoning, the possibility that the conclusion is undecidable from inside — and not on a strawman. No simulated agent should be named after a real philosopher, and any quotation from a simulated agent should be invented, not attributed.

After the postmortem, append a structured summary as a single JSON object with this schema:

- "title" (string): the postmortem's title.
- "target" (string): one sentence on the reasoning style or position being satirized.
- "jokes" (array of objects), each with two fields: "engineering_surface" (the heading, metric, or tool the joke is dressed as) and "philosophical_feature" (the actual feature of the simulation argument it exaggerates).
- "out_of_scope" (array of strings): the questions the postmortem declines to answer on purpose.
- "fairness_check" (string): one sentence stating whether any joke misstates the argument, and if so which one.
