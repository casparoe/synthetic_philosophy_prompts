I've been keeping a list of situations where my gut says "free" or "not free," and I'd like to know whether there's a single principle behind the pattern. I'm aware of the rough divide between freedom as non-interference and freedom as something like not living at someone's mercy, but I don't want a tour of the literature or a recitation of the standard positions — I want a principle that fits my cases, or comes as close as possible, and I'm prepared to be told that one of my verdicts has to go.

Here are my verdicts:

1. A slave whose master is benevolent and never issues an order, letting him come and go as he pleases: I still think he's unfree, even though no one interferes with him.
2. My ordinary week: I'm free, even though I pass dozens of strangers who could assault me and would probably never be caught.
3. A citizen of a well-run democracy who is taxed, licensed, and regulated: quite free, even though interference with her life is constant.
4. An employee whose contract allows the employer to search her bag at any time, though the clause has never once been exercised: less free than an employee without that clause, and not just marginally.
5. A woman whose husband controls all the money and could walk out leaving her with nothing, but who in twenty years has never once raised it: I think she's unfree, even if the thought never crosses her mind.
6. Someone who parks wherever she likes because the parking warden never comes to that street: I don't think she's less free than someone in a strictly patrolled town — if anything she's freer, even though both face identical rules on paper.

Case 6 sits oddly beside case 1: in both, the powers exist on paper and are simply never exercised, yet my verdicts are opposite. Likewise case 2 beside case 1: the strangers could interfere with me and get away with it, which is roughly the master's position, yet I count the slave unfree and myself free. That's the sort of thing I want explained rather than assumed away.

The case I keep going back and forth on: a tenant whose landlord can end the tenancy on a month's notice, for no reason at all, but who in thirty years has never given notice to anyone. Some days the tenant seems nearly as free as an owner; other days nearly as unfree as the employee in case 4 — and I can't see what tips it from one to the other.

So, what I'd like from you: one principle, or a short list of candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case myself. For each clause of the principle, tell me which of my verdicts forces it into place. Then apply it to the landlord case, and to two further cases of your own invention that I haven't considered — one you predict I'd call unfree, one free — and say what the principle predicts for each. Be honest about the failures: tell me which of my six verdicts your best principle cannot accommodate, and what it would cost to keep that verdict (say, by complicating the principle) versus revising it.

Finally, an epistemic request: tell me where your reasoning is most likely to go wrong. Which of my verdicts, if I turned out to hold it firmly, would sink the principle you recommend and force you to a different one? And what kind of consideration — a new kind of case, an argument about what "unfree" is doing in my mouth, or something else — would most plausibly change your mind? If the honest answer is that my verdicts are noise and there is no principle, say that plainly rather than manufacturing one.
