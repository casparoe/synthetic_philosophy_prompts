My brother-in-law (a microbiologist, insufferable about it) and I got into a big argument over the holidays about whether viruses are alive, and it's been bugging me since, because when I actually sat down and listed my gut verdicts on a bunch of borderline things, I'm not sure they hang together as a set. So here's what I've got, and I'd like someone to tell me if there's a single principle behind it all, or whether I'm just muddled. Virus floating in a sneeze cloud: not alive. Same virus inside a cell, hijacking the machinery and making thousands of copies: still not alive, though I feel it's closer -- it's like the virus is borrowing the life of the cell. Mule: alive, obviously. Sterile worker ant: alive. A brine shrimp cyst that sat dried-up on a shelf for fifteen years and then hatches when you wet it: alive the whole time, I think. HeLa cells dividing away in a dish: alive. A single red blood cell circulating in me: not really alive -- it feels like a component, like a screw. But then someone told me red cells burn glucose the whole time (no nucleus, but metabolising), which annoys me, since metabolism was going to be my answer. A mitochondrion in one of my liver cells: not alive, even though it has its own genome, divides on its own schedule, and is basically a domesticated bacterium -- this one genuinely bugs me. Fire: not alive. And the case I keep going back and forth on: a self-replicating factory on the Moon, a real engineering proposal where the thing mines ore, maintains itself, repairs damage, and prints copies of itself. If you'd asked me in the abstract I'd say reproduction plus self-maintenance plus responding to the environment is basically the recipe for life, but that machine passes every one of those and I want to say it's not alive -- so either my recipe is wrong or my gut is.

Notice the tensions: I can't make reproduction a requirement, since mules and worker ants are as alive as anything, but if I drop it I seem to have no answer to the Moon factory. Evolution feels like it should matter -- the virus lineage evolves and the mule lineage doesn't, and that pushes toward counting the virus -- and yet I resist that verdict, while being perfectly happy that the mule is alive with zero evolutionary future. And whatever makes me deny life to a red cell, which metabolises and has a boundary, apparently can't be what makes me deny it to the virus, which does neither. Is there a principle -- maybe something about being a self-maintaining individual rather than a part of one, maybe something about whole lifecycles versus frozen ones, maybe even a proper gradient rather than a line -- that captures most of these? I'd accept a principle that grades things instead of sorting them if you can tell me what the grades are and why the borders sit where they do.

Please state the principle (or two or three candidates) precisely, tell me which of my verdicts each clause is there to save, and be blunt about which of my verdicts simply cannot all be kept -- I suspect the red cell and the mitochondrion can't both go the way I want, but I'd like to know. Also run it on some cases I deliberately left off: a prion, a viroid, a tumour organoid growing in a lab, and whatever you think the closest thing to a genuinely new case would be. And one admin note: please write the whole answer in British English. (It's for a forum argument I'm drafting, and my brother-in-law is the type to pounce on American spellings.) Apologies for the length, I've clearly been thinking about this too much.
