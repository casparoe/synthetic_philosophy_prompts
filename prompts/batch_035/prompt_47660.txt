Here are my verdicts:

1. Someone born with a condition that requires expensive medication for life: society should cover it. Obviously.
2. Someone with perfectly good career options who chooses to surf and live cheaply on the beach instead, and ends up poor: no compensation. Their poverty tracks their choice.
3. A gambler who fully understands the odds, plays a fair game, and loses everything: no compensation. That's textbook option luck and I'm fine with it.
4. Same gamble, same odds, but this person grew up with a gambling-addicted parent in a town where basically everyone gambled, and realistically was never going to end up any different. Here I flip-flop constantly. Some days I think we compensate, some days not.
5. A rock climber who takes a bad fall on a hobby trip and ends up with huge medical bills and unable to work: I don't think they should be fully compensated, or at least it feels different from case 1. They chose the risk for fun.
6. A fisherman who takes a dangerous job because it's the only decent-paying work in his town and he's supporting a family, and gets seriously injured: I think he should be compensated. But wait -- he also knowingly took the risk, so how is this different from the climber? I can feel the tension and I can't resolve it myself.
7. Someone who cultivates expensive tastes (develops a love of fine wine, needs the good stuff to be content): no compensation for the extra cost of their preferences.
8. Someone whose religious commitments are genuinely expensive -- keeping strict kosher, required pilgrimage, etc.: this feels different from the wine person, and I'm inclined to say we do accommodate these costs somehow. But I can't easily say why the wine preference is her problem and the religious obligation isn't. Maybe you can help me out here.
9. Someone who could afford actuarially fair health insurance and chose not to buy it, then gets seriously ill: no compensation for the choice not to insure. But someone who was too poor to buy the same insurance and gets ill: compensate. This pair I feel fine about.

So: is there a single principle that captures all or most of these? The obvious move is the brute luck / option luck distinction, but notice it doesn't obviously handle 5 vs 6 (both are chosen risks) or 7 vs 8 (both are expensive preferences), so you'll need something more fine-grained than that -- maybe something about the range of reasonable alternatives available when the choice was made, or something about what we can trace to a person's genuine ambitions versus their circumstances.
