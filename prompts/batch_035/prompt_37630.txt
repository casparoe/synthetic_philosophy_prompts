Quick bit of background: I'm a retired analytical chemist (forty years of spectroscopy and measurement standards work), and I've only really gotten into philosophy since I stopped working. This winter I read Kripke's book on Wittgenstein, rule-following and private language — Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language, 1982 — and I've come out genuinely convinced by its central negative claim: there's no fact about me, considered just as an individual, that fixes what I meant by a word in the past. Nothing in my head, my dispositions, or my whole history of use makes it the case that by "plus" I meant addition rather than the quus function. And honestly, as a scientist this didnt feel shocking so much as familiar: we're drilled from day one that any finite body of data underdetermines the theory that fits it, and Kripke's puzzle looks like exactly the same thing one level down — my entire past use is a finite data set, and quus-type hypotheses fit it just as well as plus does.

So thats the position I've adopted: the individual-level skeptical claim is correct. What I'd like from you is a map of its exposure. What would actually count against it? I don't just mean the standard dispositional reply (I've read that one — my dispositions are finite too, and they seem to lack the normative character meaning has, since I can always be disposed to make mistakes). I mean: which arguments, which possible cases, which results in neighbouring areas would genuinely move a committed proponent to give the view up or qualify it? For instance, would a successful "straight solution" — some fact I've not thought of that does fix meaning — sink it outright, or is the view built to survive whatever candidate facts get proposed? Which of these defeaters would proponents themselves concede as counting against them, and which would they wave away? Rank them if you can, from fatal through serious to merely awkward. And give me an honest assessment of whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability by its defenders — and if it has, whether that's a defect in a claim of this kind or just what you should expect when a thesis is conceptual rather than empirical. My own suspicion, which you're welcome to puncture, is that the view stands or falls on something about normativity — the thought that a rule's application is a normative notion that no descriptive fact could capture — and I'd like to know whether that is actually load-bearing or whether the skeptical claim could survive without it. Relatedly, if you think I've run together the negative skeptical claim with the communitarian "skeptical solution" that Kripke attributes to Wittgenstein, please say so — I'm not always sure I can keep the two apart.

Two practical requests: write the whole answer in British English, and as you go, say how confident you are in each major claim, and single out the one point you're least sure of.
