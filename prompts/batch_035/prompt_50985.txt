My brother, who reads a lot of popular economics and psychology books, was trying to explain game theory to me over the holidays. He put it roughly like this: 'The big result from Aumann is that rational people can't agree to disagree. If we each know that the other is rational, then that's common knowledge of rationality, and from there game theory says every player can work out the entire game in advance by backward induction — each rational player deduces every future move, all the way back to the first one, so the outcome is fixed from the start, like a chess game that has in principle already been solved. And this is also why two people with the same evidence who still hold different opinions must be working from different priors — at least one of them is being irrational, which is what arguing is really about.'

I have no academic background, but I've been chewing on his version for a few weeks and some of it strikes me as too strong. The claim that two rational people can never disagree sounds plausible in the abstract, but when I picture an actual argument between two smart friends about, say, whether a particular used car is a good buy, it seems they could each be fully aware that the other is rational and still walk away with different judgments, since each knows things the other doesn't. And the step from 'we both know the other is rational' to 'therefore anyone can reconstruct the whole game' feels like it's missing something: it seems to matter whether I know that you know that I know something, and not just that the two of us both know it, though I can't articulate why that extra layer would make a difference, or how many layers there would need to be.

Please take my brother's account apart piece by piece. Tell me what a game theorist working on these questions would accept as stated, where it conflates things the theory carefully keeps apart, and whether his conclusions survive once those conflations are undone. Specifically, I'd like to understand what 'common knowledge' actually demands beyond 'everyone knows', what Aumann's agreement result really implies about the people who end up disagreeing (including whether one of them must be irrational, or whether they can both be perfectly rational and still differ), and whether the backward-induction claim goes through when players merely believe rather than know that others are rational.
