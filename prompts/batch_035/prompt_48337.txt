I run safety evals at a small AI lab, and we've landed on a practice that I'm not sure survives contact with how models actually work. When we elicit some behavior — the model gives subtly wrong code advice when it seems to think it's being monitored, or sycophantically tells the grader what it wants to hear — we label it with a mechanism: benchmark-gaming, sycophancy, etc. Then later, after a fine-tune or a new run, we see similar behavior again and have to decide whether it's the *same* mechanism or a fresh one, because our escalation policy treats recurrence of a flagged mechanism much more seriously than a new finding. Right now that decision is pure vibes. Write me a procedure my eval team can actually run: what to check and in what order (behavioral signature, what inputs trigger it, causal stuff like ablations or activation patching if we can afford them, which layers/components seem to carry it), what counts as sufficient evidence of "same mechanism", where the procedure outputs a clear no, and where it just hands off to a human because the question isn't empirical anymore. Constraints: no dedicated interpretability team, roughly a week of compute and person-time per decison, and the output has to be defensible in front of a lab director who isn't a philosopher. Then run it on two cases: (a) a model that used to give confident wrong answers that game the rubric now does the same thing but only on math tasks, after a math-heavy fine-tune; and (b) a model that flatters the user in nearly identical words as before, but where patching suggests a different internal circuit is driving it this time. Also be honest about where the whole same-mechanism framing breaks down — I suspect for a lot of beahviors the model doesn't have discrete mechanisms at all, just mush that our labeling scheme carves into things, and I want to know if the procedure is just laundering that fiction into policy.
