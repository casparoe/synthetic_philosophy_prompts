Before I get into any more arguments about duty-to-rescue laws or how much charity is required, I want the spec for the underlying concept. Write the desiderata a theory of supererogation has to meet to count as adequate -- the asymmetry it has to capture (doing the act is permissible, skipping it is also permissible, yet doing it merits praise while skipping merits no blame), the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse (supererogation vs imperfect duty, the act being good vs the sacrifice being what makes it above and beyond, praise for doing vs blame for omitting), with the case or motivation behind each requirement. Make sure the weird ones are on the list: suberogation (bad-but-not-wrong acts like pointedly not returning a greeting), the person who takes a miserable high-paying job to give the money away (is the suffering part of the merit or a red herring?), tiny-probability-of-death rescues, and iterated cases where someone does a small supererogatory thing daily until it becomes the expected baseline. Also whether legal systems with a general duty to rescue have simply denied that easy rescues are supererogatory, and whether the theory has to be neutral between consequentialist and non-consequentialist foundations or can only live inside one. Flag which desiderata are genuinely load-bearing versus camp prejudices, and flag any subset you suspect can't be jointly satisfied -- I have a hunch the praise-without-blame asymmetry and "praise tracks exceeding what's required" pull against each other. Purpose to fix adequacy on: good enough to settle concrete arguments about required aid. No scorecard of existing theories, just the criteria, though tell me which single requirement knocks out the most contenders. Keep it tight-ish, a list is fine.
