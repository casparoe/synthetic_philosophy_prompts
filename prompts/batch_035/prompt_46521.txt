My closest friend from graduate school and I spent most of last weekend arguing about the ethics of war, and by the time she left I had defended a bundle of positions that I am no longer confident I can hold all at once. For context: she argued the revisionist line that whether a soldier may permissibly kill depends partly on whether his side's war is just — that an unjust combatant wrongs the defender he kills even while obeying every rule of proportionality and discrimination. I pushed back hard on behalf of something like the traditional picture, and I'd like to know whether what I ended up defending actually hangs together before I write any of it up.

Here is roughly where I've landed. (1) The justice of the war and the permissibility of the soldier's conduct are independent questions: a conscript fighting within the rules on the unjust side does nothing wrong in fighting, just as his counterpart on the just side does nothing wrong — the moral equality of combatants. (2) What makes combatants permissible targets is that they pose threats, and what protects civilians is that they do not. (3) Even so, I believe the civilians of an aggressor state — the munitions workers, the taxpayers, the voters who returned the government to power — bear some genuine share of responsibility for the war their state fights, such that I'd resist describing them as innocent in the way a farmer supplying only his own village is innocent. (4) Consistent with that, I think a democracy's volunteer soldiers, who chose to fight for a government they could have voted out or simply not enlisted for, are more responsible for an unjust war than conscripts pressed into service by a dictatorship. (5) Unjust combatants who fought within the rules should nonetheless not be punished after the war ends. (6) A sniper acts wrongly in shooting an enemy soldier he has watched bathing unarmed in a river, though he would act permissibly in shooting the same man an hour later, back in uniform on the road.

My friend insisted the whole set collapses the moment you press on (2) and (3) together, but I genuinely can't tell whether she's right or whether most of my worries dissolve with more care. Some things I keep circling: if liability to attack really tracks posing a threat, why does the rifleman lose his immunity while the munitions worker — whose output arguably kills more people than he ever will — keeps hers, and does (3) simply make that worse? Does (4) smuggle responsibility back in through a door that (1) was supposed to hold shut, or is there a way of reading (1) as a claim about permission rather than blame under which they coexist? And does (5) sit badly with (4), or are those two talking past each other? Please rank whatever tensions you find by severity rather than treating them all as equally fatal; for each, say what would have to give and what the revision would cost me elsewhere in the set; and then tell me which single change would clean up the most and which apparent conflicts evaporate once you draw the right distinctions. I don't want a survey of just war theory — what I need is a diagnostic of my own position, and I'd rather you be blunt about which of my commitments is doing the least work.
