Write the joint statement that two members of a well-being research group should have produced after three years of arguing past each other. The first is a hedonist who answers the heterogeneity problem — the worry that the pleasure of eating mango, the pleasure of following a complicated proof to its conclusion, and the pleasure of a warm bath share no single felt quality — with an attitudinal or add-on account: pleasure is not a sensation but a way of taking states of affairs, roughly enjoyment that things are thus-and-so, which can attach to wildly different experiences and even, in cases like being pleased that a friend abroad is thriving without knowing how her week went, to no experience at all. The second began as a hedonist and defected: she now holds that pleasure is at best a cluster or disjunctive kind, held together by family resemblance and our word rather than a shared property, and that because there is no single thing pleasure is, there is no single thing hedonism says well-being consists in — her view is a perfectionist or objective-list pluralism on which the heterogeneity data are the smoking gun.

The deliverable is the adversarial-collaboration document itself, not a verdict on who is right. It should contain, in this order:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — about the heterogeneity data (that the introspective reports of diverse pleasures seem to resist any common feeling, and that a Benthamite homogeneous sensation of pleasure is off the table), about what is wrong with defining pleasure via what people desire (the circularity worries that go back at least to Butler's observation that we desire things because they are pleasant rather than their being pleasant consisting in being desired), and about which formulation of hedonism is actually at stake — the claim that well-being consists in the balance of attitudinal enjoyment over its opposite, not in any felt-quantity doctrine. Each agreed claim must be worded so that neither side is committing to the other's residual position.

2. A disagreements table with, for each point of divergence, one formulation the hedonist would sign describing her own view, one formulation the pluralist would sign describing hers, and a note on whether the divergence is substantive or partly verbal — for instance, whether the hedonist is entitled to concede that pleasure is disjunctive at the level of underlying properties while insisting it is unified at the level of the attitude, or whether that concession already gives the pluralist everything she needs.

3. The cruxes, stated as questions such that settling them would move at least one party. I suspect they include: (a) whether a phenomenal inversion between two people's pleasures is fully coherent — one person's felt quality of muzak-listening being the other's felt quality of solve-proving, with all attitudes and reports intact — and if so, whether it is the hedonist's or the pluralist's view that survives it; (b) whether the add-on theorist can give a non-circular account of what makes an attitude enjoyment rather than mere desire-satisfaction or preference; (c) whether the pleasures that seem least intentional — orgasm, the runner's high, sun on skin — are recalcitrant data for the attitudinal account or merely limit cases of it; and (d) whether the hedonist's fallback to attitude has quietly changed the subject, so that well-being hedonism and the metaphysics of pleasure have come apart and the debate is now about the former only.

4. For each crux, the test both sides agree bears on it — thought experiments like Feldman-style cases of unexperienced pleasure, imagined agents who enjoy only the logical structure of a piece of music with none of its sonic surface, or memory swaps of felt qualities across activities — together with each party's prediction about that test, recorded in advance, in wording each would accept.

Throughout, guard against two failure modes: agreed claims so watered-down that they say nothing (both accept that pleasure is interesting), and disagreement formulations that smuggle in one side's terminology as neutral. If part of the dispute turns out to be about how to use the word pleasure rather than about what there is, say which part and why — but also say why the remainder does not collapse along with it.
