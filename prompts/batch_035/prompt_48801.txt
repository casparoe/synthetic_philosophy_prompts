Our reading group has got stuck on something and, embarrassingly, it's mostly my fault for pushing it. We were talking about whether my flatmate — who, let's say, is creative with his tax returns — can properly blame his colleague for fiddling her travel expenses. Everyone's instinct was no, that's just hypocrisy, and someone said the technical term is that he lacks "standing" to blame. Fine. But then I tried to figure out what actually grounds the loss of standing and ended up with an argument that seems to prove way too much, and now I can't find the exit.

Here it is. (1) When A blames B for a wrong that A is himself guilty of, A's blame is pro tanto wrong: in blaming, A claims a kind of authority over B that his own conduct has forfeited. (2) The forfeiting can't require the exact same wrong — the tax cheat plainly loses standing to blame the expenses-fiddler even though tax fraud and expense fiddling are different wrongs — so what does the work must be relevant similarity between A's wrong and B's. (3) But relevant similarity comes in chains. Fiddling expenses is relevantly similar to shoplifting (minor dishonesty for personal gain); shoplifting to pocketing a found wallet; that to keeping the change when a shop assistant overpays; that to not correcting a friend who generously assumes you paid for dinner last time when you didn't. No single link in such a chain looks like a stretch, and each pair is, on its own, a case where we'd at least say the blamer's hands aren't clean. (4) Everyone is guilty of something in the chain that terminates in any ordinary wrong. (5) So everyone's blame of anyone, for any ordinary wrong, is pro tanto wrong — everyone is to some degree a hypocrite. (6) But if everyone's blame is always at least a bit wrong, then wrongness of the blame can't be what made the original tax case objectionable, because we don't think ordinary, everyday blame of friends and colleagues is even slightly wrong — it's just what holding each other to standards looks like. (7) So either standing doesn't have anything to do with hypocrisy after all, or one of the earlier steps is rotten, and I honestly can't tell which.

For what it's worth, I'd very much like to keep (1) — the tax cheat's blame really does seem off in a way the reformed ex-cheat's maybe doesn't — and (6) feels non-negotiable. Someone suggested that the fix is that you get standing back once you've repudiated your own past wrong, so only current, unrepudiated similarity counts against you, but that seems to make standing far too cheap and conveniently recoverable just by saying the right things. And my tutor just muttered something about maybe the problem with hypocrites being not the similarity as such but that they're setting a standard they implicitly exempt themselves from — which I can't see how to turn into a line I can actually draw in the chain. So: which step should I give up, why did it look fine in the first place, and what do I pay for each way out — including, if you think that's the honest answer, giving up on standing being about hypocrisy at all?
