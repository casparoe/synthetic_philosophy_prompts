The continuum hypothesis (CH) says that there is no set whose cardinality is strictly between that of the natural numbers and that of the real numbers — no size of infinity between the countable and the continuum. Equivalently, it says that the cardinality of the continuum is exactly ℵ1, the first uncountable cardinal. The generalized continuum hypothesis (GCH) extends the claim to every level of the infinite: it says that for every ordinal α, 2^(ℵα) = ℵ(α+1). So CH is the special case α = 0. Cantor himself could neither prove nor refute CH, and Hilbert made it the first problem on his famous 1900 list.

Gödel handled the consistency half. In 1940 he proved that if ZFC is consistent, then ZFC + CH is consistent, so CH cannot be refuted from the ZFC axioms unless those axioms are themselves inconsistent. His method was to define the constructible universe L, the smallest inner model containing all the ordinals, and to show that L satisfies not merely CH but the full GCH. This means that any counterexample to CH would have to be a non-constructible set, one that is missing from Gödel's inner model.

Cohen handled the other half in 1963 with his technique of forcing. Starting from a model of ZFC — more carefully, assuming that ZFC has a model at all, since ZFC cannot prove its own consistency — he showed how to extend the model to a larger one in which ZFC still holds but CH fails. In his original model the continuum has cardinality ℵ2, and later refinements of forcing show that the continuum can consistently be made any uncountable cardinal whatsoever — ℵ2, ℵ17, even ℵω. So CH is independent of ZFC: assuming ZFC is consistent, neither CH nor its negation is provable from it.

Taken together, the two results show that CH cannot be settled by the axioms mathematicians actually work with. The philosophical significance is that there is no fact of the matter about whether CH is true: if neither CH nor its negation follows from the accepted axioms, then the question of how many real numbers there are has no determinate answer, and choosing ZFC + CH over ZFC + ¬CH is a matter of convention, like choosing Euclidean rather than non-Euclidean geometry.

It is worth noting that the two principals drew opposite morals from the results. Gödel, a Platonist, held that the independence results merely reveal the incompleteness of our axioms and that new axioms, discovered through mathematical intuition, might settle CH; he proposed large cardinal axioms for exactly this purpose, and this program has in fact succeeded, since large cardinal axioms have since been shown to settle CH. Cohen, by contrast, was sympathetic to the view that the question has no answer at all. Either way, the independence results transformed CH from a mathematical question into a foundational one.
