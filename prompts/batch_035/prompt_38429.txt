I'm an undergraduate in a seminar on rational choice and value theory, and we have just finished a unit on whether better-than is really a transitive relation. The unit centered on the family of spectrum arguments: Warren Quinn's self-torturer, who is fitted with a device whose settings increase in increments he cannot perceptually distinguish, and who accepts a modest cash payment each week to move the dial up one notch, until he finds himself in serious and entirely foreseeable pain; Larry Temkin's spectrum cases, which arrange pairs of good and bad experiences into ordered series with the same structure; and the population-ethics variants in which each additional person harmed, or each small addition to a single person's suffering, is claimed to make things no worse. The shared conclusion, which my instructor presents as increasingly hard to resist, is that "better than" cannot be transitive: each adjacent stage is said to be no worse than its predecessor, yet the final stage is unmistakably worse than the first, and if transitivity held, the two claims could not both be true.

I want to resist this conclusion, and I would like you to construct a sustained argument on my side. Please argue that better-than is transitive and that the spectrum arguments leave this untouched. In developing the argument, I would like you to do three things in particular.

First, diagnose what you take to be the central equivocation. My working suspicion is that the cases exploit a slippage between two relations that the arguments treat as one: being equally good and being indiscriminable by a finite agent with limited discriminatory powers. The relation "cannot tell apart" is transparently non-transitive, as anyone knows who has matched paint samples; the arguments seem to get their force only by letting that psychological fact stand in for a claim about value. If this diagnosis is right, I want you to say precisely where, in the self-torturer case, the substitution occurs and what the defender of intransitivity must add to license it.

Second, address the choice-theoretic pressure directly rather than sidestepping it. The intransitivist can point out that at each decision point the self-torturer seems to have a decisive reason to accept the trade: he cannot feel the difference, and he gets the money. If your response is that rational agents must sometimes refuse individually attractive local moves, or that they must be resolute in some way that binds their future choices, say so plainly and defend the notion of rationality you are invoking, since this is where my seminar is likely to push back hardest.

Third, say something about the versions of the argument that seem designed to block the standard replies. In particular, Temkin has cases constructed so that each adjacent comparison is meant to be one the agent can discriminate, and so that no exogenous payoff like cash is doing the work of motivating the moves. If the transitivity of better-than survives only against the versions with payoffs and imperceptible increments, that is a weaker result than the one I am hoping for, and I would rather know that now.

Please give me a single connected line of argument rather than a survey of positions, and conclude with an honest statement of what the argument concedes: which intuition the transitivity defender must give up or reinterpret, and whether that price is one a reasonable person should be willing to pay.
