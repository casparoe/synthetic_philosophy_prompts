**Chasing the podium: what the happiness rankings actually rank**

Every year the world happiness rankings come out and every year we're told the Finns are at it again. The headline number comes from a single survey question: people are asked to picture a ladder with the best possible life for them at the top and the worst possible life for them at the bottom, and to say which rung they stand on. That's it. Nobody is asked how they felt yesterday, whether they laughed, whether they were sad. The ranking that governments brag about — and that some are starting to set policy targets around — measures where people think they stand relative to the best life they can imagine.

The trouble is that "the best life you can imagine" is not a fixed ruler. What counts as possible is set by your culture's reference points: advertising, media, what your society treats as having made it. A Dane whose life is pleasant but ordinary measures herself against a modest, secure horizon and gives it a 7.5. Someone with the same material life in a culture that frames the good life as exceptional — one that celebrates only a narrow set of winners — may rate that identical life much lower, not because it's worse but because the ceiling she's comparing it to is further away. Cross-country comparisons assume everyone's 10 means the same thing, and I don't think we have any reason to believe it does.

The affect data tell a slightly different story anyway. Questions about smiling, enjoyment and worry on the previous day seem to flatter some countries the ladder treats unkindly — several Latin American countries do noticeably better on those questions than on the ladder. So even within the same survey, "how you judge your life" and "how your life feels" can come apart, and the rankings crown the judge, not the feeling.

So should a country try to top the table? Only if it wants to top *this* table. The moment "move up the rankings" becomes an official goal, you've Goodharted yourself: the cheapest routes up are managing expectations downwards or coaching people in how to answer — changes that improve the number while doing nothing for how anyone actually feels. The sane version is to aim at what we think the number tracks — security, trust, having someone to count on — and let the ranking take care of itself.
