so I'm a novelist, three books into a family saga, and a question I can't shake keeps showing up in my drafts. I spend all day writing sentences like "the wedding party spilled onto the lawn" or "she gathered what was left of the apartment — the photos, the piano, the burnt crib — into one heap." Every time I do that, I'm kind of implying that a bunch of things is itself a thing. The party is a thing, the heap is a thing. But is it? Or is it just lots of things arranged party-wise?

I gather philosophers call this the special composition question, and I've bumped into the main camps: composition never happens (so there's no heap, just heap-arranged photos and piano parts — and actually no piano either?), composition happens whenever, so there's an object made of my left shoe and the Eiffel Tower, and van Inwagen's line where only living things get to compose. What I want is a plain-language explanation of what the question is actually asking, why the obvious answer — the things compose when they're stuck together or form a unified whole — is supposed to be so bad, and where each of the main answers wins and loses intuitively.

Then the part I actually care about: does any of this matter for fiction? My working theory is that novelists are basically forced to write as common-sense realists — I can't narrate a house fire without treating "the house" as one thing that gets destroyed — but I've noticed I do weird stuff at the edges, like describing a family as one organism when it suits me and as five separate people two pages later. Is that sloppy writing, or am I (per my editor, who took exactly one phil class) helping myself to a metaphysics at the sentence level and then switching it whenever its convenient? If a novelist had to commit to one of the answers for the sake of consistency, which one would do the least damage to normal narration? I'm not asking you to settle the metaphysics — just explain it well enough that I know what I'm smuggling into my sentences, and tell me if my instinct is right that this stuff matters more for prose than most people think.
