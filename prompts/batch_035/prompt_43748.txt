So in two weeks I'm giving a talk at a department that's mostly formal semanticists plus a couple of old-school Oxford-style philosophers of language, and I'm going to argue that Russell basically got definite descriptions right -- that a sentence with a definite description in it says there's exactly one F and that thing is G, and that the sentence is just false when uniqueness or existence fails, not truth-valueless. My line is that most of what Strawson threw at Russell in the fifties (the truth-value gap, the idea that presupposition is semantic, the charge that Russell confused meaning with assertion) survives only if you refuse to split semantics from pragmatics, and that once you make the Gricean move -- treated as having existence entailed rather than presupposed, gaps handled by implicature rather than semantics -- Russell's analysis does fine. I even want to defend the scope-ambiguity story for negative existentials, that the present king of France doesn't exist is true on the wide-negation reading and that's the reading we normally want.

The audience worries me though. The formal semanticists will come at me with incomplete descriptions (someone points at a table and says the table is covered in books -- there are a million tables, so where's the uniqueness?), domain restriction stuff, and the fact that nobody in the actual semantics literature writes Russell's three-clause analysis into their compositional semantics anymore. The Oxford crowd will hammer me on Strawson's point that it's bizarre to say someone who utters the king of France sentence has *asserted* that France is a monarchy, and on the phenomenology of referring -- the feeling that I'm talking *about* the table when I point at it. And I suspect someone will bring up Donnellan's referential use and claim it shows descriptions sometimes just refer, full stop.

Draft me a Q&A prep sheet: the eightish objections I'll actually get from this specific audience, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way a formal semanticist or a Strawsonian would actually put it (their vocabulary, not mine), with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft -- be honest about that, I don't want to walk in overconfident. Say explicitly which objections I should just concede. And include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, which for me is probably something about whether my Gricean story even works when the speaker doesn't notice the presupposition failure (the man drinking a martini who's actually drinking water case) -- I've never felt like I had a clean answer to that one. Also flag if you think I'm just wrong to treat the Strawson critique as mostly a semantics/pragmatics confusion, since that's the load-bearing assumption of the whole talk and I'd rather find out now than in the Q&A.
