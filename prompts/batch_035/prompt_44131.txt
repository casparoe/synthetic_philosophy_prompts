A friend of mine claims that Zorn's lemma is not really a choice principle at all but something close to a theorem of plain good sense, and he has an argument that I can feel going wrong without being able to say where. He calls it the keep-climbing argument, and when I pushed him on it he agreed that the same picture, applied to the ordinals, would deliver a largest ordinal — which I am quite sure is false. Here is the whole thing, as he gives it:

(1) Let P be a partially ordered set in which every chain has an upper bound.
(2) Start at any element x₀ of P.
(3) If x₀ is not maximal, some element of P lies strictly above it; pick one and climb to it.
(4) At a limit stage, take an upper bound of the chain climbed so far — available by hypothesis — and continue.
(5) The climb cannot run through all the ordinals: the elements visited are strictly increasing and hence distinct, and an injection of all the ordinals into the set P is impossible.
(6) So the climb must stop, and it can only stop at an element with nothing strictly above it, i.e., at a maximal element.
(7) Nothing in this picture appeals to the axiom of choice or to any other assumption beyond the ordering; so Zorn's lemma is not an axiom in disguise but an observation about any ordered structure in which every chain is bounded.
(8) In particular, the picture nowhere used that P is a set.
(9) Now take for P the ordinals themselves, under their natural order. Every chain of ordinals — every set of ordinals — has an upper bound, namely its supremum.
(10) By the same climb, there is a maximal ordinal.
(11) But there is no largest ordinal, since every ordinal has a successor.
(12) So the keep-climbing picture collapses.

My friend is not bothered. He says the class case is a freak show — proper classes are notorious lawbreakers — while for ordinary sets the picture stays self-evident, and he rests on that. But this strikes me as too convenient: whatever quietly blocks the argument at (8)–(10) may be exactly the assumption that was doing the real work all along in (2)–(6), in which case the "self-evident" gloss was oversold from the start. My own nagging suspicion is that the "pick one" in (3) is doing more than the word suggests; my friend answers that in the ordinal case there is an obvious thing to pick — the least ordinal above the current one — so if the class argument fails, it cannot be the picking that fails it. I cannot see through this exchange.

So: which step should be rejected, and what exactly does rejecting it cost? I would like an answer that explains why the rejected step looked fine — why the keep-climbing picture is seductive precisely where it breaks — and that faces the ordinal application head-on rather than waving at the strangeness of classes. And I want the further question answered honestly: once the picture is repaired enough to avoid a largest ordinal, does it still establish Zorn's lemma for sets without any appeal to choice, or does the repair quietly reintroduce a choice principle under another name? For what it is worth, I would like to keep the thought in (5) in some repaired form, because the impossibility of an endless strict climb is what makes Zorn's lemma feel obvious to me in the first place, and I would like to know whether that feeling can be vindicated or has to be written off as an artifact of only ever climbing finite ladders.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — name the step you reject — and then give the reasoning. No preamble, and do not restate the setup.
