You are a professional philosopher specializing in metaethics, with particular expertise on evolutionary debunking arguments. I am writing the final exam for an upper-level metaethics seminar, and I want a single essay question on evolutionary debunking that cannot be answered by reciting the standard literature. Before drafting anything, brainstorm the question's design: identify what the genuinely hard, non-memorizable crux of the debunking debate is — I'd suggest it is the overdetermination/overgeneralization problem, i.e., that debunking reasoning which only appeals to the evolutionary origins of our value beliefs seems to debunk too much (mathematical, logical, and perceptual beliefs also have evolutionary origins or evolutionary filters applied to them) unless it is supplemented with something that distinguishes moral beliefs, and that supplement is where the argument either stands or falls.

With that crux in mind, here is the argument I want the question built around; it is a hybrid I have constructed for this exam, so students will not find it in the secondary literature. Call it the Tracking Dilemma:

1. Either our evaluative faculties were shaped by evolutionary forces that were sensitive to the independent evaluative truth, or they were not.
2. If they were not so shaped, then the correlation between our evaluative beliefs and the evaluative truth is inexplicable — a massive coincidence — and so those beliefs are unjustified.
3. If they were so shaped, then natural selection's evaluative judgments are themselves tracking something real, which means either evaluative facts are reducible to facts about reproductive fitness (which no realist can accept) or selection was sensitive to an irreducibly normative property (which is unintelligible, since fitness effects are exhaustively described in non-normative terms).
4. Therefore, realist metaethics is false either way.

Note that premise 2 is doing more work than in the standard Darwinian Dilemma as students will have encountered it in Sharon Street's work: it does not merely say that an evolutionary explanation of our evaluative beliefs is available and does not mention the truth, but that the absence of an evolutionary truth-connection makes the correlation a coincidence. This stronger reading is exactly what invites the overgeneralization worry, since our mathematical beliefs also show no sign of having been selected for their sensitivity to mathematical truth.

The question should require students to do all of the following:

- Reconstruct the Tracking Dilemma accurately, including noticing where it departs from the standard Darwinian Dilemma (in particular, the coincidence clause in premise 2 and the two-pronged dilemma in premise 3).
- Assess whether the argument overgeneralizes: state precisely the principle about explanatory origins and justification that premise 2 needs, and test it against mathematical intuition, perceptual belief, and introspective self-knowledge. Students should recognize that the standard reply — that perception has a genuine truth-connection in the causal sense that distal objects cause our perceptual states — either fails for normative properties (which seem causally inert if irreducible) or succeeds only by conceding reducibility, but that this asymmetry must be argued, not asserted.
- Evaluate the third-factor style of reply (as in Enoch's work, and in the more general "defensive" strategy): that some independently justified belief can rationalize trusting a faculty whose origins do not mention truth, as we trust arithmetic before we have any evolutionary story about arithmetic. Students should have to say whether the third factor itself survives debunking or merely relocates the problem.
- Take a final position on whether the realist can block the Tracking Dilemma by giving up the coincidence premise: namely, by arguing that the demand for a non-coincidental explanation of the truth-connection is itself a substantive epistemological commitment that not everyone accepts, and that if the realist rejects it, the debunking argument loses its leverage. This is the hardest part and should be flagged in the question as such.

Deliverables, in this order:

1. The essay question itself, phrased so a strong student could answer it in 90 minutes, with the Tracking Dilemma stated in full in the question (students should not be asked to reconstruct it from scratch, since I have given it to them here).
2. A model answer of roughly 700 words that does not merely summarize positions but actually executes the reasoning the question demands, ending in a reasoned verdict.
3. A marking scheme worth 25 marks total, structured around five to seven must-hit points, each with its mark allocation and a one-line note on how students typically get that point wrong. At least one must-hit point should specifically reward students who notice that premise 2's coincidence clause is a strengthening of the standard argument, and at least one should penalize the reflexive but shallow move of declaring that the argument "proves too much" without articulating the exact principle being tested and the exact point at which the realist can break the analogy with mathematics. Make clear in the marking scheme what separates an A answer from a B answer.

Please think carefully about the level: this is an upper-level undergraduate seminar, so students will know the basic Street/Enoch dialectic but should not be assumed to know the hybrid argument, which is the point of introducing it. Do not include any reading list, bibliography, or citations in the question or model answer.
