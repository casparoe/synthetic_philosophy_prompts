Quick background on me so you know where I'm coming from: I spent thirty-four years as a geochemist, mostly doing isotope work on very old rocks, and since retiring I've gotten deep into reading ancient history for fun, which led me to philosophy of history, which led me to realize I have all these gut reactions to sources that are basically unexamined habits from the lab. I'd like you to help me figure out if there's an actual principle underneath them. Here are my verdicts on a bunch of cases, told straight: a Babylonian astronomical diary recording a lunar eclipse within days of it happening, I trust basically completely. A victory inscription put up by a king right after his campaign, I discount the bragging but still believe the campaign happened. Two medieval chronicles agreeing on a weirdly specific detail (say, the enemy king fleeing barefoot at night) felt like strong evidence to me until I read that both chronicles descend from a single lost one, at which point the agreement counted for nothing, even though nothing about the detail itself changed. A memoir that admits something embarassing to its own side, I trust more than the same fact reported by a neutral party. A chronicle reporting something embarrassing about the chronicler's rivals gets no such bump from me. An account that names a provincial governor who the inscriptions show wasn't in office until years later, I now distrust across the board, even for the parts that aren't checkable, more than I would distrust a source that was merely vague. And a story written down four centuries after the events, I'll believe the big war happened but not the speeches, even though I can't say what changed between the war-sized facts and the speech-sized ones.

Here's the pair I suspect is the real problem: I treat the silence of an enemy's records as evidence that something didn't happen, so if the Hittites never mention a huge battle we heard about from Egypt, I get suspicious. But I also think a defeated people systematically don't inscribe their losses, so their silence about a defeat they plausibly suffered tells me almost nothing. Silence seems to have different evidential weight depending on who would have been doing the silencing, and I can't tell if that's a real distinction in the principle or just me rationalizing case by case.

So what I want is this: state the principle, or maybe two or three candidate principles, that best captures all those verdicts. Tell me exactly which clause handles which case, like I'd want it in a methods paper. Then tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle can't reproduce and whether I should drop the verdict or complicate the principle (I suspect the barefoot-king one, where I flipped my weight the moment I learned about the shared source, is secretly doing a lot of work and might be incompatible with how I treat partial independence elsewhere). Then apply it to a case I haven't given you: a report of a comet and a famine, preserved in a single manuscript that also contains one demonstrably wrong date, attributed to a monk writing two generations after the events -- I want your predicted verdict and why. And since my whole framing comes from lab science: point out where my evidence-intuition about testimony is just wrong, if it is, because I know testimony isn't an instrument reading, but I honestly don't know which of my habits transfer and which don't. Please answer in American English.
