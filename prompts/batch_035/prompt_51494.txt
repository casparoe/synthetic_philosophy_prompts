I chair the incident-review panel at a company that fields deployed AI systems — a voice assistant, warehouse robots, a document classifier — and after every incident our report has to say what the system took something to be: that the classifier read the invoice as fraudulent, that the robot took the pallet for a stationary one. These attributions are not decoration; they decide where the fix goes — retraining the model, rewriting the engineering spec, or reclassifying the incident as an upstream data problem. My panel keeps splitting along a line I now recognize as the old dispute between Searle and Dennett over original versus derived intentionality. One faction holds, with Searle, that only people can genuinely misrepresent, so any machine error is really an error by whoever designed or trained the machine, and our reports should never attribute content to the system itself. The other faction follows Dennett's two-bitser: a soft-drink machine built in the United States to detect quarters ends up in Panama, where quarter-balboas circulate that are indistinguishable from quarters by weight and size, and Dennett's line is that what the machine really detects is fixed by its design history rather than by what it currently happens to discriminate — so attributing a specific content to an artifact can be legitimate, but the content is not always the one current behavior suggests. I want to settle this with a standing procedure the panel runs on every incident, usable by members with no philosophy background within the hour we have per case. Please give me exactly five numbered questions, asked in a fixed order, each phrased in a sentence or two, that jointly determine: whether it is legitimate to attribute any specific content to the system at all in this incident; which of two candidate contents to attribute when the system's current behavior fits both equally well, as the two-bitser's fits both quarter and balboa; and at which point the remaining question is one of policy rather than fact, so the panel must stop and hand the call to a person. Each question should indicate what counts as a sufficient answer and which answer terminates the procedure with no further questions asked. Give just the five numbered questions with no introduction or conclusion.
