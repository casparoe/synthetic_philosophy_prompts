I work as a biodiversity assessor for a regional environmental consultancy, and a weirdly large chunk of my job comes down to one question that my zoology degree never actually equipped me to answer: are these two populations the same species or not? It's not academic for us — whether a client needs protected-species surveys, translocation permits, or can just build depends on the call, and a listing decision can hold up a development worth tens of millions, or conversely leave a genuinely distinct lineage with no protection at all. The wrinkle is that my training gave me the standard menu of species concepts — reproductive isolation, smallest diagnosable monophyletic clade, morphology, gene flow, ecological niche — and told me the "species problem" is unresolved, but nobody ever told me what to do on a Monday morning when I have two rodent populations on either side of a river valley that intergrade morphologically, hybridise a bit where they meet, and come out as seperate branches in one published phylogeny and a single branch in another. These calls come up maybe a dozen times a year, I usually have no genomics lab and rely on morphology plus whatever sequences are already published, our ecology lead has to sign off on every one, and if the regulator disagrees with our reasoning we can lose approved-assessor status. There's also an awkward incentive I want the procedure to be robust to: clients prefer lumping (fewer species, fewer obligations), and I don't want a procedure that quietly drifts toward whatever's convenient.

So what I'm after is a genuine decision procedure — something I can run in an afternoon on a spreadsheet, with the steps in order, what each step asks, what evidence feeds it, what counts as a sufficient answer to stop, and where it stops with "one species", "two species", or "insufficient data, commission genetics". Crucially, mark the points where the biology genuinely underdetermines the answer and it becomes a values or policy judgement that should get handed off to my ecology lead or the regulator rather than smuggled into the procedure as if it were science. I'd also like you to run it on three worked cases so I can see whether it actually works. Case one: two brown cricket populations, morphologically near-identical, that differ only in male calling song and, where their ranges overlap, don't respond to each other's songs. Case two: two orchid populations my field colleague swears are obviously distinct species because they flower four weeks apart and seem to attract different pollinators, but with no usable genetic data and only subtle morphological differences. Case three: a group of apomictic dandelions — the asexual, clone-forming kind — where the field team informally treats about fifteen "microspecies" as real and our plant taxonomy book lists hundreds. On that last one I suspect there's no biological fact of the matter in the way I want there to be, which is exactly the kind of answer I won't like, so please be straight about it rather than papering over it. Also tell me where the procedure will predictably misfire — e.g. ring species, recent divergence, or hybrid swarms — and what I should do when it does.

For the deliverable, could you give the procedure itself as a table with columns: Step number, Question asked, Evidence it uses, What counts as a sufficient answer, and Fallback if inconclusive. Then after the three worked applications, a short JSON object summarising them, with one entry per case having the fields "case", "verdict", "confidence" (high / medium / low), and "what_the_procedure_couldnt_settle". Plain English between the table and the JSON is fine — I just need the structured bits so I can drop them straight into our QA template. Cheer!
