I am a graduate student in bioethics writing a paper on a tort doctrine, and I would like help reconstructing the theory of harm it implicitly relies on. The doctrine is this: when a physician's negligence — a failed sterilization, a missed genetic diagnosis, inaccurate testing — results in the birth of a child with a serious impairment, the parents can generally sue for wrongful birth and recover the extraordinary costs of raising that child. But the child herself, suing for wrongful life, is far worse off: most American jurisdictions refuse the cause of action outright, typically on the ground that a court cannot compare the value of an impaired life with the value of nonexistence, and a handful of states (California, New Jersey, Washington, Maine) allow it only in a truncated form — the child can recover the objectively provable costs attributable to the impairment, but nothing for the fact of having been born into a compromised life at all. Germany took a starker line: the Federal Constitutional Court held that a wrongful life claim is incompatible with the constitutional guarantee of human dignity, reasoning that it treats a disabled person's life as less valuable than nonexistence, though German courts still managed to award families something by recasting the loss as the parents' maintenance obligation rather than the child's impaired existence.

What I want analyzed is the implicit theory of harm that this pattern presupposes, especially given an awkward feature: tort law ordinarily asks whether the plaintiff is worse off than she would have been but for the defendant's negligence, but in these cases the but-for world is one in which this particular plaintiff never exists — the negligent doctor did not make her life worse than an alternative life she would otherwise have had; the alternative was no life. So does the majority rule rejecting the claim presuppose that harm requires a person who is worse off in a comparison between two states of her own life? Does the four-state compromise presuppose some non-comparative notion of harm — that impairments are bad for a person regardless of the counterfactual — while refusing to extend it to existence itself? And what view of the value of existence does the German dignity rationale presuppose, and is it actually consistent with a system that nevertheless compensates parents on the premise that having this impaired child was a loss for them?

Please reconstruct the doctrine's implicit commitments, distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what it merely coheres with, say where it is underdetermined enough that several theories of harm would license the same outcome, and explain what the doctrine would have to look like if a different account of when a person is harmed by being brought into existence were adopted. In your analysis, please separate what is genuinely settled — the actual reasoning courts have given, and points of agreement among people who work on this — from what remains contested among philosophers (for instance, over whether existence can be worse than nonexistence, or over how to handle harms where the alternative is the victim's nonexistence), and flag your own interpretive choices rather than presenting contested readings as the consensus view.
