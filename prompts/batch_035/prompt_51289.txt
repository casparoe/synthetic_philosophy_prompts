I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on theories of justice — eighteen students, one seventy-five-minute session on Nozick's entitlement theory. Design an in-class game that stages the Wilt Chamberlain argument rather than describing it. Participants should first impose a distributional pattern on themselves (they will probably choose equality, and I want them to own that choice), and then, through voluntary transfers alone — including transfers to a designated star whose advantage is a real but trivial skill that you have me test for openly at the start — the pattern should visibly break. The game needs an institutional layer: an elected Pattern Commission empowered to restore the chosen pattern after it breaks, where each restoration costs the whole class tokens and trading then resumes, so that the treadmill of continuous interference to maintain a pattern is something they experience rather than hear about. Add a second round that replaces the skilled star with a purely lucky one, decided by coin flips, so the debrief can separate discomfort with broken patterns from discomfort with undeserved endowments. Give me the full rules, a token payoff scheme that requires no real money, the point at which I should reveal that this was Nozick, and a debrief that maps each phase onto the components of the entitlement theory — acquisition, transfer, rectification — and onto the objection that patterned principles are unstable precisely because of the liberties the students exercised. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I expect refusals to trade, sandbagging, and awkwardness around the star), and be honest about which part of the argument the game cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself — I suspect the theory of just acquisition, since the game stipulates the initial distribution rather than justifying it.
