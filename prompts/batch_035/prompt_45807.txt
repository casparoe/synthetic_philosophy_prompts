For a term paper in my philosophy elective, I need to compare Peirce's account of truth with William James's, and I have gotten stuck on a case my teacher posed. Peirce holds that truth is what inquiry is destined to converge on: the settled opinion that investigators would ultimately agree to, given enough time and no obstacles. James, by contrast, seems to tie truth to what a belief does for the believer — the true is, roughly, what works, or what is good in the way of belief. The case is this: suppose humanity discovered, with certainty, that all inquiry would end in fifty years. On Peirce's view, questions that would not be settled in time would seem to become neither true nor false, which makes truth hostage to an accident of survival; on James's view, the problem is the opposite, since a sprinter who falsely believes she is the favourite runs faster, and it looks like the belief's usefulness should then count toward its truth. So my two questions: Is there a defensible version of Peirce's view — for instance, reading convergence counterfactually, as what inquirers would settle on given unlimited time — that escapes the fifty-year problem, or does that counterfactual reading quietly collapse into a correspondence theory that no longer deserves the name pragmatist? And can James's view distinguish the sprinter's useful delusion from a genuine truth without smuggling in a standard of accuracy from outside the theory? Please explain which of the two accounts you think comes out of this comparison in better shape, and why, as I need to take a defended position rather than just survey both sides.
