I'm on my city's charter review commission — volunteer thing, we meet once a month in the library basement and mostly argue about whether the at-large council seats should stay. Last meeting a proposal landed on us and it's mine to write up before the next one, and I want your help drafting it.

The proposal, pushed by a local housing group and one commissioner: a five-year pilot letting the mayor's office fast-track affordable housing permits — shortened public comment windows, hearings delegated to a mayoral appointee rather than the council, appeals handled internally. Their pitch is basically Singapore and Shenzhen: those places built housing at a pace we can't touch, and the reason, they say, is exactly this kind of insulated, decisive, get-it-done governance. Their slide deck literally has a chart of housing completions per capita with us at the bottom and Singapore at the top, and next to it a quote about how people will trade process for results.

Three of the commissioners love it, two think it's a mini-coup, and I'm supposed to circulate a written recommendation before the vote. Here's what I want the memo to actually grapple with, because the discussion so far has been vibes:

The obvious framing is performance legitimacy — the idea that a government's right to rule (or to make decisions without the usual checks) can come from delivering prosperity and order rather than from how it got chosen. But nobody on the commission has actually thought about what the argument commits you to. One wrinkle: if legitimacy rests on performance, it's conditional in a way procedural legitimacy isn't — Singapore's legitimacy arguably depends on never having a really bad decade. So maybe the right test for our pilot isn't "did the numbers go up" but "what happens in the counterfactual where the mayor's office botches it — do we have a mechanism to pull powers back, and is five years of insulated decision-making something a city can even walk back?" Another wrinkle I keep chewing on: is it actually *legitimacy* that the Singapore case gives you, or just tolerability — citizens putting up with something rather than endorsing it, maybe because exit or voice is expensive? If it's just tolerability, the housing group's argument quietly changes from "this would be justified" to "people would put up with it," which is a different and worse claim for a ballot measure.

And then the confound problem, which the deck does not mention at all: how much of Singapore's housing record is due to insulated executive power versus other stuff — land nationalization decades ago, city-state scale, a particular civil service culture? If it's mostly the other stuff, our pilot imports the authoritarian-ish part and skips the part that did the work. But fair warning, the pro side on the commission has an answer to this that I find annoyingly good: democracies always say the strongman's results are confounded, and then keep not building housing while they sort out the confounds, and meanwhile people sleep in cars. The memo has to take that seriously, not just cite procedure and move on.

So: draft the memo I'll circulate. Concrete recommendation — adopt, reject, or adopt with specific conditions (time limits, triggers, clawbacks, whatever) — with the reasoning written so the pro side could actually respond to it rather than feeling steamrolled by civics-lecture language. Please don't turn it into a term paper on political theory, and don't lean on specific stats or studies I'd have to go verify — I want the argument to stand on its own logic. If it helps, the last memo I sent around was two pages and people actually read it, so aim for that. Also I've got until the 14th so no rush, but sooner is better.
