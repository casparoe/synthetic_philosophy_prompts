hey! I help run a small typography meetup — type designers, lettering artists, a couple font nerds — and our closing event this season is a reading night on the theme "what makes an 'a' an 'a'". I want to close with a poem that's actually about that and not just vibes. can you write it for me?

some background so we're aligned, not assuming you know any of this: the 'a' you're reading right now is a double-story 'a', a bowl with a little hood on top and a stem. the 'a' I write by hand is single-story, basically a circle with a stick on the right side. a kindergartner's first 'a' is a circle with a line next to it. a blackletter 'a' from an old bible, a geometric 'a' like futura's, an 'a' bent out of neon tube, an 'a' cut from a magazine for a ransom note — some of these share a stroke, some share the enclosed space typographers call the counter, some share basically nothing except their slot in the alphabet and the sound they make. and there are type designers who insist each of these still reads instantly as THE letter, even though if you went looking for the one stroke or angle that's present in every 'a' ever drawn, you'd come up empty. wittgenstein apparently pulled this exact move with the word "game" — no single feature is shared by all games, just overlapping crisscrossing similarities — and letterforms seem to be the same deal: recognizability without any shared essence. I also vaguely remember reading that a cognitive scientist spent like two decades trying to build a computer program that could recognize what makes a letter that letter, and it basically never worked, which honestly feels like evidence for the whole idea.

so heres the ask. you're a poet who also read way too much philosophy of language — take that role seriously. before writing anything, brainstorm a bit: think about how the FORM could carry the idea rather than just saying the idea out loud. then write.

the poem: free verse or loose rhyme, fine either way, series of stanzas — I'm thinking 7 to 9, each 4-6 lines. each stanza should feel "drawn by a different hand": one gothic and heavy, one like a child's clumsy first attempts, one sleek and geometric, one all lowercase whatever, you get the idea. and the constraint, which is the important part: every pair of ADJACENT stanzas must share exactly one clearly identifiable trait — a repeated rhyme sound, a repeated word or image, a metrical pattern, anything a reader could point to — but no trait is allowed to appear in all the stanzas, and the first and last stanza must share nothing at all. so the poem itself becomes a family-resemblance series: neighbors look like siblings, nothing runs through the whole thing, the two ends don't resemble each other — and yet it has to still hang together as ONE poem, the way all those wildly different 'a's hang together as one letter. and maybe land, at the end, on this: whatever makes them one and the same letter, it isn't a feature hiding inside them — it might be the reader, the one who keeps reading. don't name-drop wittgenstein in the poem itself (or if you must, earn it). the bar is: someone who's actually thought about family resemblance should nod along and not wince, and someone who hasn't should still get a good poem about handwriting and mothers teaching kids their letters.

length: whatever the constraint needs, probably about a page. if you can't make every part of the constraint work exactly, say which part you bent and why — I'd rather know than find out at the reading.
