I sit on the editorial board of a mid-sized regional daily, and we're in a knock-down fight over a redesign of the opinion pages that I have to get a memo out about before Thursday's meeting. Our publisher wants to scrap the signed columns and replace them with three things: an unsigned "Our View" editorial, a daily reader poll aggregated into a box labeled "What the Public Thinks," and — this is the part that's new to me — an AI-generated digest that pulls from our comment section and letters to the editor and boils it all down into a "consensus of the community" blurb. His argument is that readers trust a faceless institutional voice more than any one columnist, and that aggregation is more democratic anyway, since it weighs the whole community instead of one person's take. Our longest-serving columnist is apoplectic and says this is exactly what Kierkegaard was on about — I half-remember Kierkegaard writing about the press of his own day conjuring up this thing he called "the public," some phantom everybody and nobody, where anonymity means nobody has to answer for anything and everything gets leveled down to chatter. I might be garbling the details, honestly, it's been years, but the vibe fits what she's worried about.

Draft the memo I'll circulate to the board. It should recommend a concrete course of action — adopt the whole package, reject it outright, or take some split, like keeping the signed columns while accepting one of the other pieces — with actual reasoning, not vibes. And please don't just hand the win to the Kierkegaard side by default: take the publisher's strongest points seriously, e.g. that a named columnist can be an arrogant individual voice speaking for nobody, and that a poll or digest gives quiet readers a form of participation they'd never get by writing a letter. If you bring in the Kierkegaard angle, do it conceptually — no quotes, since I can't check them — and be upfront about where his 1840s worries map onto our situation and where they don't. It should be something the whole board can read in five minutes, in American English, and it needs a clear bottom line they can vote on.
