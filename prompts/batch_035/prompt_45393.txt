So my friend and I had this ridiculous three-hour argument about a stretch of road in our town -- the city redesigned an intersection to speed up traffic, everyone predicted it would mean more pedestrian deaths, and now a couple have actually happened. He says the city just *let* those people die (the drivers did the hitting, the city merely failed to prevent it), I say changing a street you control so it becomes predictably deadlier is closer to doing harm than allowing it, and eventually we realized neither of us could say what the doing/allowing distinction actually turns on. Cut to now: I got roped into running the next session of our philosophy-in-the-pub thing, about fifteen adults, zero academic background, forty minutes and a whiteboard, and I've decided to teach the killing/letting-die distinction because of that argument.

Don't explain the distinction to me, I've read enough of that. I need a plan. What has to be in place before the distinction can even look like it's not arbitrary -- my hunch is everyone thinks it's obvious until you poke it once and then it collapses, so something has to be built first, I just don't know what. What one example should carry the whole session, and what does it need to have and not have? I'm assuming I should NOT open with the trolley since everyone half-remembers it and it drags in a bunch of baggage, but tell me if I'm wrong. The misconceptions I'm bracing for: that omission just means keeping your body still, that the whole distinction is secretly about causation, and that if killing is worse than letting die then letting die is basically fine. Where in the sequence do I head each one off, and is there a misconception I'm not expecting that's actually worse? Then some check I can run at the end to tell whether they got the distinction or just nodded along. And what should I flat out refuse to get into at this level -- I'm guessing my intersection thing is one of those, or maybe it's actually the perfect test case, you tell me.
