I'm the vice chair of the medical executive committee at a mid-sized community hospital, and I've been asked to draft our written opinion in a peer review case that has split the committee badly. The case: an inpatient died when her potassium climbed to a lethal level over roughly fourteen hours. Three omissions are documented. The night-shift resident saw the flagged lab value but, covering three admissions, ordered no repeat test. The telemetry technician noticed an abnormal rhythm and escalated it to the charge nurse, who was told by a supervisor that rhythm changes were expected on this unit and let it drop. And the attending physician received two pages over the course of the evening and acknowledged neither. Any one of the three, acting as they should have, would almost certainly have caught the trend, and the patient would have lived. Under our bylaws, we can restrict or revoke clinical privileges only upon a finding that a physician caused or materially contributed to an adverse outcome — so the question of who caused what is not rhetorical; it determines what we are permitted to do.

The committee has fractured along what are really philosophical lines, though no one is using those words. One faction holds a strict counterfactual view: an omission caused the death if and only if, had the person acted, the patient would probably have survived — and on that test each of the three omissions qualifies, so all three caused it. That strikes the rest of the committee as causation inflation, since it seems to license finding dozens of further causes: the hospitalist who wasn't assigned, the pharmacist who didn't review the medication list. A second faction takes the opposite line: nobody's omission caused anything, because an omission is nothing at all, and the true cause was the patient's underlying condition plus bad luck — convenient for the physicians, but to my mind an evasion of the bylaws' plain intent. A third view, which I find most promising but can't articulate well, says we are really asking whose conduct deviated from what the situation demanded, so that the normative background decides which absences count as causes — which would explain why we all want to hold the attending more responsible than the technician, even though the counterfactual dependence runs equally through all three of them.

Please draft the written opinion I'll circulate before next month's meeting, structured as follows, in this order: "Summary and recommendation" (one short paragraph, bottom line first — what we should find and what sanctions or corrective actions follow); "Factual chronology" (about 150 words, neutral, no editorializing); "The causation question" (the longest section, roughly 500 words — engage the three positions seriously, say which one the committee adopts and why, and apply it to each of the three individuals by name or role); "The strongest opposing view" (about 150 words, the best honest case for whichever position the recommendation rejects); and "Findings and conditions" (about 150 words, concrete: what happens to whom, and what policy change we forward to administration). The memo should read like a real institutional document rather than a philosophy seminar — but it needs to get the philosophy right, because our bylaw turns on it.
