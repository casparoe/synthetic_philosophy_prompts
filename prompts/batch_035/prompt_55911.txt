I run a weekly reading group for fourteen second- and third-year philosophy undergraduates, ninety minutes per session, and we have just finished the first two essays of Nietzsche's On the Genealogy of Morality. They have absorbed the vocabulary but treat genealogy as a claim that moral views merely have historical causes. I want a game in which they feel the structural point instead: that once you fix who can act and who cannot, you can predict what vocabulary the powerless will coin, before anyone argues for anything. Please design that game.

Specifically: roles and rough numbers, an explicit rule or token system standing in for power (the strong should be able to take resources or set the agenda; the weak should have no move that changes any resource), and one permitted action for the weak — coining public evaluative words for what the other players do. Build in a round structure in which the strong can either ignore, adopt, or contest the vocabulary, and give the strong a standing incentive to adopt at least some of it, so that the likely end-state is the weak group's coinages governing everyone's reputation. I want the inversion to emerge from the rules rather than from anything I say: the terms "slave morality", "ressentiment", and "inversion" must not appear until the debrief. Then design the debrief: which moments of the game map onto which concepts, including the point about how the victorious vocabulary spread to the strong themselves. Be honest about what a ninety-minute game cannot reproduce — above all that Nietzsche is making a claim about our actual history, not exhibiting a possible dynamic — and tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle it. Please write your answer in American English.
