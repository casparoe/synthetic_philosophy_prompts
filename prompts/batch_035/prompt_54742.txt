I've been reading around the levels-of-selection fight and keeping a list of gut calls about what selection is "for" in particular cases. I'd like to know whether there's one principle behind all of them, or whether some of my verdicts just have to go.

The list. Sterile worker ants: their whole existence only makes sense for the colony, and I'd say its genuinely colony-level selection, not just a bookkeeping device. Genomic imprinting, where maternal and paternal copies fight over how fast the embryo grows: real gene-level selection, genes competing against their own teammates inside the body. Meiotic drive, where a gene cheats its way into more than half the gametes and the organism suffers: also real, maybe the clearest case of all. Alarm calls in ground squirrels: here my gut refuses the group story and says kinship and reciprocity explain it at the individual level. A hitchhiking gene sitting next to a favored gene in an asexual lineage: I say that's selection at the whole-genome level and the gene itself is just a passenger, nothing is "for" it. And bacterial biofilms where most cells secrete a shared enzyme and cheaters skim off it — that's the one I keep going back and forth on, dont ask me why.

My tentative principle is that selection at a level is real only when entities at that level compete with each other: genes compete inside a body, colonies compete with other colonies, but species don't really compete, so explanations like aging exists for the good of the species are pop biology and I'm right to scoff at them. But something still feels off — the worker-ant verdict and the scoffing-at-aging verdict look like the same move made in opposite directions. Test my principle against the cases, patch or replace it, tell me honestly which of my verdicts it can't save, and say what it predicts for cases not on my list — for instance whether cancer counts as cell-level selection, and whether an organism's body is itself just a group of genes that solved its free-rider problem.
