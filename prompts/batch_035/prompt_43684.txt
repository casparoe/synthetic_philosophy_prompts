I'm writing a novel where a sign-painter has a stroke and relearns the alphabet one letter at a time, and somewhere in the resarch I realized I have strong gut verdicts about when a mark counts as an "a" -- and now I want to know if they add up to anything. The single-story handwritten one (looks like ɑ) is definitely an a. A blackletter a is still an a once you've learned to read it. The Greek α in my old math notes reads as an a to me, no question. A logo where the "a" is just a circle with a little flag: still an a. But a rotated a is not, the @ is not an a even though it's supposed to have grown out of one, and a blobby child's a that I genuinely misread as "o" -- I'm not sure it was an a during the time I was misreading it. Meanwhile capital A and lowercase a are the same letter to me despite sharing almost no shape, æ is its own letter in Danish but just a typographic nicety when I use it in "archæology", and my grandmother's shorthand squiggle for a, which she read instantly and nobody else can -- I flip-flop on that one daily. Is there a single principle behind all this -- essential features, family resemblance, contrastive role in an orthographic system, historical descent, something else? Tell me what it is, or which two principles I'd need instead, and be honest about which of my verdicts it has to throw out to work. Then tell me what the best candidate says about the case I'm actually stuck on for the book: a made-up letterform in the invented alphabet that occupies the a-slot -- is it an a? Please keep it under 350 words.
