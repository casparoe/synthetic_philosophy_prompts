I've been making up little Bongard-style puzzles with my nephew (like the ones Hofstadter has a pile of in Gödel, Escher, Bach -- twelve boxes, six on the left and six on the right, find what every box on the left has in common that no box on the right has) and I've noticed I have really firm gut verdicts about which candidate answers "count" as the concept, even when several candidates fit all the boxes. I want to know if there's a principle behind my verdicts or if they're just vibes. Here's a dump of my gut reactions:

1. Left boxes each contain a triangle plus some other shapes; right boxes contain circles, squares, wiggly lines, but never a triangle. Candidate rules: "contains a triangle," "contains a shape with fewer than four sides," "contains a shape with no curved edges." My verdict: the concept is "contains a triangle," and the other two are just the same fact described worse. They don't feel like different hypotheses at all.

2. Every left box has exactly one shape, every right box has three or more. Also, by accident of how I drew it, every left shape is black and every right one is white. My verdict: the rule is "exactly one shape," and the color is noise. When my nephew confidently said the answer was "black things," I told him no. I couldn't really say why not -- it seperates the sides just as cleanly.

3. But then last week I made one where the left boxes are all black shapes and the right boxes all white, and the shapes themselves overlap between sides (triangles appear on both). There my verdict flips: now "black" clearly IS the concept and I'd be annoyed at anyone who answered "shapes, uh, of the left kind."

4. Rules mentioning where a box sits on the page ("the bottom-left box always passes") never count, even when they fit all twelve boxes. Gerrymandered disjunctions don't either: "an even number of shapes OR at least one triangle" is not a concept, while "an even number of shapes" on its own totally is.

5. The one I keep flip-flopping on: a puzzle where each left box contains exactly three shapes that also happen to be arranged in a triangle, and both "contains a triangle arrangement" and "contains exactly three shapes" fit the data. I genuinely don't know which one I'd tell my nephew the answer is, and I'm not sure it's even well-posed before you see more boxes.

So: is there one principle that gets all of this? The candidates I can half-articulate are something like shortest-separating-description (pick the rule you can write in fewest words that splits the boxes), and something like author-intent (pick the rule a cooperative puzzle-maker would have had in mind), and maybe something about which predicates humans find natural vs. which ones feel like trivia-machine gerrymanders. Do any of these, alone or patched together, capture my verdicts -- including why the color is noise in case 2 but the concept in case 3? What does the winning principle say about my nephew's newest puzzle, where the left boxes all have shapes whose total number of sides is a multiple of five: real concept or noise? And please be honest about which of my verdicts no clean principle can save -- I suspect case 1 is the shaky one.
