I'm a novelist drafting a scene in which my protagonist, a woman locally famous for decisiveness, must choose between two identical offers to buy her family orchard — same price, same terms, buyers equally sympathetic — and the scene turns on whether her eventual pick can be rational rather than merely lucky. Write the dialogue between the two philosophers she overhears arguing about this at the café counter where she sits deciding. One holds that a choice without any discriminative preference is not a choice at all but a reflex or a coin flip: agency requires a reason that distinguishes, so Buridan's ass starves not because hunger is weak but because choosing between perfect ties is conceptually confused, and the ass would starve even at a single bale if hay required deliberation. The other holds that the capacity to settle ties by fiat is precisely what elevates an agent above a mere deliberator: issuing 'this one' without a discriminative reason is an exercise of sovereignty, not a failure of rationality. Make them genuinely engage rather than talk past each other. The first should press on whether a reasonless fiat can be intentional rather than a tic, and on whether flipping a coin merely outsources one's agency to brass. The second should press the regress — if every choice needs a discriminative reason, what discriminates among the candidate tie-breakers? — and should deploy the point that my protagonist's options are not two identical worlds, since each world contains a different choice she has made, such that the arbitrary pick manufactures the preference that was missing before it. Have them use the orchard rather than the ass as their running example, keep both equally sharp (no strawman), write in natural spoken prose of roughly 700–900 words, and end with a final line beginning 'In sum:' — one sentence naming the sharpest point that still divides them when they leave the counter.
