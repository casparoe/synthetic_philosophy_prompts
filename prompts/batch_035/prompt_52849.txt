I teach an upper-level seminar on anthropic reasoning and the philosophy of probability, with twelve students, and I am writing the take-home final. Draft one essay question on the doomsday argument that cannot be answered by reciting the standard summary: that one's birth rank among all humans is, on the self-sampling assumption, evidence that humanity ends sooner, and that the self-indication assumption, which weights hypotheses by the number of observers they contain, is the standard reply. A student who has memorized only that dialectic should top out at a B.

The question should instead make students confront a connection the standard summaries never draw: the observer-count weighting that cancels the doomsday shift is the very same device that makes the presumptuous philosopher willing to bet, before any experiments, on whichever cosmological theory posits more observers. The question should require students to (i) reconstruct both effects with a numerical example of their own construction — a prior over two total population sizes, an observed birth rank, and the posterior under each assumption — showing where in the calculation the cancellation appears and where the arrogance appears; (ii) determine which of the two effects depends on which assumptions, and in particular whether cancelling the doomsday shift requires anything beyond the observer-count weighting; and (iii) defend a position on whether accepting the self-indication reply to the doomsday argument commits one to the presumptuous philosopher's confidence. If a student maintains that the two can be pried apart, the answer must identify where in the calculation the wedge is inserted, not merely report that intuition rebels.

The exam gives students 90 minutes and answers are capped at 800 words, so the question should signal that a compact numerical example beats an exhaustive derivation.

Please provide, in order:

1. The question as it would appear on the exam paper, including the length and time instructions.
2. A model answer of about 700 words. It should stand on its own reasoning, with no citations, reading lists, or appeals to named authorities — where it invokes a view, it should characterize the view rather than attribute it.
3. A marking scheme out of 20, with the marks for each must-hit point, that separates an A from a B by naming the specific moves each requires. Be explicit about what a B answer that recites the standard dialectic fluently will still lack, and add a one-line note per point on how students typically lose it.
