I am writing the historiographical chapter of a thesis on the American decision to use the atomic bombs against Japan in August 1945, and I have run into a problem that no further archival work seems able to solve: the debate about the bombings has been going for eighty years, the documents are largely open and largely read, and yet nobody has moved in decades. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not another verdict on the bombs.

The shape of the debate will be familiar. The traditionalist position holds that the bombs were used primarily to end the war quickly and avoid the enormous casualties anticipated from the planned invasions of Kyūshū (November 1945) and Honshū (early 1946). The revisionist position, descending from Gar Alperovitz's Atomic Diplomacy (1965), holds that ending the war was at best a secondary motive, the primary one being diplomatic leverage over the Soviet Union in the emerging Cold War, and that Japan was already looking for a way out. Between them sits a large post-revisionist middle, which concedes that Soviet considerations weighed on Truman and Byrnes, that the treatment of the emperor in the Potsdam Declaration was a serious blunder, and that Soviet entry into the war on 8–9 August matters enormously to any explanation of the surrender, while still maintaining that the bombs were used, first and foremost, to end the war. J. Samuel Walker observed in a 2005 historiographical review that "the controversy over the use of the bomb seems certain to continue", and identified the fundamental dividing issue as "whether the use of the bomb was necessary to achieve victory in the war in the Pacific on terms satisfactory to the United States."

What puzzles me is the following. Over the past several decades the participants have actually converged on a great many facts: what was said at the 18 June 1945 White House meeting, what the pre-bomb casualty estimates actually contained (the Joint War Plans Committee's paper for Truman projected roughly a quarter of a million casualties, nothing like the million of postwar memory), what the intercepts of Japanese communications showed about peace feelers, and so on. Yet the verdicts have not converged at all. Scholars who agree about every document still divide cleanly on whether the bombings were justified, and argue as though the next memorandum would settle it — when a generation of newly released memoranda has settled nothing.

So here is my question. What kind of disagreement is this, really? My own hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that the word "justified" is silently doing three distinct jobs in this debate: (a) an ex ante judgement of whether the decision was reasonable given what the decision-makers could know in July 1945, when casualty projections ranged from tens of thousands to a million and nothing in the evidence available to them could discriminate between those figures; (b) an all-things-considered moral verdict on an act that killed tens of thousands of civilians at a stroke — where the same camp that condemns Hiroshima tends to shrug at the firebombing of Tokyo, which killed as many in a single night months earlier; and (c) a proxy verdict on something much larger — the moral standing of the United States, the legitimacy of area bombing as such, or the acceptability of the Cold War that this weapon inaugurated. If the real disagreement lives at level (c), then no document about 1945 could possibly settle it, which would neatly explain the stall; but I am not confident of this, because the disputants themselves keep arguing as though it were a documentary question, and because the counterfactuals each side treats as its default — "Japan would have surrendered within weeks without the bombs" versus "the war would have ground on into 1946" — are precisely the sort of claims that cannot be checked against anything.

Diagnose the disagreement for me, using what each side actually claims rather than a caricature of it. Is it substantive or verbal, or two different questions being asked under one heading? Does it bottom out in facts, in standards of evidence, in divergent assignments of the burden of proof on an undecidable counterfactual, or in starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? Is there some genuinely open archival question that people have simply stopped looking for, or is the counterfactual undecidability the whole story? And then tell me honestly what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a conceptual distinction that separates the three verdicts, an explicit convention about who bears the burden when the counterfactual cannot be decided, a reframing of the question — or an argument that the debate is not really about 1945 at all and will move only when its participants decide, openly, to argue about the other thing. I intend to build a chapter on whatever diagnosis I accept, so be self-critical: tell me what would change your mind about your own diagnosis, and where in your reasoning you think you are most likely to have gone wrong.
