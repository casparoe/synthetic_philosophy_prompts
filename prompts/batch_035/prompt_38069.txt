I've been in a egalitarianism reading group for about two years now and we keep circling the same wall. On one side the luck egalitarians: what's bad, fundamentally, is unchosen inequality — if you end up worse off through brute luck, you have a claim to compensation, and if you end up worse off through your own choices, you don't. On the other side the relational equality people, following the big Anderson/Scheffler critique form the 90s: equality isn't about the pattern of holdings at all, it's about the relations between people — no domination, no one having to bow and scrape, everyone able to stand as an equal in civil society.

Here's the thing though. Both sides have long since absorbed each other's bullet points. The luck egalitarians concede the harshness objection (the imprudent gambler bleeding out in the street) and patch it — sufficiency floors underneath the responsibility-sensitive component, caveats that option luck only bites when the choice was genuinely informed and the stakes reasonable, and so on. The relational people concede that their view seems silent about unchosen inequality between people who already relate as equals — one person blind and in pain, the other fine, both standing at the same civil level — and they patch that too, or shrug. And yet nobody moves an inch. When I read the back-and-forth it reads like two ships passing: the luck people answering "who is entitled to what, and why" and the relational people answering "what kind of social world should this be," with the word 'equality' being the only point of contact.

My working hypothesis is that this is mostly a verbal disagreement kept alive by a shared habit of stating both views as answers to the single question "what is equality?" — and that if you forced the two questions apart ("what does justice require in the distribution of welfare-affecting luck" vs "which social relationships are intrinsically objectionable"), you'd find that most people on both sides actually accept both answers, with a real but much thinner residue of disagreement underneath about whether responsibility-sensitivity is a requirement of justice at all or just a policy heuristic.

Is that right? Diagnose the disagreement using what each side actually says — and I want you to engage the specifics, not the slogans: the brute luck / option luck split and whether it can be made stable, the expensive tastes problem, whether the harshness cases refute the view or only sloppy versions of it, whether relational equality is even complete enough to count as a rival theory rather than a different topic. Then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress here — a distinction, a reframing, a certain kind of case — or whether no progress should be expected and why. And be honest if you think the residue of genuine disagreement is bigger than I'm estimating, or smaller, or if you think the whole fight is an artifact of doing normative theory by slogan ("equality is X") instead of by question.
