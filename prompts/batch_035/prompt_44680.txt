As a science journalist, I've noticed that articles on the neuroscience of decision-making almost always lean on one of two analogies. The first, popularized by Jonathan Haidt, pictures the conscious self as a small rider on a very large elephant: the elephant — the automatic processes that actually drive behavior — goes pretty much where it goes, and the rider's main job is to produce a plausible narrative about the journey afterward. The second casts the conscious self as a press secretary, announcing and creatively explaining decisions that were made elsewhere in the building. Both analogies are routinely used to reach a bold conclusion: that free will is an illusion, that our sense of authorship is post-hoc confabulation, and that our practices of praise and blame need rethinking. I'd like an audit of the analogies before I decide how much weight to give that conclusion in my own reporting. Please set out what maps onto what in each case, then identify where the mappings break — for example, whether the rider can train the elephant over time in a way a real press secretary cannot change the administration, and whether either analogy has room for the agent's reflecting on reasons in advance rather than only rationalizing afterward. Then tell me which disanalogies actually matter for the illusionist conclusion and which are incidental. Finally, address the suspicion my more philosophical sources keep raising: that these analogies quietly measure the self against a ghost-in-the-machine standard of free will — authorship ex nihilo, uncaused by anything — which, they argue, is not a variety of free will anyone ever wanted in the first place. If the analogies do depend on that standard, say so plainly; if the illusionist conclusion survives even against a more defensible standard, explain how.
