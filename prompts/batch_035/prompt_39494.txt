I write a science-and-ideas column for a mid-size paper, and I'm doing a feature on whole brain emulation — the claim that you could swap someone's brain out for silicon, a bit at a time, and the same person walks out the other end. My editor has banned the teleporter (she read her Parfit in the 90s and is done with it) and she's also banned the obvious "surgeon replaces your neurons one by one while you chat about the weather" scene, unless I can put a genuine twist in it. Here's the twist I want, and I need you to actually build the thing, not gesture at it.

Construct a thought experiment where every premise of the gradual replacement argument is satisfied — the replacements go in bit by bit, the person stays awake and keeps reporting they feel completely normal, consciousness never seems to dim — and yet at the end there are two fully functional continuers, not one, so "the person survived" can't simply be true in the ordinary one-to-one way. The hard part: the branching has to fall out of the replacement procedure itself, something about how the hardware does load-balancing or redundancy, and not from some bolted-on extra copying step at the end. And no perfect-lap-of-nature stipulations — the silicon only has to match biological neurons at whatever level the argument actually needs, and you should tell me what level that is and why. One paragraph for the scenario itself, no jargon a smart lay reader can't unpack, and from the inside it should never feel like more than one subject: no moment where they can tell anything split.

Then do the check. Walk through what the gradual replacement theorist has to say, stage by stage — where exactly does the argument stop proving what it seemed to prove, and which premise breaks? Tell me which feature of the setup is doing all the work, what happens to the case if you remove that feature, and how someone who wants to keep both the gradual replacement argument and a clean one-person verdict would attack it. My editor will definitely ask whether this is just classic personal-identity fission in a costume, so answer that head on: what's genuinely new here, versus what's the oldest move in the literature wearing fresh hardware. And since it's for a column, finish with your own call — does this case make you more or less confident that gradual uploading preserves the person, and say why in a couple of sentences, not a shrug.
