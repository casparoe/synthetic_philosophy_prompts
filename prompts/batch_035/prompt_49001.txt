My colleague teaches introductory statistics to social science graduate students, and she showed me the paragraph from her course reader that introduces Simpson's paradox before they discuss the Berkeley admissions case. It reads as follows:

"Simpson's paradox is the reversal of an association when data are pooled: a treatment can do better than its alternative within every subgroup of patients yet lose overall, as happened in the well-known 1986 kidney stone study, where open surgery beat percutaneous nephrolithotomy for both small stones and large stones while the less invasive procedure came out ahead in the combined data. The phenomenon is really just the ecological fallacy under another name: drawing conclusions about individuals from numbers that were computed over aggregates. It only arises when the groups being pooled are of very unequal sizes; with groups of roughly comparable size the effect washes out. The moral for practice is straightforward: whenever the pooled result and the disaggregated results disagree, the pooled result is an artifact and the subgroups give the real answer. This is why the Berkeley graduate admissions data for fall 1973 settled the discrimination question — the aggregate figures showed apparent bias against women, but the department-by-department numbers showed the bias was an illusion created by where women chose to apply."

She clearly trusts the last two sentences most, since she tells her students that the first thing to do with any pooled statistic is break it down and believe the breakdown. I was more or less convinced until I happened to recall that somewhere in the causal inference literature the same reversal appears in examples where the conclusion goes the other way — cases where the disaggregated comparison is the one you shouldn't use for a decision. I don't remember the details, so I can't tell whether that memory undermines her moral or not.

Please audit the paragraph for me. Sort its claims into what a working statistician would accept as stated, what is a simplification they would want qualified and how, and what is outright mistaken — and then address her closing moral: does the advice to always trust the disaggregated numbers over the pooled ones follow from the accurate parts of the paragraph, or does it depend on something the paragraph never mentions? I'd especially like to know whether the Berkeley sentence, which she presents as the demonstration of her rule, actually supports it.
