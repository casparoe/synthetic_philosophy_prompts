**The Calibration Gap: Why Some Questions Have No Answer**

Start with a heap of sand and remove one grain. You still have a heap. Remove another; still a heap. It seems that no single removal can take you from heap to non-heap. But if that's right, then repeating the harmless step enough times proves that a single grain is a heap, or even that zero grains are — which is absurd. This is the sorites paradox, and it is ancient, going back to Greek philosophers who puzzled over heaps and bald heads.

Philosophers have two main reactions, as I understand them. The first, called epistemicism, says there really is a sharp cutoff: some exact number of grains is the last that counts as a heap. We just can't know where the line is, though in principle enough careful linguistic science might someday locate it. The second, called supervaluationism, says that once you press into the borderline region, statements like "this is a heap" are simply neither true nor false; a borderline sentence has no determinate truth value, though it will be true under some ways of making the word precise and false under others.

My own view is that both reactions treat the problem as deeper than it is. I call it the calibration gap. Think of a word like "heap" as a measuring instrument — something a community builds for a job. Nobody builds "heap" in order to count grains. They build it to communicate roughly: to distinguish a heap from a mess, from nothing, from a small pile. For that job, the instrument works fine. The sorites paradox is what happens when you demand that this coarse instrument resolve a question it was never built to resolve. Consider a kitchen scale: it is useless for weighing a single atom, but we don't conclude that the atom has an unknowable weight as measured by the kitchen scale. We conclude that the kitchen scale is the wrong instrument, asked the wrong question. Borderline cases, on my view, are not places where our knowledge runs out or where reality is mysteriously fuzzy; they are points where we have pushed a deliberately uncalibrated instrument past its resolution.

Here is a thought experiment I call the Legislators. Imagine a village whose elders, tired of arguments about sand, decree by law that "heap" shall mean a pile of at least one thousand grains. From that day, the word has a sharp boundary; a pile of 999 grains is, in that village, definitely not a heap. I claim the Legislators' "heap" is a perfectly legitimate heir of our "heap" — they did not change the subject; they made a decision their language had left unmade. If that's right, then the vagueness of our "heap" is a contingent fact about our conventions, not a metaphysical feature of the concept. And the "unknowable cutoff" that epistemicists posit looks, from the village, like nothing more than a decision that was never made. Why posit a hidden fact when the unmade decision explains everything?

One objection worries me. Mathematical predicates are perfectly precise — "prime number" has no borderline cases — yet nobody ever legislated that precision. So why couldn't natural-language terms be implicitly precise in the same way? My response is that mathematical terms got refined by proof, while ordinary terms get refined by use, and use never converges on a number. I'm not confident this response works, and I'd like help with it.

My conclusion is that the sorites paradox is less a paradox than a category-error detector: it fires precisely when we take an instrument calibrated for coarse use and force it to answer a fine-grained question.
