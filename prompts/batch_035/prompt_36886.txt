Some background, since it bears on what I need. I spent a career in physical chemistry, and since retiring I have joined a philosophy reading group — seven of us, all retired academics or professionals, none with formal training in philosophy. We have been working our way through metaphysics, and at the next meeting I am to lead the session on dispositions and the counterexamples to the conditional analysis: finks and masks. I believe I understand the material, but I do not yet know how to teach it, and that is what I am asking for — not the explanation itself, but a plan for delivering it in the seventy-five minutes we have together.

The plan should contain the following.

1. The order of ideas. Set out the sequence in which the components must be introduced, and why that order rather than another. In particular, I want to know what has to be in place before the group can hear about finks, and what has to be in place before masks appear as something other than a variety of fink — I take it that if the pieces come in the wrong order, the fink/mask distinction will collapse, and the session with it. If any component can safely be deferred to the end or dropped, say so.

2. The carrying example. One concrete case should bear the whole session. It must be familiar to a room of retired scientists, and — this seems to me the crux — it must admit both a fink version and a mask version of itself, so that the contrast is drawn between cases that are otherwise identical. Propose the example, and explain what it must have and what it must lack; if there is a natural candidate that fails one of these conditions, tell me why it fails.

3. The misconceptions. Predict what this particular audience will get wrong, and where in the sequence each error should be headed off. I already suspect two, both born of our training: that a disposition is nothing more than a prediction about what will happen, and that once the microstructural basis is named, the philosophical problem dissolves — we will reach for reduction to categorical bases as a reflex, and be satisfied too early. There will be others I have not anticipated, including the habit of treating every preventive interference as a fink. For each misconception, give the point in the sequence at which to defuse it and the move that does so.

4. A check for understanding. One question I can put to the room at the close that would reveal whether they have grasped the distinction itself rather than merely enjoyed the examples — ideally a novel case, not one used earlier, whose classification requires the distinction rather than memory.

5. What to leave out. I gather there are further debates: whether dispositions are real properties or reducible to their bases, and the various repairs to the conditional analysis. Tell me which of these should be openly declined at this level, and how to decline them without implying the group could not have managed them.

A few constraints on your reply. Write it in British English throughout. Do not give me a reading list or citations; I want the plan itself, as a document I can work from at the meeting. And please keep the weight on the sequencing and the misconception map — that is where my own preparation is thinnest.
