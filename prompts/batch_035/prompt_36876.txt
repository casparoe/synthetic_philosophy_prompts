Last week my phil of science reading group got completely derailed by the raven paradox, and two members, Dara and Miguel, spent forty minutes arguing about how to fix it. I'll try to reconstruct the exchange because I genuinely can't tell who won.

The setup everyone agreed on: "all ravens are black" is logically equivalent to "all non-black things are non-ravens." The Nicod criterion says a black raven confirms the hypothesis. And the equivalence condition says whatever confirms a sentence confirms anything logically equivalent to it. Put those together and a green apple -- a non-black non-raven -- confirms that all ravens are black. That's the paradox, and then it went like this:

(1) Dara said there's no real paradox, just a lesson about degrees. The green apple really does confirm the hypothesis, but by a vanishingly tiny amount, because there are so incredibly many non-black things and so few ravens. The problem only looks bad because we're using a crude "confirms / doesn't confirm" notion. Go probabilistic and it dissolves.

(2) Miguel said that's a dodge that proves too much. Take "all unicorns are white," i.e. "all non-white things are non-unicorns." On Dara's account, doing your laundry and confirming that your socks are non-white thereby confirms that all unicorns are white. A hypothesis about animals that don't even exist gets confirmed by your sock drawer. Any account with that consequence has gone wrong somewhere, he said, no matter how small the degree.

(3) Dara bit the bullet, sort of. She said: yes, actually, if you take seriously the possibility of unicorns (or of white ravens in some unexplored place), then checking a non-white thing and finding no unicorn genuinely does raise the probability that all unicorns are white, by a tiny amount. That's not a reductio, it's just how probabilities behave for universal claims -- they can be broken by any single exception, so every non-exception nudges them up. The laundry result feels absurd only because the evidence is microscopic, and microscopic evidence toward a silly conclusion is still microscopic.

(4) Miguel then made what I thought was his best move. He said the probabilistic story smuggles in a hidden assumption about where your observations come from. If you sample objects at random from the whole universe, sure, a non-black non-raven is weak evidence. But nobody samples the universe at random. If you deliberately go look among green things, or in your sock drawer, the observation tells you nothing about ravens at all, because you selected the observation with the outcome baked in. So "e confirms h" isn't a two-place relation between evidence and hypothesis the way Hempel wanted -- it's three-place, involving the sampling procedure. Which means Hempel's project of a purely formal, syntax-driven theory of confirmation is just dead, and Dara should stop presenting her fix as a rescue of confirmation theory when it's really the death certificate.

(5) Dara's reply: maybe that IS the resolution. The paradox is a proof that syntactic confirmation can't work, and the fix isn't to patch it but to notice that confirmation was never going to be capturable from logical form alone. She says that's a result, not a concession.

Can you adjudicate this move by move? Specifically: is Dara's degrees reply in (1) enough on its own, or does it only work combined with background assumptions she's not stating? Is Miguel's unicorn point in (2) a fair reductio or does it trade on treating a dead hypothesis as a live one? Did Dara's (3) actually answer it, or just cheerfully accept the weird consequence? And is Miguel right that selection effects make confirmation three-place -- if so, does that really kill the syntactic project, or only Hempel's particular version of it? I'd also like to hear what, if anything, both of them are missing. Neither would give an inch, and the rest of us just wanted to get back to the chapter.
