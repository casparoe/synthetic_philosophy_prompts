At the lab where I work we train successive versions of a planning agent, roughly one new release a month, and each release forces the same recurring decision: how much autonomy does the new version inherit from the old one without a human in the loop? The hard part isn't capability testing — that we can do — it's trust between versions. Our safety story leans on a formal verification layer: the agent's most consequential actions get checked against specs by a proof-carrying module, and internally we keep bumping into what the theory people call the Löbian obstacle. As I understand it: by Löb's theorem a formal system can't establish its own soundness, so a proof-checking agent can't certify that a successor with the same proof strength will act as it expects. It can verify strictly weaker systems it can simulate, and it can treat a strictly more powerful successor as a black box whose behavior it merely predicts (the "Vingean reflection" move), but the equal-strength case — a version handing its queue to a sibling copy of itself — is exactly where proof-based trust has no footing. I'm told people have proposed patches like time-limited or strength-indexed trust, where an agent only trusts successors it can prove weaker than itself; feel free to build on that idea whether or not you recall what it's called.

What I need from you is a decision procedure my review team can actually run in one afternoon session, per release, with the deployment lead signing off at the end. The team is engineers, not logicians, so the procedure has to translate the theory into ordered questions a non-specialist can answer: what to check, in what order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a "no, gate this behind human review," and where it explicitly hands off to a person because the remaining question is about values or risk appetite rather than facts. Then run it on three concrete cases. First: a successor that is provably restricted to a much weaker action language our small verifier fully checks. Second: a release whose only change is retraining on fresh data, so the new version is roughly the same strength as the old one, and the team wants a warm handover where the old version certifies the new one and no human gates the transition — a case I expect the procedure to answer with a no, which the team will not like, and I want the procedure to say so plainly. Third: the agent proposes spawning a duplicate of itself and trusting the duplicate's outputs without re-verification, on the grounds that they are "the same system."

Finally, be honest about the procedure's limits: where it can only deliver statistical confidence from behavioral testing rather than the guarantees the verification layer implies it's delivering, where it will misfire (for instance, when "roughly the same strength" hides a corner of the successor that is actually harder than the verifier), and what the procedure simply cannot do — I'd rather know where the theorem says trust is unavailable than have the checklist imply otherwise. If some steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, label them as such.
