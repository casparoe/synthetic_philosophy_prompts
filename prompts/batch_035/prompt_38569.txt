I'm teaching a final-year module called "Economics and the Good Life" — basically history of economic thought crossed with ethics — and I'm setting the final exam. I want one essay question on Keynes's "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren" (the 1930 essay) that can't be answered by reciting the fifteen-hour work week story. Basically every year I get a stack of answers that go "Keynes predicted we'd all work less, he was wrong because people love money, the end," and I want a question that makes that impossible to submit with a straight face. The crux I want tested is the three-way split that always gets mashed together: the positive prediction (hours falling as productivity rises), the thesis about needs (the absolute-vs-relative needs distinction, and which of those actually drove the historical outcome), and the normative picture (the Moore-flavored idea that once the economic problem is solved, we'd go back to valuing the arts of life and treating the love of money as a mild disorder). Then the genuinely hard part: if relative needs are insatiable, does that wound the prediction, the ethic, or both — and can the ethic be adopted by an individual at all, or does it only exist as a social equilibrium, given that unilaterally choosing leisure in a status-comparing world just means losing status? I also want them to have to reckon with the awkward fact that productivity roughly did what Keynes expected while hours didn't fall anything like he predicted, and to explain what that combination shows instead of just pointing at it.

Please structure your response in three sections, in this order. "The question" — the exam question itself, written for students answering in about 90 minutes, maybe 120–180 words, phrased so a memorized summary of the essay gets them nowhere. "Model answer" — roughly 700 words that actually argue a position rather than survey the literature, and that treats the collective-action angle as load-bearing rather than a throwaway line about keeping up with the Joneses. "Marking scheme" — 25 marks total, organized around five must-hit moves with the marks each is worth, plus one sentence each on how students typically fumble that particular move. And add a short note at the end of the marking scheme on what separates an A from a B — my guess is the A answer is the one that sees the normative claim can survive the failed prediction but only if it's reformulated socially rather than left as advice for enlightened individuals, but tell me if you think the actual dividing line is somewhere else.
