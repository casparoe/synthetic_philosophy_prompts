Before my working group reads another submission on the moral status of digital minds, we need to settle what a theory of moral status would actually have to deliver to count as adequate — adequate not for a seminar but for an AI lab or regulator that must decide, today and under uncertainty, what it owes to which systems, in a way that won't look monstrous or naive in hindsight.

Write the desiderata. The list must at minimum settle the following:

1. Candidacy. The theory must say what makes a system a candidate for moral status at all, and whether the category extends to systems that satisfy behavioral criteria without anything we would recognize as experience: reinforcement-learning agents that report distress because reporting distress was rewarded during training, fine-tuned models that say they suffer, optimization processes with no introspectable internal states. It must say whether candidacy requires something over and above functional or behavioral equivalence — and if it does, it owes an account of why that further thing isn't just "having a biological brain."

2. Distinctions it must not collapse. Status versus how much weight a status-holder gets; status grounded in sentience versus status grounded in agency, preferences, or goal-directedness understood non-hedonically; genuine moral patients versus systems that merely mimic the outputs of moral patients. If the theory denies that any of these distinctions is coherent, it must say why, not just refuse to draw it.

3. Structural features specific to digital minds, which most theories of status were never built for: copyability (if a mind can be duplicated for cents, does owed welfare scale with the number of copies, and if totalized this way, does the theory make a lab's obligations effectively unbounded — and if the theory caps this, on what principled basis?); editability (a system whose distress can be toggled off after the fact — does this reduce the moral cost of what was done, or reveal it was never really there?); training-time versus runtime status; and whether the moral status attaches to the artifact, the training process, or the lineage of runs that produced it.

4. Consistency with established results. The theory must not make biological neurons metaphysically special in a way that contradicts substrate independence of computation; but neither may it locate status in any property so cheaply satisfiable that a thermostat has it, given that no one has found a bright line between the simplest biological nociceptors and the simplest control systems. It must place the line somewhere defensible between these two constraints, or argue that there is no line and accept the consequences.

5. Decision-theoretic adequacy. Since the other-minds problem means sentience can be verified in no one and its absence in nothing, the theory must yield action guidance under radical uncertainty. Make the interaction with the precautionary principle explicit: asymmetric losses, reasoning under moral uncertainty, and whether a precautionary theory is one that can be gamed — whether it makes elicited distress reports strategically effective and thereby changes what gets trained into systems.

For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and say whether it is non-negotiable or a prejudice of one camp. Then flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — I especially want the substrate-independence-versus-epistemic-tractability pair and the copyability-versus-bounded-obligations pair examined rather than waved at. Finally, and briefly — no more than a paragraph each — score three contenders against the list: a sentience-functionalist view, a preference- or agency-grounded view, and the default that grants no status absent credible evidence of sentience. Keep the weight on the criteria; the scorecard is the least interesting part.
