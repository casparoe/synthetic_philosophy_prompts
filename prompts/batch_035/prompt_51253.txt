I'm a philosophy professor — decision theory and phil sci, mostly — and a former student of mine works as a program officer at a state public health agency. Her job, roughly a dozen times a year, is deciding how to split a preparedness budget between interventions where the risk is well quantified (flu vaccination campaigns, where we literally have decades of data) and ones where the probabilities are basically made up (some novel zoonosis, a vector-borne disease creeping north with the climate, bioterror stuff). She's smart enough to see that every number attached to the second kind is invented, but she can also tell her committee treats those numbers differently depending on whether they arrived with a decimal point. She asked me for help and my honest instinct is to send her Ellsberg's urn paper and let her figure it out, but that would be useless to her, and she knows it.

So what I want from you is a decision procedure her office could actually run in an afternoon, on paper, no software. Ground it in whatever decision theory actually knows about the difference between risk (probabilities you know) and ambiguity (probabilities you don't and can't), but translate — keep only the distinctions that earn their keep, and say which ones you dropped. It should include: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure is allowed to say "stop, this one's a values call, hand it to the committee," and — this is the part I care most about — some way of telling apart two things that look identical in a meeting: being rationally cautious because the estimate is fabricated, and just disliking an option because its number looks made-up. The first is defensible, the second is a bias her office will institutionalize if nobody names it.

Then run it on three cases: (1) shifting money from flu campaigns (well-quantified, boring, saves a predictable number of lives) into surveillance for a possible spillover event (no real probability exists, worst case is very bad); (2) the reverse — a vendor shows up with a slick risk model for the spillover side, complete with confident numbers, and the committee relaxes because now it "looks like" the flu side; (3) a case where you think the procedure should end by refusing to rank the options at all. For at least one of these, give the answer her office won't want to hear, and say so plainly.

Keep it usable by people with no theory background. If a step only works because someone exercises judgment, mark it as that — don't dress it up as procedure. And please don't give her a reading list; she's never going to read it and neither are her colleagues.
