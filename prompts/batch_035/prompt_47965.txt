I teach a grad seminar on philosophy of language, and this week we did the Quine gavagai material. Two of my students, Maya and Dev, stayed behind after class and argued for the better part of an hour, then asked me to referee, which I did a bad job of — partly becuase I honestly wasn't sure who was winning — so now I'm handing it to you. Here's the exchange as best as I can reconstruct it:

(1) Maya opened by saying the gavagai thought experiment shows translation is basically impossible: for all the evidence a field linguist could ever collect, "gavagai" might mean rabbit, or undetached rabbit part, or a momentary rabbit-stage, or rabbithood-on-the-loose. There's a fact about which one it really means — we just can never get at it. She framed it as skepticism about other languages, analogous to skepticism about other minds.

(2) Dev said that's the standard misreading: Quine's point isn't that the evidence underdetermines which translation manual is right, it's that there's no fact of the matter for the manuals to be right or wrong about. He stressed that indeterminacy of translation is supposed to be stronger than ordinary scientific underdetermination — with rival physics theories, one of them is really true even if we can't tell which; with rival manuals, neither one is, even in principle.

(3) Maya said that can't be right, because the native's utterances are plainly true or false. When the native points at a rabbit and says "gavagai," the sentence is true if it means lo, a rabbit and false if it means lo, an undetached rabbit part. One of them has to be correct, so there's a fact of the matter, and Dev's stronger claim is just confused.

(4) Dev answered that Maya is sliding between sentences and terms. Whole observation sentences are pinned down by their stimulus meanings — any acceptable manual will preserve the native's patterns of assent and dissent, so the sentence-level truth values come out the same either way. What varies is the reference of the terms, and that only shows up when you try to ask identity questions. If you ask the native whether this gavagai is the same as that one, you're asking through your English word "same," which already presupposes it's own analytical hypotheses about what a gavagai is. Truth is invariant across manuals, reference isn't.

(5) Maya said the thesis then proves too much: if radical translation is indeterminate, then so is translation within English, including her own idiolect. Nothing stops her word "rabbit" from meaning undetached rabbit part. So her own thoughts have no determinate content — and, the part she really pressed, Quine's thesis itself is stated in language, so it inherits the indeterminacy and isn't even a definite claim that could be evaluated.

(6) Dev more or less bit that bullet: he said that's just the ontological relativity line — reference isn't something a term does absolutely, only relative to a background theory or language, and giving up the absolute notion isn't skepticism, it's retiring a distinction that does no explanatory work. He said Quine is entitled to state his thesis from within his own theory, and that Maya's self-refutation charge confuses "no absolute fact" with "no fact at all." Maya's comeback was that Dev can talk himself into his own coffee-mug talk being referentially up for grabs if he likes, but she knows what she means, and any theory that denies it was bought too cheap.
