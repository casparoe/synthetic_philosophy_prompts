I teach a graduate seminar on truth and meaning this term, and I begin it, as one does, with the dispute between Dummettian anti-realism and its realist rivals: the thought that the meaning of a declarative statement is fixed by its assertibility conditions rather than by recognition-transcendent truth conditions, so that where no possible verification exists, we ought not to say there is a truth there waiting at all. I confess to a standing sympathy for the anti-realist side, and my students know it, which is why one of them spent an entire office hour pressing me on Fitch's knowability paradox: the derivation that takes the innocent-looking principle that every truth could, in principle, be known, adds little more than the factivity of knowledge and some unexciting modal logic, and lands on the conclusion that every truth is already known. Nobody believes that, and so the knowability principle apparently dies by its own hand.

Here is what I want, and it is not the standard textbook run-through. I want a map of the exposure of the anti-realist position: what, concretely, would count against it, and by how much? Take the escape routes I know of and treat each as a hypothesis under test — Dummett's own suggestion that the anti-realist only ever committed to the knowability of the statements her verificationist theory of meaning leaves intelligible; Tennant's restriction of the principle to so-called cartesian propositions, the ones consistent with their own knowability; Edgington's trick of keeping the principle true by restricting it to what could be known to be actually the case; and the revisionary-logic route, where the anti-realist simply declines the classical steps the derivation needs, which at least has the virtue of being independently motivated by her own views about excluded middle. For each, tell me what would have to be true for it to fail, and whether anything in the neighborhood could show that: Do the restrictions solve the paradox while preserving a recognizably verificationist position, or do they amputate exactly the cases that made anti-realism attractive? Does the anti-realist make any predictions at all that a realist could point to as risky — about how meaning should be fragmentary or indeterminate in domains with no verification procedures, about the failure of classical inference for undecidable statements, about the past, or about mathematical statements independent of our proof procedures?

And then the meta-question my student actually raised, which I could not answer well: is this position insulated against falsification by construction? Every apparent counterexample — the deep past, undecidable conjectures, the unknowable conjunction of a known truth with an unknowable one — is met by a local retreat to a restricted principle, and each restriction looks engineered precisely to dodge the last objection. Rank the defeaters from fatal through serious to merely awkward, tell me which of them an honest anti-realist would concede as counting against her rather than absorbing, and say whether the realist is any better off: can the realist ever point to a single determinate truth that is unknowable in principle, or is she as protected as her opponent? If both camps are insulated, I want to know whether that is a defect of this particular debate or just what metaphysical disputes are like; and if it is a defect, what would have to change in the anti-realist's program — Dummett's own suggestion that it ultimately vindicates a revision of classical logic seems to me the one place the position genuinely sticks its neck out — for the disagreement to become the kind that something could settle.
