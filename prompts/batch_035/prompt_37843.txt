A friend and I spent most of Sunday arguing about Strawson's appeal to the reactive attitudes, and I came away with a position I now have to defend in public. My view is that the reactive attitudes — resentment, gratitude, indignation, and the rest — can be justified even if hard determinism turns out to be true, but not, as Strawson implied, by placing them beyond the reach of rational justification altogether. My friend pressed me until I admitted the stronger claim I actually wanted to make: that a justification is available, but a forward-looking one. On my account the reactive attitudes are defensible insofar as they operate as a calibration system for shared expectations of conduct — resentment is less a verdict on a past deed than a signal that recalibrates what the community can expect of one another going forward — so their proper target is character formation rather than backward-looking desert.

In three weeks I present this at a workshop on criminal responsibility at an institute of criminology. The room will be roughly a dozen retributivist criminal law theorists who take desert to be basic and non-derivable; one legal psychologist who studies how juries actually assign blame; and two graduate students sympathetic to the view that neuroscience has already dissolved desert. My friend will be in the audience and argues like a prosecutor. His core objection, which I expect others to echo, is that the whole thing quietly collapses into consequentialism: if the attitudes are justified by what they do for future behaviour, then sentencing policy should simply be set by outcomes, and the reactive attitudes become at best a sentimental instrument for achieving them. I half suspect he is right in at least one place, and I would much rather know where before I stand up.
