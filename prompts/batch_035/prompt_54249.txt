I help run a philosophy reading group that has spent this term on free will, and we're closing with Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility. For the final session I'd like to open with a poem — specifically a pantoum — and I'm hoping the form can carry some of the philosophical weight rather than just decorating it.

In case it helps to have the target clearly in view: the Basic Argument runs, roughly, that to be truly responsible for what you do, you'd have to be responsible for the will or character from which your actions flow; but you can't be responsible for that character unless you somehow brought it into being yourself — and bringing it about would require having existed, with the relevant intentions, before you existed at all. Since nothing can be causa sui, ultimate moral responsibility is impossible. The pantoum seems almost made for this: each stanza takes two lines from the previous one and puts them to work in a new context, and the form conventionally ends on the poem's opening line. So the poem can enact the regress rather than merely describe it — the same demand for responsibility reappearing at each level of explanation, the grounding pushed back one step every time it's examined, until the loop closes and we find ourselves exactly where we started, with the ground we were looking for still missing.

A few constraints I'd ask you to honor. The poem should render the argument accurately enough that someone who knows Strawson would recognize the structure rather than a name-drop — in particular, please do not ground the conclusion in determinism, since the Basic Argument is supposed to hold regardless of whether character is fated, random, or anything in between; the impossibility comes from the self-creation requirement, not from causal necessity. And the repetitions shouldn't feel ornamental: each time a line returns, its meaning should have shifted a little, the way the argument's demand feels newly urgent each time it is applied one level further back. Someone who knows the Basic Argument should be able to say which lines carry which steps of it; someone who has never heard of it should still find the poem worth reading on its own.
