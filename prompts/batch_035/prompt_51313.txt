I teach a 90-minute session of an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar (14 students) on Dennett's quining of qualia and the coffee cases he and others pressed about someone who liked coffee for years and now doesn't. Design an in-class tasting experiment that makes the students run into the problem before it is named: when a taste judgement changes, nothing available to introspection can settle whether the stuff changed, the experience changed, or only the standard changed. The vehicle should be a two-phase task within the one session: a disguised "calibration" rating of several dilutions of the same bitter drink at the start of class, treated as warm-up data for some other purpose, then a re-rating near the end -- with a physiological manipulation (say, a sugar-rinse adaptation) given to only half the room and the samples provably drawn from the same flasks throughout. Students should end up confidently reporting "it tastes different" when only their tongues changed, and showing drift in their standards across the session that they cannot localize even when shown their own first-phase sheets. No real-money payoffs; team accuracy points or nothing at all. Students should not hear the words "qualia" or the authors' names until the debrief. The debrief must map each moment onto the three-way distinction -- the unchanged flask, the changed tongue, the moved standard, and the introspective report that cannot tell them apart -- and disclose the mild deception used at the start. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (adaptation effects too weak, supertasters and non-tasters, students who insist they detected everything), and be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: the years-long timescale of the original cases and the question of whether the earlier experiences themselves were of the same kind, which I will have to supply by hand in the debrief rather than stage.
