I'm a novelist trying to work out what I actually believe about whether the language you speak shapes how you think — the Sapir-Whorf thing, though I confess I've never been sure I'm using that name right — and I want to do it by my gut reactions to cases, not by reading another round of the debate. So here are my verdicts, and I'd like you to find the principle behind them, or tell me they don't add up.

1. My narrator speaks a language with no separate grammatical future tense — no "will," she just says things like "I go tomorrow." I've always written her, and believed, as someone who experiences the future as less of a separate country than my English-speaking characters do. Grammar, I feel, seeps into experience.

2. I've spent a whole afternoon trying to render Portuguese saudade into English and I'm convinced something real is lost — that a single word doing the work of a paragraph changes what the feeling itself is. Lexical gaps matter to me.

3. But I don't buy the famous bridge studies, or at least my gut doesn't: German speakers calling a bridge elegant because it's grammatically feminine and Spanish speakers calling it strong — fine, maybe there's a measurable nudge in a lab, but I don't think it reaches anything I'd call thinking. Grammatical gender feels like noise at the surface.

4. I write my first drafts in English, my second language, and I become a different writer — shorter sentences, harder, more declarative, less forgiving of my characters. When I draft in my first language I'm more meandering and sentimental. This feels like the strongest case of all, almost embarrassingly strong.

5. And yet I recoil from saying a monolingual speaker of any language is blocked from any thought. Give my narrator time and patience and she can think anything my English characters think. Nobody's trapped.

6. Speakers of languages that chop up the color spectrum differently (two basic words where English has one, like the light blue / dark blue case) are faster at telling those shades apart. My verdict: interesting, but that's perception, not thought, and it shouldn't count as evidence for the interesting claim.

So: is there one principle that gets all six, or close? The obvious tension I can see is between 2 and 5 — if a paragraph can paraphrase saudade, why isn't my future-tense narrator equally unblocked, and why did I treat the loss as real? And I suspect 4 is doing something sneaky, because it's about me and my craft, and I'm the least reliable witness I have. Tell me which verdicts have to go, and give me the principle stated precisely enough that it predicts verdicts on cases I haven't listed — then tell me what it says about one: would it predict that a language with obligatory evidential markers (you must say whether you saw something yourself or heard it secondhand) produces speakers with better episodic memories, worse ones, or neither?

And please don't hand me confident pronouncements. Tell me where your reasoning is most likely to go wrong, and what would actually change your verdict on the cases — e.g. what kind of consideration would make you bite the bullet on grammatical gender mattering after all, or on nobody being blocked in principle. If the principle you build requires overturning my verdicts, say so plainly rather than dressing it up as a refinement.
