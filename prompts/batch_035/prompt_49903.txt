I'm a TA preparing for a discussion section in an introductory philosophy of religion course, and I expect many of my students will arrive already holding a rough version of the free will defense picked up from popular media. The formulation I keep hearing is close to one from a philosophy podcast I listen to, which put it roughly as follows: "For a long time, critics of theism claimed that the existence of evil is logically inconsistent with the existence of an omnipotent, perfectly good God, so that theism entails a contradiction. Alvin Plantinga ended that debate. He showed that no matter which free creatures God creates, each of them would do at least one wrong thing in every possible world — he called this condition transworld depravity. Given that, there was no possible world God could have created in which free creatures always freely choose the good, so the existence of God and the existence of evil are logically compatible after all, and free will is in fact the reason God permits evil. The logical problem of evil is dead, and since the evidential version of the problem never amounted to more than the complaint that evil looks bad in hindsight, the problem of evil as a whole is essentially closed."

I'd like a fair-minded audit of this summary rather than a defense or prosecution of it. Please go claim by claim: which parts would a philosopher of religion working on this literature accept as stated, which would they wince at as a familiar simplification, and which are just wrong? I have my suspicions about particular spots — the sentence about "every possible world" strikes me as too strong, and the move from "logically compatible" to "free will is in fact the reason" feels like it changes the subject midstream — but the host sounded confident enough that I can't tell whether I'm objecting to Plantinga's actual position or only to the gloss. So I'd especially like you to distinguish what Plantinga's argument actually requires (and what it deliberately does not claim) from what the podcast attributes to it, and to tell me whether the closing claim about the problem being "essentially closed" follows from anything accurate in the summary, or whether it quietly shifts which version of the problem is on the table.

One more request: if you think my suspicions themselves rest on a confused premise — for instance, if I've misidentified which sentence carries the distortion, or if the podcast summary is actually closer to the literature than I'm giving it credit for — please say so plainly rather than politely accommodating me. I'd rather be corrected before I pass along a bad version of this to thirty students.
