I'm giving a talk at a business school finance seminar next month defending roughly the following: everyone treats the no-trade theorem (Milgrom-Stokey, building on Aumann's agreeing-to-disagree stuff) as a cute formality that collapses the moment you relax common priors, and I think that's backwards. My line is that the theorem is best read as a measurement device: it tells you that whenever you observe net speculative trade, something specific has to be failing -- common priors, or common knowledge of rationality, or agents being payoff-motivated in some way the theorem rules out -- and the interesting question is which failure you're looking at, because volume is data about the epistemic state of the market. I want to push a slightly provocative corollary too: heavy volume in an asset is weak evidence that someone is irrational or operating from different priors, so in principle a regulator or market maker could read volume the way a doctor reads a fever -- as a symptom, not a cause.

The audience is mostly empirical finance people: behavioral folks who've spent whole careers documenting overconfidence and disagreement with surveys and lab markets, a couple of market microstructure people who think of noise traders as a benign lubricant that supplies liquidity and keeps prices informative, and maybe one theorist who knows the formal literature cold. No philosophers, though apparently one guy dabbles in formal epistemology on the side.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get from this crowd, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way a finance professor would put it (not the way a philosopher would), with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. I especially want to be ready for the Grossman-Stiglitz point -- someone will say prices can't be fully informative when information is costly, so of course there's trade, the assumptions fail trivially and watching volume tells me nothing I didn't already know. Also include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, and be honest about whether my fever-thermometer analogy survives hedging demand and ordinary liquidity trading, which I suspect it barely does. There's alot riding on the corollary landing well, since the measurement framing on its own probably reads as old wine to this crowd.
