For a workshop on the metaphysics of mind, each participant has been asked to circulate a short statement of their standing views so the group can probe them. Mine is below, in seven theses. Before the meeting, I want an audit of it, and you are the auditor: please take the role of a professional philosopher of mind with high standards and no stake in sparing my feelings.

Here is the package. (1) The hard problem is real: a complete physical description of the world leaves out the phenomenal facts — I accept the knowledge argument, so Mary, given all the physical information, still learns something new when she first sees red. (2) I remain a naturalist about the extra facts: they belong to nature, not to something beyond it, and I hold that qualia are caused by physical processes in the brain, with the causation running strictly one way — (3) physics is causally closed, every physical event has a sufficient physical cause, and no phenomenal state is ever among them. (4) On mental content I lean causal: what a thought or report is about is fixed by what actually causes and guides it. (5) On introspection, I hold that sincere present-tense judgments about one's own current experience are incorrigible — if I judge that I am experiencing red, I cannot be wrong — and that in making such a judgment I know the experience itself, not merely one of its neural correlates. (6) Phenomenal consciousness is an adaptation: it is present in us because it was selected for, and it could only have been selected for if it made a difference to behavior. (7) Everyday psychological explanation is in order as it stands — we avoid injury because it hurts, and the felt badness of pain does genuine explanatory work there.

Please work carefully. Start with some private brainstorming before writing the audit: for each thesis, spell out what it commits me to beyond its surface wording, and only then compare them pairwise. Then deliver the following. First, sort the pairs: which are genuinely inconsistent, and which merely appear to be. Second, rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as an undifferentiated list. Third, for each genuine tension, say exactly what would have to give, what the cheapest repair is, and what that repair costs elsewhere in the set. Fourth, for each merely apparent tension, locate the source of the appearance and show how it dissolves. Finally, tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I honestly do not know where the problems are, so do not soften anything on the theory that I would prefer to keep a particular thesis.

At the very end, state your conclusion in a fixed machine-readable form: a final line of the form \answer{...} listing, in order of severity, the theses that must be revised or dropped (for instance \answer{(6) weaken; (2) drop}), or \answer{consistent} if you judge the set tenable as it stands.
