I've been going down a historical Jesus rabbit hole for a few months now (started with a podcast on gospel scholarship, ended with me actually reading the synoptics in paralell columns like a weirdo) and before I write all this up for my book club I want a sanity check on whether the views I've landed on actually hang together as a package. Here's roughly where I am: (1) I think the criteria of authenticity — embarrassment, dissimilarity, multiple attestation, coherence — are basically the only real tool we have for this, and the people who want to ditch them in favor of "social memory" approaches or just reading each gospel as a coherent whole seem to me to have quietly given up on doing history at all. (2) Of the criteria, I take dissimilarity to be the strongest and most reliable: if a saying or deed cuts against what the early church would have wanted to invent, or stands out from its Jewish context, I think it's very likely to go back to Jesus himself. (3) At the same time I'm actually quite confident about the big picture: Jesus was an eschatological prophet announcing the imminent arrival of God's kingdom, someone deeply at home in first-century Jewish apocalypticism — that's the one claim I'd bet money on. (4) I hold Markan priority and the two-source hypothesis, and I'm inclined to write off the special material unique to Matthew and to Luke as later theological development, so that stuff shouldn't really even count as single attestation. (5) I also think that after forty-plus years of oral transmission we can at best recover the gist of what Jesus was about, themes and reputation rather than exact words — no one was preserving sentences verbatim in that world. And (6) the two bedrock facts for me are the crucifixion and the baptism by John, both nailed down by embarrassment; and while I'm personally an atheist and don't buy the miracle stories, I think the historian's conclusion there has to come from evidence about how legendary material develops and how one should weigh miracle claims, not from some rule that says doing history just presupposes miracles can't happen.

My question isn't really whether this is all correct — it's whether it's internally consistent. Which of these commitments are in genuine tension with each other, which only look like they conflict, and if something has to give, what's the cheapest revision and what would it cost me elsewhere in the package? I'd especially like the tensions ranked by severity rather than a flat list of problems, because from the inside this feels like a pretty standard mainstream position, but I have a nagging suspicion I'm holding at least one pair of views whose usual motivations actually point in opposite directions — I just can't see which pair it is.
