I've been going back and forth on the panpsychist combination problem, and instead of asking the standard version of the question I want to lay out my gut verdicts on a bunch of small cases and see if there's a single principle underneath them. If there is, tell me what it is; if there isn't, tell me which of my verdicts has to go.

Here are the verdicts. Take two electrons that each carry some tiny flicker of experience, sitting a micron apart but not interacting. I don't think there's any further experience that has both flickers as parts — two point-views side by side don't make a wider view. Now put them in a wire so they're constantly exchanging signals, and I still can't really picture a combined experience showing up; it still feels like two flickers with traffic between them. Yet inside my own head, neurons in seperate cortical areas are also just exchanging signals, and there I have no trouble at all believing in a single experience that spans them — right now I seem to have one experience containing both the music and the itch on my ankle. Same kind of causal traffic, opposite verdicts. That pair is the one I can't reconcile.

Some more data points. A bee swarm: tons of interaction and coordination, but my gut says the swarm itself doesn't have an experience over and above the bees'. Though I notice I'm less sure there than about the two-electron case, which is odd, since the swarm interacts way more. Split-brain patients: mostly I want to say there are two subjects after the surgery, which suggests a subject is just whatever the integration spans and can be cut in half with a scalpel. But then I look at two people deep in conversation — causally coupled, coordinating, each modeling the other — and I say definately no combined subject appears there, which seems to contradict "integration spans a subject" unless brains have something conversations lack, and I can't say what.

So: is there a principle that fits all this? Candidates I can half-see are "combination only within one integrated process" or "only within a shared boundary," but each gets flattened by one of the cases above, or gets so vague it predicts nothing. Please state whatever principle you land on precisely, tell me which of my verdicts it can't save, and say what it predicts for two cases I haven't mentioned — say, a slime mold solving a maze, and two brains linked by a prosthetic bridge whose bandwidth gets turned up slowly from zero. One more ask: keep clear for me what's actually settled among people who know this stuff (like that nobody has an agreed solution, or what the split-brain behavioral data actually show) versus what's genuinely contested — don't sell me one side's theory as if it were the consensus.
