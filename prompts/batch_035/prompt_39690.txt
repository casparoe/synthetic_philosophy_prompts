I'm putting together case material for a seminar on resistance to unjust authority, and I want an episode analyzed through Aquinas's treatment of tyrannicide. The episode is the plot of 20 July 1944: Colonel Claus von Stauffenberg, a serving officer acting with a network of senior officers and civilians, planted a bomb at Hitler's East Prussian headquarters; Hitler survived the blast, and the conspirators then tried to seize control in Berlin by invoking the army's own contingency plan for internal unrest (Operation Valkyrie), issuing orders under the false pretext of an SS coup, with the aim of overthrowing the Nazi regime and negotiating an end to the war.

Here is Aquinas's framework, as I understand it: killing a person, even a criminal, is reserved to public authority, and a private individual who kills on his own presumption commits sin; the private killing of a tyrant is additionally condemned on prudential grounds, since the assassination usually produces an even worse successor and provokes greater tyranny; yet a community that originally had the right to appoint its ruler retains the right to depose a tyrant, and anyone with authority from a higher power may lawfully act against him. Now do the mapping with discipline rather than forcing the framework to speak. Is Stauffenberg a private person, or an officer invoking public authority — and does invoking a contingency plan under a false pretext count as exercising public authority or merely counterfeiting it? Is Hitler, in Aquinas's terms, a tyrant by usurpation or a legitimate ruler degenerated into tyranny, given that he came to power through legal channels and then dismantled the legal order that brought him there? Who, if anyone, plays the role of the "higher authority"? And what becomes of Aquinas's prudential warning about the worse successor in a case where the regime itself, not the assassin, is the engine of escalating destruction?

Then run the analysis back the other way: does the case confirm the framework, strain it, or expose a genuine silence — for instance, on whether a legal order so corrupted by a tyrant can still generate the public authority Aquinas's permission depends on? Throughout, mark how confident you are in each attribution to Aquinas (rather than to later scholastics or to the popularization of his view), and single out the one point in the analysis where you are least sure of yourself and say why.
