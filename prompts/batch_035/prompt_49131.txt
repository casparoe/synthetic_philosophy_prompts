I've been reading the rationalist blogosphere — LessWrong, the decision-theory and AI-safety adjacent forums — for well over a decade, so my native philosophical vocabulary is Bayesian and computational: priors, posterior updating, identifiability, utility functions, world-models, Solomonoff induction and Kolmogorov complexity, AIXI, mesa-optimization, embedded agency. Formal epistemology feels like home to me; mid-century analytic philosophy of language mostly doesn't. Help me with Davidson.

Take his project of radical interpretation: an interpreter confronted with a speaker whose language and psychology are entirely unknown, starting from the data of which utterances the speaker holds true under which observable circumstances, fitting a Tarski-style truth theory to that data, and constrained by the principle of charity so that the speaker comes out believing mostly truths about the world. Restate this project in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Some suggestions for how the translation might go, which you should feel free to correct or discard: radical interpretation as an inference problem — recovering an agent's world-model and utterance-generation policy from behavioral and environmental data; the principle of charity as either a prior over agent-models (agents are more likely to be mostly-right than mostly-wrong) or a flat identifiability constraint (no mostly-wrong agent-model is even distinguishable in the data); disagreement over utterance truth-values as the signal that distinguishes hypotheses; the Tarskian apparatus as a specification language for the hypothesis space. Then do the same, briefly, for the neighboring Davidsonian theses: the argument of "On the Very Idea of a Conceptual Scheme" that radically incommensurable schemes cannot be so much as described; anomalous monism about mental events; the claim that thought requires language and that interpretation is triangular, demanding a second interpreter and a shared world.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Which claims translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all in the Bayesian-computational vocabulary? For each item in the residue, take a stance on whether the failure reflects a limitation of my toolkit or a lack of substance in the original claim. For instance: Davidson's claim that most beliefs must be true *by the nature of interpretation* — does that survive as the observation that from behavioral data alone, mostly-correct world-models are the best explanations available, or does something normative get lost? Does the impossibility of a truly alien conceptual scheme come out as a triviality about the expressive reach of Turing-complete world-models, or does it say something the identifiability framing can't? And be honest about smuggling: if rendering charity as a prior quietly assumes what Davidson was arguing for, or if the Bayesian picture's assumption that agents have determinate utilities and world-models begs questions Davidson was trying to raise, flag it. I want to know whether radical interpretation is a formalizable inference problem wearing prose, or whether the residue marks a real gap between how my community thinks about agents and what Davidson thought interpretation was.
