I've been second violinist in a professional string quartet for about fourteen years, and my training is entirely practical: ensemble skills, rehearsal craft, and the unwritten pedagogy you absorb in conservatoire chamber seminars. A friend with a philosophy degree recently told me there's an entire literature on what it is for people to do things together — philosophers asking whether a group can literally intend something, whether shared activity reduces to individual intentions plus mutual knowledge (I intend my part, you intend yours, each of us knows this, and each knows the other knows), or whether instead there are irreducibly collective states — we-intentions that belong to no individual mind — or whether the fundamental thing is neither a mental state nor an agreement but a joint commitment the parties create between them, a kind of plural subject that can owe things, be entitled to reproach, and be blamed as a unit. I know this only second-hand and may be mangling it, so treat my paraphrase as a rough sketch rather than an authoritative one.

I'd like this debate translated into the vocabulary I actually have, which is the vocabulary of playing quartets: entrainment and the shared pulse; cueing off a breath or a bow-lift; the 'one voice' ideal we chase in blend and matched vibrato; the four-way negotiation of rehearsal, where nobody owns the interpretation and yet the quartet unmistakably comes to have one; the fact that we say 'the quartet rushed' and mean something no individual rushing adds up to; the experienced sense, in a good performance, that the rubato happens to you rather than being chosen by you or by anyone; and the ethics of the ensemble — what showing up prepared owes the others, and why leaving a quartet mid-season feels more like breaking something than like resigning from a job.

So: restate each of the main positions in that debate in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, saying what maps onto what. Then give me the residue report — which parts of each position translate cleanly into ensemble experience, which parts fit only if you distort something (and say what gets distorted), and which parts have no expression at all in what I know. Two things I especially want adjudicated in terms I can check against my own experience: whether the mutual-knowledge-plus-individual-intentions picture can capture the difference between four competent session players sight-reading a quartet and a group that has rehearsed a piece into something none of them individually decided; and whether the joint-commitment picture captures what makes quartet obligations feel different from contractual ones. Finally, tell me honestly whether the residue points to something the philosophers have noticed that working chamber musicians tend to miss, or to something the musicians' vocabulary handles easily that the philosophical framework has to strain to express. Please answer in British English.
