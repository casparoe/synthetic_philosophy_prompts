I'm a graduate student in an axiomatic bargaining theory reading group, working through Kalai and Smorodinsky's 1975 Econometrica paper in which they propose their solution as an alternative to Nash's, and I've been ambushed by an argument from the economist in our group that appears to prove far more than he intends. The setup, which I take to be standard: a two-person bargaining problem is a pair (S, d), where S is a closed convex set of feasible utility vectors and d is the disagreement point; a solution picks a point of S. Kalai and Smorodinsky characterize their solution by four axioms — Pareto optimality, symmetry, invariance under independent positive affine transformations of each player's utility scale, and individual monotonicity, which says that if one player's prospects improve across the board while the other's best attainable payoff is unchanged, the improved player's assigned payoff may not fall. Geometrically, their solution is where the segment from d to the utopia point m (the point collecting each player's best attainable payoff) crosses the Pareto frontier, so each player ends up with the same fraction t of her maximal gain above the disagreement point.

Now the ambush, with his steps numbered as he gave them:

(1) Affine invariance is the official statement that utility here is only cardinal: facts about a single player's own utilities and their ratios are all that carry information, and no comparison between player 1's utilities and player 2's means anything. (2) But the symmetry axiom presupposes exactly such a comparison: whether a problem "is" symmetric depends on the arbitrary units we happened to choose, since rescaling one player's utilities by any positive factor destroys the apparent symmetry while leaving the underlying situation — the preferences, the outcomes — untouched. Concretely: two players with d = (0,0) and the quarter-disc S = {(x, y) : x² + y² ≤ 1, x, y ≥ 0} look perfectly symmetric, and the solution lands at (1/√2, 1/√2); replace player 2's utility scale by v = 1000u and the very same situation is now represented by a lopsided ellipse on which the symmetry axiom is silent. (3) The Kalai–Smorodinsky construction makes the same illicit presupposition: to pronounce that player 1 has covered the same fraction of the distance from disagreement to utopia as player 2 is to measure how far each is along her own scale and judge the two distances equal — an interpersonal comparison of utility if ever there was one. (4) So either these cross-person comparisons are meaningful, in which case affine invariance was never a good gloss on what utility is and the axioms misdescribe their own subject matter, or they are meaningless, in which case symmetry and the proportional-gains construction are ill-posed. (5) Either way, the entire axiomatic approach — Nash's solution no less than Kalai and Smorodinsky's — presupposes an interpersonal welfare scale it officially forbids, and is normatively empty until we bite the comparability bullet.

I can't accept (5); it seems to prove too much — by parity of reasoning, it would be meaningless to say that two investors' portfolios returned the same percentage, or that two marathoners had each completed the same fraction of the race. And yet each step looked defensible to me when he delivered it, and I have a nagging feeling the slip is somewhere between (1) and (3) without my being able to name it.
