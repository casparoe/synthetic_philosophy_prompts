My roommate heard a podcast on decision theory and now won't stop explaining acausal trade to me. Here's his version: two agents that can never interact at all — no signals, not even light, maybe in different universes — can still trade, because each one can simulate the other, predict what it would do, and act accordingly. Since each sees that cooperating pays off if the other cooperates, both cooperate, and you get trade with zero communication. He says it's basically Hofstadter's superrationality generalized: agents with the same values realize they're effectively one agent and should move in lockstep, and that's how you beat the defect equilibrium in a prisoners dilemma without ever talking to the other side. He also said this is the real reason future superintelligences would coordinate instead of fighting, and that some people even think we should cooperate acausally with civilizations we'll never meet. But then he kind of undermined the whole thing: since neither agent can causally affect the other, and neither can verify the other even exists, the whole arrangement rests on pure trust, so critics call it faith rather than trade, which he took to mean the idea is basically empty. Something felt off to me — the "same values / one agent" bit especially, since that doesn't sound like trade at all. Is this a fair summary? Which parts would someone who actually works on this accept, which are conflations, and does the faith objection actually land once you get the mechanics right?
