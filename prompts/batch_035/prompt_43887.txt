I've been reading one of those pop-philosophy books people buy in airports, and its chapter on Kripke sums up Naming and Necessity like this:

"Kripke's insight was that names are basically just labels. A description like 'the inventor of bifocals' carries a whole bundle of information, but the name 'Franklin' carries none — it's a rigid designator, a term whose referent, once fixed, can never change, which is why a name can't turn out to be wrong about its bearer. The same holds for natural kind terms: 'water' rigidly picks out whatever stuff actually fills our rivers and oceans, and chemistry discovered that this stuff is H2O. So water is necessarily H2O — there is no possible world in which the clear drinkable liquid in the oceans turned out to be some other chemical. And since we had to do chemistry to learn that, some necessary truths are only discoverable empirically, which blurs the line between the analytic and the synthetic that philosophy had been policing since Kant. Kripke got all this from his possible-worlds machinery — he believed possible worlds are real, a kind of modal realism — so the upshot is that essences, which metaphysicians used to merely speculate about, are actually handed to us by science."

Something about the possible worlds part smells off to me, and the "can't turn out to be wrong" bit seems too strong, but the rest sounded plausible enough that I can't seperate the load-bearing errors from the harmless simplifications. Go through it claim by claim: what would a Kripke scholar nod along to, what's a simplification they'd let pass for a popular audience, and what's just a misreading? Then tell me whether the chapter's big finish — essences delivered by science, metaphysics continuous with chemistry — actually follows from whichever parts of the summary survive your audit, or whether it needs more than Kripke gives it.
