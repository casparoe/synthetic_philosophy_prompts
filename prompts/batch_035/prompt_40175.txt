I sit on the allocation committee of a national research council that distributes block funding to university departments. For decades the split was set by expert panel review, which lost legitimacy after recurring accusations of favoritism toward established fields and a few well-connected departments. Two years ago we piloted a formula in its place: 40% weighted by publications in top-quartile journals, 25% by external grant income secured, 20% by doctoral completion within five years, and 15% by final-year doctoral student satisfaction scores, each component computed on a three-year rolling average. The pilot data are now in, and they are uncomfortable. Journal output rose by roughly 30% while average citations per paper fell; grant applications exploded while success rates and the size of funded projects shrank; completions rose alongside a rise in supervisors signing off on visibly thinner theses; and satisfaction scores became suspiciously inflated after one department began coaching its students before the survey window.

The committee is split three ways. One faction wants to abandon the formula entirely and return to expert panels, accepting the old problems as the price of judgment. A second wants to refine the index -- citation weighting, co-authorship discounts, penalties for the specific behaviors the pilot surfaced. A third, led by our data director, proposes something stranger: keep a formula but rotate its components unpredictably from cycle to cycle, so that departments can never optimize against a fixed target.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before our June meeting. It should recommend one concrete course of action with an implementation timeline, and it should do more than tally the pilot evidence. In particular, it should distinguish the failures we observed that flow from the metric merely being an imperfect proxy for underlying quality from those that flow from departments deliberately gaming a target they know is fixed, since the case for each remedy differs; and it should evaluate the rotation proposal seriously rather than dismissing it as gimmickry -- if departments cannot predict what will be measured, can they coordinate on anything at all, and does the answer to that count for or against the scheme? Finally, the memo must engage honestly with the strongest point on the losing side of whatever you recommend: if you advise moving away from quantitative allocation, you owe the committee an account of the concern that human judgment simply relocates the problem from people optimizing a number to people optimizing relationships and reputations, which we have even less visibility into.
