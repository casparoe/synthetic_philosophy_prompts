For a few years now I've had a running argument with my brother-in-law about whether happiness can compensate for suffering. His position is that it can't, at least not for real suffering: no amount of bliss, however widely you spread it, ever adds up to the moral weight of one person's torment. At Thanksgiving I raised what seemed to me the obvious problem with this — that if happiness can never pay for suffering at all, then preventing a single pinprick should matter more than creating any amount of joy, which even he found unacceptable. He said, no, that's not the view: ordinary small pains are comparable to pleasures, and it's only suffering past some level of severity that stands apart from any amount of happiness. Fine. But when I asked which suffering is the first one past that level, he said there's no sharp line — that the badness of suffering grows faster and faster as it intensifies, until at some point it stops being commensurable with happiness at all. And I noticed that every time we pointed at a specific comparison — this much pain against that much pleasure — the action turned out to be somewhere else: either that pain was still on the comparable side, or it was so far past the break that the comparison was laughable, or the real work was being done in between, in the transition he never had to display.

I've since noticed the same shape elsewhere. A friend of mine believes the badness of agony doesn't just add up — that doubling agony more than doubles how bad it is — so enormous disasters belong to a "different universe" of concern than small ones. But if I ask whether a catastrophe of size N plus one additional victim is in a different universe from the catastrophe of size N, of course it isn't; the special region always begins beyond wherever my example happens to reach. So here is the pattern as I'd state it: a view announces a qualitative break — two things differing in kind and not merely in degree — but places all of its practical content inside the transition between the kinds, and whenever you confront it with an actual case, the case turns out to sit safely on one side or the other, and the view survives without ever having to exhibit the transition itself. It's not that the person is confused about where the line falls; the line's unfindability is what does the defending.

Is there an established concept for this? The nearest things I know are the sorites puzzle, which is about vagueness as such, and the slippery slope, which is about claiming that one small step forces all the rest — but my brother-in-law isn't doing either of those, since he cheerfully grants that there's a fact of the matter about every particular comparison while refusing to ever be caught on one. It also seems adjacent to a debater's trick where a claim retreats when attacked, except that here nothing retreats; the claim just has no particular commitments to retreat from. I don't want just a label. I want to know what exactly the phenomenon is and isn't, which of the neighboring ideas it should be distinguished from and how, and — if nothing established fits — a name and a careful definition of your own, with some attention to the boundary cases: does the pattern require the person to believe the transition is sharp but hidden, or vague but real, and does it still count when the vagueness is honest rather than strategic? And tell me what would force the pattern into the open: what specific comparison would someone with this view have to either affirm or deny before it counts as a genuine commitment rather than a shield?
