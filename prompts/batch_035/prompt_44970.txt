You are a philosopher of mind and perception with additional training in cognitive science, and I want you to approach this carefully: before committing to a diagnosis, brainstorm at least three candidate readings of what the disagreement is really about, then argue for the one you find best rather than surveying all of them equally.

Here is my situation. I help run a graduate reading group that has spent two semesters on Molyneux's problem — the question, posed by William Molyneux to Locke in the early 1690s and published in the second edition of Locke's Essay, of whether someone born blind, who has learned to distinguish a cube from a sphere by touch, could on first receiving sight distinguish the two by vision alone. The group has read the classical answers: Leibniz's cautious yes resting on the idea that the two senses deliver cognitively accessible geometric structure that could in principle be matched, and Berkeley's and Condillac's a priori no, resting on the claim that visual and tactile shape are initially just different contents. We also looked at the long run of supposed empirical evidence: Cheselden's celebrated 1728 report of the boy whose cataracts were removed, whose confusion after the operation was taken to settle the matter for the negative side, and the mid-twentieth-century compilation of other cataract cases, where the reports were messy enough to support either side. Finally, we studied the 2011 Project Prakash study by Held and colleagues, in which children in India whose congenital blindness was surgically treated performed at chance when asked to visually match an object they had just felt, and improved substantially within days of visual exposure.

And here is the thing: the group fell apart over interpretation. Some members said the Prakash result settles the question — no — while others insisted it barely touches it, since rapid improvement within days suggests the cross-modal mapping is either latent or cheaply acquired, and Molyneux's question, if it is about anything, is arguably about exactly that threshold between what must be learned and what merely needs triggering. Others went further and claimed the whole question is ill-posed. The disagreement has completely stalled, and I want to understand its structure rather than pick a winner.

My own hypothesis, which I'd like you to take seriously but also to challenge, is that the stall comes from a silent mutation in the question across its history: what began as a thought experiment about whether ideas of shape are modality-specific has become a psychophysical question about cross-modal transfer, and the two camps in my group are answering these different questions under one name. I also suspect that the verb in Molyneux's question — in modern paraphrase, whether the newly sighted person can tell, know, or recognize which shape is which — is doing hidden work, since it could mean performing above chance in a forced-choice task, confidently and explicitly identifying the object, or having the felt shape present to one as the same shape one now sees, and each reading yields a different verdict on the very same data.

So, three questions. First, is my mutation hypothesis right, or is this a single substantive question that just happens to be empirically hard to test cleanly, given the confounds (residual vision before treatment, degraded retinal input afterward, unfamiliarity with the testing situation itself, and the pressure the original thought experiment puts on the word immediately)? Second, is there a residual question about cross-modal concepts or amodal content that survives every psychophysical result, and if so, whose position, if anyone's, is that really the heir of — Leibniz's, Berkeley's, or something in the neighborhood of Gareth Evans's claim that the senses present information under a common spatial framework that a newly sighted subject could draw on? Third, given your diagnosis, say concretely what would count as progress: a sharper formulation, a better-designed experiment, a philosophical distinction, or an argument that the question should be retired, and what would be lost if it were.
