I've been working through a question about love and duty on my own, without any training in philosophy, and I keep arriving at a conclusion that strikes me as too strong to be right, so I want to lay it out and ask what to do with it.

Some years ago I came across a point a philosopher made, and though I can no longer remember where I read it or the man's name, it stuck with me. As I recall it, the case involves a man who can rescue his drowning wife or a stranger, and who justifies rescuing his wife by telling himself, in effect, that in a properly impartial view the fact that this particular woman is his wife rather than someone else's gives him no special reason to prefer her, but that he may nonetheless reasonably choose her, since everyone is permitted their personal attachments. The philosopher's remark was that if the man needed that thought, it was one thought too many — the reflection was somehow out of place, and its presence would be a defect in the act or in the man himself.

Accepting that, I can't see how to avoid the following: moral reflection as such damages our closest relationships. The more carefully a person thinks through what they owe to their loved ones, the worse a partner or parent they are; the ideal spouse is one who never pauses to ask whether partiality toward his own is defensible. And this doesn't apply only at the waterside. If deliberation of that kind is one thought too many in the moment, it's hard to see why it wouldn't be equally corrosive beforehand — when deciding how to divide one's time and money between family and strangers, whether to keep a difficult promise, and so on. Reflection seems to be either appropriate or inappropriate, and the point says it's inappropriate precisely where the people we love are concerned.

So my question is practical. Should I be training myself to switch off the deliberative part of my mind whenever family and close friends are concerned — treating any scrupulous weighing of their claims against others' as a character flaw to be rooted out? Is conscientiousness toward loved ones something a good person should aim to eliminate, and thoughtless devotion something to cultivate? That is where my reasoning has taken me, and while I can't find the flaw in it, I confess I hope someone can.
