I got into a huge argument with my friend Dana last week (she just started an econ PhD, I'm doing philosophy of science) about whether economists get to assume people are rational utility maximizers. Her entire defense was the physics analogy: physicists assume frictionless planes and perfectly rigid bodies all the time, nobody calls Galileo unscientific, so why is it embarrassing when economists assume rational agents with stable preferences? She kept leaning on Friedman's 1953 methodology essay — the one where he says the more significant a theory, the more unrealistic its assumptions tend to be, and gives those examples of the expert pool player who shoots as if he'd done all the trigonometry, and tree leaves positioned as if each one were deliberately maximizing sunlight. I'd read somewhere that critics of that package call it the F-twist, and I also half-remember a paper by a guy named Musgrave, from around 1981 I think, that splits so-called unrealistic assumptions into three kinds — negligibility assumptions, domain assumptions, and heuristic assumptions. My gut says that distinction is exactly where her analogy lives or dies, because "ignore friction" looks like a negligibility claim (friction is tiny for our purposes) while "everyone is a rational maximizer" seems to be doing something else entirely, but I couldn't articulate it in the moment and she basically walked away with the win.

So here's what I want from you: audit the analogy properly, like a referee. Start with the correspondence table — what exactly is the frictionless plane supposed to map onto (rational agency? perfect information? the representative agent?), what plays the role of friction (bounded rationality? transaction costs? habit?), and what's the analogue of the fact that physics can measure the correction terms independently and knows the idealization is only approximately true. Then lay out where the mapping breaks, and be honest about which breaks actually matter for her conclusion versus which are just pedantic. The one I keep circling is that in physics the whole point of the frictionless plane is that you know how to put the friction back in, and the de-idealized theory is the goal; in economics it's not obvious the "friction" is small, or that anyone is on their way to putting it back in. Does that kill the analogy, or does Friedman have a comeback (maybe the theory only needs to predict, not describe the mechanisms)? Also worth checking: whether the analogy even fits Friedman's own examples — the pool player and the leaves are "as if" claims, and it's not clear Galileo's inclined planes are anything like that.

Bottom line questions: once the disanalogies are on the table, does her conclusion — economists shouldn't be embarrassed, it's just methodologically identical to physics — survive, or was the analogy carrying the whole thing? If the analogy dies, give me the better one, because rational-agent assumptions clearly do some legitimate work in economics; I just don't believe the Galileo comparison explains what work it is. And if you can work in the negligibility/domain/heuristic thing, do it — I want to actually understand it well enough to use it on Dana next time, not just win the point but explain why the physics comparison feels right and isn't.
