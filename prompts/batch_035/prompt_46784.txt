A classmate in my phil of language course sent me her notes from a lecture she only half-attended, and her summary of the norms of assertion debate goes something like this: "The dominant view, pushed by Timothy Williamson, is the knowledge rule — assert only what you know. Since knowledge requires certainty and can never be mistaken, this makes every assertion a kind of personal guarantee, like a promise: if you assert something and it turns out false, you didn't just get unlucky, you broke the rule of assertion itself. This basically refuted the older view that the rule is to say what's true, and it also explains the Moore sentences: 'It's raining but I don't believe it' sounds perfectly fine, whereas 'It's raining but I don't know that it's raining' sounds like a contradiction, and the truth rule can't account for that difference. That's why basically everyone working on this now accepts the knowledge rule."

Something about this feels off to me — especially the certainty part and the "everyone accepts it now" part — but I honestly don't know the literature well enough to put my finger on where. Could you go through her summary piece by piece and tell me which parts a defender of the knowledge norm would happily accept, which parts are conflations or overstatements (e.g., does the knowledge norm really bring certainty along with it, or is she importing that from somewhere else?), and whether the parts that ARE accurate actually support her conclusion that the debate got settled? Separately, I'm not sure what to make of the way she slides between the "rule" being like a law of grammar and it being a norm speakers can blame each other for breaking — does that distinction do any work here, and if so which reading is the one people in the debate actually use? For what it's worth, I'm inclined to think there's something right in the summary even if it's sloppy, so tell me if I'm being too charitable.
