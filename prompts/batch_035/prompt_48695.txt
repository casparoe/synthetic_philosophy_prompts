Next month I lead the discussion at my lifelong-learning institute's philosophy of science group — mostly retired engineers, physicians, and research chemists like me, with a few curious spouses, and a strict seventy-five-minute slot. The topic is Hempel's raven paradox. I don't want you to explain the paradox to me; I've read enough to state it. I want a teaching plan. The group will arrive thinking this is a philosopher's parlor trick, and I want them to leave genuinely troubled by how hard it is to say what evidence is.

Tell me the order the pieces must come in, and what depends on what: I'm torn over whether to define confirmation first and get Nicod's criterion on the table, or whether the equivalence condition has to precede any of it, and I suspect if I derive the contradiction too early the room will just shrug and blame the philosophers. Advise me on the carrying example: Hempel's ravens are famous but invite ornithology jokes, whereas something from my own shelf — say, hypothesizing that every batch of a compound I ever handled melts above a fixed temperature, and asking what my batch records confirm — would feel real to this room; but I worry a lab example either makes the resolution seem obvious or smuggles in a sampling assumption I haven't earned. Tell me what the example must have and must lack, and which to use.

Then the misconception map. I can already predict several, and I'd like you to place each at the point in the sequence where it should be defused: that the whole thing is a verbal trick; that the paradox is dissolved by saying we'd never go looking at shoes, so the finding is that confirmation depends on sampling procedure and that's that; that the Bayesian point that a white shoe confirms only infinitesimally settles everything, which my engineer contingent will find satisfying and I suspect is too quick; and — from the one actual philosopher in the room — that the equivalence condition itself is what should be sacrificed. Say for each misconception exactly which move in the sequence heads it off, and where you'd deliberately let it surface first.

Finally, give me one question to end on that would reveal whether they've understood the issue rather than merely been amused by it — and tell me what to explicitly refuse to cover in seventy-five minutes, grue presumably among them, and why the refusal itself should be announced to the room.
