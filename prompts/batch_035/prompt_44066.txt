Longtime SSC/LW lurker here. I've been on a Popper kick lately, and I keep running into a corner of modern discourse where he'd definitely have opinions if he could see it — but he died in '94, so he missed all of it. I want your best reconstruction of what those opinions would be.

Background as I understand it (correct me if im mangling it): early Popper had this thing where he treated Darwinism as barely falsifiable — natural selection, taken as a super-general theory, doesn't stick its neck out the way relativity does; he described it as something like a metaphysical research program, valuable heuristically but not testable in his strict sense. Then late in life (late 70s) he published a partial walk-back saying selection is testable in some weaker sense after all. Whether that walk-back was sincere or damage control, and whether the original complaint was ever really the crude it's-a-tautology one that gets attributed to him — I genuinely don't know, and I'd like you to flag that.

Now the fun part. Assume you can sit the old man down in 2025 and show him three things he never saw:

1. The alignment-adjacent claim that natural selection is a textbook mesa-optimizer — a blind outer process that built agents (us) whose goals don't match its own, because it never had any. Does this vindicate his line that the environment never instructs, only selects? Or would he grumble that the mesa-opt people are smuggling teleology back in?

2. The gradient-descent-vs-evolutionary-search distinction people draw in ML: backprop copies information from a loss signal into the model (instructionist, almost Lamarckian, inductive), while population-based search throws variants at the wall and keeps what works (selectionist, Darwinian). Would Popper actually endorse that dichotomy, or would he point out that error-correcting local search is doing trial-and-error elimination in both cases? And which side would he put an LLM on?

3. Dennett's mid-90s stuff about Popperian creatures — organisms that can let their hypotheses die in their stead — and the whole universal Darwinism wave after that, including people describing human culture as Lamarckian because we transmit acquired improvements during our own lifetimes. He'd presumably be smug about getting credit in a book that came out after he died. But would he resist any of it? My vague sense is he'd hate the culture-is-Lamarckian talk, since his whole biology-meets-epistemology story was that even a bacterium is a trial-and-error theorist and instruction from outside is a myth — but I'm not confident, so flag that too.

So: for each of the three, give me your best reconstruction of his reaction, anchored to things he actually wrote wherever you can. And this part really matters to me: clearly separate (a) what's settled about his actual views and the history, from (b) stuff where people who actually know Popper genuinely disagree, and from (c) your own extrapolation. In particular, don't hand me the pop-history version where the tautology complaint and the retraction get flattened into one simple story, and don't present any single reading of the retraction as the consensus when it isn't. If a reconstruction is speculative, say so in so many words — I'd rather get a honest "plausible but underdetermined" than a confident guess dressed up as history.
