Suppose a small foundation can fund four researchers for three years on conceptual work in the philosophy of probability — no empirical statistics, no large-scale simulations. The target is the principle of indifference and its pathologies: Bertrand's chord paradox, the wine/water and cube-manufacturing examples where uniformity in one parameter contradicts uniformity in another, the Jaynes-style program of fixing answers by invariance under symmetry transformations, the question whether indifference is even coherent for continuous quantities, and the more recent thought that indifference-like reasoning has quietly migrated into machine learning, where default priors and symmetry assumptions baked into model architecture play the epistemic role indifference once played. There is also a deflationary view I keep encountering: that Bertrand's paradox is like an ambiguous figure — the different "solutions" are answers to different, equally legitimate questions about how the chord is generated, so nothing remains to be solved once the generating procedure is specified. The reply, of course, is that this just relocates the problem, since nature does not tell us which procedure describes an unattended random process.

Which open questions should the foundation prioritize, in what order, and why? I care less about the list than about the reasoning: for each candidate, whether it is upstream of the others, how tractable it is for philosophers rather than statisticians, and what developments — say, in how ML practice treats symmetry and default assumptions — would move it up or down the list over the next few years. Also tell me which candidate you would cut as overrated, where settling the question would not change much downstream, and whether you would bet any of the foundation's money on the deflationary view, since its truth would make a good part of the agenda moot.
