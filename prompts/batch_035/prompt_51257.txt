I'm a senior on my high school debate team, and in a practice round on whether people's preferences have to be consistent, my opponent ran the following: I had admitted to preferring pizza to sushi, sushi to burgers, and burgers back to pizza, and she argued that a street vendor who knew this about me could start me off holding the burger, trade me the sushi for the burger plus a cent, then the pizza for the sushi plus a cent, then the burger for the pizza plus a cent, and repeat until my wallet was empty — and that since no rational person can be drained this way, circular preferences are irrational and her consistent preferences win the round. Before the rematch I want to understand what the slogan she kept repeating — that circular preferences let someone turn you into a money pump — actually amounts to. Give me exactly five numbered readings, one or two sentences each, with no introduction or conclusion. For each, state the precise claim, say whether it is trivial, substantive, or outright false, flag whether the verdict on it is settled among people who study decision theory or genuinely contested — please do not present a contested reading as the consensus — and say whether that reading, if it held, would actually entitle my opponent to her conclusion that circular preferences make me irrational.
