Quick background so you know where I'm coming from: I spent 35 years doing single-unit electrophysiology on moth auditory systems, retired two years ago, and a neighbor dragged me into a philosophy of mind reading group, which has sort of wrecked me. Turns out recording neurons for a living does NOT settle the question of whether anybody is home. Now my old department has me on the animal care committee because I'm "the invertebrate guy," and we have a case next month that I'm supposed to draft the written opinion for before the meeting. Help.

The case: a postdoc wants to study decision-making trade-offs in hermit crabs. Crabs would receive mild electric shocks depending on which shells they occupy, and the point is to see whether they'll abandon a preferred shell to avoid further shocks, accept a suboptimal shell to avoid them, and whether they groom or protect the shocked spot afterwards. Since the regs basically stop at vertebrates, she technically needs no special welfare review for this at all. Some of us are uneasy anyway. My philosophy reading has actually been useful here: instead of asking flat out whether crabs are conscious, there's a markers-style approach you can take -- things like motivational trade-offs (giving up something valued to avoid a noxious stimulus), localized attention to an injury site, preference for anesthetized areas, avoidance that flexes with context rather than firing the same way every time. The wierd thing is her design is basically an implementation of that exact checklist, which is either reassuring (we'd finally learn something) or a bit gross (we'd be manufacturing the evidence on live animals for animals we decided not to protect on the basis of not having the evidence).

The committee is split three ways. One faction says arthropod ganglia are reflex plumbing -- fast nociception without any felt pain, no more welfare status than a thermostat -- so approve freely and don't insult the postdoc's time. Another faction says if we voluntarily extend welfare protections to crabs we will have no principled stopping point before insects, worms, maybe nematodes, and the whole field drowns in paperwork. And there's a reading-group paper that keeps nagging me which argues sentience arrived when animals became capable of open-ended associative learning -- felt valence being what lets you bind brand-new situations to brand-new actions -- and on that view crabs sit right on the borderline, which explains why everyone's so sure in both directions.

So: draft the decision memo I'll circulate. Approve, approve with conditions, or reject, with conditions that are actually enforceable (shock caps, pilot numbers first, retirement to an enrichment tank instead of culling, whatever you think follows). It has to engage the reflex-plumbing faction seriously rather than just repeating "but the markers," and it has to propose a line-drawing principle we can write into protocol review for future invertebrate work, because this will not be the last such protocol. My own instinct is that the slippery-slope faction proves too much -- by their logic we should never have protected vertebrates either -- but I don't want the memo to just be me smuggling that in, so if you think they're partly right, say so.

After the memo proper, append a JSON object with exactly these fields: "recommendation" (string, one of "approve", "approve_with_conditions", "reject"), "confidence" (number 0-1 for how confident the memo's bottom line survives committee pushback), "core_line_drawing_principle" (string, the invertebrate grouping rule in one sentence), "conditions" (array of strings, each one enforceable and specific), "strongest_counterargument" (string, the best version of the losing faction's view), "response_to_counterargument" (string, why the recommendation survives it), "slippery_slope_ruling" (string: which invertebrate groups get full protection, partial conditions, or none, and what the stated reason is), and "open_questions" (array of strings, things the committee honestly can't settle yet).
