and here's the student's answer, copied verbatim (sorry for typos, it was handwritten and i transcribed it):

"Scheffler's paradox starts from the observation that consequentialism, by definition, is agent-neutral: everyone has the same reasons, namely to bring about the best overall outcome. Deontological constraints break this. If I am forbidden to kill one innocent person to prevent five other killings (each performed by someone else), then by respecting the constraint I bring about a worse world — five dead instead of one — and this looks irrational in a strict sense, since the constraint is formally inconsistent with the requirement to minimize harm. This is the paradox: the constraint forbids exactly the action that would make things go best, so the constraint itself becomes inconsistent, and Williams, in his famous critique of consequentialism, was the first to press this point.

The natural way to ground such a constraint is the doctrine of double effect: what matters is whether I intend the death or merely foresee it. The paradox only bites when the harm is intended, so the constraint is best understood as a rule about intending.

Threshold deontology, as Moore explains it, says that absolute constraints hold only up to a threshold: never torture — unless the stakes rise above some enormous level, at which point consequences govern. So below the threshold the constraint binds absolutely, and above it we revert to maximizing the good. This means threshold deontology agrees with consequentialism whenever the stakes are high — that is, whenever it actually matters — while preserving deontological verdicts in ordinary cases. So the answer to the paradox is elegant: threshold deontology escapes it by becoming consequentialism exactly in the cases where the paradox would arise. The paradox is relocated to the threshold itself, but since the threshold is only crossed in extreme cases, this is a cost worth paying, and arguably the two views give identical verdicts in all the cases that matter.

One might object that the threshold is arbitrary, but Moore handles this by making the threshold vary with the seriousness of the act, and anyway all moral theories draw lines somewhere."
