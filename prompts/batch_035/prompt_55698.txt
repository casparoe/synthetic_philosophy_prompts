So I've been lurking on LessWrong and the Alignment Forum since like 2017, and the thing that's recently clicked for me is how much of the disagreement in alignment discourse is secretly just worst-case vs average-case, without either side noticing that's the actual crux. Like, the people who are basically fine with RLHF-ish training and the people who say we need interpretability with actual guarantees — a lot of the time they're not disagreeing about how hard alignment is, they're disagreeing about what kind of claim "this system is safe enough to deploy" is even supposed to be. A probabilistic claim about the worlds we'll actually encounter, or a universal claim over all inputs including the ones an adversary constructs?

I want to turn this into a decision-tree handout for my local rationality meetup's AI-safety reading group. The tree should start from "someone says system X is safe enough to deploy — what are they actually claiming?" and branch down to positions. Some branch points I've sketched (improve them if you can think of better ones):

- Is your threat model adversarial — do you assume the model itself (or whoever is wielding it) is actively searching for your weak spots — or do you think the deployment environment is mostly non-adversarial and you're optimizing for the average case?
- Do "robust to distribution shift" and "robust to adversarial inputs" come apart for your purposes, and if so which one do you actually need?
- Can the property you care about (honesty, corrigibility, whatever) even be specified well enough to verify in the worst case, or does precisely specifying it just hand the adversary a fixed target to Goodhart around?
- If full worst-case guarantees turn out to be intractable in practice, do you retreat to partial worst-case guarantees (bounded loss, quantilizer-style "behave like the top q% of actions would"), or do you fall back on average-case performance plus monitoring?

The leaves should include at least: the full worst-case guarantees position (interpretability-or-nothing), quantilizer/soft-robustness as its own leaf (not just a footnote to worst-case), average-case with post-deployment monitoring and incident response, the "evals as evidence" position (we had red teams poke at it and it didn't fall over, which is real evidence but proves nothing), the "we'll iterate and patch" position, and the compliance-shaped version where the guarantee mostly exists for auditors. For each leaf, one sentence on its most embarrassing failure mode — the thing the most annoying person at the meetup would say to someone standing at that leaf.

Rules: every node has to be phrased as a question someone could answer without knowing which leaf it leads to (so no "are you an ARC-style theorist?"). Flag any two leaves the tree can't genuinely seperate — my suspicion is quantilizers and distribution-shift-robustness end up looking identical from the outside, but check. And tell me honestly where you think the tree is rigged by my framing of the branch points, because I definately have priors here and I'd rather the handout not smuggle them in.
