Until last month I had never thought about generations. Then I was promoted into managing a team of eleven people whose birth years run from the late 1960s to the early 2000s, and I was handed a document about engaging Gen Z that I am now expected to fold into a mentoring policy. I can't tell whether the framework I am being asked to institutionalize tracks anything real, and my attempts to figure this out have landed me in a debate that appears to have been running for decades without either side moving an inch.

Here is the shape of the dispute as I have been able to reconstruct it. On one side are the users and producers of generational discourse — consultants, journalists, some sociologists — for whom a generation is a meaningful kind: people shaped by formative events during adolescence or young adulthood, such that those who came of age around a financial crisis or got smartphones at puberty differ stably from those who did not. On the other side are skeptics, mostly demographers and statistically minded social scientists, who make three points. First, the identification problem: any observed difference between cohorts confounds birth cohort with age and with historical period, and without strong assumptions the three cannot be disentangled. Second, the effect-size point: measured average differences between cohorts are usually small relative to the variation within them, so a claim about what Gen Z is like is mostly false of most of Gen Z. Third, the boundary point: the cutoff years are arbitrary, they drift depending on who is writing, and they do not even travel — the formative events that supposedly define an American cohort often have no counterpart elsewhere, so the kinds are not even the same kinds across countries.

What I find striking is that none of this appears to resolve anything. The skeptics' points get granted, and the generational discourse continues to expand — in HR, in marketing, in politics — largely undisturbed. Nobody converts.

So I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is this a substantive disagreement about a causal question that the evidence currently underdetermines? A hidden empirical question about effect sizes that better research designs — cross-national comparisons, anything that exploits the fact that supposed cohort boundaries and formative events do not line up the same way in different countries — could settle? A verbal dispute, with one camp using "generation" for a demographic measurement tool and the other for something like a cultural identity? Or a deeper difference about what makes a kind real: the skeptics demanding that a kind support prediction, the users treating generations as something like interactive kinds — categories that, once people believe in them, partly constitute the self-understanding, sorting, and behavior of the people they sort, so that they become real through use in a way age-period-cohort models cannot capture? And if the last answer is right, then my policy decision is not downstream of the metaphysics but partly constitutive of it: writing generations into a mentoring document makes them more real, and refusing to does its small part to dissolve them. What kind of disagreement is that, when one side's being correct depends partly on how the disagreement is institutionally settled?

Finally, given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress in this debate, and what someone in my position should actually do: whether there is a defensible middle course between writing something astrology-adjacent into an official document and telling senior leadership that the framework everyone has just paid a consultant for is unfounded.
