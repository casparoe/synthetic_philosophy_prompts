I help moderate a large online community, and for a while now one of the other moderators has been settling our disagreements with the remark that you can't tolerate intolerance, because tolerance of the intolerant ends up destroying tolerance itself. Last month she used it to justify permanently banning a member who kept posting in bad faith; this month she refused to apply it to a friend of hers whose posts several members called intolerant, on the grounds that the principle only covers people who would abolish tolerance altogether if they ever gained power. I can't work out whether she is applying one principle inconsistently, or whether the phrase itself bundles together several quite different claims that only come apart in awkward cases like these.

I have no academic background; I've simply been turning it over on my own. I'm told a philosopher wrote something about this decades ago, but I don't want the history — I want the analysis. Could you set out, as a plain list I can bring to our next moderation meeting, the distinct precise things the sentence could be claiming. Candidates I half see myself: a prediction that a community extending tolerance to the intolerant will in fact be taken over or wrecked by them; a charge of straightforward inconsistency in tolerating people who would end tolerance; a claim that tolerating the intolerant is permitted but not required; a stronger claim that it is forbidden; and a view of tolerance as a reciprocal arrangement whose protection you forfeit the moment you withdraw it from others. For each reading, say whether it is plausible, whether it is trivially true, and — most importantly — which of them would actually support a permanent ban rather than a warning, and whether any of them could justify treating the two cases differently. Please write the answer in British English.
