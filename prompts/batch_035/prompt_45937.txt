My friend and I spent most of an evening arguing about whether habitat destruction could ever be good for wild animals, and I came away knowing her verdicts on a string of cases but not the rule behind them. She said strip-mining old-growth temperate rainforest is plainly bad for the animals; that paving over a weedy field of mice, who mostly die of starvation, disease, or predation in their first months, is fine and possibly mildly good; that screwworm eradication was an unambiguous win, since the flies' larvae eat living mammals from the inside and killing the parasite spares the host; that reintroducing wolves to a deer-overpopulated range was good because the deer had been dying slowly of starvation; but she wavered on a DDT-style insecticide campaign, inclined to call it good because the insects' own short lives are probably not worth living, yet troubled by the birds that starve afterward. Next month we're taking up a clear-cut of a boreal forest whose animals are already food-stressed after a cone-crop failure: by her own accounting their lives this winter are slightly net negative and most would die of starvation anyway, but the logging itself would leave many animals wounded and dying over weeks rather than killing them outright. Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) your prediction of her verdict on the clear-cut, (2) one rule that fits her track record and what it predicts here, (3) a second rule that also fits her track record but predicts the opposite, (4) which rule you think she is more likely running and why, and (5) a further case that would best tell the two rules apart.
