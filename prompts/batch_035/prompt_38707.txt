So I'm a philosophy professor who, against my better judgment, runs a small podcast for our department — mostly grad students and the occasional brave undergrad listening in. Next week's guest is a philosopher who defends strong longtermism: not just "the future matters a lot" but the full claim that positively influencing the long-term future is the *key* moral priority of our time, so that reducing existential risk dominates (in expectation) pretty much any other thing you could do with your resources. He's good, and he's rehearsed. On fanaticism he retreats to "these are real, estimable probabilities, not Pascalian fictions"; on cluelessness he likes to point out that ordinary charity is equally clueless about its long-run effects; on population ethics he waves at "the verdicts are robust across a wide range of views."

Draft me eight to ten interview questions that get past the rehearsed answers. At minimum I want one each on the following, with a follow-up keyed to the reply he's most likely to give: (a) the fanaticism worry — that expected-value maximization with tiny probabilities of astronomically huge payoffs ends up endorsing things that look insane, where the follow-up should press on whether "but the probabilities are real" does any work once the payoffs get large enough that our probability estimates stop being meaningful; (b) cluelessness, pressed as coming from *inside* the expected-value framework — it's not a rival moral theory saying you're wrong, it's the observation that our best causal models can't even tell us the sign of our long-run effects, so where exactly does the dominance argument get its numbers; (c) the population-ethics hostage situation — whether strong longtermism just is total utilitarianism plus empirical speculation, and whether the "robustness" reply smuggles in a framing where only the far-future-affecting verdicts get counted as varying across views.

I also want a question about something I've been chewing on and can't quite settle. As I read the literature, there are two framings floating around and they're not equivalent: one is a *dominance* claim, descended from Bostrom's old line about giving priority to maximizing the probability that humanity ends up okay — the idea being that when the downside is permanent, tiny reductions in risk swamp competing considerations without ever doing expected-value arithmetic; the other is a straight expected-value claim about the size of the future. My suspicion is that longtermists slide between these depending on which objection is live — the dominance framing when someone attacks the probability estimates, the EV framing when someone attacks the prioritization — and I'd like a question (plus follow-up) that corners him on which one he actually believes and what breaks if he commits to it. And end with one question about what would count as evidence *against* strong longtermism, since he's fond of saying it's just decision theory plus astronomy and I want to know if there's any observation that could, in principle, unseat it.

Don't worry about name-dropping beyond the obvious stuff, and keep each question self-contained enough that a reader with no background can follow it — my listeners are smart but the guest has a habit of answering the version of a question he wishes he'd gotten, so phrasing has to do real work. Thanks a ton.
