My reading group has spent the last month on Du Bois and the whole art-as-propaganda debate from the Harlem Renaissance, and we've gotten to the point where we're trying to predict what the man would say about stuff that came long after him, kind of as a game. Since you seem to know some philosophy, I want your read. Here's the track record we're working from, as best I can reconstruct it: he treasured the sorrow songs and thought they were the one authentic American music; in Criteria of Negro Art (1926) he praised a small realist play with an all-Black cast precisely because it showed Black folks as ordinary human beings with everyday problems, while in the same piece he grumbled about younger writers whose poems and stories wallowed in the seamier side of Black life; a few years later he reviewed Claude McKay's Home to Harlem so harshly he said reading it made him feel like he needed to take a bath; he had zero patience for art for art's sake, which he took to be a hobbyhorse of people who already have money and security; and of course he capped all this off by declaring that all art is propaganda and always has been, the only real question being what it's propaganda for. Oh, and separately he insisted that Black Americans can see America in a way white Americans can't, that being outside the color line gives you a view nobody else has.

Next week's cases, which we argue are exactly the kind of thing you can't decide without knowing which of his commitments is doing the real work. Case one: a contemporary Black painter doing huge color-field abstract canvases — pure form and color, no racial subject matter whatsoever, not even obliquely, though by all accounts they're stunning. Case two: a gritty prestige series from a Black showrunner about poverty and gun violence in a Black neighborhood, unflinching and pretty brutal, which critics defend as exactly the truth-telling that comfortable white audiences normally get to avoid. Case three, just to mess with everyone: an inspirational feel-good film about Black genius and success that's gorgeous and moving but that our resident historian says is roughly 40 percent made up.

So: verdicts on all three, please. But the part I actually care about is the rules underneath. At least three seem to fit his record as described — (a) the effect-on-the-struggle reading, where art by Black artists is judged by whether it advances the race's standing; (b) a beauty-and-nobility standard, where the sin is depicting Black life as ugly or degraded, and Home to Harlem fails for that reason rather than a political one; and (c) the true-vision idea, where the point is that art should show white America what it can't see for itself. Maybe there's a fourth. I'd like to know which cases above these rules actually disagree about, which rule you'd bet on him running and why, and what further case (can be hypothetical) would most cleanly force the rules apart — my friend Devon is convinced the abstract painter settles everything, and I think he's wrong but can't quite say how. Also, small thing: does his blanket claim that all art is propaganda even leave room for him to dislike the color-field stuff, or is that just a fact he thinks is true of all art regardless of whether it passes his tests? The distinction seems to matter here but I keep losing my grip on it.
