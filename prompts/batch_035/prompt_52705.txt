I teach a graduate seminar on moral psychology, and one of my colleagues — an ethicist who has spent her career on the question of whether guilt or shame is the better moral emotion, and on whether a culture can deliberately redesign its emotional repertoire — has built up a track record of judgments on concrete practices that I would like help reasoning forward from. Her verdicts so far: she praised a university that abolished its practice of posting the names of students convicted of honor-code violations and replaced it with private conferences in which the student faces the affected classmate and negotiates restitution; she condemned a municipal program that posted photographs of convicted shoplifters on an official website; she praised a school, modeled on Japanese classroom practice, in which a child who stole from a classmate sits with a small circle of peers, hears how the theft affected each of them, formally apologizes, and is then explicitly welcomed back into the group; she condemned a parenting manual's advice that parents correct children in front of dinner guests by unfavorably comparing them to their siblings; and she praised traditional confession-and-absolution, in which penitents privately acknowledge specific wrongs, receive penance, and are granted forgiveness. In conversation she has also said that she doubts a culture can simply choose its dominant moral emotion, the way it might choose a tax code, though she grants that institutions can nudge the balance between guilt and shame at the margins.

Next month she has agreed to give a verdict on a proposed corporate policy: an employee found to have harassed a colleague would be required to stand before an all-hands meeting of several hundred employees, describe what they did, and apologize, and then, upon completing a restorative training program, would be formally welcomed back by the CEO in a ceremony marking their full reintegration into the company. I would like you to predict her verdict. Her record strikes me as consistent with at least two principles — that shame is morally acceptable when reintegrative but indefensible when stigmatizing, and that the decisive variable is instead the size, permanence, and composition of the audience rather than any subsequent reintegration — and perhaps with a third, that what matters is whether a practice targets deeds or instead targets character. Please say what each candidate principle predicts about the corporate policy, whether her skepticism about wholesale cultural choice should pull her toward any of them, give your best guess as to her verdict along with your reasons for preferring that reading, and describe the further case — a practice we could put to her — that would best distinguish the principles from one another. Please write continuous prose only, as in a letter or email, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
