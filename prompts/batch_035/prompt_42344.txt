My brother-in-law and I have been arguing for months about whether a theory can be "right but early," using Malthus as the test case. He says Malthus was just wrong: he predicted population pressing against food supply, the industrial revolution and modern agriculture blew through that, and saying he was "early" is just rescue-work, the same move every failed prophet makes. I keep pushing back that Malthus got the mechanism right -- any population that expands exponentially against a resource base that improves only arithmetically eventually hits the wall -- and the wall just kept moving. But I also have a pile of gut verdicts on other cases and I can't find the principle behind them, so I want your help. Here they are. Malthus: wrong in his own century, but I half-buy the "right but early" line, because the mechanism only sleeps while productivity keeps growing. The Limits to Growth people from the early 70s who ran simulations saying industrial growth would run into resource and pollution constraints somewhere in the 21st century: also wrong on their specifics, but I feel they were even more clearly right-but-early, especially now that climate change looks like their pollution constraint showing up. Paul Ehrlich writing at the end of the 60s that hundreds of millions would die in famines in the 70s and 80s: just wrong, full stop, and the more he doubled down afterwards the more wrong he got. Peak oil people in the 2000s who said extraction was about to top out: wrong, and not right-but-early either, even though conventional extraction in plenty of places did eventually peak. A friend of mine who has predicted a stock market crash every single year for fifteen years: when a crash finally came, obviously nobody said he'd been "early." And then the personal one, my uncle who said my cousin's startup would collapse within a year, and it actually took six years and two pivots before it folded -- with that one my gut is right back at "right but early," which is weird because it looks exactly like the crash-predictor friend. Oh and one more contrast: I think the climate models from the 80s that roughly hit were simply right, not right-but-early, so whatever the distinction is it isn't just "the event eventually happened."

Here's my own attempt at a principle, which I'd like you to stress-test and repair or replace: a prediction is right-but-early rather than just wrong if the theory named a real mechanism, and the reason the deadline passed was a countervailing force that keeps needing to renew itself, so the failure is a delay rather than a refutation. That saves Malthus, the Limits to Growth people, and my uncle. But it also saves Ehrlich, since you can tell the same story about the Green Revolution, and it might even save the crash-predictor friend if he insists bull markets are a "countervailing force needing to renew itself," which he absolutely would. It also does nothing to explain why the 80s climate models count as right rather than right-but-early, since you could say they were just lucky with their timing.

So: is there a principle, or a couple of competing ones, that captures the pattern in my verdicts? State it precisely, tell me which case motivates each clause, and be honest about which of my verdicts you'd have to overturn -- I'm fine biting the bullet on one of them if you convince me it's the odd one out, and the Ehrlich vs. Limits to Growth pair is the one I'm most suspicious of myself, since they honestly look structurally identical to me and I just like one better. Then tell me what the principle says about a case I haven't listed, e.g. someone in 1995 who said Japan's economy was heading for decades of stagnation but predicted it would start immediately (it did happen, eventually, sort of, and definately with the decades). Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble and no restating my question back at me.
