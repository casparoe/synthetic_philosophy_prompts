Animal welfare law almost everywhere draws a sharp line through the animal kingdom. In the United States, state anticruelty statutes and the federal research regulations protect vertebrates — a biologist needs institutional approval, justification, and anesthesia protocols to experiment on a fish, while nothing at all is required to do the same thing to a cricket or a honeybee. Recently, though, the line has been redrawn in ways that don't track any obvious biological boundary: the UK's 2022 animal sentience legislation recognized decapod crustaceans (crabs, lobsters) and cephalopod mollusks (octopuses, squid) as sentient beings, while leaving insects and other invertebrates outside, even though an octopus's nervous system is organized on a plan radically different from our own — distributed through the arms, diverging from the vertebrate lineage over half a billion years ago — and even though social insects display learning and communication at least as sophisticated as some protected vertebrates. Meanwhile, fish sit awkwardly inside the line: they are technically covered by research regulations in many jurisdictions, yet are farmed commercially in conditions that would be criminal if applied to mammals.

What view of mind or consciousness does this pattern of protection presuppose? That moral status tracks phylogenetic proximity to humans — but then the inclusion of octopuses makes no sense. That it tracks a particular neural substrate, like a centralized brain — but then one would expect insects' mushroom bodies or the diffuse architecture of the octopus to be treated alike, as both depart from the vertebrate plan in different directions. Or that it tracks behavioral and functional evidence of sentience — but then the exclusion of bees and the hollow protection of fish suggest the evidence is not really doing the work. Reconstruct the implicit theory of consciousness and moral consideration that this legal architecture seems to rest on, say which parts of the practice strictly presuppose that theory and which merely fit comfortably with several alternatives, and identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined. Finally, tell me what would have to change in the law if the presupposed view were replaced by, say, a functionalist account of consciousness combined with a precautionary principle under uncertainty — would the protected class expand smoothly along the lines already being traced, or would the entire vertebrate-first structure of animal welfare law have to be abandoned?
