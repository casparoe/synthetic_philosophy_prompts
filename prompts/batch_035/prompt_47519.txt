I'm on the committee for our PhD program's comprehensive exam in metaphysics and philosophy of language, and this year it's my job to draft the question on vagueness. Previous years' questions have basically been answerable by reciting the standard contrast — epistemicism says there's a fact of the matter about exactly where "heap" stops, supervaluationism says borderline statements lack a truth value, cite Williamson, done. I want something where the actual crux is higher-order vagueness, since that's where I think the debate really lives. Specifically: supervaluationism was in large part motivated by the promise of handling iterated determinacy ("determinately determinately F"), but once you spell out the penumbral constraints that make the view work, you seem to get sharp boundaries of its own — between the definitely-F things and the penumbra, and between the penumbra and the definitely-not-F things — so the question is whether the supervaluationist just relocated the epistemicist's problem one level up, and whether the epistemicist's unknowable cutoffs are honestly a worse cost than the penumbral sharpness the rival ends up with. I'd also like the question to force candidates to say whether the sorites paradox itself is load-bearing here or a red herring that tempts them into burning half their answer re-deriving it.

The deliverables: (1) the essay question itself, something a strong candidate could answer in about 90 minutes; (2) a model answer of roughly 600–700 words that actually takes a side — I don't care which side it picks, but it should commit to a definite verdict on which view is better placed with respect to second-order vagueness and defend it, not lay out both options and shrug; and (3) a marking scheme out of 20 points, with five must-hit points, the marks each is worth, and a one-line note on how candidates typically fumble each one. Two constraints from me: don't make any part of the question depend on reconstructing Williamson's margin-for-error epistemology (that's covered by a different question on our exam), and don't require any reading outside the standard canon — if the question needs some specific framing or setup, state it within the question text itself. And please write everything in American English throughout — "gray" not "grey," and so on. One small preference: if a candidate could plausibly get full marks while remaining neutral between the two views, that's a sign the question is too soft, so design it so that neutrality costs marks somewhere.
