I sit on a regional economic policy commission that has been asked to recommend whether our government should pilot a Nordic-inspired "flexicurity" package: easy hiring and firing for firms, generous and relatively unconditional income support for people who lose their jobs, and heavy public investment in retraining so that they move into new work quickly. The proposal has split the commission down the middle. One faction — including our two employer representatives and, I suspect, the chair — argues the package is self-undermining in a polity like ours, where trust in government and in fellow citizens is measurably lower than in Scandinavia: the Danes can combine lax job protections with generous benefits only because dense norms of reciprocity make people actually take the retraining seriously and return to work, whereas here the same design would either be abused or trigger a backlash that guts it at the next election. The other faction, which I incline toward, thinks the causation mostly runs the other way: it is precisely the universal, no-stigma design of such programs that manufactures trust over time, by removing the suspicion that benefits go to undeserving free-riders and that the system is rigged for insiders — so waiting for trust to appear before legislating is like waiting for the fire before striking the match. The circularity worry — that the institutions require a trust they are supposed to produce — haunts both sides, and the chair has asked each of us to submit a written opinion that confronts it rather than quietly assuming it away.

Draft the memo I will circulate before the commission's final session next month. It should contain a concrete and actionable recommendation — pilot the full package, sequence its elements over time, pilot a stripped-down version, or reject the idea — with the reasoning spelled out, a treatment of the circularity problem that commissioners on both sides could accept as fair, and an honest account of the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than a caricature of it. Since our recommendation will accompany implementing legislation, it should say what specifically would be piloted, at what scale, over what time frame, and which observed outcomes five or ten years out would tell the legislature that it got the causal story right or wrong (and whether those outcomes could even be disentangled from everything else happening in the economy). Assume the readers are intelligent nonspecialists: define the few terms of art when they first appear, avoid jargon beyond that, and keep any comparative gestures at the Nordic countries honestly hedged rather than invented — the conceptual question of whether trust travels with the policy is what the memo is for.
