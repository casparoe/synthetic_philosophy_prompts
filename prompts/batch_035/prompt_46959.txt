I'm a retired astrophysicist who's been reading philosophy of science stuff for a few years now, and I've gotten interested in anthropic reasoning. I read a piece on a physics blog recently that summarized the two big camps, and something about it doesn't sit right with me. Here's roughly what it said:

There are two main schools of anthropic reasoning. The self-sampling assumption (SSA) says you should reason as if you were a random sample from the observers in your reference class, and this leads directly to the Doomsday argument — you're probably not among the very first humans, so humanity probably doesn't have that much longer to go. The self-indication assumption (SIA) says instead that any theory positing more observers gives you more chances of being one of them, so your own existence is evidence proportional to how many observers a theory predicts. Bostrom's "presumptuous philosopher" thought experiment shows that SIA is the right way to go: if two cosmological theories fit the data equally well but one predicts vastly more observers, then SIA says your existence overwhelmingly confirms the bigger theory, and most philosophers have accepted that this is just the correct conclusion. Either way, the article concluded, anthropic reasoning pushes us to revise our cosmological theories in the direction of predicting more observers.

I'm suspicious of the last two bits in particular — the thought experiment sounds like it was meant to be a reductio of something, not a selling point, and I don't see how the SSA side, which supposedly supports doom soon, also tells us to favor theories with more observers. Can you go through the summary and tell me which parts a working philosopher of the anthropic stuff would accept, which are garbled, and whether the article's final conclusion follows from either assumption once they're stated correctly? I'd also take bookkeeping on how the Doomsday argument actually relates to each of the two, since the article made it sound like only one of them has that problem.
