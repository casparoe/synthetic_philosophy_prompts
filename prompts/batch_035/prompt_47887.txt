I write novels, and I'm deep into drafting a scene I can't seem to get right: a five-person jury for a small literary prize, where the chair — charming, not villainous — quietly controls the order in which the shortlist gets voted on, and her personal favorite wins even though a majority of the jury would have beaten it in a straight head-to-head against any other contender. Everyone in the room behaves reasonably, no one lies, and the "fair" procedure manufactures the outcome. I've read enough pop-math to know this is somewhere in the neighborhood of Arrow's impossibility theorem, but I don't want to write the scene secondhand off a Wikipedia summary — I want the actual texture of the moment a room full of people realizes the procedure, not their preferences, picked the winner. So I want to run it on real people first, then write it.

Here's the setup I can offer: my writing group, fourteen adults who meet fortnightly, and I can get one extended evening session (call it two hours) for a "special event." Every quarter we pick one member's piece to feature on our little group website, so there's a genuinely desired prize with no money involved, and I can put five real stories (or excerpts, if that's better) on the table as the options. Design me the game. What I want is for them to collide with the problem themselves before anyone hears the word "Condorcet" or "Arrow" — I'm imagining something like printed preference cards that hand each person a strict ranking over the five stories, a secret role card for one participant (the chair stand-in, with agenda-setting power they're told to use quietly), and at least two rounds using different aggregation rules so the winner flips even though nobody's preferences change between rounds. You'll need to actually construct the preference profiles so the cycle is there — tell me how many of the fourteen need which ranking, and make it robust to one or two people voting out of pettiness. For the debrief, map what happened onto the actual conditions — unrestricted domain, the Pareto condition, independence of irrelevant alternatives, non-dictatorship — and structure it so the group ends up arguing about which condition they'd sacrifice, because that argument is the scene I'm really trying to write. No lecture up front, and I'd rather they not hear the theorem's name until the end.

Two more things. First, be honest with me about what the game can't reproduce: I assume one evening and one engineered preference profile can't make anyone *feel* a theorem about all possible profiles, and I don't want to overclaim in the scene. Second, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — my lot include a retired math teacher who will smell a rat by round one, and at least two people who will vote strategically the moment they sense stakes, which maybe ruins the premise or maybe is itself interesting, you tell me. And when you write this up, don't ease in with any preamble or restate what I've asked for: lead your very first sentence with the single design decision that does the heavy lifting, then give me the rest of the reasoning after.
