I'm a junior taking AP United States History, and our unit on the early republic ended with a Socratic seminar on whether Thomas Jefferson can be both admired and held responsible for slavery — the man who wrote the Declaration's famous claim about human equality while enslaving hundreds of people over his lifetime, and who, in Notes on the State of Virginia, speculated about the natural inferiority of Black people in terms that went beyond what merely owning slaves required. Most of my classmates reached for the "man of his time" defence: that condemning Jefferson by our standards is anachronistic, since nearly everyone in his world took slavery and racial hierarchy for granted, and it would be unfair to punish him for losing the lottery of birth century. Our teacher pushed back by noting that this can't be the whole story, because some of Jefferson's own contemporaries plainly did know better — Quaker abolitionists like John Woolman and Anthony Benezet had been making the case against slavery for decades, and Olaudah Equiano, who had himself been enslaved, published his autobiography during Jefferson's lifetime — so the better moral view wasn't unavailable in 1785; it was just rare, socially costly, and easier to ignore if your own comfort depended on ignoring it.

Here is the argument I'd like you to construct: that the "man of his time" defence, as my classmates used it, fails, but that a narrower and more honest version survives — and that the surviving version gives its users far less than they want. The narrow version would concede the teacher's point that Woolman and Equiano refute the claim that the better view couldn't have been known, and would instead rest on something like moral or cultural luck: that Jefferson's inability to take the abolitionists seriously is itself a product of his upbringing, wealth, and interests, and that this diminishes his personal culpability without excusing the acts or the institution. The "less than they want" part matters to me: on this version, mitigation of blame is not restoration of admiration, and it doesn't follow that Jefferson stays on the pedestal or that we keep teaching him as an uncomplicated hero. I'm especially interested in whether this middle position is stable — whether it can resist collapsing into either "everyone held these views, so no one was responsible" or "he knew better, so he's simply a hypocrite" — and in the future-facing version of the problem: if our descendants condemn something we currently take for granted, which version of the defence would we actually be entitled to invoke, and would we be satisfied with the narrower one?

One request about how you answer: if you think the position I've asked you to defend is confused or untenable — for instance, if you think the distinction between the two versions of the defence doesn't hold up, or that moral luck can't mitigate anything, or that I've misdescribed what my classmates were claiming — please say so plainly and argue for what you actually think is true, rather than building the case I outlined. I'd rather learn that our seminar framing was broken than receive a polished defense of it.
