So here's a thing that's been eating at me. I'm in a community garden club, and we have this rule that to keep voting rights you have to show up in person for at least three workdays a year. Most stuff happens online now, half the members live 40 minutes away, and at the last meeting Rick proposed just scrapping the rule. Maya stopped him and said it's like that Chesterton thing about the fence -- you know, if you come across a fence in a field and you don't see the point of it, you shouldn't tear it down until you know why it was put up, because the guy who built it maybe knew something you don't. Rick said he actually asked around, and the two oldest members both said the rule started decades ago basically to guilt people into helping with the weeding, which isn't exactly a sacred purpose. Maya said that's not good enough -- the people who wrote the rule might have been solving a problem they never put into words, like maybe showing up in person is what builds the trust that makes the workdays function at all, so "the old-timers don't remember a reason" isn't the same as "there is no reason." Rick got a bit heated and said that makes the whole fence idea unfalsifiable: if anything goes wrong after you remove a rule, you can always claim that was the hidden purpose, and if nothing goes wrong you got lucky, so the principle can never lose an argument, which is a bad sign. He also said keeping a rule is itself an action with costs -- younger members have quit over this exact rule -- so it's not like the fence side is the "do nothing" side.

Maya came back with what she called the Burke point (I only know him as some old conservative guy, she's the one who reads): she said the idea isn't that you can never change anything, it's that existing arrangements carry accumulated knowledge that's invisible to you, so the burden of proof sits on whoever wants to remove them -- change should be like editing an old document, not deleting it. And she added what seemed like the smartest thing anyone said that night: the fence principle really bites when removing the thing is hard to reverse, and maybe rule removals are more reversible than fences in fields, so if Rick wants to suspend the rule for a year and watch what happens, that's not tearing down the fence, that's opening the gate. Rick's last word was that this is a big concession: once she admits reversibility is what matters, she's abandoned Chesterton's fence for some cost-benefit thing about experiments, and the fence metaphor is just doing emotional work for people who already liked the old rule. Maya said no, the fence is precisely what tells you when the experiment is safe to run, so it's not doing emotional work at all. They asked the room to settle it and nobody wanted to.
