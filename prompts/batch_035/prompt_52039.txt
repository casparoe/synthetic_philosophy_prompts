A longtime lurker of the rationalist blogosphere here. In our philosophy-of-science reading group I keep a log of one member's verdicts, because she seems to be running some implicit theory of lawhood that she refuses to name. Her track record so far: the laws of nature are contingent — an inverse-cube gravity world is genuinely possible; laws are literally true, not just useful summaries we project onto data; a true but accidental regularity (all the coins currently in my pocket happen to be copper) is not a law; the law of gravitation genuinely explains why the apple fell; and a world could have exactly our laws and a wildly different history. Next meeting takes up two new cases. First: could there be two worlds that are perfect duplicates in every particular fact — every event, every arrangement of local properties — yet differ in their fundamental laws? Second: a world whose entire history is a single particle coasting forever in a straight line — what are its laws, and is there even a fact of the matter? Predict her verdicts on both. Note that more than one rule fits everything she has said so far — best-system Humeanism, governing-law primitivism, necessitarian dispositionalism, or something else — so lay out the live candidates, what each predicts for these two cases, your best guess with your reasons, and the further case that would best tell the remaining candidates apart. Keep it under 450 words, state how confident you are overall, and single out the point you are least sure of.
