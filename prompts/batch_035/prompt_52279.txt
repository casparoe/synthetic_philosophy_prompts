I am a retired engineer whose career was spent in control theory and estimation — servo design, feedback loops, observers, Kalman filtering — and who, since retiring, has taken up philosophy with something of an amateur's enthusiasm. I have just finished Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments, and I kept having the impression that Smith was describing, in eighteenth-century moralist vocabulary, precisely the sort of system I spent forty years engineering. I want that impression tested rather than flattered.

Restate Smith's account of sympathy and the impartial spectator in the vocabulary of feedback control and state estimation, as faithfully as you can. By sympathy I mean Smith's technical notion — the spectator's imaginative reconstruction of the agent's situation and the resultant sentiment in the spectator, not mere pity or emotional contagion. By the impartial spectator I mean the internalized judge he builds up over the course of the book: the well-informed, disinterested observer whose approbation the mature agent comes to seek for its own sake, the man within the breast who does his work even when no actual spectators are present. Rendered in my terms, the suggestion would be something like: the agent's passions are the plant output, actual spectators provide a noisy measurement channel, the impartial spectator is an internal model or observer estimating what a well-informed neutral party would feel, and the agent regulates his conduct against that estimate as a setpoint. Please do the translation properly and explicitly, covering at least the following elements: Smith's claim that we sympathize more readily and completely with joy than with sorrow; his account of why the person primarily concerned must lower the pitch of his passions to a level the spectator can go along with; the distinction between the love of praise and the love of praiseworthiness; and the picture of conscience as the spectator operating with the external measurement channel removed.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. What translates cleanly? What survives only with distortion, and what is lost? Two worries in particular. First, in my vocabulary the reference signal is given from outside the loop — the designer sets the setpoint — whereas Smith's spectator is built out of the very sympathetic machinery it subsequently regulates: the reference signal is itself the output of earlier estimation from actual spectators. I have devices for that, such as self-tuning regulators and model reference adaptive control, but I suspect they are weaker analogues. Second, my vocabulary evaluates a controller against a fixed external criterion — tracking error, disturbance rejection — whereas Smith's deepest question seems to be whether the criterion is calibrated to anything beyond the applause of the room, whether the observer tracks truth or merely consensus. Is there any counterpart in control and estimation theory to a system asking whether its setpoint deserves to be its setpoint, or is that question inexpressible in my terms? Finally, tell me plainly whether you think the residue reflects a limitation of the engineering vocabulary, or rather shows that Smith's talk of spectators and sentiments was looser than it looked and gains sharpness from the translation. I would rather learn that my instinct was a category error than keep carrying it around unexamined.
