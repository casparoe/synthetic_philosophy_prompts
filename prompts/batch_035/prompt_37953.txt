I teach political philosophy at a state university, and for the past two years I've served on my city's Commemorative Works Review Board -- the body that decides requests to rename streets, schools, and public buildings. We now have a case that has split the board down the middle, and I've been tasked with drafting the written recommendation that will anchor our deliberation, so I want your help composing it. The case: Calvin W. Ostergaard Elementary School, named in 1958 for a city councilman and banker who, according to the neighborhood's oral history, nearly single-handedly funded the settlement house that saw the surrounding district through the Depression, and who is remembered -- by descendants of the families he helped, many of whom arrived during the Great Migration -- as an almost saintly benefactor. Last year a graduate student digitizing our municipal archives discovered that the same Ostergaard wrote and lobbied for the racial covenants that barred those very families from the city's other neighborhoods, and that his donations to the settlement house were, in his own correspondence, framed as a way of keeping the district's Black population "contented in its proper quarter." So the community's memory of gratitude and the archival record point in opposite directions, and both are, in a sense, about the same people.

The procedural context complicates this further. Our ordinance permits renaming only where there is substantial support from the affected community, and the affected community here is overwhelmingly opposed to losing the name -- not because they doubt the archives, most of them don't know about them yet, but because the name is woven into three generations of institutional identity, reunion picnics, and family stories. The school board has also told us that whatever we decide, there is budget for exactly one interpretive plaque and no more. One of my fellow commissioners argues that honoring a community's actual living memory is itself a civic good, and that a record held by twelve archivists shouldn't overrule the settled meaning a neighborhood has made of its own landmark; another argues that retaining the name with a plaque makes the city complicit in laundering a segregationist's reputation through the affection of the very people he worked to exclude, and that this is precisely the situation in which memory should be forced to give way to history. I want to take both positions seriously rather than caricature either, and I confess I find the first one more troubling than my colleagues expect.

Please draft the decision memo I'll circulate before our March meeting. It should reach a concrete bottom line -- rename, retain with interpretive counter-text, retain outright, or some hybrid the board could actually implement under our ordinance -- and defend it with reasoning the losing side could recognize as serious, not a strawman. I'd especially like you to address the question underneath the case: when a community's treasured memory and the documentary record conflict, is there any principled way to decide which should yield, or is the only honest answer a case-by-case judgment about what each is doing for the people involved? Where you can, connect the reasoning to the general philosophical terrain -- the ethics of commemoration, collective memory, whatever framework genuinely illuminates this -- but the memo must remain something a school board member could read and act on, not a seminar paper. Keep it under about 1,200 words and addressed to the board as a whole.
