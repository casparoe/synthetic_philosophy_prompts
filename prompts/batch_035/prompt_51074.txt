I am a retired theoretical physicist — most of my career was spent on gauge field theory, and I confess that I still think of almost everything through that lens. In retirement I have been working through the philosophy of physics, and I have just reached Earman and Norton's hole argument from 1987, in which manifold substantivalism — the doctrine that the points of the bare differentiable manifold are genuine individuals over and above the metric and matter fields defined on them — combined with general covariance is said to saddle us with an outrageous indeterminism: a diffeomorphism that acts as the identity outside some small region, and permutes the points within it, supposedly generates a distinct possible world in which point p carries the metric that point d(p) carries in ours, and no boundary condition on any Cauchy surface can decide between them.

My instinct, as an old gauge theorist, is that this is all just gauge 101, dressed up in unfamiliar clothes. In electromagnetism we learned long ago not to regard the vector potential A and the potential A plus the gradient of some scalar function as representing different physical situations; calling the manifold points 'individuals' strikes me as exactly the sort of surplus structure that gauge considerations are meant to strip away, and Leibniz equivalence — the claim that diffeomorphically related models represent one and the same world — looks like nothing more than the analogue of quotienting by gauge transformations. And yet I am nagged by two worries I cannot resolve on my own. First, the analogy is imperfect: in electromagnetism the gauge group acts on the fields over a fixed background manifold, whereas in general relativity the diffeomorphism drags the very points themselves, the very things the substantivalist takes ontologically seriously, so it is not obvious that the standard gauge recipe applies. Second, we gauge theorists know that not all gauge-related descriptions are idle — the Aharonov–Bohm effect and the theta-vacuum structure of Yang–Mills theory show that transformations not connected to the identity, the so-called large gauge transformations, can carry genuine physical content, which suggests that treating diffeomorphisms as pure gauge is a substantive commitment rather than a free lunch.

So here is what I would like you to do: please argue against me. Make the case that my deflationary, everything-is-just-gauge reading fails to dissolve the hole argument — that the hole argument poses a genuine threat to manifold substantivalism that the analogy with electromagnetism cannot defuse. Along the way, please say where exactly the analogy breaks down, whether the failure is to be located in the substantivalist's haecceitism about manifold points rather than in general covariance itself, and what the best escape route for the substantivalist would be if my analogy is indeed no help (or why there is none). I should be clear that I am not looking for an annotated bibliography; I want the argument itself, worked through with care. Please write your answer in British English.
