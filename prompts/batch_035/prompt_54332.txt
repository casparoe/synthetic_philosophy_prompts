So I'm a science-and-ideas journalist working on a feature about how "consent" gets used as a magic word -- medicine, research, terms of service, cookie banners, sex-ed debates, all of it. The piece argues the word is doing wildly different jobs in different places, and the editor wants a companion quiz for the site: "What do you actually think makes a yes count?" Readers should answer concrete cases, get sorted by their answers, not by which label they'd pick for themselves.

Can you build that for me? Roughly eight to ten questions, each one a short scenario with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key at the end mapping answer patterns onto positions. To give you a sense of the cases I want in there (use these or better ones):

- a patient signs a consent form for surgery without reading any of it, surgeon never mentions the small risk of nerve damage that would matter a lot to this particular patient
- a cookie banner where "accept all" is one click and "reject all" is buried behind two extra screens
- a boss says "go out with me or I'll find reasons to make your work life miserable" -- versus a slightly different case where the pressure comes from the other person's control of something you desperately need
- a person in severe pain in the ER agrees to a procedure they clearly don't understand, just to make the pain stop
- a person who's quite drunk says yes enthusiastically to something they'd refuse sober
- after a hurricane someone buys a generator at 5x the normal price from the only seller left -- "voluntary" or not?

The positions I'd want as leaves (add or rework as you see fit): the standard three-condition liberal view (informed + competent + uncoerced), the pure performative view where consent is just a speech act that waives rights regardless of what's going on inside the person's head, the internal-attitude view where the outward yes is only evidence of a mental state, hypothetical-consent views (what a reasonable person would have agreed to), tacit-consent views (defaults and silence can count), and the skeptical view that most real-world "consent" is a legitimation ritual layered on top of power imbalances, so validity depends on the background fairness of the options, not just the moment of the yes.

A few things I care about:

1. No question should be answerable by knowing the labels. Nobody should be asked "do you believe in informed consent?" -- the whole point is sorting people by what they say about cases.
2. Include at least one or two questions aimed at the deep split most readers won't have thought about: does a valid yes actually change what's *permissible*, or does it only change what you're allowed to *complain about* afterwards? And: when someone says yes under a distorted picture of the facts, is the problem the deception, or their resulting unwillingness, or both, and can those even come apart?
3. In the key, flag which pairs of leaves the quiz can't really seperate, and if some combination of answers maps onto a position nobody actually holds, say so and tell me whether that's because the view is incoherent or just unexplored -- I might quote that part in the piece.

Keep it readable -- this goes on a general news site, not a philosophy journal. And it's fine if the quiz ends up telling people "you hold a mix," that's honestly probably the honest result for most readers and it'd be good if the scoring key admits that rather than forcing everyone into a box.
