Every year since the flood of 1981 — thirty-one dead, fourteen of them children — our town has held a memorial service on the last Sunday of September: the names read aloud, a wreath laid at the weir, then tea in the hall. This autumn will be the last one. The last surviving parent of the dead died in February; the committee voted seven to four to end the annual observance, and I, as chair, argued for ending it. The four who voted against will be in the front row, and so will the daughter of the family that lost the most, who has said in the local paper that stopping the service means the town has decided to forget.

I have to give the final address, and I cannot get it right. Please write it for me.

There are things it may not say. It may not tell the bereaved that the dead are at peace, or that the town has moved on, or healed: the first is something none of us knows, and the second and third will be heard, by the people in the front row, as permission to forget. It may not treat the four decades of services as scaffolding — a structure put up for a season and now taken down because the building no longer needs it — because that would make the whole observance an instrument, a means to an end we have now reached. And it may not imply that ending the service settles any debt we owed the dead, unless the address can show why the debt, if there is one, is not the sort of thing a service discharges.

Here is the one idea I keep returning to, and I should like the address built around it if you can make it carry the weight. Our late chaplain, who read the names for thirty years, used to distinguish between memory owed as a debt and memory received as an inheritance. A debt is owed to a creditor, weighs on you until it is discharged, and once paid is done with; an inheritance is not owed to anyone, comes to you unasked, and is not discharged but spent — altered, put to use, and handed on in a different form. Her claim, as I understood it, was that our mistake from the beginning was treating the annual service as payment on a debt, which meant that every year the town secretly hoped for a final payment while dreading it as a betrayal. If the memory is an inheritance instead, then the question for next year is not whether we have paid enough, but what we will do with what we were given.

The address should therefore do three things: justify ending the annual service without saying that the town is finished with the flood; give the people in the front row something they can accept without conceding that they were wrong to want it to continue; and propose something concrete for next September, the first year with no service — a specific act, not a vague commitment to remember, since we have all heard that phrase used to mean nothing at all.

It is to be spoken aloud in a hall of farmers, shopkeepers and bereaved families, not read by a seminar: no philosophers' names, no learned references, and no sentence that would not survive being said slowly from a lectern. About five minutes as spoken — call it 750 words. And end it on something that will hold the room in silence for a moment, rather than send them straight to the tea.
