I am at the research stage of a novel centered on an organ-transplant coordinator, and before I write a single scene set in an ICU, I need to be confident that I understand the medical and conceptual landscape correctly. A nurse friend of mine, who has worked retrieval cases, summarized the situation for me roughly as follows: in most developed countries the law defines death by the brain rather than by the heart; once the whole brain has been destroyed, a person is legally dead even though machines can keep the lungs breathing and the heart beating, which is why there can be such a thing as a beating-heart donor — dead, warm, and with a pulse. She added that the brain-death standard was created in 1968 by a committee at Harvard, essentially so that surgeons could take organs from bodies that still had a circulation without anyone being charged with homicide, and that the older criterion of waiting for the heart to stop survives only as an accommodation, used when a family refuses to consent to the brain-death examination. Something in this account makes me uneasy — particularly the implication that the criterion was reverse-engineered from the needs of transplant surgery — but I don't know the field well enough to tell whether that is an accurate description of how the standard arose and now functions, or a polemical compression of its history.

Would you go through her summary claim by claim and tell me what a transplant physician or a bioethicist working on the dead-donor rule would accept, what they would want qualified, and what is simply wrong? I am especially interested in whether her account blurs the line between the irreversible loss of all brain function and the irreversible loss of consciousness, which I suspect are kept quite deliberately apart, and in what she has omitted. For instance, I have since read that there are donation pathways in which death is declared on circulatory grounds for patients who are not brain dead at all, which seems to complicate her claim that the heart-based criterion now exists only for families who decline the neurological exam. Since my narrator will have to explain these matters to a distressed family in one pivotal scene, I need the version a careful clinician would sign off on, plus a clear sense of which remaining points are genuine disagreements among serious people rather than errors my friend made.
