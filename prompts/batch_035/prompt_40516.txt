I'm building a unit for a philosophy of work course that pairs claims about automation with the films and fiction that supposedly dramatize them, and I want to open with Chaplin's Modern Times (1936). Please read the film through the contemporary debate about the meaning of work and automation: the dispute between those who hold that work is a primary source of meaning, dignity, and structure in a human life, and those who reply that most work has never been meaningful, that we romanticize labor from armchairs, and that automating drudgery away is a gain to be managed rather than a loss to be mourned. It may help to distinguish work's several roles in that debate — providing structure and discipline, providing community, and providing a sphere of achievement or self-expression — since the standard argument is that mechanization threatens only the third. On its face the film looks like a straightforward dramatization of dehumanization: the Tramp is fed into the assembly line almost literally, caught in the gears, subjected to the feeding machine, and driven to a breakdown. But the film keeps complicating that reading. The factory scenes are comic rather than tragic; the jail turns out to be the most stable and secure place in the film, the one place the Tramp does not have to scramble; and the ending sends the Tramp and the gamin off down an open road toward a little house that no job depicted in the film could ever buy them, while the café scenes — the Tramp waiting tables, and finally, in his singing number, discovering a talent that the machines never gave him and the police never take away — portray work outside the factory as both precarious and genuinely expressive. I'd like a reading that takes both the film and the philosophical debate seriously, and answers this: does the film show that mechanization destroyed the meaning of work, or that the meaning was never in the factory to begin with — that the good life lies elsewhere, and the machines merely keep people too poor and too exhausted to reach it? Please point to specific scenes, tell me honestly where the reading strains against the film (the comedy of the degradation is the obvious pressure point), and end by saying what you think the film adds to the debate that the arguments, stated abstractly, miss — particularly on the question of which of work's several roles automation can and cannot replace.
