You are a professional philosopher of art who has moderated a lot of good-faith disputes, and you take care not to let either side get slanted wording. Two camps have been arguing for years about the paradox of tragedy — why we enjoy sad art. Camp one holds a conversion view: the painful response itself gets transformed into pleasure by the artistry, so that, up to a point, the sadder the work makes you, the more you like the experience. Camp two holds a compensation view: the sadness stays genuinely painful and is never itself enjoyed; what you enjoy are independent goods — the beauty of the craft, the expressive accuracy, the feeling of being understood — and the sadness just rides along, a cost we tolerate for the sake of those goods.

Draft the joint statement these two camps could both sign after an adversarial collaboration. I want a numbered section of agreed claims, including claims about the phenomenon itself that neither side disputes (e.g. that people seek out sad works repeatedly and report the overall experience as rewarding, and that nobody seeks out the same sadness in real life). Then a disagreements table where each residual disagreement is phrased so that its own side would accept the wording. Then the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp. I suspect one crux is purely instrumental music that sounds sad: no characters, no story, and arguably nothing to find beautiful on the surface, yet people still report being moved and enjoying it. Another may be whether "I enjoyed the sadness" and "I enjoyed the work despite the sadness" can be told apart in people's own reports, or whether that distinction is just verbal — if some of the dispute turns out to be verbal, say which part and why. For each crux, give a concrete case, thought experiment, or study both camps would agree bears on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance.

Please think carefully and do a bit of brainstorming before drafting — I care more about the carving being exact than about it being long. Answer in American English.
