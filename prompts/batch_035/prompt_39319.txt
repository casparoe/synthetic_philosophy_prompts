My friend and I keep arguing about deterrence and I've realized my gut verdicts form a pattern I can't articulate. Curious if there's a principle behind them, or if some have to go.

US massive retaliation in the 50s -- implicitly threatening nuclear response to a Soviet conventional invasion of Western Europe, a threat nobody believed would actually be carried out because doing so would be insane -- I think making that threat was rational, and I think it probably worked. The doomsday machine from Dr. Strangelove (Kahn wrote about it seriously) -- wiring things up so retaliation is automatic and the threat becomes perfectly credible -- I think that's close to monstrous, even though all it does is remove the gap between threatening and doing that made the first case incredible. Kennedy-style brinkmanship, deliberately running a risk of accidental escalation during a crisis so the outcome is partly left to chance -- I find that more defensible than the flat massive retaliation threat, which feels backwards. Keeping a hair-trigger posture with launch-on-warning, accepting some small annual risk of accidental apocalypse in exchange for deterrence -- torn, but I lean yes. A dead-hand system that retaliates automatically even after your leadership is vaporized -- no, even though the logic looks the same. And punishing an aggressor after deterrence has already failed, when the punishment serves no further purpose -- pointless in that moment, and yet I think a country that's visibly incapable of it is less safe.

So: is there one principle about when making a threat is rational that captures all or most of these? What would it say about a country considering handing launch authority to an AI on the grounds that human reaction time is now too slow to deter at all? And please be honest about which of my verdicts your principle just can't save.
