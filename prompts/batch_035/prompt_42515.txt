I'm a third-year PhD student in a cog sci program, writing a term paper on higher-order theories of consciousness, and I've constructed an argument that I half think is a decent objection and half think is sophistry. I can't tell which half is winning, so I want an outside diagnosis. Background: I'm working with the higher-order thought (HOT) theory, the Rosenthal-style view on which a first-order mental state is conscious in virtue of being accompanied by a suitable HOT — occurrent, assertoric, non-inferential — with the content that one is in that state. My argument, such as it is:

(1) On the HOT theory, the relation between the HOT and the first-order state is constitutive, not causal or evidential: the HOT doesn't register that the first-order state is conscious, it's what makes it conscious. (2) A constitutive power can't be selective about its relata without citing some property of the relatum in virtue of which the power operates — if accompaniment by a suitable HOT confers consciousness, it can't be that it confers consciousness only on mental states unless something about those states is doing part of the conferring. (3) But if something about the first-order state is doing part of the conferring, the theory has conceded that the first-order character partly constitutes phenomenal consciousness, which guts the distinctive HOT claim that the meta-level is doing the interesting work. (4) So the HOT confers consciousness on whatever it suitably accompanies. (5) Imagine a mischievous demon arranges that whenever a certain rock sits on my desk, I have an assertoric, non-inferential thought about that very rock's current state. If (4) is right, the rock's state is now conscious. (6) Rocks aren't the kind of thing that could be conscious, so (4) is false, so (1) is false, and the HOT theory isn't a constitutive theory of phenomenal consciousness at all — at best it's a theory of when we can report our states.

I've since learned this is close to a known worry people call the problem of the rock, so I'm not claiming novelty, but I still can't see my way out. The obvious reply is that the HOT theory is a theory about *mental states* and was never meant to apply to rocks — but that just strikes me as (2) being denied by fiat. If the conferring power is sensitive to whether the relatum is mental, there has to be a story about why, and the only stories I can construct end up citing the relatum's sensory or qualitative character, which hands (3) the win. The other escape I know of is dropping to a same-order or self-representationalist view, where a single state represents itself and there's no accompaniment relation for the rock to crash — but I genuinely can't tell whether that dissolves the problem or just relocates it to a state that misrepresents its own character.

Tell me which step you'd reject and why it looked fine in the first place. Then price the alternatives for me: if the right move is to reject (2), I want to know exactly what property of the first-order state does the conferring work without collapsing the theory into a first-order theory with a monitoring function bolted on; if the right move is to bite the bullet on (3), say what that concession actually costs the higher-order theorist in explanatory ambition. For what it's worth, I *want* to keep some higher-order story — the blindsight contrast, and the cases where subjects deny seeing things their behavior clearly shows they registered, both feel like they cry out for a meta-level explanation — so tell me honestly if I'm just rationalizing.
