I'm a graduate student working on the metaphysics of laws, and I've come to suspect that the Humean/anti-Humean debate about laws of nature has stopped being a productive exchange and become a pair of monologues. I'd like your help diagnosing why, and what kind of disagreement it actually is.

Here is how the stall looks from the inside. On one side: Humeans, following the best-system approach, according to which the laws are the axioms of whichever deductive system achieves the best balance of simplicity and strength in systematizing the mosaic of local, particular facts. On the other: anti-Humeans of several stripes — those who hold that laws are necessary relations among universals that contingently connect properties; those who build lawfulness into the essences of properties themselves, so that it is of the nature of charge to behave as the field equations say; and those who simply take laws as primitive entities that push events around. The arguments are all decades old and entirely familiar. Anti-Humeans press two charges: that the best-system account cannot distinguish lawlike from accidental regularities in a non-question-begging way — the classic pair being a law saying all spheres of gold are less than a mile in diameter and the merely true generalization that no sphere of uranium is; and that Humeanism cannot genuinely explain the regularities, since the systematization is selected because the pattern is there, so nothing makes the pattern hold. Humeans press the mirror charges: that governing laws are an unexplanatory posit, since saying the laws "produce" or "constrain" events just relabels the regularity with a mysterious verb; and that simplicity-balance explanations of lawhood get the epistemology right, since it really is by systematizing that we find out what the laws are. Both sides have replies to the other side's charges; the replies are also decades old; and almost no one has changed teams in living memory, despite everyone agreeing on the actual physics, the actual history of science, and the actual inference patterns of working scientists.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis only: this is not a disagreement about laws at all. It is a disagreement about what an explanation of a regularity has to be — whether to explain a pattern you may exhibit it as a consequence of a compact unifying system, or whether that is mere description, and explanation requires something that constrains the pattern from outside it, something whose non-identity with the pattern is what does the explanatory work. If that's right, the debate is a proxy war over explanatory standards, and the positions in the philosophy of laws are epiphenomena of deeper and less clearly stated commitments about explanation. But I can think of at least two alternatives, and I'd like you to weigh them honestly. One is that the disagreement is verbal: the two camps mean different things by "law," with Humeans meaning something in the neighborhood of the inferential licenses of our best science and anti-Humeans meaning something modal, so the camps are answering different questions under one word and the debate should dissolve into a taxonomy. The other is that a real modal question is doing the work underneath: whether the mosaic of facts could have been different while the laws stayed the same — and that this is substantive but currently unanswerable, which would explain why the debate is stalled rather than empty.

So here is what I want from you. First, a diagnosis: verbal, or substantive-but-stalled, or about explanation in the way I've suggested, or something I haven't listed. Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually says and does — for instance, in how each side handles the accidental-regularity challenge, or in what each side counts as an advance (new physics versus new conceptual analysis of explanation). Second, tell me whether there is any principled way to decide between the diagnoses: is there some stipulated case, some imagined scientific practice, some consequence for how we understand counterfactuals or induction, that would count as evidence for one rather than the others? Third, given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress in this debate — a distinction to be drawn, a case to be constructed, an agreed formulation of a question — or argue that no progress should be expected because the debate is permanently underdetermined. I'm not looking for a survey of positions; I know the positions. I'm looking for a structural account of the deadlock and a view about whether and how it could end.
