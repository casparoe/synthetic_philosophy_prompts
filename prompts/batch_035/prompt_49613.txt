Write a short story (roughly 800-1200 words) about a decision that can't be made rationally, in the following sense: it's the kind of choice where you cannot know what the experience you're choosing will actually be like until you've had it, and having it changes you into someone with different values and preferences. I came across this idea, associated with the philosopher L.A. Paul, in an elective course on philosophy of mind — she calls such choices transformative experiences, and argues that before making them you can't really simulate what they'll feel like, and after making them you're no longer the same person whose preferences you were trying to satisfy.

Here's the setting I'd like you to use. Maya, seventeen, has been offered a fully funded year at a boarding school in a country she's never visited, where she doesn't speak the language, while her grandfather — who raised her — is entering the early stages of a degenerative illness. Her mother frames the choice in terms of tradeoffs: a year of her grandfather's remaining lucid time versus a once-in-a-lifetime opportunity. But Maya suspects that neither she nor her mother actually knows what either option will feel like from the inside — what missing the year would mean, or what the new country would do to her — and that whichever Maya emerges on the other side will want different things than the Maya making the choice now. The story should dramatize this epistemic problem rather than solve it: show her attempting to imagine her future selves, maybe consulting people who've made analogous choices and finding their testimony strangely useless (they can describe what happened but not convey what it's like), and ultimately having to decide anyway.

I'd like the story to end with her choosing one way or the other, but not with a tidy lesson about whether the choice was right. Ideally, the ending should leave open the question of whether the person looking back on the decision is even in a position to evaluate it — whether her retrospective judgment counts as evidence, given that she's been transformed by the very choice she'd be judging. Feel free to include her grandfather's perspective briefly, since he's facing his own transformative experience in the opposite direction. Please write it as literary fiction with an actual narrative arc, not a philosophical dialogue dressed up as a story.
