I do comms review at a mid-sized company -- every external statement, press answer, and exec social post goes through me and one other person before it goes out. Lately we keep fighting about statements that are technically true but smell bad: answers that dodge, numbers with cherry-picked denominators, "we have no plans to X" said while planning X is still six weeks of sign-offs away. One of us thinks technically-true-is-fine, the other thinks the public doesn't distinguish and we shouldn't either. We clearly need an actual procedure instead of vibes, and I remember there's a real philosophical literature on whether lying and misleading are morally different -- Kant apparently thought lying to the murderer at your door is forbidden but letting him draw a false but literally-true conclusion is fine, which is wild, and modern people talk about Grice and implicature and something called "paltering."

So: turn that literature into a checklist I can actually run in fifteen minutes on a draft statement, no philosophy degree required. I want:

- a sequence of questions to ask about the statement, in order, where each one has a clear enough answer that a tired reviewer can give it, and where a "no" at some point stops the procedure and kills (or rewrites) the statement
- the key distinctions that have to survive the simplification -- I assume things like what the sentence literally says vs. what a normal reader will take it to imply, whether the misleading reading is cancelable, whether the audience would even want the true reading -- but you decide which ones make the cut and which get dropped
- honesty about the points where the procedure runs out and it becomes a judgment call or a values question that my coworker and I just have to argue about, and maybe who wins those arguments by default (e.g., when in doubt, does the tie go to the more cautious reading?)

Then run it on three real-ish cases from our queue:

1. A reporter asks "are layoffs coming?" and the proposed answer is "we're focused on supporting our team through this year" while a restructuring is in fact in final approval. (For what it's worth, I think this one should pass and my coworker doesn't.)
2. A renewal stat in a press release that says "9 out of 10 customers renew with us," where the denominator quietly excludes everyone who canceled during their free trial period.
3. A case where the procedure has to give an answer I won't like -- ideally something it clears that I still find slimy, or flags as unacceptable that I think is fine. Pick whichever case does that best, and tell me plainly that the procedure disagrees with me.

Also tell me what the procedure can't do -- I'm guessing it won't handle statements where the audience is heterogeneous and different readers take away completely different things, but there may be worse blind spots.

Keep the whole thing under 900 words. We're not trying to become philosophers, we just want to stop having the same fight every Tuesday.
