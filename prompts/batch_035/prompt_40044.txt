In my upper-level epistemology seminar — fourteen students, one ninety-minute session — we are about to reach the bootstrapping problem, sometimes called the problem of easy knowledge. Rather than lecturing on it, I want to run an in-class game in which students run into the problem before they have a name for it. The structure I want them to feel from the inside: a single application of an instrument looks like perfectly ordinary reasoning (the gauge reads full; if the gauge reads full, the tank is full; so the tank is full), yet iterating that pattern seems to let a subject manufacture a certificate of the instrument's reliability out of nothing but the instrument's own deliverances. No lecture up front; the readings and terminology come only afterwards.

Design the activity for me. It needs concrete rules, roles, materials limited to cheap office supplies (cards, envelopes, printed charts, a die — specify exactly what I prepare beforehand), and a points-based scoring scheme, since I don't want to use real money. Somewhere in the flow the students should be offered the opportunity to certify the accuracy of their instrument, and they should predictably take it: they should end up producing a formal-looking reliability report or pronouncement that feels rigorous and is circular. The game should be built so that the certificate they generate would have come out the same whether or not the instrument was any good — I will hold a hidden ground-truth key they never see until the reveal, and the reveal should land hard. After the reveal, I need a debrief script that maps specific moments in the game onto the moving parts of the problem: where the warrant for the first premise of each single step was supposed to come from; why each step taken alone looks innocent while the iteration feels like confirmation; how what they did differs from ordinary calibration against an independent standard; and at least a gesture at the live diagnostic dispute between saying the subject never legitimately acquired warrant in the first place and saying the warrant exists but refuses to transfer to the conclusion it was used to support. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it, and be frank about the feature of the philosophical problem that no classroom game with a gauge-shaped prop can reproduce — in particular, whether anything like this can be done for the versions of the problem where the instrument whose reliability is in question is perception itself, and if not, what I as the facilitator will simply have to argue for in the debrief.
