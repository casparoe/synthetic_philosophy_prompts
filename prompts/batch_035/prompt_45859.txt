A friend who took one metaethics seminar too many walked me into the following argument, and I cannot find the exit.

(1) Being admirable consists in nothing over and above this: there being sufficient reason to admire one. Admirability is just the existence of the reasons; no further fact stands behind them.
(2) There is sufficient reason to admire the demon. He will torture you for a year unless you admire him, and a year of torture decisively outweighs whatever costs attach to admiration.
(3) So the demon is admirable.
(4) But the demon is not admirable. Extortion is the paradigm of what admiration is not a response to; if admiration is ever merited, it is merited by merit.
(5) So (1) is false, and there are facts about which attitudes are fitting that reasons do not ground.

He then insists the conclusion proves far too much. If (5) is right, the same should hold for belief: what makes a belief fitting cannot be exhausted by reasons to believe either, so there could be a belief that is fitting to hold even though nothing counts in favor of its truth. Since, he says, a fitting belief just is one supported by evidence, (5) collapses into incoherence. So he keeps (1) and (2), accepts (3), and concludes that the demon has made himself admirable by acquiring power over us — and that this sounds absurd only because we confuse being admirable with being good, which (1) never claimed.

I want to keep (4), and I also cannot bring myself to deny (2): it would genuinely be stupid, all things considered, to endure a year of torture rather than admire him. So tell me the following. First, where exactly does the argument go wrong — name the step, and identify the equivocation, if that is what does the work. Second, suppose you diagnose it by distinguishing two kinds of reasons to admire: those that bear on whether admiration is a merited response to its object, and those that bear on whether having the attitude would go well for you. Can you say what makes this distinction principled, without merely re-listing the intuitions about cases it was invented to save? Third, suppose that early in his career the demon once did something genuinely courageous — not enough on its own to make him admirable, but real. Do the two kinds of reasons sum? If they do, an unfitting admiration could become fitting with the addition of a threat, which sounds mad; if they do not, explain what stops them, given that both seem to be reasons of some sort. Finally, tell me what my insistence on keeping both (2) and (4) commits me to regarding the difference between what I ought to admire and what is admirable.
