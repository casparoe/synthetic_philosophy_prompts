I've been following the quantum suicide / quantum immortality arguments since they were re-litigated on Overcoming Bias and then, year after year, on LessWrong, and I have come to realize I can predict every move in the thread before anyone posts. Since I apparently can't stop myself from reading these threads, I'd at least like to understand their strange stability. Give me a diagnosis of the disagreement's structure rather than another pass through the arguments themselves.

The setup, stated so we share terms: in the many-worlds interpretation, any death-risking event with a nonzero-amplitude survival branch leaves a branch containing a successor of you. If subjective anticipation only tracks the existence of successor observer-moments and is indifferent to their measure, then you should condition on never experiencing your own death — quantum immortality — and its corollary, that quantum Russian roulette is a lunch someone else pays for, is quantum suicide. The standard counters are equally fixed: the measure objection, that anticipation should be weighted by Born-rule amplitude, which decays multiplicatively across risky events, so the surviving branch contains an arbitrarily thin slice of you and no lunch is free; the charge that the argument equivocates on 'expect to survive' between 'some successor exists' and 'my typical successor exists'; the reply that measure is a bookkeeping device with no grip on what a physical system should anticipate; and the tu quoque from big-world cosmology, that an infinite spatial universe, Boltzmann-brain continuations and all, hands you the same immortality with no quantum mechanics involved, so the dispute is just anthropics in a costume. Add Tegmark's observation from the late nineties that the setup is the one scenario where an observer's expectations allegedly diverge between many-worlds and collapse interpretations — which makes it look like an empirical question that no ethics committee will ever let anyone run.

Two decades in, the same people hold the same positions with the same confidence, and the threads always end in measure-versus-existence rather than anyone updating. My working hypothesis is that this is a verbal disagreement about the indexical 'you': that 'I will die' has never been disambiguated between 'all of my successors cease' and 'the measure-weighted typical successor ceases,' that the two camps imported their answers from whatever they already believed about personal identity before quantum mechanics entered the picture, and that the physics is doing no work at all. But I hold this only as a hypothesis, because I can also see it as a genuine formal question wearing verbal clothing — whether there exists any coherent probability assignment over successor selves in the first place — or as a decision-theoretic question the participants keep mistaking for a descriptive one, since nothing in the setup constrains what an agent should *do* about immortality it cannot cash out.

So: diagnose it. Is it verbal, empirical, formal, or merely hard? Which of the recurring moves actually bears on the diagnosis, and which are people talking past each other under a shared word? If it's verbal, say what conceptual disambiguation would dissolve it and whether the dissolving has already happened, unnoticed, in the personal-identity literature. If it's substantive, say what would count as progress — a theorem about anticipation and measure, a completed decision theory for branch-indexical agents, an actual divergent-expectation experiment — and whether we should expect any of these in practice or whether the question is permanently insulated from evidence by the anthropic structure of the situation. Be honest about which participants' claims are answers to different questions under one name, and about whether the impasse is a sign the question is ill-posed or just unfinished.

I want the reasoning up front, engaging what each side actually says, and then a final line of the form "Verdict: <a short label for the kind of disagreement this is>" followed by one sentence on what, if anything, could move it.
