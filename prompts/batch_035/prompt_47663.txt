I'm no historian — just someone who has noticed, after years of visiting old battlefields, that countries seem to treasure their defeats more than their victories, and that the plaques never quite say who won. The pattern repeats almost everywhere: the American South's "Lost Cause," Masada, the Serbs at Kosovo field, the British at Dunkirk. In each case a military defeat was slowly reworked into a story of moral victory, and then into museums, school lessons, and gift shops.

Please write a parody piece: a glossy tourism brochure, around 650 words, for a fictional consortium I'll call the League of Glorious Defeats, marketing a multi-stop "Pilgrimage of Noble Failure" that links these sites across different countries. The voice should be deadpan throughout, exactly the way real tourism boards write, and a reader should be able to tell what is being ironized without a single wink from the writer. The humor has to come from real features of this kind of mythmaking, exaggerated rather than invented: defeat relabeled as vindication; the enemy quietly romanticized so that losing to him flatters us; the awkward parts moved off-page (in the Confederate case, what the cause was actually about; at Masada, what the defenders actually did at the end); commerce and grief blended into a single tone. Include a comparison chart rating each destination for "moral victory" and "poetry and songs inspired," and a short FAQ whose answer to the tourist who asks "but didn't we lose?" is somehow worse than the question. Name no living person, and don't let the brochure openly criticize itself — the satire should land because the brochure's logic is followed honestly to wherever it goes.

Since the jokes have to rest on real history, end with a separate short note stating how confident you are in the historical details you used, and singling out the one detail you are least sure of.
