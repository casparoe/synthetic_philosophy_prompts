Last night my closest friend and I argued until three in the morning about the paradox of deontology, and we ended exactly where we started, which makes me suspect half our disagreement was verbal. She is a consequentialist who thinks deontological constraints are irrational in a specific sense: if I can prevent five violations of the very same constraint by committing one violation of it, then honoring the constraint both increases the total number of violations and makes things worse for me, the constraint-holder, which she claims no coherent theory of practical rationality can allow. I hold that constraints mark a distinction between violating a norm myself and foreseeing that others will violate it, and that this distinction survives her arithmetic. But we did find things we agreed on: that the paradox does not touch agent-centered prerogatives, only constraints; that "doing vs. allowing" is not by itself an answer to it, since in her paradigm cases I do something rather than merely allow something; and that a constraint theorist who flips into minimizing violations whenever the numbers run against her has simply stopped being a constraint theorist. Write the joint statement an adversarial collaboration between her camp and mine should produce: a numbered section of claims both camps can sign; a table stating our residual disagreements in wording each side would accept as fair to itself; and the cruxes. I believe there are at least three, and I want them all in the document. The first is whether practical rationality assesses an agent's single decision or the policy she adopts, since she concedes that constraint-following may be the optimal policy in iterated settings while denying it is rational in the one-shot case. The second is whether her argument requires interpersonal aggregation of violations or can be run on a single agent across time. The third is a test case she raised that neither of us could settle: an official who can prevent a brutal successor regime from taking power only by forging evidence now, such that refusing the one violation foreseeably produces many more violations of the same kind by others, and each of us must record in advance what we predict a rational agent would do and what the other side's own theory commits it to saying. For each crux, state the case or argument both camps agree would bear on it, with each camp's prediction written down. If you conclude, as I suspect, that part of our dispute is merely verbal — turning on whether "irrational" is being predicated of acts or of dispositions to act — say which part, and identify exactly where the verbal portion ends and the substantive disagreement begins.
