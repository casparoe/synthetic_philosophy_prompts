I record a long-form interview series with philosophers, and the next episode features a defender of organicism about the special composition question: she holds that some things compose a further thing only when they compose a living organism, so that the xs compose a y just in case the activity of the xs constitutes a life. I'd like you to draft nine to eleven interview questions for her. I'm not looking for questions that just have her restate the position; I want questions that press on the actual stress points of the view. In particular, please include at least one question each on the following: the argument from vagueness, according to which any restriction on composition other than "always" seems to generate an object arbitrarily close to a composite organism -- a few scattered simples fewer, say, one heartbeat into the past -- so that the boundary between a life and no life looks vague in a way composition allegedly cannot be; the everyday-objects cost of the view, since tables, statues, ships, and planets all turn out not to exist as composite objects, and I want a question probing how she deals with the ordinary-language reply that our atomistically-structured beliefs about furniture are nonetheless true if paraphrased; and the question of what "life" amounts to if it is doing such heavy metaphysical lifting -- whether it can be specified non-circularly, and whether strange edge cases like slime molds, engineered cell-like systems, or a galaxy-spanning ecosystem of communicating life forms would be a single organism, and hence a composite object.

For several of the questions, please also supply a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give. For example, if the natural move on the vagueness worry is to claim that life, unlike artefactual composition, is an all-or-nothing matter with sharp though epistemically inaccessible boundaries, the follow-up should ask what could make that more than a bare assertion of the possibility -- what would the world have to be like for the life/no-life boundary to be perfectly sharp when every measurable indicator of biological activity shades off continuously? I'd also like one question about the dialectical relationship between her view and van Inwagen's own version of organicism, since his answer famously leaves him unable to say that there is such a thing as a table in the ordinary way, and it would be good to hear whether she accepts that package or thinks a friend of organicism can do better. Finally, if you can, include one more speculative question -- something about whether accepting organisms as the only composites should change how she thinks about personal identity, since on her view I am an organism but my left-hand-shaped region contains no proper part of me at all, just simples arranged variously. Please keep the tone courteous but pointed, as befits a professional philosophy audience.
