I teach an intermediate seminar on decision theory for philosophy students — about 18 people, one 75-minute session. Design an in-class experiment that makes them feel the pull of superrational cooperation rather than merely comprehend it as a position Hofstadter once defended. The core difficulty is that superrationality only bites when a player perceives their counterpart's reasoning as a kind of copy of their own, and ordinary classroom pairings do not produce that perception. So build the perception in. Have students complete a short pre-class questionnaire on their reasoning habits, and run a one-shot prisoner's dilemma under at least two framings: one in which pairs are formed by matched questionnaire answers and players are told so, and one in which they are told pairing was random. Payoffs should be in tokens redeemable for something small but real, not hypothetical money. Students should discover, before any lecture, that their own cooperativeness tracked the framing, and the debrief then has to make sense of that result: is evidence of shared reasoning legitimate grounds for cooperation in a genuinely one-shot game, or is it a dressed-up confusion about causation? Map the discussion precisely onto causal versus evidential decision theory, the symmetry assumption, and the difference between cooperating because one's cooperation is evidence the other cooperates and cooperating because both players will reason their way to the same conclusion. Please include the questionnaire items, the payoff matrix, a minute-by-minute timeline, a frank note on what the activity cannot reproduce — real one-shot conditions, a counterpart who literally shares one's reasoning process — and how I should supply those in the debrief. Finally, tell me the two or three things that will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (students conferring between rounds, someone defecting to be funny, samples too small to show an effect) and how to handle each.
