I teach a graduate seminar on punishment and transitional justice, and this term I have been assembling my own verdicts on cases of retroactive criminal punishment, in something like the spirit of reflective equilibrium, with the aim of extracting a principle from them that I can defend to my students. The equilibration is not going well, and I would like your help. The verdicts first, then what I want from you.

My verdicts. (1) The prosecutions at Nuremberg for the atrocities — crimes against humanity and the war crimes proper, much of it conduct that was lawful and even mandated under German law at the time it was committed — seem to me entirely justified, and the complaint that the applicable norms were applied retroactively has never struck me as doing any real work against them. (2) The prosecutions at Tokyo for crimes against peace — planning and waging aggressive war — seem to me much harder to defend. The states concerned had renounced war in the 1928 Kellogg–Briand Pact, but whether that renunciation made the waging of war a crime for which an individual could hang, rather than a wrong between states, was genuinely contested among international lawyers at the time, and punishing conduct under a criminality that had to be argued for rather than assumed seems to me much closer to the vice the nullum crimen sine lege rule exists to guard against. You might expect my verdicts to track the tribunal — Nuremberg good, Tokyo bad, the familiar charge of victors' justice — but they do not, at least not for that reason: I find the atrocity counts at Tokyo as defensible as their Nuremberg counterparts, and I would say the aggressive-war count at Nuremberg itself is troubled by exactly the scruple that troubles me at Tokyo. The split I feel runs through the charges, not through the venues or the identity of the victors. (3) After a dictatorship falls, the successor state prosecutes border guards who shot people trying to flee across the wall that the old regime had built; the shootings were expressly licensed by the old regime's statutes, though that regime's own written constitution promised the inviolability of personal liberty. My verdict: prosecution justified. (4) After a scandal, a democratic legislature, with overwhelming public support, passes a statute criminalizing retrospectively a tax-avoidance scheme that was lawful when used and that most practitioners at the time regarded as sharp but not wicked. My verdict: prosecution unjustified, indeed straightforwardly tyrannical. (5) The tribunal also punished the fallen regime's ministry lawyer who drafted the deportation regulations — a man who killed no one with his own hands and whose signature made nothing legal that was not legal already. Here I waver in a way I do not at the wall: punishing him feels continuous with punishing the guards, and refusing to punish him feels demanded by the very scruples I have about the aggressive-war counts. I have gone back and forth on this case for two years.

So there is, I take it, a principle in the vicinity — something to the effect that retroactive punishment is legitimate where, and only where, the conduct was discernibly a grave wrong at the time it occurred, independently of positive law — but I can see that it will not do as it stands. It cannot explain why (3) feels justified to me while (2) does not, since in both the actor acted under color of a legal order he did not make and the wrongness was discernible by moral reflection; and it is not obvious what it says about (5). A colleague tells me the fix is a two-tier principle distinguishing wrongs done by direct physical violation of identifiable persons from wrongs done to the international order as such, with discernibility doing the work only in the first tier, but that has the smell of a principle reverse-engineered to save my verdicts.

Please structure your reply as follows, in this order. First, a section titled "The principle, stated precisely," containing the best candidate principle, or the two best candidates if you genuinely cannot choose, stated in enough detail that its verdicts on cases are derivable rather than gestured at — no more than 250 words. Second, a section titled "The work each clause does," in which you motivate each element of the principle by reference to which of my verdicts it is there to capture — roughly 500 words. Third, a section titled "Predictions," stating what the principle implies for three or so cases I have not given you — for instance, the officials of a state that never adhered to any renunciation of war and whose leadership sincerely doubted its criminality, or the retroactive prosecution of a scientist who in the 1950s ran non-consensual experiments on institutionalized patients, lawful then and believed by his profession to be sound — roughly 250 words. Fourth, a section titled "What must go," in which you state plainly which of my verdicts, if any, the principle cannot accommodate, and whether the price of saving them all is a complexity that should be refused — roughly 200 words. Fifth, a section titled "The ministry lawyer," resolving case (5) one way or the other and saying why — roughly 200 words. I would rather be told that one of my verdicts is unreconcilable with the rest than be handed a conjunctive principle with a clause for each of my scruples.
