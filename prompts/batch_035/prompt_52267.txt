I've been reading about acausal threats and surrogate goals and my gut verdicts are all over the place, so I want to know if there's actually a principle behind them or whether I'm just being inconsistent. Here are my verdicts, case by case.

(1) A guy with a knife demands my wallet on a dark street. I hand it over. Obviously. The threat works on me and I don't feel bad about it.

(2) I strongly endorse the policy of never negotiating with hostage-takers, and I'd vote for a government with an actual standing rule to that effect, even knowing some hostages will die becuase of it. Also feels obvious.

(3) Someone argues a future superintelligence might be simulating me right now and will torture my simulated copies forever unless I send them $50 today. Even granting weird big numbers for the simulation chances, I don't pay, and I don't even feel tempted. It's not that the math is off — I just refuse to treat this as the same kind of thing as case (1).

(4) A schoolyard bully keeps taking small stuff from a kid — a candy bar here, a dollar there. Someone suggests the kid publicly precommit to going absolutely berserk over the very next candy bar, making himself a bad deal to threaten. Surrogate goal. I think this is genuinely clever and I'd tell the kid to do it.

(5) Same trick, but adopted as a general life policy: precommit to react to any trivial slight — someone cutting in line, a snarky comment online — as if it were a major attack, so nobody ever has a cheap lever on you. This seems unhinged to me, and kind of undignified, even though it's literally the same mechanism as (4).

(6) Once a threat has already been made and I'm actually in the negotiation, I think you evaluate from where you are — the threat was made, that's sunk, so do whatever gets the best outcome now. Mid-standoff, if giving the hostage-taker a car saves lives, give them the car, even though I endorsed the never-negotiate rule in (2).

So is there one principle that gets all six? My own tentative stab is something like: resist a threat when and only when your resistance is part of what makes threats of that type get made less in the first place — it's about the effect on the population of potential threateners, not about you. But I can't get it to say why (3) is dismissable while (1) isn't, since by my principle the acausal threatener should also be discouraged by resistance... unless the whole point is that with the acausal guy there's no reliable causal arrow from my paying to anything. But that feels like it smuggles causal decision theory in the back door, and honestly I'm not sure I'm entitled to that, because if I were to take evidential reasoning seriously anywhere it seems like it'd be about agents who can predict or simulate my choices.

Test my principle against the six cases, repair it, or replace it with something better. Tell me which verdicts the repaired version can't save, and what it predicts for the case I keep going back and forth on: an extortionist is threatening to dox members of my online community unless paid, and we're deciding whether to adopt a shared surrogate goal — every one of us precommitting to treat a doxxing attempt against any member as an attack on all members, answered with permanent total non-cooperation with the extortionist. Good move, or have we just built case (5) at community scale?
