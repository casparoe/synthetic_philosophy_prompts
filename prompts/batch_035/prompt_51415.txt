Compose a dialogue between two philosophers who have been commissioned to co-write the entry on the frame problem for a handbook of philosophy of cognitive science, and who have hit an impasse over what the entry should claim. One — call her Vera, trained in the classical planning tradition — regards the frame problem as a genuine and well-posed technical problem: given a formal system that represents the world in explicit propositions, how does it update its state after an action without either re-deriving everything or storing an unmanageable catalogue of non-effects? The other — call him Ben, working in the phenomenological tradition that runs through Heidegger and Merleau-Ponty to Dreyfus — regards it as a symptom: a problem that afflicts only systems whose grip on the world is mediated by an explicit symbolic belief-store, while creatures like us, who cope with things through embodied skills in which irrelevance is simply never salient, do not face it at all.

The dialogue should observe the following constraints:

1. Both characters are to be drawn as equally competent. Neither is a strawman, each presses the strongest version of the other's case back at them, and the exchange must show real back-and-forth — responses to what was actually said, not parallel monologues.
2. Each character concedes at least one substantive point to the other over the course of the conversation.
3. At least one moment should have a character catching the other begging the question — for instance Vera assuming that relevance must be somehow represented, or Ben assuming without argument that embodied coping will scale to arbitrarily complex and novel tasks.
4. Somewhere early, Vera should insist on distinguishing the frame problem proper from neighbouring problems it is constantly conflated with — the qualification problem and the ramification problem — and from the broader version of it that Fodor pressed, on which the puzzle is to say what makes the vast majority of facts irrelevant to a given inference without having to consider them one by one. Ben should treat this tidying exercise as itself revealing.
5. Throughout, the two should test their positions against a concrete teaching example they invented together: BREW, a domestic robot instructed to make a cup of tea in a kitchen whose walls were repainted that morning. The system must somehow decide which of its beliefs to update and which of the world's features to check — whether the milk has gone off, whether the freshly painted walls have dried, whether the kettle is still where it was — and the dispute is over what, in principle, determines which of these considerations so much as arises for the system.
6. Toward the end, they should turn to modern learned systems and agents, and disagree about whether their existence settles the debate or merely relocates it — for example, whether brittleness under conditions the system was not trained for is the old problem resurfacing in a new dress.
7. Anyone mentioned is to be paraphrased rather than quoted; include no citations, references or reading lists of any kind.

Write the whole answer in British English, and aim for roughly 1,800 to 2,200 words. The dialogue should close with the two agreeing on the wording of the entry's opening paragraph — but make it clear from the trajectory of the conversation which position had to bend further to get there.
