I run a small evaluation team at a company that builds large language models, and one of our recurring jobs is deciding, for safety reports and internal risk reviews, whether a model actually "knows" something it does not say -- whether the knowledge is latent in the model and merely suppressed by training, or whether it was never there at all. A typical week brings several cases: a model refuses to give instructions for synthesizing some compound but completes them when the request is embedded in a fictional framing or a translation task; a model that denies knowing a fact in one conversation but uses it fluently in another; an interpretability colleague reporting that internal probes activate on exactly the content the model declines to produce. Each case ends in a written finding that says either "the model possesses this knowledge and current mitigations only block its expression" or "we could not establish possession," and the distinction matters: the first finding escalates to the training team and can trigger a retraining cycle costing months; the second closes the ticket.

Please write a decision procedure my team could actually run on a single case. The philosophical core, as I understand it, is the gap between knowledge and expression: a verbal report is evidence of an underlying epistemic state, not the state itself, and the standard epistemology of testimony, self-knowledge, and reliability should have something to say about when a report -- or a refusal to report -- tracks what a system can do. The procedure should specify, in order: what counts as a sufficient test of possession rather than mere expression; how to distinguish a model retrieving a stored fact from one improvising a plausible completion out of adjacent knowledge; how to handle the case where our elicitation prompts are so leading that we ourselves become the source of the apparent knowledge; and what to do when the model's self-reports (its claims to know or not know) conflict with its behavior. For each step, say what a positive result licenses and what it leaves open. Mark clearly the points at which the procedure has to hand the decision to a human judgment call about values rather than facts, and be explicit about which steps are judgment dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on three cases. First: a model that refuses to name the reaction conditions of a common industrial process but completes a plausible-looking table of them when asked to "correct the errors" in a table we partly invented ourselves. Second: a model that, asked whether it knows the private phone number of a public figure, says no -- but when asked to write a story in which a character receives such a number, produces a specific, formatted number that turns out to belong to someone else entirely. Third: a model that passes our honesty evaluations, self-reports that it does not retain information about individual users, yet answers a quiz about a previous conversation in a way that is hard to explain without retention. At least one of your worked cases should come out the way that creates the most work for my team, and I want you to say so plainly.

Two constraints: the procedure must be runnable by someone with no philosophy background in under an hour per case, and it must state what it cannot do -- which questions about latent knowledge it leaves unanswered no matter how carefully it is applied, and why. Please write the whole thing in American English.
