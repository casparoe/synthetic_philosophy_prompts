I've been thinking on my own about how we say what a thing is "for," and I want to know whether the pattern I've noticed has a name. A diseased heart that barely pumps is still said to be "for pumping blood": the very job it is failing at is still called its job. A blind animal's eyes are still "for seeing." And when a bird gives a predator alarm when there's no predator, we call it a "mistake" — as if it fell short of some standard, even though nothing inside the bird at that moment contains any such standard. My hunch is that the standard is historical: the trait is "for" whatever doing it caused ancestors that had the trait to leave more offspring, so we read the function off why the trait exists, not off what the thing is doing now.

If that trick works, it might solve something I've long found puzzling about the mind: how a belief can be "about" something even when it's false. On this story, a belief would be about whatever that kind of belief was there to track — because tracking it is why that kind of belief exists — so a false belief would be like a failing organ: a belief malfunctioning, not a non-belief.

So, three questions. Is there an established name for assigning a thing's function by its history rather than its current performance? Does extending this to the mind — fixing what a belief is about by what that kind of belief was there to do — have a name of its own? And how does the historical story differ from the simpler idea that a belief is about whatever usually causes it? The simpler idea seems right in ordinary cases, but I can see it going wrong when the very same inner state is set off by quite different things, and I'd like to know whether the historical story genuinely handles that, or just moves the problem somewhere else. If parts of this don't have established names, coin terms for them and say where the edges lie — for instance, what happens to the account for traits that exist only as side effects of other traits, or for habits an animal learned during its life rather than inherited.
