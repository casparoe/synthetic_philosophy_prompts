I'm a philosophy graduate student whose department has spent the past two years on what I can only describe as a stalled war over metaontology, and I want a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than another round of it.

The first-order dispute is the familiar one: how many objects are on my desk right now? My supervisor is a neo-van-Inwagenian about composition — the only composite objects are living things — while others in the group accept tables and chairs, and one accepts arbitrary mereological fusions of my desk with the lamp behind it. Everyone cheerfully concedes all the facts about atoms arranged tablewise; the fight is over whether there is, in addition, a table. The meta-level fight is the one I want diagnosed. On one side sit the deflationists, in the spirit of Eli Hirsch and Amie Thomasson: disputes about composition are verbal, because a sentence like "there are exactly forty-two objects in my office" can express a truth under many equally eligible meanings of the quantifier, and the appearance of contradiction dissolves once we say which meaning is in play. On the other side sits Ted Sider's ontological realism: among the candidate meanings, some carve nature at the joints better than others, and figuring out which is a substantive enterprise — that is what makes ontology a discipline rather than a linguistic negotiation. The arguments on both sides are familiar to everyone in the room, the same papers get cited every week, and nobody's position has moved.

Here is my working hypothesis, and I want it stress-tested rather than confirmed. I've begun to suspect that the meta-disagreement is itself verbal — a verbal dispute about whether the first-order dispute is verbal. The deflationist, as I read her, is answering a question about our language: which meanings of "there are" are eligible, and whether any are ruled out a priori. Sider is answering a question about the world: whether reality has an intrinsic structure such that some eligible meanings align with it better than others. If that's right, the two positions aren't even inconsistent, and the standoff is what you'd expect from two people answering different questions under the single name "metaontology." But I can also see how the substantivist would reply: whether reality has structure is itself supposed to be a substantive question, so my move just pushes the bump under the rug — the real fight concerns whether eligibility is constrained by joint-carvingness, and that looks like exactly the sort of question that cannot be verbal on pain of regress. I don't know how to adjudicate between my hypothesis and this reply, and I may well be misdescribing one or both sides.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is the meta-dispute verbal in the way I suggest, or a substantive disagreement about what makes a quantifier meaning eligible? Is Sider's appeal to theoretical virtues — the joint-carving quantifier earning its keep by systematizing, simplifying, and unifying our total theory — doing real argumentative work there, or is it a disguised appeal to criteria a deflationist could accept while conceding nothing about structure? And does the fact that each side can absorb the other's favorite observations without budging tell us the question is ill-posed, or merely hard? I'd also like your view on what would count as progress here: a sharper distinction, a better formulation of the eligibility question, a first-order result that filters up — or an argument that no progress should be expected, and why.

If my framing is confused — if I've misdescribed the deflationist, or if the verbal/substantive contrast I'm leaning on can't bear the diagnostic weight I'm putting on it — please say so plainly rather than working around it. I would rather learn that the real disagreement is located somewhere else entirely than receive a polite ratification of where I've placed it.
