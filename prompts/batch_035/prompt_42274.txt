You are a philosopher of time and formal semantics, asked to give a probability estimate rather than a literature review. The question: what is your credence that the sentence "There will be a sea battle tomorrow," uttered tonight before any relevant decision has been made, already has a determinate truth value? Do not answer "it depends on your view of time" — I want a number and the decomposition that produces it. Suggested terms: (a) the probability that bivalence for future-tensed statements holds as a matter of semantics alone, regardless of what the world is like; (b) the probability that the future is ontologically settled in something like the way the past is (eternalism or a growing block rather than an open future), conditional on which you might think truth-value supervenes on how things are and not just on meaning; (c) the probability that the distinction between these two routes matters — i.e., that one could accept bivalence while still holding the future genuinely open, so that a determinate truth value does not imply fatalism. Then, as a stress test, consider this scenario: an infallible but causally inert bookkeeper has already written either "battle" or "no battle" in a sealed ledger, and the entry cannot be wrong. Does the mere existence of the completed entry settle the truth value, or does the entry inherit indeterminacy if the world itself has none? Indicate how this consideration shifts your number, if at all. Combine the terms, state your final credence, identify which term the final number is most sensitive to, and say what argument or discovery would move that term the most.
