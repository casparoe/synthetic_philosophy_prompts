I sit on the nosology workgroup preparing the next major revision of a psychiatric diagnostic manual — I'd rather not say which body specifically, since I want the reasoning to be about the structure of the decision, not our internal politics. A proposal before us would elevate a condition provisionally called "maladaptive daydreaming" — distressing, hours-long bouts of vivid fantasy that interfere with work, study, and relationships — from the research literature and online self-help communities into the manual as a formal diagnosis, with proposed criteria requiring, roughly, more than two hours per day of absorbed fantasy plus clinically significant impairment plus distress. The workgroup is split down the middle. One faction argues that the phenomenon is real, that sufferers currently get misdiagnosed with ADHD or OCD, that without a code there is no insurance reimbursement, no research funding stream, and no way for people to explain themselves to their families. The other faction worries that we would be minting a diagnosis out of a behavior that shades continuously into ordinary reverie, that the threshold was reverse-engineered from the most visible online cases, and that pharmaceutical and psychotherapy marketing will do the rest. I've been tasked with drafting the written opinion that will circulate before our final vote, and I want to make sure it takes seriously a concern that keeps surfacing in our sessions without ever being fully argued out: the worry, associated with the philosopher Ian Hacking's notion of "looping effects," that once a category like this exists and circulates, it doesn't just describe a pre-existing population but reshapes it — people learn how to be the kind of person the manual describes, communities converge on the canonical symptom presentation, and the criteria we write effectively manufacture the homogeneous patient population that later field trials will then "discover."

Please draft the memo for the workgroup. It should recommend a concrete course of action — full inclusion, rejection, or inclusion with conditions in a section for diagnoses requiring further study — with an explicit time frame and specified review triggers (e.g., what kind of evidence, gathered over how many years, should force us to revisit the decision in which direction). The reasoning should engage the looping-effect worry at its strongest rather than treating it as a vague caution: say where in the causal story it does real work, where it does not (categories for, say, schizophrenia seem to loop differently than categories for culturally saturated behaviors), and what, concretely, a workgroup can do about it in how it words criteria, sets thresholds, and recommends screening instruments — if anything. But it should also give the pro-inclusion faction's strongest argument its due, especially the point that withholding a label does not leave the world untouched either: self-diagnosis and community formation are already happening without us, possibly with less stable results than a formally criterialized category would produce. If the honest answer is that our epistemic position is symmetrically bad either way, the memo should say so and explain why the recommended option still wins. It should be a document my colleagues could vote on, not a literature review, and it should be written so that the losing faction could recognize their position in it.
