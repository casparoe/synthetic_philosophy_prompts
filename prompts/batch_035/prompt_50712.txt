I'm taking a philosophy of mind course this semester and we've spent like three weeks on integrated information theory now, and I've noticed something that's bugging me way more than the theory itself: the debate about IIT seems completely frozen. On one side you have the IIT people, who start from the phenomenology -- experiences exist, they're structured, they're definite, they're irreducible to their parts -- turn those into axioms, then flip them into postulates about physical systems, and out comes phi: consciousness just IS maximally irreducible cause-effect structure, so a simple feedforward network has zero of it, the cerebellum barely contributes, and even a photodiode has some tiny amount. On the other side you've got critics who think this is close to methodologically backwards -- that starting from "immediate and irrefutable" intuitions about experience and building a mathematical superstructure on top is exactly how you get a theory that can never lose, and last year over a hundred consciousness scientists signed a public letter straight up calling IIT pseudoscience, mostly because of the panpsychism-adjacent stuff (a grid of logic gates having more consciousness than a child, that kind of thing). And it's not just cranks on either side; these are serious people, they've seen all the same arguments for a decade, and literally nobody has moved an inch.

My working hypothesis, and I honestly don't know if this is right, is that the two sides aren't really disagreeing about any empirical claim at all -- they're disagreeing about what an explanation of consciousness is supposed to do. The IIT camp thinks you explain consciousness by starting from the inside, from what experience is like for the one having it, and any theory that starts from brain function and behavior gets the hard problem for free. The critics think an explanation has to be the kind of thing that could have turned out otherwise -- that makes predictions, that you could imagine discovering it was wrong, and that doesn't treat introspective reports about the "definiteness" of experience as data the way you'd treat a measurement. If that's right, then no adversarial collaboration or perturbational experiment or new formal result is going to settle it, because both camps would already predict the cortex lights up and the cerebellum doesn't. But maybe I'm wrong and there IS a hidden factual question, like whether phi computed on some substrate actually tracks anything causal, or whether the axioms are even internally consistent as axioms.

So what I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict on whether IIT is true. Is this disagreement substantive, verbal (like they secretly mean different things by "explain" or even "consciousness"), a difference about which intuitions count as data, or two groups answering different questions under one name? Use what each side actually says to make the case. And then tell me honestly: given your diagnosis, is there anything that would count as progress here -- a conceptual distinction, an experiment, a formal theorem, a reformulation of the question -- or is this a dispute where we should just expect permanent stalemate, and if so why does it feel so much more heated than other stalemates in the field? The "pseudoscience" framing in particular seems to me like it's doing something different from "I think you're wrong," but I can't put my finger on what exactly.
