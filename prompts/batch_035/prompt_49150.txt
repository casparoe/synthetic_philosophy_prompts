A post on a machine-learning blog I've followed for years offers this summary of the no-free-lunch theorems: "Wolpert proved that, averaged over all possible problems, no algorithm outperforms any other — not even guessing at random. Machine learning works anyway because our universe happens to have exploitable structure, so an algorithm's success reflects the fit between its biases and that structure, not any general superiority of the method. In effect, Hume's problem of induction has been turned into a mathematical theorem: no method of learning from past data can be justified without assuming the very regularities it was supposed to discover. That's why it's meaningless to say one algorithm is generally better at learning than another — there is no free lunch." The post then argues that since priors do all the work, Bayesian methods are vindicated and frequentist statistics is thereby refuted.

I've seen no-free-lunch invoked endlessly in online arguments about priors, but almost always secondhand, and I'm inclined to think the Hume sentence is doing something slippery without being able to pin down where. Could you audit the summary sentence by sentence? I want to know which claims a careful treatment would accept, which are harmless simplifications, which quietly conflate distinct results (I've heard the search and supervised-learning versions differ, and that something depends on how the averaging is done), and whether the Bayesian conclusion actually follows from whatever survives the audit.
