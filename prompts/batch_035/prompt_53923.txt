I am a novelist two books into what will be a trilogy set in a fictional Alpine mining town, where a shaft collapse in 1974 killed forty-one men and the last mine closed a decade later. The novels draw heavily on oral histories I collected from survivors and their descendants, and I also run a small volunteer memoir-writing circle at the parish hall, so I sit, month after month, in the middle of a community's decisions about which memories to hand down and which to let quietly lapse. Every few chapters I face a version of the same question: does this detail belong in the record, or am I merely mining these people's grief for material? What I lack is any disciplined way of answering it, and my instinct is either sentimentality or cowardice, depending on the day.

The philosophy I have read on this pulls in two directions, and I want it converted into something usable at my desk. Avishai Margalit, in The Ethics of Memory, argues that the duty to remember falls primarily on communities bound by what he calls thick relations — relations of care, kinship, and shared history — rather than on humanity at large; he distinguishes memories merely held in parallel by many people from memory genuinely shared and worked over together, and he gives a special place to the witness who records evil from within the community that suffered or committed it. David Rieff, in In Praise of Forgetting, presses the opposite worry: that the imperative to remember can freeze a community into permanent grievance, and that a certain amount of forgetting is a condition for going on living together. What I would like is a decision procedure I can actually run on a single manuscript decision in one evening: the questions to ask, in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a firm leave it out or a firm this must be preserved, and where it correctly gives up and hands the call back to me because the question is one of values that no procedure can settle.

Please then run the procedure on three cases from my current draft. First: a widow insists the novel name the shift foreman who sent the men back down the shaft despite the inspection report, and his grandchildren still live in the valley. Second: the fiftieth-anniversary committee wants to excerpt my account of the collapse for a commemorative ritual, whereas I wrote it as studiously ambiguous about blame. Third: an incidental passage recounting a cruel prank the miners played on the one Italian guest worker in the 1960s, which none of my sources considers important, and which made his only surviving relative cry with something I could not tell was relief or humiliation. Include at least one case where the procedure yields an answer I will hate, and be candid about how a procedure like this can misfire — for instance, by laundering my vanity as piety toward memory, or by letting me hide behind a community's supposed need to forget when I simply lack the courage to write the scene.
