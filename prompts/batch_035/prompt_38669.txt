My ethics reading group has been stuck on the heterogeneity problem for hedonism for basically the whole semester and I want to understand what kind of disagreement we're actually having. As I understand it, the problem is this: hedonism says pleasure is the only intrinsic good, but "pleasure" seems to cover wildly different things -- the taste of cold water when you're thirsty, the satisfaction of finishing a dissertation, the weird enjoyment of a sad movie, the relief when a migraine lifts. Bentham treated all this as commensurable, Mill split pleasures into higher and lower, and Sidgwick basically threw up his hands and said the only thing pleasures share is that they're forms of consciousness we find desirable when we reflect. In our group, one friend insists pleasure is a genuine natural kind that affective neuroscience will eventually pin down (she brings up the "wanting vs liking" literature, where liking and wanting apparently come apart in the brain), another says the whole demand for a common property is a philosophical illusion and pleasure is just a family-resemblance concept like "game," and I've been half-convinced by attitudinal views on which sensory and attitudinal pleasures are unified at the level of a pro-attitude toward contents rather than any felt quality. Nobody has moved in months.

Please act as a professional philosopher who works on wellbeing and value theory, and think carefully before answering -- maybe brainstorm the possible diagnoses first. Here's what I want: a diagnosis of what this stalled debate actually is, not a survey of positions. Is it a verbal dispute about the word "pleasure"? Is it a substantive but partly empirical question that psychology or neuroscience could in principle settle, and if so, what result would settle which part? Is it a conceptual question about what unification we should demand before a theory of value gets to appeal to "pleasure" as a single thing? Or -- and this is my working theory, but I hold it loosely -- is the whole thing downstream of the normative fight, where people press heterogeneity when they want hedonism to be false and find unifying stories when they want it true, so nobody is really asking about pleasure at all? Whatever diagnosis you land on, defend it by showing what it gets right about what each side actually says, and then tell me honestly what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, data from affective science, a reformulated hedonism, or nothing -- plus, if your answer is "nothing," why the question is ill-posed or just permanently hard rather than merely unresolved. I'm writing a term paper on this so feel free to go deep, but don't give me a reading list, I've got that covered.
