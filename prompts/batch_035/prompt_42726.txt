I'm taking a graduate seminar on population ethics — we've spent most of the semester on Parfit's Reasons and Persons, and I've just discovered that what I thought were my settled views are less settled than I'd assumed. Our final assignment is a "philosophical profile": we each write down our commitments in the area, swap with a classmate, and audit each other for consistency. Before I hand mine over, I want a professional philosopher's audit of it myself — one that goes after it rather than massaging it. Please take on that role and be rigorous rather than diplomatic. Here is my set, in no particular order:

(1) I accept a person-affecting restriction on moral claims: nothing can be wrong unless it is worse for someone — moral verdicts must bottom out in someone's being made worse off. (2) I think death is bad for the person who dies, and I accept the deprivation account of why: death harms you by depriving you of goods you would otherwise have enjoyed. (3) I think comparisons between existence and nonexistence are undefined — it makes no literal sense to say of anyone that they would have been better off never existing. (4) In cases where a couple could either conceive now a child who will have a serious but manageable condition, or wait a few months and conceive a genetically different, healthy child, I'm confident conceiving now is wrong, and wrong *to* the child they conceive — that child is the victim of the choice. (5) I think a society pursuing a policy of rapid resource depletion acts wrongly even if the depleted future contains only people with long, happy lives who would not otherwise have existed at all, and I'm equally confident this wrongness is *not* a matter of anyone being worse off than they otherwise would have been. (6) I accept the so-called asymmetry: there is no moral reason to create happy people just for their happiness, but there is real moral reason not to create miserable people. (7) Finally, I hold that procreative liberty is close to absolute — whether and when to conceive is among the most protected exercises of personal autonomy, not something other people may coercively interfere with.

Before you assess anything, please brainstorm what each commitment commits me to beyond what I've explicitly stated — the entailments and the natural bedfellows each one drags in. Then audit the set: tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like it, ranking the genuine tensions by severity rather than flagging everything equally. For each real tension, say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the set, and note carefully which of the apparent ones dissolve on inspection and why. Close by telling me which single revision would clean up the most. I honestly don't know where the deepest problem is, so don't let me off with a survey — commit to a verdict.
