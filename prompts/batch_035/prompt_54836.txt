I write novels for a living, and my craft rests on a habit of mind I have come to apply well beyond the desk: the conviction that the felt order of events is largely a retrospective construction, that the teller imposes a sequence on material which arrived otherwise. Reading Dennett's Consciousness Explained persuaded me that perception itself works this way, and no case made it more vivid than colour phi — Kolers and von Grünau's illusion in which a blue dot and then a red dot are flashed in succession at different positions, and subjects see a single dot moving smoothly between them and changing colour midway along its illusory path, even though nothing was ever presented in the middle. Dennett's response, as I take it, was to dismiss as empty the dispute between the Orwellian story (the experience occurred in one order and was then rewritten in memory, like the Ministry of Truth editing the past) and the Stalinesque story (the brain held the material back and delivered it to consciousness already doctored, like a show trial staged for a captive audience). Both stories, he argued, presuppose a determinate moment at which content enters consciousness, and no such moment exists — there is no fact of the matter about when the colour change was really experienced. I find this deeply congenial, perhaps too congenial, and I want it stress-tested. Tell me what would count against the view, ranked from fatal through serious damage to merely awkward. Which empirical results could fix a fact of the matter about the timing of experience — findings on postdictive perception, integration windows, backwards masking, Libet-style onset latencies — and what would such a result have to show beyond what the Orwellian and Stalinesque rivals already agree on, given that all three views predict the same verbal reports? Could a clever experiment ever catch the revision in mid-flight, prising apart what was experienced from what was later remembered, or does the view's insistence that reports exhaust the data render it immune by construction? What would Dennett's own defenders concede as genuine damage rather than dismiss as a category error? Has the drift from multiple drafts through to the later fame-in-the-brain reformulation quietly qualified the position into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that fatal for a view whose whole subject is timing, or simply the honest price of denying a distinction? Finally, tell me plainly whether a novelist's confidence in retrospective narrative construction is genuinely the same claim as Dennett's, or whether I have mistaken a metaphor at the heart of my method.
