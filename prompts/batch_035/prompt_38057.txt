something that bugs me about the translation industry, hoping you can untangle what it implicitly believes. The same localization shop will run technical manuals through pure machine translation, QA'd by a metric that basically scores overlap between the output and a reference human translation -- but for a novel they insist on a human translator who actually reads the book, and for contracts they want a certified human even though legal prose is the most formulaic text there is. So one industry trusts statistical equivalence for some texts and refuses it for others. What view of meaning does that pattern presuppose? Is it committed to meaning as distributional/behavioral equivalence in the low-stakes cases but something richer -- the author's intention, the experience behind the idioms, the thing Hofstadter was pointing at when he argued that a system can render every idiom "correctly" and still miss everything -- for literature? Can a single theory of meaning license the whole pattern, or does the practice only make sense if meaning comes in grades, and if so what grades? Also, notice the overlap metric compares against a human reference translation, so it sort of smuggles the human back in -- does that mean the practice doesn't really presuppose statistical equivalence as meaning, only as a cheap proxy, and if so where exactly does the proxy break down (the old apocryphal story of a line about spirit and flesh coming back as one about vodka and meat has the overlap looking perfect, I think?). Or is the honest answer that the human/MT split tracks stakes and liability rather than any view about understanding at all? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say where the practice is underdetermined between rival theories, and tell me what would have to change if the industry took seriously the view that the machine understands nothing it translates.
