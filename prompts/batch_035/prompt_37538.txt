Everytime someone explains Jain philosophy to me they reach for the same image: blind men and an elephant. One grabs the trunk and says its a thick snake, one feels a leg and says pillar, one gets the ear and says fan, one the tail and says rope. The moral drawn is the doctrine of anekāntavāda, many-sided truth: every partial perspective captures something real, no single vantage point gets the whole, so you should hold your own beliefs tentatively and grant that rival positions are true "from their own standpoint." I'm basically on board with this but I've started wondering how much work the elephant is actually doing vs how much work it just looks like it's doing. So map it for me: what's supposed to correspond to what -- the elephant itself, the men's blindness, the touching, the guy who in some versions of the story shows up and can actually see the whole animal? Because it seems to me the analogy smuggles in exactly what the doctrine is supposed to deny: there's one fully formed determinate elephant just sitting there, and the story only feels instructive because somewhere there's a correct complete view of it (the sighted person's) that we're invited to imagine. If truth really is many-sided, who or what plays the sighted observer, and if the honest answer is "nothing," is the elephant still an argument for tolerating rival views or just a nice picture that concedes the whole game to the absolutist? Related worry: the blind men's claims are each supposed to be partly true, but "snake-like," "pillar-like," "fan-like" don't obviously compose into one unified description the way parts of a jigsaw do -- the analogy seems to assume partial truths add up, and I dont actually know whether anekāntavāda needs them to (some readings sound more like: each claim is true only relative to its standpoint, and standpoints never merge). I want to know which disanalogies actually threaten the conclusion I want to keep -- "be epistemically humble about your own take, the other side usually sees something real" -- which ones dont, and whether there's a better image than the elephant that carries what I want to carry without smuggling in a God's-eye view.
