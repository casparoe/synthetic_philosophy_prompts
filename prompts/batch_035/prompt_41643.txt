Write a sestina on the two-envelope paradox, told from the point of view of the total amount in the pair — a sum that never changes no matter how many times the switching argument recommends a trade. For readers who need the setup: one envelope holds twice what the other holds; you take one; the argument that the other is equally likely to hold double your amount or half of it yields an expected gain of a quarter and so says switch — and the argument survives the switch unchanged, saying it again in the other direction. The six end-words of the first stanza should be *envelope*, *switch*, *double*, *half*, *keep*, and *expect* (inflected as needed), cycled by the standard sestina permutation; the ceaseless redistribution of the same six words among the line-ends should enact the exchange on the poem's own body, so that the form itself shows a fixed stock being endlessly reshuffled with no net gain. The three-line envoi must use all six end-words and carry the actual diagnosis: that "the amount in my envelope" is treated as one quantity when the two cases — the one that doubles it and the one that halves it — cannot both be half-probable at every value, so the promised quarter is an artifact of describing two different situations with a single variable. Someone who has worked through the paradox should be able to point to the exact line that does this work; someone who hasn't should still come away with a good poem about a sum that watches two hands keep trading it.
