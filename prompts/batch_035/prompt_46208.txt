so, background: i'm a novelist, and the secondary narrator of my current book is a philosopher of biology, which means i've spent the last six months reading the function literature so she doesn't sound like a costume. cummins on functional analysis from the seventies, millikan on proper functions, neander's selected effects view, godfrey-smith's attempted truce. and the thing i keep bumping into as an outsider is how utterly frozen this fight is. the etiological side says the heart's function is pumping blood because pumping blood is what ancestors' hearts were selected for doing — that's what licenses saying a diseased heart is malfunctioning rather than just doing something else. the causal-role side says that's smuggling teleology back in after darwin supposedly evicted it: a function is just the contribution a part makes to some capacity of the containing system that we happen to care about, and selection explains why the part is there, it isn't part of what the word means. and the two sides have been at it for decades, everybody knows everybody's best cases — the etiologists pointing at sperm, most of which never fertilize anything and yet seem to be doing their job just fine, or at lab-grown organs with no selection history at all; the causal-role people pointing at cancer, which does enormous causal work inside a system without anyone wanting to call that its function — and as far as i can tell nobody has switched teams since before i was published.

my working hypothesis, and i want you to take it as a hypothesis and not let me off easy, is that this is two questions wearing one word: the etiologists are really answering something like "what makes normative talk about bodies possible, why can a heart fail at its job," and the causal-role people are answering "how do we decompose a complicated system into parts whose activities explain what the whole thing does," and the fight can't be won, only escaped — which is maybe why the pluralists (godfrey-smith and company, "both are fine, use whichever fits your purpose") are the only ones who seem to have made any headway. but i've also read people who insist it's genuinely substantive, a real metaphysical dispute about whether there's any fact of the matter about what an organ is for, and i'm honestly not positioned to tell. so: diagnose it for me. is it verbal, is it two-questions-under-one-heading, or is there a hidden substantive issue — maybe about norms, maybe about teleology — that keeps it alive? ground the diagnosis in what each side actually says, not in labels. and then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a case someone hasn't tried, an empirical finding, a reframed question — or whether the pluralist ending is actually an ending and not just a refusal to pick, which as a novelist i'll tell you upfront feels emotionally false to me. my character is going to hold whichever view you diagnose as the losing one, incidentally, so don't sand off the edges. oh and please write it as continuous prose, no headings or bullet points or bold or anything, just paragraphs like a long email — i read everything on my phone.
