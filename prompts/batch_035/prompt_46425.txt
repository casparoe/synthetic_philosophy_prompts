In a seminar on autonomy and influence that I've been sitting in on this term, we've circled the manipulation-versus-persuasion line for weeks without ever pinning it down, and I've realized the problem may be that I can't even state my own pattern of judgments. So instead of another round of theory, I want to try laying out my actual case-by-case verdicts and see whether any principle underlies them, or whether I'm just applying the label 'manipulation' to whatever I happen to dislike. Here are the cases and my gut calls, which I'm fairly confident about except where noted. A car salesperson opens with an inflated price she has no intention of holding to, purely to anchor the negotiation: not legitimate, though I notice I'd call it 'sharp practice' rather than something that wrongs me in the way fraud does. A surgeon tells a worried patient the procedure has a 90% survival rate rather than a 10% mortality rate: fine, though both framings are true, and I can't say why the choice of words doesn't bother me when the salesperson's opening number does. A charity fundraiser structures its appeal around one named child with a photograph, knowing from its own research that this raises far more than statistics about the ten thousand children in the same region: fine, and I'd feel pedantic calling it manipulation. A government pension program enrolls everyone by default with an opt-out, deliberately exploiting inertia, and openly says so: fine, maybe even good. A friend asks me for a favor immediately after doing me a good turn, having timed the request: borderline, and this is one I genuinely waver on. A romantic partner responds to disagreement not by arguing but by withdrawing warmth until I give in: not fine, clearly, even though nothing said is false and no bias is exploited that I can name. A supermarket charges $8.99 rather than $9: fine, though everyone concedes the left-digit effect is doing the work. And a father lets his small child 'choose' between two vegetables he has already decided to serve: fine, though I've gone back and forth on this one more than any other.

What I'd like from you is a principle — or a small set of competing candidates — that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, stated precisely enough to decide new cases, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it. Two features of my pattern seem to me to need explaining, and I want you to address them explicitly. First, the salesperson and the surgeon are both trading on non-rational features of my cognition (anchoring in one case, framing in the other), yet only one bothers me, which suggests the principle can't just be 'bypasses the rational faculties' — or if it is, I need to hear what distinguishes the two. Second, the partner's withdrawal of warmth and the friend's well-timed request look structurally similar, yet I condemn the first firmly and waffle on the second, and I want to know whether a principled account would side with my firm verdict or my wavering one. Please tell me plainly which of my verdicts your favored principle cannot accommodate, and what it predicts about two cases I deliberately left out: outright gaslighting (getting someone to doubt their own perceptions in order to secure compliance), and a political campaign that micro-targets ads to each voter's specific psychological vulnerabilities, with every individual ad factually accurate.
