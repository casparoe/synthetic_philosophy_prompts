I run a small podcast on medieval philosophy and next week I've got on a scholar who just finished a book arguing that Avicenna's flying man is not just a cute thought experiment but a genuine demonstration that self-awareness is primitive -- that the person created fully formed while floating in air, blindfolded with limbs not touching, still knows he exists without needing any sensory input or introspection of a body. She's big on the claim that the flying man shows self-awareness can't be an inference from experience, since the man has no experience to infer from.

Draft me 9 or so interview questions that actually engage the substance. I'm tired of guests just retelling the thought experiment. Specifically I want at least one question each on the following, and for each question include a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give:

- The begging-the-question worry: critics say the experiment smuggles in its conclusion, because Avicenna just stipulates that the flying man is aware of something, and the interesting claim (that what he's aware of is *himself*) is exactly what's in dispute. If you stipulate awareness, why couldn't a Humean say the man just has some free-floating phenomenal states without any self?
- Whether she thinks the flying man is an argument or more of an intuition pump that only works on people already sympathetic -- I've read some people argue Avicenna himself used it as a quick illustration, not a proof, and I want to know if she thinks the "strong" reading is anachronistic.
- The embodiment objection: cognitive science types would say an actual brain without proprioceptive or vestibular input wouldn't have anything like a unified self, so the experiment proves at most what would happen in an impossible world. Does she take the modal character of the case to be a feature or a bug?
- Whether the flying man's self-awareness, if genuine, would have *content* -- could the man know he's the same person as yesterday, or does he only have a featureless "I am"? This connects to the prince-and-cobbler case in Locke and I'd love a question that bridges the two.
- Whether her reading of Avicenna commits her to saying infants or animals have this primitive self-awareness too, and if so whether that strains the text.
