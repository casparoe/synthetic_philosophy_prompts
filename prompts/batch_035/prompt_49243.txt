I run fact-checking at a consumer science magazine, general audience, mid-size outlet. Every few weeks a draft or pitch leans on a heritability number: "schizophrenia is 80% heritable so it's basically genetic," "BMI is 70% heritable so diets don't work," "this trait is 50% genetic in this population." My checkers are sharp but aren't statisticians, and they need something they can run in fifteen minutes without tearing apart the source papers. You're a philosopher of science with a strong quantitative genetics background -- think carefully, and maybe brainstorm a bit first, before settling on the structure. Write me a decision procedure our checkers can follow for vetting heritability claims: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops the claim cold, and where it has to hand the call back to me because the question is editorial rather than factual. The core confusions should show up as questions a checker can actually ask, not as a lecture: what population and what range of enviroments the estimate was measured in, why high heritability doesn't by itself say anything about how malleable a trait is or about differences between groups, what "missing heritability" does to a number from a GWAS versus a twin study. Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a draft saying a personality trait is "60% genetic" off a single twin study conducted in one country, (2) a pitch arguing that high heritability within two countries proves the average difference between them is innate -- I want the procedure to kill this one even though the writer is a name we've published before and the editor loves the piece, and (3) a claim that a trait's heritability has risen over recent decades in the same population, where the right verdict is genuinely subtler and the procedure should show that. Flag where the procedure will misfire or lend false confidence. Keep it in American English.
