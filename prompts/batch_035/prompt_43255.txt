I am a novelist by trade, and I am drafting an essay for a journal that publishes both philosophy and literary nonfiction. The working title is "First Drafts." The thesis: dreams are experiences, but compositional ones — of a piece with the experiences of fictional characters rather than with perception. When I write a scene, my character's experience does not precede my writing of it; the experience exists only in the being-told. My essay claims dream-telling works the same way: the content is fixed at the moment of narration, on waking, and not before. I plan to argue this in four notes, and I would like your comments on the plan before I commit it to prose.

The notes, as they stand:

1. Against Malcolm. Malcolm held that dream-reports cannot in principle be verified and that this disqualifies dreams as experiences, since experience requires the possibility of being checked at the time. I want to reply that Malcolm has identified a feature, not a defect: a great deal of what we count as experience is unverifiable in the same way, and my trade is the proof. Nobody can verify what Anna Karenina felt, but I composed her feeling, and readers receive it as an experience had. The analogy I want is between the reader's relation to the character and the dreamer's relation to last night's dream.

2. With Dennett, but against his tone. Dennett's model — that the dream is composed at the moment of waking-report, rather than recorded beforehand and played back — is usually presented as deflationary, as though it shows dreams to be less than experiences. I propose to accept the model and reject the deflation. On my view Dennett has simply discovered the truth about a large class of experiences: fictional ones. His account doesn't demote dreams; it promotes fiction to the status of an ontological category.

3. The second-draft illusion. Here is my own contribution. Dreamers are typically convinced they experienced the dream before waking — that the report is a second draft of something first drafted overnight. I claim this conviction is the same illusion readers have that the world of a novel extends beyond its pages: in both cases, the content of the telling generates the sense of an antecedent. The narrative smoothing, the feeling of temporal depth, the sense that there was more before the fragment we remember — all of it is produced at composition. The dream is not a memory of an experience; it is an experience in the mode of a memory.

4. What follows. If this is right, the interesting question shifts: not whether dreams are experiences, but how much of waking experience is also compositional in this way — how much of ordinary perception is, so to speak, dictated rather than transcribed. I want to end on this, briefly, and not overreach.

Please act as a philosopher of mind with a serious literary sensibility — someone who has actually thought about the difference between describing an event and composing one. Think carefully before judging, and begin by brainstorming freely about the thesis. Then give me comments of three kinds. First, accuracy: have I represented Malcolm and Dennett fairly, and where does my characterization of their positions need correction or hedging? Second, substance: does the fiction analogy do genuine philosophical work here, or does it merely lend the essay charm? In particular, tell me whether step 3 survives without the analogy — I suspect it does not, and I would rather know now. Third, discipline: identify where the essay claims more than the notes earn, and which single passage, if a hostile reader got hold of it, would sink the piece. Be candid; flattery is of no use to me at this stage.
