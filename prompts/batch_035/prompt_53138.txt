I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums since the late 2000s, long enough that David Pearce's hedonistic imperative feels like an old acquaintance — I've absorbed the whole ecosystem of counterarguments to it, from wireheading to "would you still love your family." Next month I'm presenting it outside that ecosystem for the first time: a bioethics colloquium at a medical school, roughly a third clinicians with practical-ethics instincts, plus a Kantian who works on consent theory, a disability-studies scholar, and a natural-law theorist. Nobody there will ask the stock LessWrong questions; they will ask their own.

My thesis is deliberately modest by Pearce's standards: not universal paradise engineering, but that we have moral reason — and, as germline editing matures, will soon have the technical means — to raise humanity's hedonic floor by recalibrating the reward system, preserving motivation and accurate representations of the world rather than flattening them into euphoria; and that keeping the ancestral default of chronic low-grade suffering is itself a policy choice that requires justification, not a neutral baseline we're entitled to keep.

Prepare my Q&A sheet with these sections, in this order: "Open with concessions" (~150 words on what to grant before the first hand goes up); "The objections" (eight or so, ordered by how much damage each would do, each phrased in that audience's vocabulary — I expect the cochlear-implant analogy, consent of unborn generations, medicalizing ordinary mood, mood as authenticity — each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft); "The question I hope nobody asks" (name it and give the best available answer); and "The one reply that has to land" (~100 words). Be committed throughout: where my thesis needs trimming to survive that room, trim it and say what I've given up; where it can hold, hold it. Don't hand me a menu of defensible positions to choose among — you choose.
