I edit the interview series of a small journal of media criticism, and for our next issue we are running one of our recurring "imaginary interviews" with a historical figure. Our guest is Søren Kierkegaard, caught in Copenhagen in the autumn of 1855, in the middle of the pamphlet war of his final year, but far enough from 1846 that his literary review Two Ages — the portion now usually translated as The Present Age — is well behind him. I want to steer him toward his diagnosis of the press and of "the public," but without letting him settle into the role of a revered predecessor of media criticism. Draft ten to twelve interview questions for this exchange.

The questions should do more than invite him to restate his well-known charges about leveling and passionlessness. I would like the sequence to press on the actual structure of his view: at least two questions on the public itself — whether it is a real collective agent or an abstraction he constructed in order to explain the leveling he detested, and what evidence, if any, would falsify his claim that publicity dissolves passionate commitment into mere reflection; at least two questions on the tension between his critique of the press and his own conduct — the pseudonyms, the deliberately engineered provocation of Goldschmidt's Corsaren, the pamphlets of his last months — that is, on whether he is best understood as a critic of the press or as one of its most sophisticated early practitioners; at least one question on whether his ideal of the passionate individual who stands against the crowd amounts to aristocratic nostalgia dressed up as religious seriousness; and at least one question on which parts of his diagnosis he would concede fail to transfer to a world where the press is an algorithmic feed and every reader can also be a pamphleteer, and which parts he would insist carry over unchanged.

For each question, please also include the follow-up keyed to the reply he is most likely to give, given what he actually argues. For example, if he falls back on his claim that the public is a phantom that no actual individual can ever equal, ask him why a phantom deserved a decade of polemic; if he maintains that his pseudonyms were an act of religious self-erasure rather than an authorial game, ask how that sits alongside the articles in which he openly invited the Corsaren to ridicule him. Keep the register of the questions respectful but adversarial in the way of a good journalist — we want him cornered into giving his best defense, not honored into vagueness.
