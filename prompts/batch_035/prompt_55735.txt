I was at my flatmate's birthday drinks on friday and two of her friends from her metaphysics course got into a proper argument about Max Black's two-sphere universe — the thought experiment where literally all that exists is two qualitatively identical iron spheres floating some distance apart in otherwise empty space. I followed it well enough to see that neither of them was being stupid, but not well enough to referee it, so maybe you can.

It went roughly like this. Priya opened by saying the case just kills the identity of indiscernibles: there are two objects in that universe, they share every property, so Leibniz's principle has a counterexample and its false, full stop.

(1) Daniel said not so fast — the spheres differ in their relational properties. One is the left sphere and one is the right sphere, so each stands two miles from a sphere in a specific direction. Different relational profiles, so they were never indiscernible to begin with.

(2) Priya said directions are a cheat. In a universe with nothing else in it, nothing makes one way left rather than right — you can flip your coordinate system, the descriptions swap, and nothing in the universe itself has changed. So he distinguished the spheres with labels, not facts.

(3) Daniel pivoted: forget directions, then. There's still a relational difference if you allow yourself to name them — call them a and b. a is two miles from b and b is two miles from a, and those are two different states of affairs. Plus a has the property of being two miles from b, which b lacks, since b isn't two miles from itself.

(4) Priya said that's smuggling. Any property built out of a name for one of the spheres will of course tell them apart, but the Leibnizian only ever cared about purely qualitative properties — ones you can state without pointing at either sphere. If you allow properties like being-this-very-sphere, the principle becomes trivially true and also trivially useless.

(5) Daniel's last move was that this makes the whole thing a definitional dodge: if property means only qualitative property, the principle can't lose, but it also stops saying anything — the thesis people actually cared about was that qualitative facts settle everything, including how many things exist, and by Priya's own lights they don't. Priya answered that this IS the result: a complete qualitative description of a world doesn't fix how many objects it has, and that's a discovery, not a trick.

Then someone put music on and the whole thing dissolved. Can you adjudicate move by move — who's right at each of the five steps, and where exactly the losing moves go wrong? Also two follow-ups I couldn't answer on the night: does Priya's final reformulation (qualitative facts don't fix object count) actually show the identity of indiscernibles is false, or does it quietly change the subject to something weaker than Leibniz claimed? And is Daniel's step (3) really hopeless, or is there a version of the relational-difference argument that survives what she said in (4)? And generally, what were both of them missing, if anything?
