I write novels for a living — literary stuff mostly, nothing with robots in it — and earlier this year I got briefly obsessed with this framework called embedded agency, which comes out of the AI alignment corner of the internet. The short version as I understand it, since it's niche and I want us on the same page: the classic picture of a rational agent is basically dualist. The agent sits outside its environment like God over a chessboard, fully models it, is in some sense bigger than it, and never gets touched by the thing it's modeling. Embedded agency is what you get when you drop that assumption: the agent is a physical system inside the environment, made of the same stuff as everything else, part of what it has to model. And apparently that one move fractures into a whole cluster of subproblems: (1) embedded world-models — the world is too big to fit inside a model that is itself part of the world; (2) self-reference — the agent has to model a system that includes its own model, so a fully accurate self-model runs into liar-paradox-style trouble; (3) reasoning about your own successors — an agent that could perfectly predict its own future reasoning gets stuck, because to plan what to conclude later it would need to have already done the concluding; and (4) subsystem alignment — the agent is built out of parts, the parts have their own local optimization pressures, and what the parts "want" can drift from what the whole thing wants.

I ended up structuring a subplot around this — an AI that has to model a world it's inside of — and now my writing group wants an evening session on "that thing you kept ranting about at the retreat." So: one evening, maybe ninety minutes, audience of sharp novelists and essayists with zero math or CS background, a whiteboard, alot of coffee. Don't give me the lecture — give me the teaching plan.

Specifically: what order do those four subproblems go in and why, i.e. what actually depends on what? What should be the carrying example for the whole evening? My instinct is the novelist one, obviously — the author who tries to write themselves writing the novel, inside the novel, and hits regress after regress — but tell me straight whether that example carries all four or quietly fails on one of them, and if there's a better one (I've heard thermostats and ant colonies floated). If the author-in-the-novel thing only carries part of the load, say which part and what to use for the rest. Then the misconceptions: I'm betting at least one person says "oh, it's just map vs. territory," another goes "isn't this Gödel?", and someone confidently concludes "so this proves real AI is impossible." For each one, where in the sequence do I head it off instead of letting it fester until Q&A? What should I flat out refuse to cover at this level and openly admit to refusing? And give me one question I can end the evening on that would actually show me whether a fiction writer got the idea, not just whether they found it charming.
