Can you write me a satirical excerpt from an employee handbook? Specifically the "Culture & Heritage" section of the onboarding docs for a made-up mid-size SaaS company (pick a blandly plausible name), written in totally earnest chipper HR-speak, Notion-style. The joke is that the company, without anyone noticing, has organized its whole relationship to its own past into exactly the three modes of history from Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation. So the handbook has a "Learn From the Greats" section (monumental history: founder myths, the legendary daring bets of long-gone VPs, the whole past curated into a chain of great moments you're meant to emulate), an "Honor Our Roots" section (antiquarian history: the preserved first office couch in the lobby, Heritage Days, the wiki page commemorating the company's first customer, near-religious reverence for the original codebase), and a "Leave the Past Behind" section (critical history: a Legacy Modernization team whose whole job is condemning and demolishing the company's own past, framed as self-improvement).

The humor has to come from getting Nietzsche's actual analysis right, not from generic corporate comedy. So the monumental section should exhibit the specific pathologies he worries about: emulation flattening real differences between eras, effects getting confused with causes (middle managers coming away convinced the great man succeeded BY wearing a black turtleneck to the keynote), the "great moments form a chain" logic, and the way what reads as inspiration to some employees reads to others as a party line that quietly crushes them when their own moment doesn't fit the chain. The antiquarian section should show its pathology too: the reverence shading into reflexive hostility to anything new, the maintainer who squats defensively over their little corner of the past and treats every proposed migration as vandalism. The critical section should show its danger: the demolition that clears the ground but leaves nothing to build on, and a faint bitterness in always treating the past as a defendant. And close with the handbook's policy on forgetting, something like "we're a forward-looking company" -- which is Nietzsche's real point about needing history only in the measure that serves life, but deployed here as a way to make people stop asking about the layoffs.

A specialist in the essay should be able to point at passages and say "yes, that's precisely the monumental-history failure mode" rather than "generic office joke." Aim for about 650 words, formatted as the actual handbook with section headings, a pull quote or two, and maybe one italicized 'culture interview' quote from a fictional VP. Nobody in the piece should have heard of Nietzsche, and don't quote him verbatim or cite him anywhere. American English please.
