I'm a high school junior, and this year I was elected to sit on our school's Community Standards Committee — a mixed group of students, two teachers, and the vice principal that meets weekly to rule on complaints, club funding requests, and proposals to change school rules. Recently I've noticed that almost every disagreement we handle comes down to the same unresolved question, and nobody on the committee has a principled way to answer it: when a student or teacher says "this is wrong" or "we shouldn't allow this," are they reporting a genuine moral judgment, or are they just dressing up a personal preference — or worse, a prejudice — in moral language?

The cases make this feel urgent rather than abstract. Last month a student formally objected to our cafeteria adding halal options, saying it "isn't right" for school resources to go to some groups' dietary rules; two weeks later a different student objected to a classmate's disability accommodation on the grounds that it "isn't fair" to everyone else. In both cases, the committee split, and the arguments went in circles because we had no shared test for whether the stated moral reason was the real reason, or whether it was discomfort, self-interest, or bias wearing a moral costume. At the same time, I don't want to assume that every complaint from a majority group is bias — sometimes majority discomfort with something is a legitimate moral signal, and I worry that a procedure that too quickly reclassifies people's sincere moral claims as "just bias" becomes its own kind of unfairness.

For background: in my civics class we touched on the idea that you can test a rule by asking whether you'd accept it without knowing what position you'd occupy under it, and I've read a little about how psychologists separate automatic gut reactions from considered judgments — but I have no training in philosophy, and neither does anyone else on the committee. What I need is not theory but a working tool.

So here is my request. Please act as a professional philosopher specializing in moral psychology and applied ethics, and write a decision procedure the committee can actually use — a step-by-step checklist we could realistically run in fifteen to twenty minutes per case during a meeting. Before writing it, briefly brainstorm the different things that can hide behind the words "this is wrong" (considered moral conviction, self-interested preference, in-group bias, status quo bias, discomfort misread as moral objection, and anything else that belongs on the list), and decide which distinctions must survive into the procedure and which are too fine-grained for a committee of teenagers to apply. For each step of the procedure: state the question to ask, say what counts as a sufficient answer, say where the procedure stops and returns a verdict of "preference, not moral claim" or "bias, not moral claim," and mark clearly the points where the question is genuinely one of values and must be handed to a vote or to the faculty advisor rather than settled by the checklist. Be honest about where the procedure will misfire — in particular, how it handles the case where someone's bias and someone's moral conviction point in the same direction, and the case where a bias is sincerely experienced as a moral conviction by the person holding it.

Then run the procedure on three cases from our actual docket: (1) the objection to the cafeteria adding halal options, (2) the objection to a classmate's extended-time accommodation, and (3) a proposal, which I personally support, to stop funding student clubs that don't open membership to everyone — including the religious clubs who say requiring them to accept members who reject their beliefs violates their conscience. Please think carefully and work through the reasoning step by step rather than jumping to conclusions; on the third case especially, tell me honestly whether the procedure supports my position or undermines it. Finally, include a short section on what this procedure cannot do — the kinds of cases where distinguishing preference from bias is not a factual question a checklist could ever settle. Keep the language clear enough that a fifteen-year-old committee member could follow it, but don't dumb down the underlying philosophy; if a concept like the distinction between the content of a judgment and its causal origin matters, teach it to me in a paragraph and then build it in.
