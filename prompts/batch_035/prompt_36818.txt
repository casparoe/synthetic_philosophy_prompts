I am preparing a seminar on the definition of art — institutional theories, historical definitions, formalism, expression theories — and I want to open the semester not with the theories but with a practice, so that the students have something concrete to push against. The practice I have in mind is the art world's treatment of authorship, attribution, and forgery, and I would be grateful for a careful philosophical reconstruction of it.

The case is familiar in outline: a canvas hangs in a museum for years, is admired, studied, written about; its perceptible properties are everything the viewer could want. Then laboratory analysis or archival work establishes that it was painted by Han van Meegeren in the 1940s rather than by Vermeer in the 1660s — and nothing about the canvas changes, yet almost everything else does. The market value collapses, the wall label is rewritten, insurers are notified, and viewers who once stood before it in rapture now report feeling deceived. Curiously, though, the same institutions that treat forgery as a scandal happily display Roman marble copies of lost Greek bronzes, catalogue paintings as "workshop of Rembrandt," and acquire ancient works whose attributions are frankly uncertain — cases in which the work's historical relation to the revered name is also mediated, indirect, or unknown, but where no one feels betrayed. So the practice is not simply "we only show originals." It seems to draw a line, but I cannot see exactly where it is drawn or why.

What I would like is an analysis of what this cluster of practices presupposes about art's definition. Must the collapse of the forgery's status be explained by a theory on which art is essentially historical or intentional — so that a perceptually identical object made under different causal circumstances is simply not the same artwork, or not an artwork at all? Or is the practice compatible with a purely formalist or aesthetic definition plus some independent account of markets, trust, and provenance, so that the betrayal we feel is a betrayal about testimony rather than about art? I would also like you to address whether the practice is internally consistent: can the market's treatment of forgery be reconciled with the museum's comfortable display of copies and workshop products, or does the practice presuppose views that cannot all be held at once? And finally, suppose we adopted a strictly aesthetic definition of art — what in the actual institutions would have to change, if anything? Please respond in exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
