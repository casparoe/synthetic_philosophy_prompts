I'm the convener of a graduate metaphysics reading group that has spent the semester on the dispositions literature. We opened with the simple conditional analysis — the thesis that something is disposed to give manifestation M in stimulus circumstances S just in case, were S to obtain, it would M — and then worked through Martin's famous wire cases from his 1994 paper (the live wire hooked to a device that disconnects it the instant current begins to flow, so that it is disposed to conduct when touched but would not; and the mirror-image dead wire hooked to a device that makes it live the moment anyone touches it, so that it would conduct when touched though it is not so disposed), followed by Lewis's "Finkish Dispositions" (1997) and the surrounding literature on masks, antidotes, and mimickers, where the fragile glass packed in straw and the poison counteracted by an antidote live. For our last meeting of term I want to hand out a classification instrument: a decision tree that takes as input a cold description of a case — an object, a disposition ascribed to it, a stimulus, a manifestation, and whatever else is going on — and sorts it into a category. The diagnostic questions at the branch nodes must be answerable by someone who knows nothing about the literature's terminology: questions like whether the object's underlying basis for the manifestation is actually present, whether the manifestation would occur if the stimulus did, whether whatever interferes operates before or after the manifestation gets underway, and whether it acts on the disposition itself or on the route from stimulus to manifestation. The leaves must include at least the ordinary case in which the conditional analysis holds, the fink, the mirror-image case from Martin's dead wire, the mask or antidote case, and the mimicker case in which something would manifest although it lacks the disposition; add further leaves if the hard cases below demand them.

The tree must be able to handle three cases of my own that the stock taxonomy leaves uneasy, and I want each one explicitly run through the finished tree with the path shown. First, the annealer: a glass with a genuinely fragile molecular structure which, when struck, begins to crack, whereupon a watching demon instantly anneals it back together mid-crack, so that after the strike the glass is intact. Is the disposition finked (removed before manifestation), masked (blocked after onset), or did the manifestation partly occur, and does that partial occurrence matter to the tree? Second, the self-neutralizing stimulus: a poison that reliably induces immediate violent vomiting when swallowed, so that it would never kill anyone, though it is lethal once in the bloodstream. Here the interferer is internal to the chain the stimulus itself sets off — the body's response to the poison — and I cannot tell whether the tree should treat this as a fink, a mask, or something the standard categories simply miss. Third, the overdetermined glass: a genuinely fragile glass that is also targeted by a demon who, at the exact moment of any strike, casts a spell that shatters any object whatsoever. The glass breaks when struck, and it would have broken anyway; the conditional is satisfied, but I have doubts about whether the breaking manifests the fragility, and I want to know which node of the tree catches that doubt.

Beyond the tree itself, I want two further deliverables. First, a short key mapping each root-to-leaf path to its category with a one-sentence characterization, together with a paragraph on the unoccupied paths: which combinations of your diagnostic answers no stock case in the literature fills, and whether each vacancy reflects an incoherent combination, an unattractive one, or merely an unexplored one. Second — and here I want a committed answer, not a survey — a defense of your ordering of the questions. You will have noticed that the two natural roots, "would the manifestation occur if the stimulus obtained?" (a question about counterfactual structure) and "does the basis for the manifestation obtain?" (a question about what the object is actually like), carve the terrain differently, and each choice collapses or cleanly separates a different pair of categories: one ordering may leave the mask and the fink on the same leaf until late, the other may merge the mimicker with the mirror-image wire case. Pick one ordering, argue for it, and then flag honestly any two categories your tree cannot ultimately separate, rather than papering over the collapse with an extra question that merely relabels the problem. I would rather receive a tree with two flagged failures and reasons than a menu of options with no one behind the wheel. Keep it to five to seven branch questions and at least six leaves, and write for readers who have done the reading but never had to organize it.
