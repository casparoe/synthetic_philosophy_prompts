Been going down a rabbit hole on the Kantian thing where lying is categorically forbidden but you're allowed to say something technically true that you know will mislead (the murderer-at-the-door setup). For a talk I'm prepping I want a kind of "deception exchange rate": hold the outcome fixed and ask how bad each *mode* of producing a false belief is, as a fraction of what a barefaced lie would be.

The setup: someone asks where I was last night, and in every case they end up believing I was home all evening when I was actually at the pub. Same false belief, same level of trust betrayed, only the mechanism differs. Please rate these five modes:

(1) the flat lie — I just say I was home all evening
(2) paltering — I volunteer true but cherry-picked facts, like mentioning the TV show I watched when I got in, and never mention the pub
(3) implicature — I say something literally true but crafted so the obvious inference is false, e.g. that I had a quiet night in
(4) standing assumption — they say "so you were home all night like usual?" and I just nod and hand them their coffee
(5) pure omission — I deflect the question entirely, knowing their default assumption does all the work for me

Give me exactly five numbered items, one per mode, each one or two sentences long: state the fraction and why it lands there. No intro or conclusion, just the list. Also, if you think the whole exchange-rate idea is confused — like if you think the outcome being identical means all five should get the same score — say so within the relevant item rather than adding extra commentary.
