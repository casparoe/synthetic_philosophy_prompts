For a graduate seminar on metaontology that I'm co-teaching this term, I've been asked to prepare a diagnostic handout that sorts students by their actual intuitions about verbal disputes and existence questions, rather than by which labels they self-apply. The instrument I want is a decision tree. It should start from a concrete ontological dispute and lead, branch by branch, to a named position on whether such disputes are substantive. I have in mind as test-bed cases the following: (i) the mathematician who says there are infinitely many primes and the nominalist who says there are no numbers at all, only mathematicians writing symbols; (ii) a dispute over whether there really are holes in a block of Swiss cheese or merely perforated cheese; (iii) the question whether there exists an object composed of my left shoe and the Eiffel Tower; and (iv) whether there are chairs in my office, or merely particles arranged chair-wise. The tree should be usable by someone with no background in metaontology, so each node must be phrased as a question about these cases that could be answered without knowing which position it leads to — for instance, whether the two parties to the prime-number dispute would make different predictions about anything observable, or whether a perfectly ordinary translation of one party's sentence into the other's vocabulary would dissolve the disagreement. Please do not include any node of the form "do you believe in deflationism?", since that defeats the purpose of the instrument.

The leaves I'd like to see represented, in whatever placement the logic of the tree dictates: a neo-Carnapian quietism on which external questions are ill-posed outside a linguistic framework; Amie Thomasson's easy ontology, on which many existence questions are settled by trivial inferences from uncontested facts; Eli Hirsch's quantifier variance, on which the parties to certain ontological disputes mean different things by "there is"; Ted Sider's ontological realism about structure, on which some carvings of reality are genuinely privileged and the disputes are substantive; and a revisionist or error-theoretic leaf on which ordinary existence claims are strictly false though useful. If the logical space you construct also contains combinations nobody in fact occupies, list them and say briefly whether you think they are empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. And please flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate — I suspect, for example, that quietism and easy ontology will come out nearly indistinguishable when run on the holes case, but I want the handout to be honest about such failures rather than pretending a node does work it doesn't. As for scale: aim for roughly five to seven internal nodes and one awkward commitment per leaf, stated in a single sentence. The students are sharp but new to the literature, so the tree should trade on their reactions to cases, and the prose around it should be compact enough to fit a two-page handout.
