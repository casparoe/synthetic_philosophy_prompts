I teach an upper-level seminar on contemporary normative theory — advanced undergraduates alongside first-year graduate students — and I am writing the take-home final. I want a single essay question on Scanlon's contractualism and the aggregation problem: specifically, whether the test of reasonable rejectability can tell us what to do when a very large number of individually minor complaints stands against a small number of very serious ones. The question must not be answerable by reciting the textbook statement of the view; it should force students to construct and evaluate a principle rather than apply a label. Please write everything in American English.

Embed the question in a case you invent for this purpose, along the following lines but yours to vary: a town with a one-time public health budget must choose between (A) retrofitting the reservoir, which would spare each of 40,000 residents a single two-day episode of debilitating but temporary nausea, and (B) funding a program that would prevent 30 children from a rare degenerative condition causing lifelong severe mobility loss. Engineer the case so that no individual suffers more than once on either side, the largest complaint on side A is small, and the complaints on side B are enormous but few. Within the question, introduce a fictional principle that the student must evaluate — call it the Cumulative Complaint Principle: a principle is reasonably rejectable if the sum of the strengths of all individual complaints against it exceeds the corresponding sum for any alternative. The student must decide whether this principle preserves the contractualist form of justification while smuggling in utilitarian content, or whether it is precisely the sort of consideration the contractualist test was designed to exclude — and, crucially, whether rejecting it leaves the theory with anything to say about the case at all.

The question should have three parts: (i) a short setup asking the student to state the aggregation problem and why the contractualist test appears not to resolve it, phrased so that a memorized summary earns very little; (ii) the main part, asking for an evaluation of the Cumulative Complaint Principle that distinguishes aggregation within a single pairwise comparison from aggregation across all affected persons, and that takes seriously the principle's best defense — namely, that it is obscure why one person's grave complaint should silence 40,000 modest ones when no individual burden on either side is extreme; and (iii) a closing part asking what Scanlon himself should say, invoking the individualist restriction and Parfit's worry that any threshold view can be destabilized by a chain of intermediate cases, and whether any principled stopping point exists between the view that numbers never matter and the view that only totals matter.

After the question, write a model answer of about 600 words, and then a marking scheme out of 20 marks built around five must-hit points, stating the marks each is worth and adding a one-line note on how students typically miss each one. Close the marking scheme with a short paragraph naming the specific moves that separate an A answer from a B answer — the B answer, for instance, will correctly state the individualist restriction and criticize the invented principle for being utilitarian in disguise, but will not engage the question of what the theory should then say about the case, whereas the A answer must.
