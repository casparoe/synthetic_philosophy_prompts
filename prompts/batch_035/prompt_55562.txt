I'm writing a final essay for a third-year seminar on theories of history, and I've become somewhat preoccupied with Ibn Khaldun. As I understand him, the Muqaddimah, written in the 1370s, holds that polities cohere through asabiyyah — group solidarity or group feeling, forged in harsh conditions at the periphery of civilisation — that dynasties founded on this solidarity gradually squander it as luxury, factionalism and the softness of settled rule set in, and that a dynasty's natural life span is roughly three generations of about forty years each, after which new outsiders with fresh asabiyyah arrive to replace it. Our assignment is to stress-test a pre-modern theory of decline against a case its author could never have known about, and I've settled on the Terminal Classic Maya collapse.

Imagine, then, that we could sit Ibn Khaldun down with the modern archaeological picture: the southern lowland cities — Tikal and Calakmul with their generations of dynastic rivalry, and places like Copán and Palenque — supporting large populations through intensive agriculture; warfare and elite display escalating in the eighth century; a run of severe droughts in the eighth and ninth centuries; dated royal monuments ceasing around 900; the southern cities largely emptied within a few generations, while northern centres such as Chichén Itzá carried on and the Itzá kingdom centred on the lake Petén remained independent until 1697. Which parts of this record would he happily conscript as confirmation of his cycle, and where would the case strain his machinery — given that no fresh wave of hardened outsiders swept in to found new dynasties in the south, and that the population did not vanish but dispersed into communities whose descendants are still there? Would he stretch the theory, revise it, or file the episode under some other category of misfortune altogether?

I'd also like you to run the analysis in the opposite direction, because the seminar convener is keen on that. Many modern scholars object that collapse is the wrong word for what happened — that it was political fragmentation and decentralisation rather than a civilisational fall, and that the drama of the term distorts the record. Would Ibn Khaldun find this objection intelligible, or would he insist that losing dynasties, monuments and cities is precisely what he means by decline, so that the modern rebranding is the real mystification? Please conclude with your own judgement about which framework — his cyclical decay of solidarity or the contemporary transformation-not-collapse framing — actually fits the Maya evidence better, and be candid about where each is silent. Please write your response in American English.
