I have recently joined a regional committee that advises on the allocation of a fixed public health budget, and a familiar fault line has opened within it: whether health gains should be weighted by the severity of the recipient's condition, or whether every unit of health should count the same regardless of whose it is. The severity-weighters on the committee describe their position as prioritarian rather than egalitarian -- they claim to give priority to the worse off without attaching any value to equality itself -- and they attribute this distinction to Parfit. One of the economists accepts their description of the view but thinks it lands them in trouble, and before our next meeting he circulated an argument that I have been unable to shake off. It runs as follows.

(1) A moral theory is unacceptable if it implies that a purely leveling-down change is an improvement in any respect -- that there is anything at all to be said for a change that makes some people worse off and no one better off. (2) Prioritarianism differs from utilitarianism only in counting benefits to the worse off for more. (3) Whatever makes the worse off special must be comparative: what grounds the greater weight of Ben's claim, when he is worse off than Ana, cannot be any intrinsic feature of Ben's condition -- being at 40 rather than 80 units of well-being -- since utilitarianism already cares about that condition, merely at equal weight; so if priority is not grounded in Ben's standing relative to Ana, the extra weight is ungrounded, and prioritarianism is an arbitrary departure from utilitarianism. (4) The weights are therefore grounded in comparative standing, and prioritarian value is sensitive to relative position: it is worse, in one respect, that one person stands lower than another. (5) A disvalue that attaches to one person's standing lower than another attaches however the difference in standing is reduced, including by leveling the higher person down. (6) Prioritarianism therefore implies that a purely leveling-down change is an improvement in one respect, and by (1) it is refuted. (7) It follows that there is no stable position between counting everyone's health at equal weight and a covert egalitarianism that the leveling-down objection destroys; the severity-weighters are, on his view, refuted egalitarians who have not yet noticed.

The conclusion seems to me to prove too much, yet whenever I go through the steps I find myself accepting them one by one. If forced to choose, I would far rather keep both (1) and (2) than follow him to (7). Which premise or inference should I reject, why did it look fine when I first accepted it, and what does rejecting it cost -- in particular, what would the severity-weighted policy we are debating have to give up? In your answer, please separate what is settled among philosophers who work on distributive ethics from what is genuinely contested among them; if the question of what prioritarian weights are grounded in is itself disputed, or if the standing of the leveling-down objection is less settled than my economist colleague assumes, say so explicitly rather than presenting one reading as the received view.
