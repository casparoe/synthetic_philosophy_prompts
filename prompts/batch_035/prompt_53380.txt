Every few years I teach Bell's theorem in my philosophy of physics course, and every time I get sucked back into the same argument about what the theorem actually rules out, and I've come to realize the arguement hasn't moved anywhere in basically forty years. One side -- the Bohmians and fellow travellers -- says Bell proved that nature itself is nonlocal: the correlations can't be explained without a real physical connection between the two wings, so some theory has to build it in (pilot wave, GRW flashes, whatever your poison), and relativity's ban on faster-than-light influences has to be re-read as a claim about signalling or about a preferred frame we happen not to be able to detect. The other side says not so fast: what Bell's derivation actually used was a factorizability condition, and once Jarrett and Shimony in the 80s split that condition into parameter independence and outcome independence, it became clear that the quantum statistics only violate the outcome half, which never allows anyone to signal -- so nothing needs to travel anywhere, and locality survives as no-signalling. And then everyone digs in. The Bohmian crowd points out that the theory they actually believe violates parameter independence outright, so the "outcome independence is the harmless part" line just doesn't describe any theory they'd accept; the no-signalling crowd replies that a "real influence" you can't use, can't detect, and can't exclude by any experiment is a metaphysical extra, and there the thing sits for another decade.

I've tryed out a hypothesis on my departmental reading group and want a second opinion on it: that this is really a disguised disagreement about what an explanation of the correlations has to be. One side demands a common-cause-style account in which each outcome is fixed by local physical states, and when factorization fails they hear "nonlocality"; the other side has quietly relaxed to a different standard, on which it's enough that no controllable influence exists, and so they hear the same theorem as a result about explanation rather than about space. Is that diagnosis right? Or is there a genuine formal or factual question hiding in here -- for instance whether outcome independence ever fails outside the quantum case, or whether a genuinely relativistic theory is even compatible with violations of parameter independence? And given whatever diagnosis you land on, tell me honestly what would count as progress here: a sharpened distinction, a new theorem, an experiment, or nothing at all -- and if nothing, explain why forty years of standing still shouldn't just be read as evidence that we're fighting about the word "local" while calling it physics.
