I've been in a long-running argument with two friends from my effective altruism reading group about what consequentialism can honestly demand of us when we can't trace the effects of our actions. One of them holds a roughly Lenman-style position: since the consequences of any act ripple indefinitely into the far future, and we are utterly ignorant of those ripples, we can never know which act maximizes the good, so consequentialism fails as a practical guide — our longtermist ambitions especially, since the farther out the effects go, the more completely clueless we become. My other friend takes the optimist line: she leans on the thought (which she attributes to G.E. Moore, and which I've always found a bit too confident) that we can often know we've made things better on net even without a full causal inventory, and she also invokes Hilary Greaves's distinction between "simple" cluelessness, where our ignorance is unsystematic and symmetry considerations arguably let the unknown effects wash out, and "complex" cluelessness, where we have identifiable but unquantifiable reasons pointing in opposite directions. She thinks most everyday cases are simple, that the wash-out reasoning handles them, and that the residue of complex cases is just an epistemic limitation any moral theory would face. My first friend replies that longtermist cases are precisely the complex ones: we have concrete reasons to think, say, that speeding technological development both raises extinction risk and builds the capacity to avert it, and nothing about symmetry tells us how to weigh those against each other.

Here is my own tentative take, which I'd like the essay to engage rather than merely mention. I suspect my second friend is right that cluelessness, properly described, is not an objection to consequentialism in particular: her rule-deontologist will also be clueless about whether a lie's knock-on effects violate the rule she's actually licensed to follow, so the ignorance is a general feature of agency under long causal chains, not a special tax on thinking about consequences. But I also suspect this "everyone's clueless, so it's a wash" move is too quick, because consequentialism is unique in claiming that the rightness of an act is exhausted by a comparison among acts, and if the comparison is impossible, the theory may fail in its own terms in a way competitors don't. It feels to me as though the real issue might be about decision theory rather than ethics: perhaps what we need is a way of acting under an imprecise collection of probability functions rather than a single one, and some of the disagreement about cluelessness is really a proxy fight about whether rational choice requires sharp probabilities at all.

Write an essay on whether cluelessness is a decisive objection to consequentialism. Weigh the pessimist's argument from indefinite ripples, the optimist's symmetry and wash-out responses, and the question of whether non-consequentialist theories really do enjoy the immunity from cluelessness that their defenders claim. Engage my two hypotheses above: the "general condition of agency" diagnosis and the "imprecise probabilities" reframing. You're welcome to endorse one, amend it, or reject both, but don't end on a non-committal note — defend a verdict, even if it's a conditional one (e.g., that cluelessness defeats only certain versions of consequentialism, or that it defeats none of them but undercuts particular longtermist applications, or that it is a genuine objection but only because of decision-theoretic facts). Please justify whatever conclusion you reach.
