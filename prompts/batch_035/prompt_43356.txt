In my Buddhist study group the teacher explains anatta with the chariot dialogue from the Milindapañha: the monk Nāgasena tells King Milinda that Nāgasena is merely a convenient designation for the five aggregates, just as a chariot is merely a convenient designation for pole, axle, wheels, and yoke — no one part is the chariot, and there is no chariot over and above the parts, and yet we go on using the word without error. She concludes that the no-self doctrine is a mild claim: the I is a convention for a bundle, so ordinary person-talk is fine and there is nothing existentially threatening here. A visiting philosophy student objected that the analogy buys that conclusion too cheaply: the tradition's denial is also meant to exclude any persisting subject behind the stream of moments, and a chariot — which endures for years, can be repaired part by part, and, unlike the aggregates, has an owner — cannot model that at all. Audit the analogy for me. Set out what maps onto what; sort the disanalogies into those that matter for the teacher's use and those that do not; and say whether her reassuring conclusion survives once they are on the table, or whether the chariot was carrying it the whole way — and, if the analogy fails, whether some other comparison would do the job better.
