I've been thinking about the paradox of fiction, and a variant of it that seems nastier than the standard one. Classically the tension is that we pity Anna Karenina while believing no such person exists. But consider grief for someone you beleived was real and turns out to be fictional in a very specific way: a woman corresponds with her adult son for five years -- actually a fabricated persona run by scammers, maintained through deepfake video calls -- and grieves when the persona "dies" in a car crash. Years later she learns there was never anyone there. Standard resolutions of the paradox lean on the audience knowing all along it's fiction, so the make-believe machinery (Walton's quasi-emotions and all that) can do its work. But she never knew. Her belief was always that he existed, exactly like ordinary grief. So what's your credence that her grief was a full-blooded emotion, in every sense that matters, rather than something somehow lesser?

Give me a number and the decomposition: roughly, does genuine emotion require its object to actually exist; does it require belief in the object, or only a representation of one; and does the later discovery retroactively downgrade the grief, or merely change how we describe it after the fact? Assign a rough probability to each with a line of justification, then say how they combine into your final credence. Tell me which sub-question the number is most sensitive to, and what argument or consideration would actually push you across to the other side of 0.5 -- I want to know where your reasoning is most likely to fail, not just where it lands.
