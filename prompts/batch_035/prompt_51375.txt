I'm a retired engineer who reads a lot of popular archaeology, with no training in the field, and something keeps bothering me about it: the confidence. A book will tell me that grave goods prove a belief in an afterlife, or that a pattern of burnt grain stores proves ritual feasting rather than an accident of preservation, and I can never tell which links in those chains of inference are solid and which are guesses dressed up. Help me build a thought experiment that makes the problem vivid.

Here is the setup I'll fix: archaeologists of some far-future civilization excavate our society, and our written records simply haven't survived. They recover a specific assemblage — a suburban cemetery of uniform headstones with repeated floral traces; small white objects with cords, one bundle in nearly every home, each paired with a smooth-faced slab whose function resists analysis; standardized buildings full of exercise equipment; a landfill overwhelmingly composed of glass-faced devices. Build on this to the following specifications. Construct two rival reconstructions of what we believed and how we were organized — say, something about death, and something about whether property belonged to households or to a larger unit — where both reconstructions are reached by methods archaeologists genuinely use (analogy with their own society, uniformity assumptions, patterns of wear and distribution, context), and neither is a strawman or an ancient-aliens fantasy. The disagreement must fall out of legitimate inference from the same objects, not be stipulated. Then tell me which single auxiliary assumption the two readings turn on, what happens to the disagreement if the future archaeologists drop it, and how a defender of the losing reading would push back rather than concede. The case is a tool for me to understand how objects get turned into beliefs and institutions, so it fails if one side of it is made sloppy on purpose. And as you go, please keep a clear line between what is genuinely settled among archaeologists about such inferences and what is contested — for instance, how much burial practice can really tell you about belief — and don't hand me a contested assumption as though it were the profession's consensus.
