I've been keeping a list of cases where I have a clear gut sense of whether someone was okay asserting something, and I want to know if there's one rule behind all of them or if I'm just making it up as I go. My doctor telling me a medication is safe when she's about 90% sure it is: fine, honestly I'd be annoyed if she hedged more than that. A friend telling me 'the door's locked, let's go' when he's 90% sure he locked it: also fine, but I can't tell if it's fine for the same reason as the doctor case or a totally different one. Me telling a coworker 'the boss approved it' when he strongly signaled approval in a hallway chat but never actually said yes: not fine, I should've said 'I'm pretty sure he's on board.' Same credence as the doctor, opposite verdict. A weather app saying 'rain tomorrow' at 80%: fine. A pilot saying 'the runway is clear' at 80%: obviously not fine. And there's one I keep flip-flopping on: telling someone 'the coin landed heads' because I watched the video of the toss, when the toss happened last week and my knowledge of the past is entirely downstream of a reliable recording. Certainty-wise it feels airtight, but something about it feels off, like I'm entitled to the assertion from the wrong direction somehow. Is the knowledge norm of assertion actually what's doing the work in my verdicts, or is it some context-and-stakes thing dressed up as knowledge? Give me a principle, or maybe two competing candidates, tell me which of my verdicts the best one can't capture, and say what it predicts about a case I haven't listed -- like whether it's okay to assert 'the meeting is at 5' in a message you're sending precisely to make the meeting be at 5.
