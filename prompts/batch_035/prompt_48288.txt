My sister's a neurology resident and she cooked up an argument over Thanksgiving break that I still can't get out of my head. It's about pain asymbolia -- the condition where patients (usually after damage around the cingulate cortex, or as a side effect of certain lobotomies back in the day) seem to truely feel pain but are completely unbothered by it. They'll tell you the pinprick hurts, they can localize it and rate how intense it is, they just don't mind. They don't withdraw, don't ask you to stop, sometimes they laugh while describing it. Her argument:

(1) Asymbolics feel genuine pain -- the reports, the localization, the intensity ratings all line up with how pain normally presents.

(2) If pain is intrinsically bad -- bad just in virtue of how it feels -- then whenever anyone feels pain, something bad is happening to them, regardless of their attitude toward it.

(3) So if pain is intrinsically bad, something bad is happening to asymbolics when they get pinched.

(4) But nothing bad is happening to them: no distress, no frustrated desire, no downstream injury from the pinch itself, and they would give up nothing at all to make the sensation stop.

(5) Therefore pain is not bad in virtue of how it feels.

(6) What asymbolia removes is the aversive reaction -- the minding, the wanting-it-gone -- so that reaction, not the sensation, must be what makes ordinary pain bad.

Here's the part that seems to prove too much. Push (6) forward and you get: a tranquilizer that switched off all distress while leaving every sensation fully intact would make any amount of pain harmless. Chronic pain, burn injuries, torture, whatever -- as long as nobody minds, nothing bad is happening. She actually bites that bullet, which honestly scares me a little.

She also thinks the morphine cases clinch it -- patients on opioids sometimes report that the pain is still fully there, same intensity, but they just don't care about it anymore -- and if those patients have the entire feel of pain plus total serenity, then it looks like the sensation really is harmless on its own. And to preempt me she says: don't tell me asymbolics are just missing part of the feeling, like the felt badness itself got subtracted and what they call pain is pain in name only -- because if the morphine patients have the whole sensation intact and still aren't harmed, that move doesn't save anything anyway.

So which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? For what it's worth I'd most like to keep (1), since it's the actual clinical data and everything else in the argument is armchair. But I'm not married to it. And if the whole thing is sound and I just have to bite the tranquilizer bullet, tell me that too -- I just want to know exactly what I'd be signing up for.
