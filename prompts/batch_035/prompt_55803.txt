I've spent my career in physics and have lately joined a philosophy reading group that works through topics in philosophy of science. Last week's session was on Gödel's rotating-universe solutions to Einstein's field equations, and two members got into a dispute I couldn't referee. Marta, who teaches philosophy, began: in Gödel's spacetime there are closed timelike curves, so no observer's worldline can serve as a cosmic time axis, and there is no objective fact of the world lapsing from an unsettled future into a fixed past; since relativity's own equations allow such worlds, and no possible observation could settle whether our universe is one of them at sufficiently large scales, the passage of time cannot be an objective feature of physical reality at all — which, she said, is precisely Gödel's own Kantian conclusion in the essay he contributed to the 1949 Einstein festschrift. Bill, a retired astrophysicist, objected that this is a non sequitur: exact solutions are a dime a dozen, most describe nothing actual, and a permitted model tells you nothing about our world, which manifestly contains no time machines. Marta replied that the argument turns not on our world being Gödel's model but on in-principle undecidability: she noted that in drafts published after his death Gödel even conceded that a perpetually expanding universe would contain no closed curves, and answered that whether the expansion lasts forever is itself undecidable by observation, so objective passage fails the only test a physical fact could meet. Bill's last word was that undecidable is not the same as unreal, that this verificationism would render subjective every fact beyond our light cone, and that relativity had already abolished the global present without Gödel's help. Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: who is right about which specific point, and is there anything both of them are missing?
