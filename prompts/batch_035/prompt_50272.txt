A guy in my political philosophy reading group thinks he's found an argument that kills luck egalitarianism, and honestly each step sounds fine to me, but the conclusion seems way stronger then the premises could possibly license. It goes:

(1) It's unjust when someone is worse off than others through no choice of their own.
(2) Resource egalitarians measure how well off you are by what you have: money and property, sure, but also internal things like health and talents, since those are things you have too.
(3) So someone born with a body that will never walk has a resource deficit they didn't choose, and justice requires correcting it.
(4) But the only corrections available are external ones — money, equipment, carers. No amount of external stuff gives anyone the internal resource of an able body.
(5) So the view faces a dilemna: either it claims the deficit is corrected once enough external stuff has been handed over (which is absurd — the person still can't walk, so what exactly got corrected?), or it admits that some unchosen inequalities are never corrected, which flatly gives up (1).
(6) And notice this isn't special to resources. No external stuff makes a permanently melancholy person cheerful, so the same move takes out welfare; no external stuff gives anyone the functioning of walking, so it takes out capabilities too. Every version of "equality of X," where X can fall short just because of the body you were born with, either denies (1) or pretends otherwise.

So he concludes luck egalitarianism is dead across the board. Here's my problem: when I tried to defend the resources view, I caught myself saying something like "well, on the insurance idea the disabled person is owed what they would have insured for, not full correction," and he said that just IS the second horn of (5), so I was defending a corpse. I'd rather keep (1) than give up on egalitarianism entirely, but I can't see which step to reject, why it looked fine to me in the first place, or what rejecting it costs. And one more thing that bugs me: if (6) is right that the dilemma hits every metric equally, does that actually support his conclusion, or is the fact that it generalizes so smoothly a sign some earlier premise is doing all the work under cover?
