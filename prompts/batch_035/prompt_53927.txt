Write me a satirical FAQ page, roughly 600–700 words, in the flat, eager-to-help register of an official EU institutional website. The conceit: a fictional Directorate-General for the Recovery of Misplaced Sovereignty — call it DG RECOVER — which exists because member states' governments keep writing in to ask where their sovereignty has gone, and the FAQ is there to help them locate it. I've been arguing with friends about the EU's democratic deficit for years and I've become convinced the funniest version of the point is the one where nobody in the document realizes there is a point. So keep it deadpan throughout: no winking, no exclamation points of self-awareness, just the anodyne helpfulness of a website that really believes it is solving your problem.

The humor has to come from getting the actual substance of the sovereignty debates right, so that a scholar of EU integration nods rather than winces. Some material you might use, bent but not invented: pooled sovereignty explained in the tone of a left-luggage office — you keep full use of it, only several other people are also using it, and it is worth more now that everyone is using it together; a section on opt-outs written like a ticketing policy for an attraction you may decline to enter while still being inside; the ever-closer-union commitment handled as a subscription whose cancellation procedure exists, is technically available, and takes several years during which you remain subscribed; subsidiarity as a lost-property desk that is only open when the item in question isn't actually lost; and a Q&A about the democratic deficit that reassures the reader the deficit was solved by an institution they did not elect, whose decision they may appeal to a court that outranks the one they thought was theirs. Include at least one exchange where the official answer quietly contradicts itself within two sentences and neither sentence notices. And include a testimonial box with a short quote from a satisfied member state — invent the state's minister, and let the quote be a government speaking in the first person singular about something it did collectively.

Two constraints on the jokes. First, every gag should be an exaggeration of a real feature of how the EU actually works or how its defenders actually talk about it — garden-variety Eurocrat-bashing will make the piece worse, and I'll notice. Second, the piece should remain legible as satire to a reader who knows nothing about the EU beyond the headlines, without any footnote or aside explaining the joke; the framing and the officialese should carry the irony on their own. End with a contact block in the style of a real institutional footer — a citizens' enquiry line, response times, and an address in Brussels — in which one last small, quiet joke is buried.
