I'm a litigator of about twenty years, mostly commercial work but with a deep sideline in professional-responsibility defense. I am fully fluent in the vocabulary of that world: bright-line rules versus balancing tests, per se prohibitions, scienter ladders from "knowing" down to "should have known," rules of construction in the rules of professional conduct themselves (the difference between "shall not" and "shall not except where," and what it takes for an exception to swallow a rule), the distinction between a rule violated, a rule waived, and a rule that never applied, the way discipline is administered and rationalized by bar authorities, and the standing debate between reading a provision by its text and reading it by its purpose. What I do not have is any training in academic philosophy — one survey course in college, decades ago.

From a talk I attended (and some asking around afterward), here is the puzzle as I understand it, stated plainly so you need not rely on my garbling it. Some moral rules present themselves as constraints rather than goals: it is impermissible for me to kill one innocent person, full stop, even to prevent five other killings of innocents that other people will then go on to commit. The paradox arises because the deontologist — the person who holds such constraints — insists on honoring the constraint in that very case, even though honoring it means that more of the very conduct the constraint prohibits will occur. The constraint seems to defeat its own point. I'm told there is a literature about how this is not simple incoherence, and I want to understand it in terms I actually command.

Restate the deontologist's position in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Treat the following as things needing translation: the constraint itself; its claimed bindingness in the single case; the fact that it seems indexed to me — that it is my killing one that is forbidden, while the five killings by others, however much regretted, are not mine to prevent at that price; and whatever the deontologist says about why following the rule despite its self-defeating consequences in this instance is not, as it would be for a lawyer advising a client, "a rule rightly applied producing an unjust result."

Then give me the residue report, in three parts: what maps cleanly onto something a lawyer would recognize (an absolute privilege, a per se rule, a rule whose violation is not cured by good consequences downstream); what can be said in my vocabulary only by distorting the original claim; and what cannot be said in my vocabulary at all. One warning, and I want it taken seriously: my world has a standing temptation that you must resist. Our rules are almost always justified systemically — administrability, predictability, the health of the institution — and we tolerate hard cases on that basis. If you find yourself translating the deontologist's constraint by giving it that kind of justification, you have not translated anything; you have quietly converted the position into one the deontologist explicitly rejects. Flag any such moment as residue rather than letting it pass as a successful rendering.

Close with your verdict: is the residue a defect in my vocabulary — something the legal way of talking about rules simply cannot express, and if so what — or does it show that the deontologist's constraint was covertly systemic all along, and that the paradox dissolves once that is noticed? I want your honest view, including the view that both of those framings are too tidy.
