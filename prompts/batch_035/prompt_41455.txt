I've been chewing on safe Pareto improvements (SPIs) for a bit and have rough notes for an essay, but I'm honestly not sure which parts of it survive contact with someone who knows the literature. Would you look over the sketch below and tell me (a) where the arguments actually work, (b) where I'm attacking a strawman or missing an obvious reply, and (c) which of my three worries is the strongest and which one I should just cut?

Quick setup for the essay: an SPI is a deal that two (or more) parties can sign *before* they play some costly game, where the deal is guaranteed to leave everyone at least as well off as playing the game would, no matter what their actual preferences turn out to be. The classic picture: two countries heading toward a war over some territory. Neither knows exactly how the war would go, both think it could go badly. So they agree in advance: instead of fighting, flip a coin -- heads you get the territory, tails I get it. Whatever else you want, you'd rather have a 50% shot at the territory than a fight you might lose anyway, so the deal is a Pareto improvement for each of you given your own beliefs, and crucially you never had to compare your welfare to mine or even state your full utility function. There's a version where you both delegate to a third party (or an AI) that gets to pick among your strategies. And there's a cousin idea, "surrogate goals," where you pre-commit to caring a lot about something cheap (some worthless token), so that other players can threaten you over the token and you can safely concede it, which makes threats against your real interests less attractive to make in the first place.

My thesis is supposed to be: SPIs are real but oversold, because they quietly depend on *not everybody using them*. Three worries, in decreasing order of how confident I feel:

1. **Surrogate goal inflation.** The surrogate goal trick works when you're the only one (or one of few) doing it -- threats against your token are cheap to concede, so would-be threateners get deterred and you never pay. But imagine it becomes a standard convention, everyone adopts token goals, everyone knows everyone has them. Now threatening tokens is profitable again (they're the standard thing to threaten, concessions get made all the time), the tokens start carrying real expected cost, and the Pareto gain evaporates. The mechanism eats itself at scale. Kind of like how insurance works great when only some people buy it but the analogy probably breaks somewhere and I haven't pinned down where.

2. **Tail risk / ruin.** The coin flip is better in expectation than the war-risk lottery only if you're a clean expected-utility maximizer. A player who is ruin-sensitive (the territory is their capital, they literally cannot function without it) can rationally refuse the coin flip even while preferring to avoid war, because the coin flip concentrates all the downside in one branch. So the "everyone is better off, no matter their preferences" claim is only as strong as the EU assumptions behind it, and the essay wants to say the SPI literature undersells this.

3. **SPIs neutralize threats but don't divide surplus.** In a pure bargaining problem (how do we split the dollar), SPI machinery doesn't tell you anything about the split; it just removes some of the credible threats you might've used to get a better split. So fans of SPIs sometimes make it sound like a general solution to conflict when it's really just threat-reduction, and removing threats might even change the bargaining equilibrium in ways that aren't Pareto improvements over the original game at all.

So: which of these is actually a problem for the SPI idea rather than a problem for a naive gloss on it? Is the inflation argument in (1) even coherent, or does the formal setup somehow already handle universal adoption? And is there an obvious fourth thing I should be worried about instead? Be blunt, I'd rather cut the weak parts now than after I've written the full thing.
