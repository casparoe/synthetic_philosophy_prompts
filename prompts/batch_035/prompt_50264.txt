My flatmate Theo and our friend Wren got into it for like an hour after a seminar on Zeno, and they roped me in as referee, except I genuinely couldn't call it. It started with Thomson's lamp: the one where you press the button at half a minute, then at three-quarters, then at seven-eighths, and so on, each press toggling the lamp, and then you ask whether it's on or off at the one-minute mark. Theo's opening move was that the lamp is neither on nor off at one minute, which is a contradiction, so completing infinitely many tasks in finite time is flat-out incoherent. Wren answered with something she said comes from a guy named Benacerraf: the story just doesn't say what happens at one minute, and "the lamp is on at one minute" and "the lamp is off at one minute" are each perfectly consistent with everything described, so you have underdetermination, not contradiction.

Then Theo, who refuses to lose, invented a variation he thought would break her. Same schedule of presses, but the button doesn't toggle: each press just halves the lamp's brightness. The lamp starts at full brightness, and since it gets dimmer and dimmer by halving, he admitted the brightness converges to zero, so at one minute the lamp is dark, determinately — his point was that no press ever turns the lamp off. At every single moment before one minute the lamp is on, however faintly, and then at one minute it's dark, with no last press, no event that did it. A machine changing state with nothing causing the change is impossible, he said, so the paradox is still there, it just moved house. Wren shot back that this is just what limits do: a sequence of positive numbers converging to zero never contains zero, and nobody thinks that's spooky — the lamp's value at one minute doesn't need to be inherited from any earlier moment any more than the runner in Zeno's dichotomy needs some particular step to land exactly on the finish line. She went further and said the dimmer case actually vindicates supertasks: every graded quantity in it (brightness, total energy delivered, how far the finger travels) has a perfectly good limit, and on/off was only ever a discrete yes-no sequence that doesn't converge — Thomson's trick, she said, was to pretend a toggle that never settles should inherit some "last" value when the whole setup guarantees there isn't one. Theo's last word was that you can't just help yourself to the limit state as a real state of a physical lamp: it was never in that state before, and nothing put it there, and re-describing the process with nicer math doesn't conjure up an event.
