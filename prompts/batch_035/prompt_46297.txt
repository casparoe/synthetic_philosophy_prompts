I have a set of verdicts about when a referendum's result ought to bind, and I suspect they don't all hang together, but I can't see which one has to go. Here they are.

Switzerland's optional referendums, where a modest number of signatures puts a parliamentary law to a national vote and a bare majority, sometimes on a turnout in the forties, can strike it down: legitimate. Parliament can legislate again, and often does. Brexit: a bare 52-48 on a question of enormous constitutional consequence, with no supermajority requirement, formally advisory but politically impossible to ignore: not legitimate. California's initiative process, which amends the state constitution by simple majority, including propositions that mandate spending without specifying where the money comes from: not legitimate. The Irish and Danish pattern, where a referendum defeats a treaty, elites extract some clarifications and guarantees, and substantially the same question is put again and passes on the second attempt: not legitimate, though I can't fully articulate why, since the people were allowed to keep saying no. The Swiss minaret ban, passed by a solid majority through a procedurally impeccable process while restricting the religious freedom of a small minority: I find myself wanting to say both that the procedure was fine and the outcome was wrong, and that a majority vote on minority rights cannot be legitimate no matter how clean the procedure — and I can't hold both. Finally, a case I keep going back and forth on: a mandatory referendum on joining a confederation, turnout of 41 percent, the result 91 to 9 in favor.

My tentative principle is something like this: a referendum's result ought to bind to the extent that the magnitude and irreversibility of the decision match the robustness of the procedure — simple majorities suffice for reversible decisions, irreversible ones demand supermajorities, and some questions, minority rights chief among them, are off the table entirely. But I suspect this principle either gets the minaret case wrong or collapses into pure outcome-based judgment, and it is silent on why asking the same question a second time seems to cheapen the second answer.
