I hold a broadly Schopenhauerian view about music, and I would like a serious map of its exposure. The view, as I've come to hold it: the other arts operate through representation — they present objects, scenes, or Ideas mediated by concepts and images — while music alone is structurally parallel to the inner nature of the world itself, to willing in its general forms: tension and release, striving and satisfaction, suspension and resolution, arrest and resumption. On this view, a cadence doesn't depict an emotion the way a painting depicts grief; it presents the bare form of striving-then-cessation, which is why music is emotionally intelligible across situations that have no content in common. I've absorbed the standard qualifications: that music expresses the universal forms of feeling rather than particular emotions, that particular musical works are like "universal language" spoken in a particular accent, and so on. My worry is that after enough qualifications of this kind the view may no longer risk anything.

Tell me what would count against it. I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they would be, and I want them drawn from several directions at once. First, aesthetic facts: what should I make of program music, of musical imitation (the cuckoo, the battle symphony), and of the fact that a great deal of modern music — serial, drone-based, some minimalism — seems engineered to defeat exactly the striving-satisfaction schema the view says is music's essence? Does a musical tradition that largely abandons tension and resolution count against the view, or is that an accidental feature of Western tonality that a defender can shed without loss? Second, empirical developments since Schopenhauer: Pinker's suggestion that music is evolutionary byproduct — "auditory cheesecake" — and related adaptive accounts (sexual selection, mother-infant bonding, social coordination); Leonard Meyer's expectation-based account, on which musical emotion arises from the arousal, delay, and violation of learned stylistic expectations, which would make music's expressivity a cognitive mechanism rather than a metaphysical channel; and the cross-cultural and animal evidence, from societies whose music lacks functional harmony to songbirds and humpback whales, which seem to produce structured sound with the same tension-resolution grammar without anyone supposing they are objectifying the Will. Third, the internal question: is there any possible observation that the qualified view forbids? If every apparent counterexample — imitative music, atonality, culture-bound listening — can be reclassified as inessential to music's true nature, has the view been insulated into unfalsifiability, and for a claim of this metaphysical kind, is that fatal or acceptable? I'd like you to distinguish what a committed Schopenhauerian would concede as counting against the position from what they would dismiss, and to give an honest verdict on whether the core claim — that music's connection to the emotions is metaphysically deep rather than psychologically or culturally mediated — is genuinely at risk from any of this, or whether it sits too far above the evidence to be touched by it.
