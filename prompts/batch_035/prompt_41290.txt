I am drafting an essay on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem for a philosophy of economics workshop, and I would be grateful for your comments on the notes below. To fix terms, and to ensure we share the same picture: the theorem says, roughly, that if (1) each individual's preferences over lotteries on social states satisfy the expected-utility axioms, so that each person has a von Neumann–Morgenstern utility function; (2) society's preferences over lotteries on those same states also satisfy the expected-utility axioms; and (3) society's preferences respect Pareto indifference (if everyone is indifferent between two lotteries, so is society), then society's utility function must be a weighted sum of the individuals' utilities. The theorem thus appears to derive utilitarian aggregation — summing utilities across persons — from thin rationality assumptions alone.

My essay's central claim is that the theorem does not derive utilitarianism but merely permits it, because the derivation has the structure of a gauge theory. The argument as it stands:

1. Each individual's vNM utility is fixed only up to a positive affine transformation: individual preferences determine utility ratios within a life, but neither the unit nor the origin of anyone's scale.
2. The theorem delivers a weighted sum, but which assignments of weights count as "equal" depends on how each person's scale has been normalized. Equal weights on 0–1 normalized utilities embed, rather than establish, a substantive assumption of interpersonal comparability: treating each person's best and worst outcomes as the endpoints of a common scale is exactly what the utilitarian needs and exactly what the individual data cannot supply.
3. The social ordering over lotteries is invariant under shifts of any individual's origin, but the sum is not: adding a constant to one person's utility changes social welfare as calculated while changing nothing in anyone's preferences or in the Pareto facts. The sum representation therefore carries surplus structure — a "zero problem" — over and above what the theorem's premises determine.
4. The upshot: the move from the theorem to utilitarianism is a gauge-fixing, a convention akin to the choice of units in a physical theory. The invariant content of the theorem is only that social evaluation is Pareto-compatible and additively separable across persons; that everyone's utility enters with the same coefficient is a stipulation, and stipulations do no argumentative work.

I develop two further points on which I especially want your judgment. First, I reply to the anticipated objection that Harsanyi's later appeal to empathetic identification — an ideal observer who imaginatively occupies each person's situation and thereby extends her preferences — supplies the missing comparability. My reply is that identification transfers the observer's scale onto each person's outcomes, which at best fixes ratios within a life and leaves the interpersonal unit free, so the gauge freedom survives the ideal observer. Second, I close by arguing that a gauge-fixing is not ipso facto empty: the right follow-up question is which convention is natural, in the way that choices of units in physics are guided but not dictated by theory. I gesture at a candidate natural gauge — calibrating interpersonal units against choice dispositions people actually display under shared risks, such as insurance behavior and willingness to pay for safety — but I have not worked this out, and I suspect it may collapse back into ordinary empirical welfare economics.

Please comment on the following, in whatever order is convenient:

- Accuracy. Are the formal claims in points 1 through 3 correct as stated, and is my statement of the theorem itself faithful? In particular, have I misdescribed the invariance claims in points 1 and 3?
- Distinctiveness. Is the gauge-theory framing more than a restatement, in new vocabulary, of the familiar objection that Harsanyi's theorem presupposes interpersonal comparability of utility? If it is merely a restatement, should I say so outright, or is there residual value in the reformulation?
- The proposal. Is the "natural gauge" closing worth developing, or should I cut it and let the essay end at point 4? If it is worth developing, what would the argument have to show for the appeal to shared-risk choice dispositions to be more than an appeal to the status quo of applied welfare economics?
- Placement. One reader suggested I connect the essay to Peter Diamond's objection that expected-utility social evaluation is indifferent to how chances are distributed across persons. I currently treat my point and Diamond's as orthogonal — his concerns the social level's treatment of risk, mine the calibration of individual scales. Is that right, or are they secretly the same complaint?

Please write your comments in American English, and feel free to criticize any part of the notes, including the framing of the question I am asking.
