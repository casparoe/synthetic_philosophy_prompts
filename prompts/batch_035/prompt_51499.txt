My friend and I got into a whole arguement last night about whether you can ever trust your own senses, and at some point he pulled out this thing he'd picked up from a philosophy podcast, the "bootstrapping problem," and summarized it for me like this: you can't check whether a belief-source is reliable by using that very source, because that's circular — a witness can't vouch for himself, a thermometer can't confirm its own readings. So if you want to know your thermometer is accurate, you have to test it against some other instrument, and the same goes for your eyes: perception can't certify perception, since that would just be the source vouching for itself in one step. And he says this is what sinks reliabilism: relibilism says knowledge is just true belief produced by a reliable process, but if you can never establish that a process is reliable without already trusting it, then the reliabilist can't explain how we know anything at all, and he says that's basically why the reliabilist program collapsed decades ago.

Some of this felt right to me in the moment — the thermometer bit especially — but a couple of things seemed off. The claim that the whole program collapsed doesn't match the fact that people still argue about reliabilism constantly, and the "witness vouching for himself" framing felt almost too easy, like it made the whole thing sound like an obvious fallacy, which makes me wonder why it'd be famous. Can you go through his summary piece by piece and tell me what an epistemologist who actually works on this stuff would accept, what's a simplification, and what's just wrong? And most importantly (this is what we actually fought about): does his conclusion follow from the parts that are right?
