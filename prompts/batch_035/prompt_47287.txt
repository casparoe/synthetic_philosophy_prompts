A chapter in my philosophy of language course reader, a popular introduction to pragmatics, summarises Grice roughly as follows: "Grice's central idea was that speaker meaning is just sentence meaning plus inference. When I tell a stranded motorist 'There's a garage just up the road', the sentence contributes only its literal content; everything else — that the garage is open, that it sells petrol — is a guess the hearer makes on his own initiative. The Cooperative Principle and its maxims amount, in practice, to 'be relevant', and they license these guesses. Because implicatures are only guesses, a speaker is never strictly responsible for them, which is also why they are cancellable: one can append 'though it's probably shut' without contradiction. So what a speaker means never exceeds the conventional meaning of the words plus the hearer's fallible guesswork."

I can't tell whether this is a legitimate simplification or subtly misleading. The cancellability observation sounded right when I first read it, but the claim that speakers bear no responsibility for what they implicate seems to conflict with how readily we blame people for insinuations, so I don't know how much of the chapter to trust. Please audit the summary part by part: which claims would a Gricean accept as they stand, which are distortions, and does the concluding sentence follow from whatever parts of the summary survive?
