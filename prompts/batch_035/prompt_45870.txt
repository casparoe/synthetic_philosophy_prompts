After a career in chemical process safety, I have recently found myself reading philosophy of mind and, to my surprise, the literature on AI safety. I now serve on a small review panel at a public-sector organisation that deploys language-model assistants, and I have noticed that the field speaks of alignment and control as if they were clearly distinct. My understanding is that a control approach does not ask whether the model is well-behaved but what happens if it is not: you assume the model may be misaligned and ask whether the surrounding system of monitoring, permissions, and audits would catch the failure before harm is done. This feels familiar. In my old trade we never asked whether a pressure vessel was safe; we asked what would contain the blast when it ruptured, and every layer of protection was sized to a failure we had already assumed.

I would like that principle turned into a procedure our panel can actually run before approving a deployment. Fix the setting as follows: the panel has six members, only one with any machine-learning background; we meet for two hours before each rollout, roughly monthly; we cannot run experiments on the models ourselves; and sign-off must be defensible in writing to the organisation's board. Please give us the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a rejection rather than a condition, and where it must hand the decision to a human because the question is one of values or institutional appetite for risk rather than fact. Then run the procedure on two deployments we actually face: first, a model that drafts replies to benefits claimants, with a caseworker reviewing every reply before it is sent; second, a model with write access to a case-management database that produces summaries which staff increasingly rely on without checking. Flag the cases the procedure cannot settle, and say plainly where it would misfire — including, if you can, one deployment where it would reject something I would personally like to approve.

One further request, and I mean it: if you think the distinction I am leaning on is confused — if control measures quietly presuppose enough alignment that my containment analogy breaks down, or if the procedure I am asking for cannot honestly be built — tell me that directly rather than writing around it. I spent thirty years reviewing hazard studies, and the reports that politely worked around a false assumption were the ones that frightened me most.
