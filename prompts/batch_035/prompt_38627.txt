A dean at my university is pushing a pilot for our gen-ed critical thinking requirement, and I want a second opinion on whether my objection to it actually holds up. The idea: instead of a course teaching students to reason, we give them a faculty-vetted bank of claims — every item checked by instructors and marked true or false — and they satisfy the requirement by drilling until they can reliably sort the true ones from the false ones. The dean's pitch is that this gets them all the true beliefs the old course was after, cheaper and with better completion rates.

At the curriculum meeting I said something felt off: a student could pass with zero grasp of why anything on the list is true. The dean's reply was basically, so what — if they walk away holding the true beliefs, there in exactly the state we wanted, and the whys are a luxury for people who already have the beliefs. Then a student of mine who sat in on the meeting pressed me afterward with an even sharper version: if the end goal is true belief and the drill bank gets you there, what exactly does the student who can also explain the claims have that the driller lacks, and is that extra thing worth the extra tuition hours?

I left without a good answer and its bugging me. Is there genuinely some added value in the one state over the other, or am I just being a snob about memorization? And if there is added value, what kind is it — practical, or something else? I'd especially like to know whether it gives me a principled case against the pilot, or only a squeamishness I should get over before the next meeting.
