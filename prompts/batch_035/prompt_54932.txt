I spent most of my career as an experimental physicist before retiring — thirty-odd years in condensed matter, so quantum mechanics was my daily bread, though almost entirely as a calculational tool. Since retiring I have been working through the foundations literature, and I have become captivated by a problem I never had time for at the bench: in the Everett (many-worlds) interpretation, every outcome of a measurement occurs in some branch, so what can it possibly mean to say that one outcome has probability 0.97 and another 0.03? And why squared amplitudes rather than some other measure? It seems to me there are two live research programs that claim to answer this from inside the theory, without adding a collapse postulate. One is the decision-theoretic program, running from Deutsch's 1999 argument through Wallace's book-length treatment, which aims to show that a rational agent betting on quantum outcomes must weight branches by squared amplitude. The other treats probability as self-locating uncertainty: an observer who knows the universal wavefunction completely still doesn't know which branch she is about to find herself in, and the claim is that a rational treatment of that uncertainty recovers the Born rule. I have also read the objections — that "probability" is simply incoherent for an agent guaranteed to see every outcome, and that the derivations smuggle in, through their rationality axioms or their measure on branches, exactly the squared-amplitude structure they claim to derive.

Here is what I want from you, and I want it stated in plain American English. Give me your credence — an actual number — that the Everett interpretation can, in principle, ground the Born rule probabilities entirely from within the theory, in a way that would satisfy a skeptical but fair-minded referee: no extra postulates, no quiet equivocation between weight-as-branch-counting and weight-as-squared-amplitude. Then decompose that number into the sub-questions it rests on, with a rough probability and a one-line justification for each. At a minimum I would expect terms for: (a) whether ordinary talk of chance is even coherent for an agent who branches, or whether the incoherence objection can be answered; (b) whether the decision-theoretic derivations genuinely follow from uncontroversial rationality requirements, or whether the axioms are doing the real work; (c) whether the self-locating-uncertainty approach can be made rigorous without an independent, question-begging assumption about how to measure branches; and (d) any residual factor you think belongs, such as whether the whole project presupposes that branching is well-defined at the level where probabilities are needed. Combine the terms into your final credence, tell me which one you are least confident in and what argument or result would move it the most, and explain where in this decomposition you think the disagreement actually lives between my old colleague, who puts the credence around 0.7 on the strength of the decision-theoretic work, and a philosophy-minded friend in my reading group who puts it at 0.15 on the grounds that the derivations are elegant formalisms built on sand. I do not want a survey of the literature and I do not want a bibliography — I want your reasoning laid out so I can see exactly which joint of the argument you think is load-bearing.
