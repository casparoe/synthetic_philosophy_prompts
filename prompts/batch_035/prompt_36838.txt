Write an essay on whether the numbers should count when a rescuer must choose between saving one person and saving several, where no difference between the parties other than their number is stipulated. Take as your foil the position associated with John Taurek: that no one of the many, considered individually, has any greater claim on the rescuer than the single person has; that the rescuer wrongs no one by flipping a fair coin between the two outcomes; and that increasing the size of the larger group therefore never changes what may permissibly be done. Present the strongest version of that position you can, taking care to include its least intuitive commitment: that a fair coin is no less fitting when the contest is one life against a thousand than when it is one against one.

Then subject the position to three tests. First, the gradualist test: either the numbers start to matter at some point as the larger group grows, in which case there should be a principled account of where and why, or they never matter, in which case one must accept the coin flip at one-versus-a-thousand; say plainly which horn you take, and do not avail yourself of a vague middle position without specifying its principle. Second, the complaint test: if the many lose the coin flip, no individual among them was treated worse than the single person would have been; so any wrong done in not counting the numbers must be a wrong to no one in particular, or else an appeal to the aggregate loss. Consider whether either route is really available to a defender of counting, and what each costs — the first risks making the wrong invisible to every victim, the second risks positing a bearer of value that no one in the situation cares about. Third, the staggered rescue: suppose the rescuer meets the parties sequentially, first confronting a choice between the single person and just one member of the group, and only afterwards, if the group member was saved, the remaining members; a fresh coin at each stage gives the single person the same chance of survival regardless of how many others there are. Ask whether this invariance is a virtue of the position or a sign that it has lost track of what is at stake.

The essay should reach a definite conclusion — either that the numbers never count, or that they count from some point you specify, or that they count in some other way you articulate — and defend that conclusion against the strongest opposing argument you have developed along the way. Do not rely on appeals to authority or on a survey of the literature; the arguments must stand on their own.
