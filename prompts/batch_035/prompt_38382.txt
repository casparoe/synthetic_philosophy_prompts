A popular book on reasoning that a relative lent me summarizes Aumann's agreement theorem like this: "In 1976 the future Nobel laureate Robert Aumann proved mathematically that rational people cannot agree to disagree. If two people have the same evidence and know each other's conclusions, then their continued disagreement shows that at least one of them is failing to update properly. The theorem is the formal reason stubborn disagreement is a red flag: once the two sides have genuinely shared their reasons, holding out is a symptom of motivated reasoning, not a legitimate epistemic position." I am inclined to think the book overreaches, but a colleague in economics assures me the result really is as strong as advertised, so I would like a careful audit rather than a reaction. Please go sentence by sentence: which parts would a formal epistemologist accept as stated, which are simplifications that obscure a load-bearing assumption (about priors, about what "knowing each other's conclusions" amounts to technically versus common knowledge of posteriors), and which are outright errors — in particular, whether the premise is shared evidence or something else, since my understanding is that the agents in Aumann's setup may hold different information. Then assess the inference the book draws from the theorem to practical advice: does persistent disagreement after a real conversation reveal irrationality on the result itself, or only on later work about how agents who exchange information actually converge, and if the advice does not follow as stated, say what would have to be true for it to follow.
