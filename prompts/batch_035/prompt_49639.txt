Six weeks ago I started a new job, and it has landed me in the middle of a philosophical argument I am not equipped to referee. By the terms of my contract, my manager has essentially unchecked discretion over my position: he can dismiss me without cause, reassign me, or change my schedule, and there is no appeals process and no one above him likely to second-guess him. In practice he has never exercised any of this power. He is courteous, he praises my work, and he has told me he hopes I stay for many years. And yet I find myself arriving early, staying late, rereading my emails before sending them, and quietly shelving suggestions I suspect he might not like. Over dinner last weekend, two friends of mine, Marcus and Elena, began arguing about whether I am free.

Marcus opened by insisting that nothing has been done to me at all. Nobody has prevented me from doing anything; I face no obstacles; I am paid well and treated well. Feeling watchful is not being unfree, he said — freedom is a matter of what others actually do to you, not of your nerves, and I should stop confusing anxiety with constraint.

Elena replied that the situation itself is the problem, not any incident within it. I live at this man's discretion. He holds a standing power over me that he can invoke at any moment, for any reason or none, and I have no way to contest it. A person in that position is not free even if the power is never used; the self-censorship isn't a quirk of my temperament but the rational response to the power.

Marcus said this proves far too much. Everyone lives under standing powers they cannot contest — landlords, tax authorities, any stranger who could assault them on the street. If mere vulnerability to interference is enough for unfreedom, then no one in history has ever been free, and the word has stopped doing any work.

Elena answered that there is a difference in kind between a random aggressor and a power that is licensed, institutionalized, and answerable to no one. The mugger's power over me is backed by nothing; my manager's power is baked into my contract and the law. The question is not how likely he is to use it, but that using it is entirely his call.

Marcus countered that likelihood is all that matters in practice — a manager who would never actually fire me is less of a constraint than the ordinary risk of walking home late at night — and that my self-censorship is my own doing, since the man never asked for it and I could stop it tomorrow.

Elena said I can't stop, not rationally, because the adaptation is what such power predictably produces whether or not he intends it; and that if my freedom amounts to a powerful person's continuing goodwill, calling me free merely dresses up dependence.

At that point Marcus made what seemed to me a different kind of move. Even granting everything Elena said, he argued, the conclusion would be that I am insecure, not that I am unfree. Freedom and security are distinct goods, and Elena had been sliding between them — from someone could take things from me at will to I am not in control of my own life. The second claim is much stronger, and by its standard no employee anywhere has ever been free. Elena said she was prepared to accept that conclusion.

They asked me to settle it, and I honestly could not. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: who is right about which specific point, and what, if anything, are both of them missing? I would also add that if you think the question they were arguing over is itself confused — that whether I am free does not have an answer of the kind they were both looking for — please say so plainly rather than splitting the difference to be diplomatic.
