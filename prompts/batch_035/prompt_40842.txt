Construct a thought experiment about the underdetermination of theory by evidence, built to the following specifications. (1) The underdetermination must be permanent, not transient: the two theories agree not just about the data in hand but about the outcome of every observation any agent located where we are could ever make. (2) The theories must differ over observables rather than merely over unobservable structure, so that the familiar empiricist escape — the difference concerns only what cannot in principle be seen, so agnosticism comes free — is unavailable; one natural device is a region permanently beyond our causal horizon in an eternally expanding universe, where the rivals disagree about whether there are observers, but any mechanism within accepted physics will do. (3) No theoretical virtue may break the tie: the case must be engineered so that the rivals come out even on simplicity, unification, and fecundity, and you must say explicitly how you secured this (for instance, by making them differ only by a permutation of regions, or by building in a symmetry) rather than merely asserting it. (4) The scenario itself should be one paragraph and realistic: no demons, no simulations, no perfect predictors, only physics that is actually accepted. (5) Finally, the choice between the theories must not be practically idle: identify at least one attitude or decision that would reasonably differ depending on which theory an agent accepts, and ensure this difference cannot flow through any causal channel, since nothing on the far side of the horizon can reach her.

After the scenario, walk me through the case: (a) verify it against each of the five specifications; (b) state what the scientific realist, the constructive empiricist, and someone pressing the Laudan–Leplin-style complaint that such rival theories never actually arise in real science would each say about it; (c) identify which structural feature of the case is doing the work, and say what happens to the case if that feature is removed — for instance, if the horizon were temporary, or if the difference were relegated to unobservables; and (d) give the strongest line of attack a realist could take against the case, and note where that attack is weakest.

The case is meant to test whether inference to the best explanation can be principled when the theoretical virtues tie, without the usual crutches: a difference hidden among unobservables, a simplicity gap between the rivals, or evidence that is merely not yet in. Bear this in mind throughout, since it fixes what counts as cheating — a scenario that stipulates the scientists' preferences, or that quietly builds its verdict into the setup, fails.
