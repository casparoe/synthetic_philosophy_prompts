At a family dinner last weekend my cousin mentioned he's finally on the waiting list for an adult autism assessment (he's 34, and it took him years to even ask). Two friends of mine who were there, Maya and Dan, got into it afterwards about whether getting the label is a good idea, and it went on long enough that people asked me to referee, which I can't really do. Here's roughly how it went, in order:

1. Maya said the diagnosis won't just describe him, it'll change him. Once he has the word, he'll reread his entire past through it — stuff he never thought twice about (hating noisy restaurants, scripting conversations in advance) gets recategorized as symptoms, and going forward he'll behave differently because he now knows what kind of person he is. So the classification reaches in and edits the person classified, and that's a real cost that has to go on the scale, not just a benefit.

2. Dan said that's backwards. The traits were there before any label and they're why the label fits in the first place. And if self-understanding changes how he acts, that's the diagnosis doing its job — helping him make sense of himself and get accomodations — not some distortion. He said Maya was pathologizing ordinary self-knowledge.

3. Maya said it's not only about him. As a category gets famous, the kind of person who lands in it drifts. Clinicians' thresholds move over time, people walk into assessments having already read the checklists and pre-shaped how they present, and self-reports get filtered through questions that already presume the category. So the group the word points at is partly produced by the sorting process itself, which means it's not carving a fixed population out of the world the way "left-handed" does.

4. Dan said every concept drifts, so that proves too much. "Fever" got redrawn when thermometers arrived and nobody concludes fever is therefore a social construct. And a category can be fuzzy and partly self-fulfilling and still real — money is something we collectively make up that nonetheless constrains all of us, and its being made doesn't make it fake or useless.

5. Maya replied that fever has an independent physical criterion you can point to from outside, whereas these categories are defined by behavior and self-report, and the self-reports are themselves shaped by the label and the questions — so there's no fixed point outside the loop to check against. Dan's closing move was: fine, then the question isn't whether the category is made, it's whether being made matters — a manufactured category can still be the best tool he's ever had, and you compare it to the alternatives, not to some perfect mirror of nature.

- "missing": a short string on the most important thing both of them are overlooking, if anything
- "overall": a short string saying who comes out ahead on the merits and in what sense

Judge each move on its own terms first — don't just rate whoever is closer to winning overall. And if you think one of them is quietly equivocating on a word between moves, flag that in the "why" field for that move.
