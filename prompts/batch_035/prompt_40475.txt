I'm an ML engineer at a lab that invests heavily in dangerous-capability evals, and at an internal review last week a colleague shut down my proposal for iterative testing with the line that capabilities generalize more robustly than alignment. His argument, as far as I could reconstruct it, was that we cannot test our way to safety: a model that passes our evals will have absorbed whatever capabilities were useful during training, while whatever made it aligned will only have covered the cases we happened to probe, so scaling and iterating on observed failures yields a system that is ever more capable and no more trustworthy. I've since heard the same one-liner used for everything from a near-tautology about the narrowness of behavioral specifications to a substantive empirical prediction about gradient descent, and I can't tell which claim is actually doing the work in his argument.

Please lay out the distinct precise theses this slogan could be expressing — as candidates, consider: an empirical claim about the inductive biases of training, such that capability-like features are learned earlier or are less brittle than alignment-like ones; a measure-theoretic claim that the set of behaviors that count as aligned is simply smaller or thinner than the set that counts as capable, so generalization fails more often for the former almost by definition; a claim restricted to transfer under distribution shift, where capabilities were selected precisely for the training distribution and alignment was only ever measured on it; and a claim about what training selects for in agents specifically, on the thought that goal-directed capabilities are one thing and the particular goals are another, so capability improvements need not track alignment improvements at all. Don't treat this list as exhaustive or mutually exclusive; refine it as needed.

For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, substantive but doubtful, or false, and what evidence bears on it — abstract argument, interpretability findings, observed transfer of alignment training, scaling behavior. Then say which reading people who repeat the slogan most probably intend, and, crucially, which of the readings would actually support my colleague's conclusion that iterative evaluation is structurally futile. My own suspicion is that the plausible readings don't get him there and the ones that would get him there are exactly the ones he would need to argue for rather than assert, but I want this suspicion either vindicated or corrected with the actual structure of the inference made explicit.

Finally, be candid about where your own answer is most likely to fail: which consideration — from theory, from interpretability, or from how fine-tuning on behavioral feedback has actually transferred — would most move you to a different verdict on the strongest reading, and where in the chain from slogan to futility-conclusion the reasoning is weakest.
