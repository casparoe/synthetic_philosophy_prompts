I serve on the board of a regional land trust, and we are preparing a petition to have a stand of quaking aspen designated as a protected "heritage organism" under our state's natural-areas statute, which extends protection to individual organisms of exceptional age or size. The stand looks like a few hundred separate trees spread over several acres, but the geneticist we retained tells us the trunks are all shoots from a single shared root system — genetically identical, physically connected underground, and, judging by the age of the roots, regenerating from the same structure for thousands of years. Our board has split into three camps, and I am hoping for help thinking through which camp deserves my vote.

The first camp holds that each trunk is plainly its own tree: it takes in light, grows, dies, and reproduces independently enough that counting anything other than trunks would be double-counting. The second camp holds that the clone as a whole is the single individual, with the trunks better thought of as limbs or leaves — structures the organism grows and sheds — and that protecting "one tree" here means protecting the whole connected system. The third camp, led by a colleague of mine, insists both are wrong in the same way: there is no further fact about how many trees are out there at all. Nature, she says, supplies only a continuous root-and-shoot structure, and the boundaries of "an organism" are lines we draw for our own purposes; she thinks the honest petition would describe the stand as a process we have chosen to care about rather than as a thing, and that the statute's word "organism" is a bureaucratic fiction we should quietly work around.

There is also a wrinkle in the field itself that troubles me. Two of what the geneticist identified as adjacent clones appear, in one place, to have grown into each other's roots — our forester calls it a natural graft and says the tissues have fused so that water and sugar move across. On the second camp's own reasoning, that would seem to make the two clones one tree now, and if we count organisms by functional integration, I cannot see what stops the counting there, since the stand's roots also thread together with fungal networks shared by half the forest floor. But if the graft really did turn two trees into one, I would like to know when that happened — at first contact, at the moment the tissues fused, or only when some threshold of exchange was crossed — and I find that I have no idea how to answer.

I would be grateful for your view on which of the three positions is correct, if any. I am less concerned with the legal strategy than with the underlying question: is there a fact of the matter about how many organisms occupy that hillside, with our board merely discovering it, or is my colleague right that the number depends on our conventions? And if the two clones genuinely became one thing through the graft, I would like to understand at what point, and in virtue of what, that transition occurred — since the roots involved were, after all, in some modest contact all along.
