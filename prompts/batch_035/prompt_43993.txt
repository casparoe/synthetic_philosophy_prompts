I've been trying to work out what I actually think 'equality of what' means by listing my gut verdicts on cases, and I'd like someone to tell me what principle, if any, is behind them.

Same income, but one of the two is paraplegic: not equal — we owe the second person more (ramps, care, equipment) even though the money is identical. Same income, one of them has a medical condition that makes staying alive cost twice as much: also not equal, compensation owed. Same income, but one developed a taste for champagne and opera: equal, no compensation. Same income, one grew up genuinely deprived and has stopped wanting much of anything: it feels wrong to just say equal on the grounds that their welfare is fine. Same income, one works half-time at somthing they love and the other grinds at a job they hate for the extra money: equal, I think — nobody owes anybody here. Same income, one is chronically depressed and gets nothing out of what money can buy: not equal? Though it feels different from the medical case somehow.

My tentative thought is something like 'resources, indexed to the cost of reaching a decent threshold of functioning, where the threshold doesn't move with your tastes' — but I cant get that to tell me whether the depression case is like the medical one or not, and the deprived-childhood verdict keeps slipping away from it. So: is there one principle that gets all of these, or close? State it (or a couple of candidates) precisely, say what each clause is doing, give its verdict on a case I haven't listed — say, someone who's fully able but converts money into a good life unusually cheaply, a library-and-long-walks person — and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't save. If you have to overturn one, tell me which and why.

Please write the answer in British English.
