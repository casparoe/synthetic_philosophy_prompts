I'm a philosophy PhD student with a background in philosophy of mind and about a year of ML coursework on the side, trying to settle on a dissertation topic in the debate over whether we should attribute beliefs, desires, and other propositional attitudes to large language models. The literature I've encountered seems to fragment into several loosely connected strands, and I can't tell which of them is actually load-bearing. One strand concerns the interpretation of the intentional stance itself: on a purely instrumentalist reading, attributing beliefs to a thermostat or a chess engine or an LLM is just a predictive bookkeeping device, whereas a realist reading, in the spirit of Dennett's "real patterns" argument, holds that some stance-attributions carve genuine structure in the world and others are mere shorthand. A second strand is methodological: when interpretability researchers probe a model's activations and claim to have recovered a "belief," what exactly is being recovered, and do our ordinary belief-talk standards apply to it? A third asks whether belief-attribution admits of degrees — whether an entity can be a "sort of" believer the way it can be a "sort of" agent — and a fourth takes the normative turn: if we decide LLMs have beliefs in some serious sense, what follows for moral status, for deception, for how developers should talk about their systems internally?

Here is what I want from you: a defended prioritization. Suppose I have about three years, the analytic tools of a philosopher rather than those of an ML researcher, and the goal of producing work that would actually get cited by both philosophers and people building these systems. Which two or three of the strands above should anchor the dissertation, and in what order should I attack them? I care about the dependency structure: does anything useful in the methodological or normative strands presuppose settling the instrumentalism/realism question about the intentional stance first, or is that a trap — a debate philosophers have been circling since the 1980s that no answer to would change how anyone treats an LLM? Which of these questions are tractable with philosophical methods alone, which need collaboration with interpretability people, and which are overrated in the sense that their answer wouldn't change much downstream? And what developments over the next few years — say, interpretability tools maturing, or models that verbally report on their own internal states — would most move your ranking up or down?

As you make your case, please be explicit about how confident you are in each major claim, and single out the point in your ranking you are least sure of — the place where you suspect a reasonable philosopher of mind would push back hardest, and what would make you reconsider. I'd rather have a well-flagged ordering I can stress-test than a confident survey.
