i teach an undergrad elective on rational choice / decision theory — 16 students, one 90-minute session. we just finished expected utility and Newcomb's problem, and next week i want to get at the idea that sometimes you should decide as if you're choosing a policy for all your possible selves *before* you know which observation you'll end up with — the updateless move, though i don't want them to hear that word (or any jargon) until the debrief. design an in-class game that makes them feel the gap between deciding with the information you currently have, which looks obviously correct at the moment of choice, and having committed earlier to a rule that covers observations you might not make.

my rough idea, feel free to improve on it: run the same game twice. round one, everyone plays informed, with every local incentive pointing at "take the sensible action given what you now know" — and individually it keeps looking fine — but afterwards the group discovers that players who (unknowingly) precommitted to a policy came out better across the counterfactual draws. round two, they submit sealed strategy sheets specifying what they'd do for *each* possible observation before a role/coin assignment, envelopes opened only after. i'd rather the scenario be new to them than a recognizable version of Parfit's hitchhiker or Newcomb itself, because about a third of them read rationalist blogs and will pattern-match to the famous answer instead of feeling the pull — so some fresh cover story where the structure sneaks up on them. payoffs in course points redeemable for snacks at the end of term is fine, no real money needed.

give me: rules, materials, a timeline for the 90 minutes, the payoff tables, a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the formal distinction (choosing an action vs choosing a policy; an observation as evidence about the world vs as an input your earlier self already priced in), what will predictably go wrong the first time i run it and how to handle it, and an honest note on which part of the real idea the game cannot reproduce — i suspect it's the part where the updateless agent properly cares about worlds in which it saw different evidence, which one-shot human participants never actually experience, but tell me if im wrong and how much of it has to be supplied by hand in the debrief. last time i ran something like this we lost 15 minutes to a digression about free will, so also tell me where the game is most likely to derail into that.
