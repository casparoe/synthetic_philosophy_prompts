Here's what I want and why: I've been reading a bunch of pop-psych and decision-theory stuff that treats precommitment as basically a free win for anyone with akrasia issues — sign up for the annual gym membership, install the app blocker, put your credit card in the freezer. My hunch is this advice is way less robust than it looks once you take hyperbolic discounting seriously, because the same preference-reversal machinery that makes you weak-willed also operates when you're setting up the commitment. So the case is a stress test of precommitment advice, and I want it engineered, not asserted.

Construct a thought experiment with three agents facing the same life situation: (a) a naive agent who just does whatever their current self wants in the moment, (b) a sophisticated agent who correctly predicts their own future weakness and responds by locking in external commitments, and (c) a resolute agent — no external devices, they just make a decision once and somehow treat it as binding through later temptation (if you know this framing from the sequential-choice literature, great; if not, the three descriptions here are all you need). The scenario must come out so that the sophisticated precommitter does *worse* than the naive agent — not just worse than the resolute one. That's the whole point.

Constraints: everyday realism, no sci-fi, no willpower drugs, no hypothetical demons — think careers, relationships, money, health, hobbies. One paragraph for the scenario itself. Crucially, the precommitment can't be stupid on its face: at the moment the sophisticated agent sets it up, it has to be a genuinely reasonable move by their own lights, with the information anyone plausibly has then. The failure has to fall out of the structure, not from the precommitter being a strawman who forgot to think for five seconds. That's what would count as cheating here — a case where the verdict is stipulated by making one agent dumb, rather than generated by how hyperbolic discount curves and commitment interact. And the naive agent shouldn't win by luck either; their "win" should be traceable to the same discounting structure that sank the precommitter.
