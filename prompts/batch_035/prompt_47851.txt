I'm a retired physicist (solid state, thirty-some years, never had anything to do with eyes or brains) and since leaving the lab I've been working my way through old philosophy books, which is how I ended up refereeing a fight at thanksgiving. My nephew Danny, a postdoc in developmental psych, and his girlfriend Rosa, finishing a philosophy PhD, went at it for over an hour about Molyneux's problem — the old question of whether someone blind from birth, if they gained sight as an adult, could tell a cube from a sphere just by looking, before handling either.

Danny opened with: this was always an empirical question, and the philosophers who chewed on it for 300 years were basically waiting for better instruments. Now that surgeons can actually restore some vision to people blind from birth, we have the answer: those patients do badly at matching shapes across the senses at first, so Molyneux's answer is no.

Rosa said that's not Molyneux's question, it's a different one. He was asking whether the ideas we get from touch are already the same ideas we'd get from sight — whether the mapping is there in the subject as they are. Any real patient you test has been sighted for a while before they make it into a lab, so whatever you measure is post-hoc learning, not the pre-existing mapping Danny claims to have tested for.

Danny: fine, but then the question is untestable and arguably not a question at all. The only way to test a "pre-existing" mapping is to give someone sight and test them immediately, and immediately is doing an impossible amount of work there.

Rosa said the confound actually runs the other way, and this is where she dug in. Someone who's been blind for 30 years has a visual system that's never been calibrated — eyes, wiring, the whole apparatus — so failing to match a seen cube to a held cube doesn't show there's no shared representation. It might just show the visual signal is garbage on day one. A negative result can't distinguish "no shared idea" from "can't see well enough to have any idea."

Danny: then you need a control — first check whether they can distinguish the shapes by sight alone, ignoring touch entirely, and only count the cross-modal test as evidence if they pass that. He said he'd bet the people actually running these studies thought of it.

Rosa: fine, but the deeper issue is what counts as "the same idea" anyway. If the subject can pick up the mapping after just a few days of sighted life, maybe the shared representation was there all along and only needed some tuning to express itself — in which case Danny's "no" is really "no, modulo a few days of calibration," and for a question about the innate structure of the mind that's practically a yes.

Danny said that was moving the goalposts, since by that logic you can convert any "no" into a "yes, once they've learned it." Rosa said the learning itself is the evidence, that fast isn't fast enough to be from scratch. At that point they noticed I'd stopped pretending to follow and appealed to me, and I honestly couldn't call it. Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right about what — including whether Rosa's last move is fair or is shifting the goalposts, and whether Danny's control actually fixes the confound Rosa raised? And after all this, is there any part of Molyneux's question that's still genuinely open?
