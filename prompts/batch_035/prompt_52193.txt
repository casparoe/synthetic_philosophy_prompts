I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character is a woman who does a great deal of good — for her neighbors, for strangers, for people who never learn her name — largely, I suspect, because she enjoys being needed and admired. In deciding what judgment my narrative owes her, I have been reading about the Kantian idea that moral worth lies in acting from duty, and I want to be sure I have the view right. An essay I came across in a general-audience philosophy magazine summarized the position as follows: "Kant held that the moral worth of an action depends entirely on its motive: only acts done from duty have moral worth, and acts done from inclination — kindness, affection, the pleasure of being useful — have none. Because morality is a matter of motive alone, consequences are irrelevant to it: whether an act helps or harms anyone makes no difference to its moral standing. And since duty is most visible when it is unwelcome, Kant thought the more reluctantly a right act is performed, the more moral worth it has, and that we should guard against warm feelings, which only contaminate the purity of the motive."

Something in this feels off to me. The essay was written by a professional, and yet the picture of Kant as preferring a grim do-gooder to a loving one sounds suspiciously like a caricature, and my instinct about my own character is that her gladness in doing good is not a moral defect. Please go through the summary claim by claim and tell me which parts a careful Kantian would accept, which are conflations or overstatements, and which Kant explicitly denied or said something close to the opposite of. I would particularly like to know (a) whether Kant really held that consequences make no moral difference, or whether they instead enter some other way — for instance, in determining what our duties are in the first place; (b) whether he anywhere suggests that reluctance enhances moral worth, or whether that is a misreading of a distinction he actually draws between acting from duty and acting merely in accordance with it; and (c) what, if anything, he says about the moral value of cultivating our sympathetic feelings, since this bears directly on whether my character's loves and vanities are stains upon her or part of her goodness.

Finally, please tell me whether the essay's implicit conclusion — that a person who does good gladly is morally inferior to one who does it grimly — actually follows from anything true that survives in the summary, or whether it collapses once the misrepresentations are corrected. I ask because that conclusion, if sound, would genuinely change the ending of my book, and I would rather revise it on the basis of the view than of a distortion of it.
