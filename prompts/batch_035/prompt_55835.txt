I recently moved from volunteer coordination into a role directing donor communications at a mid-sized environmental charity, and I've discovered that the job is largely the applied psychology of giving: every appeal I sign off on is a bundle of tactics sitting somewhere on a line between persuasion and manipulation, and I can't keep working from gut feelings. Here are my gut verdicts on tactics that our fundraising consultants have either recommended or used for years, and I want to know whether one principle captures them or whether some verdicts have to go:

1. The annual appeal tells the story of a specific family whose smoky cookstove was replaced with a clean-burning one. The family is real and the program is real, but my gift doesn't literally buy their stove — it goes into the general fund, and the story was written before the appeal was sent. Verdict: fine. It's a true representative story about what money like mine does.
2. The matching-gift campaign announces that a generous donor will match all gifts "only until midnight Friday," when in fact the matching donor has committed a fixed sum that gets used whether or not the deadline is met. Verdict: I go back and forth on this one — I keep landing on "not fine," but I can't fully articulate why, since the matching really does happen.
3. Appeal envelopes printed in a child's handwriting font with "Please help my forest" on the front. There is no particular child. Verdict: fine, honestly — it's just design, like using a warm color scheme.
4. Urgency framing throughout the year: "tonight," "before it's too late," for conservation needs that are in fact chronic and unchanging. Verdict: fine.
5. Our phone officers, following the rapport techniques from a workshop we sent them to, are told to mirror the donor's emotional language back to them, and to call at the times our donor data says each person is most receptive. Verdict: fine — this feels like trained skill; our best officers have always done it naturally.
6. A vendor proposal to replace that system with an algorithm that assembles each donor's appeal from a library of story fragments, image choices, and send times chosen to maximize that individual's predicted giving. Same underlying psychological levers as the phone program, just automated and personalized at scale. Verdict: not fine, it feels creepy — though I half-know this is hard to square with my verdict on the phone officers.
7. A renewal notice deliberately styled to look like a second notice ("FINAL REMINDER — respond immediately") when it is in fact the first. Verdict: not fine.

I can't tell whether the human-versus-automation distinction is doing real work in my verdicts on 5 and 6, or whether it's a prejudice I should drop, and I can't tell whether the pattern is really something like "engaging deliberation versus exploiting it" or something about false implicature instead. Please give me a table whose columns are: Case number; my verdict; the verdict under the single best principle you can construct; the verdict under your best runner-up; and, for whichever principle you recommend, which of my verdicts it must overturn to fit. After the table, state the recommended principle precisely, note which case motivates each clause, and give its verdict on two tactics I haven't mentioned: (a) A/B-testing appeal subject lines until we find the one that reliably maximizes giving, then using it on everyone; and (b) a phone officer truthfully telling a wavering major donor that others in her giving circle have already stepped up this year.
