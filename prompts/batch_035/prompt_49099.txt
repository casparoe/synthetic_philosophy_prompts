I teach an upper-level cognitive science seminar on concepts and analogy — fourteen students, one 90-minute session — and we have just finished reading about Douglas Hofstadter's Copycat program. My students can recite the thesis (concepts are fluid; analogy-making is a kind of perception; the concepts doing the work in an act of analogy are built on the spot rather than retrieved from a fixed dictionary) but I don't think they have felt it. Design an in-class activity that makes them feel it.

The activity should be built around the letter-string microdomain the Copycat program ran on. Its rules are simple enough to state on one slide: strings are made of the letters a through z, and a problem gives a change from one string to another, then asks what the analogous change to a new target string would be. Use problems like these, which you may draw from the actual Copycat corpus or write fresh in the same style: abc changes to abd, so what should ijk change to? abc changes to abd, so what should iijjkk change to? abc changes to abd, so what should mrrjjj change to? abc changes to abd, so what should xyz change to? Sequence at least five problems so that the early ones have one obviously dominant answer and the later ones force genuine conceptual slippage — cases where the target string has no rightmost letter with a successor in the alphabet, or where the natural unit of the change might be a group of repeated letters rather than a single letter, so that students must decide for themselves whether rightmost should slip to leftmost or to rightmost group, whether letter should slip to group, whether successor should slip to predecessor or to appending an element. Do not tell them in advance that the hard problems have no single right answer; let them discover, mid-argument, that a peer who reasoned just as defensibly arrived somewhere else entirely.

Structure the session in three phases. First, students commit individually and in writing to an answer and a one-sentence reason before any discussion — the written record matters, so design the handout for it. Second, small groups argue and must converge on one group answer, under a scoring rule you will need to invent: groups earn points not for the answer itself but for the quality of the conceptual description that licenses it, so that a group that wins by articulating the slippage its answer required beats a group that got the same string by luck or by majority vote. Keep the scoring simple enough to apply live; a rubric that needs a paragraph of justification per answer will not survive contact with a 90-minute session. Third, a whole-class round displays the distribution of answers, and the debrief maps each surviving answer onto the specific slippages it required — this is where rightmost and group and successor get written on the board as pressures that were renegotiated problem by problem, not as fixed vocabulary that was looked up. The debrief should also make the deeper connection explicit: that perceiving the target string and analogizing to it are one activity, not two.

Tell me, honestly, what this activity cannot reproduce about the actual Copycat architecture. A room of fourteen humans arguing is not a parallel terraced scan, and group deliberation will not mimic stochastic spreading activation among small codelets competing to build descriptions; be specific about which features of the mechanism you are gesturing toward rather than demonstrating, and say how you would flag that gap in the debrief so the activity does not quietly teach a cartoon version of the model. Finally, include a note on what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it — including the student who treats the whole thing as a puzzle hunt with one clever intended answer per problem, and the group that reaches consensus in thirty seconds because someone dominant spoke first — with concrete countermeasures for each.
