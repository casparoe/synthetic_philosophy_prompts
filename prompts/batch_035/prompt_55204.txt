So I'm a PhD student working at the intersection of moral psych and law & philosophy, and I keep coming back to something that I cant quite file under an existing doctrine. Our legal system excuses people from criminal responsibility in roughly two kinds of cases: insanity/diminished capacity, where the defendant couldn't appreciate the wrongfulness of their conduct, and things like infancy or (informally, via competency proceedings) advanced dementia, where the person just isn't a participant anymore. Meanwhile juries are perfectly happy to convict psychopaths, who on some accounts also can't genuinely appreciate moral wrongfulness, and courts have no trouble with severely demented defendants being found incompetent after arrest but having decades of prior conduct treated as fully culpable.

The Strawsonian frame seems like it should cut through this: reactive attitudes (resentment, gratitude, the whole participant stance) are for agents whose quality of will is visible to us, and we modulate them in response to excusing conditions, whereas the "objective attitude" — treatment, management, seeing someone as an object of social policy — is what we take toward the insane, the child, the demented. On that picture the insanity defense looks like a codification of the conditions under which the community's reactive attitudes get suspended, and competency proceedings look like the moment the court acknowledges someone has crossed from participant to object-of-care. But then psychopathy is the counterexample that bugs me: the law's line (did they know right from wrong) and the Strawsonian line (can they be addressed in the interpersonal nexus) come apart, and the legal practice lands on the side of blame every time.

So what I want from you is a careful reconstruction of what view of responsibility this whole cluster of practices actually presupposes. Is it a rational capacity view dressed up in interpersonal language, a genuinely Strawsonian view that psychopathy breaks, or something incoherent that just reflects doctrinal accretion? Does the system need the presupposed view to be true, or does it merely fit comfortably under several rival theories? And if we took the Strawsonian view seriously as a matter of doctrine rather than just theory — say, excusing anyone for whom the reactive attitudes have no natural target — what would actually have to change in criminal procedure?
