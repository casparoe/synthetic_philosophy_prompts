I've become convinced of a particular reductive view about the direction of time: there is no intrinsic asymmetry between past and future in the physics itself. The direction of time, on my view, is fixed entirely by the thermodynamic gradient — the fact that entropy was very low in one direction and increases in the other — together with the Past Hypothesis, the posit that the early universe started in a spectacularly low-entropy macrocondition. On this picture, "earlier" and "later" are like "up" and "down" on Earth: not fundamental features of spacetime, but local, indexical relations that any inhabitant of a low-entropy region would use to orient itself, with the direction of increasing entropy serving as the arrow. I take this to be the mainstream view among philosophers of physics — the sort of position associated with the Boltzmannian tradition and developed more recently by David Albert — and I'd like to stress-test it.

Acting as a philosopher of physics with strong sympathies for both the view itself and for intellectual honesty about its weaknesses, please map out what would actually count against this position. I have in mind several candidate defeaters I've encountered, but I want your own considered inventory, which may include these and others: the apparent fact that our temporal experience involves something over and above an entropy gradient — a felt "passage" that the reductive view arguably cannot even describe, let alone explain; the tiny violation of time-reversal symmetry observed in neutral kaon decays, which looks like a fundamental arrow independent of thermodynamics; the charge, associated with John Earman, that the view rests on a suspect dualism between exact time-symmetric dynamical laws and a special unexplained further fact about the initial state; the worry that the Past Hypothesis itself cannot be assigned a sensible probability without smuggling in the very arrow it is meant to ground; and the question of what the view says about a hypothetical region of the universe where entropy is decreasing toward what we'd call the future — whether it is committed to time "running backwards" there, and whether that commitment is a bug or a feature.

For each defeater, tell me whether a thoughtful defender of the view would concede it as genuine counterevidence or dismiss it, and why — and please rank them from fatal to merely awkward rather than presenting them as an even list. I also want an honest verdict on whether the position has been qualified over time into something unfalsifiable: if its defenders can always reply that apparent arrows (kaon decay, our sense of passage, even the applicability of the Past Hypothesis) are "just" further consequences of the thermodynamic gradient or of our location within it, is that a principled explanatory achievement or a sign that the view no longer risks anything? I'd personally be most moved by anything showing the view cannot recover the phenomenology of temporal passage or the asymmetries of everyday agency — memory of the past, deliberation about the future — so give those worries particular attention, but don't let my biases set the agenda. Think through the dialectic carefully before committing to rankings, and be willing to conclude that some of the apparent defeaters are much weaker than they first appear.
