A close friend of mine was just diagnosed with late-stage pancreatic cancer, a few weeks after a clean physical, and it has rattled me badly. In the aftermath, my sister has been sending me brochures for direct-to-consumer whole-body MRI screening, and my own physician is skeptical of the whole enterprise. I have to decide within about a month whether to pay roughly $2,500 out of pocket for the scan. I have a working grasp of Bayes' theorem and expected utility, but no formal training in decision theory.

Please describe the most important implications of Good's theorem on the value of information for my decision. I want the implications of the actual result, not the slogan that information is good. In particular, I'd like you to address the following, though you should feel free to go beyond them:

1. Under exactly what conditions does the non-negativity of the expected value of information hold, and when does the result become vacuous — specifically, what does the theorem say about information that could not change my subsequent actions, and does most of what's contested about screening live in that gap?
2. The harms people attribute to screening — incidental findings, biopsy cascades, overdiagnosis of indolent tumors, surveillance anxiety — appear to enter through the actions the information triggers, not through the information itself. Does Good's theorem absorb this: are those harms already inside the decision problem, so the theorem still holds but the information is no longer the thing being valued? Or do they mark a genuine failure of the assumptions, e.g., that neither I nor my doctors will act as a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer once we have the scan in hand?
3. How the theorem interacts with the price of information, and with sequencing — I may be offered follow-up tests conditional on what the MRI shows, and I'd like to understand whether the value of partial information can behave counterintuitively in a sequence even when the value of the full information is guaranteed non-negative.
4. Whether the theorem's perspective changes when the decision is made against the background of my physician's incentives and my family's fears, rather than by an idealized solitary agent.

Throughout, please be disciplined about epistemic status. Separate what is settled mathematics from what is a modeling choice that could reasonably be made differently, from what is genuinely contested — either among decision theorists or among clinicians who study screening. In particular, do not present the case for or against whole-body MRI screening as settled, and flag your own contestable modeling assumptions as your own.
