In an argument with my housemate about whether some housing thing should be handled federally or by the states, I pulled out the subsidiarity slogan -- decisions should be made at the lowest possible level -- and he immediately shot back that by that logic almost nothing should be federal, and also that I clearly didn't understand what "lowest" means when problems don't come in neat jurisdictional sizes. Now I'm not sure the one-liner I reached for actually does the work I wanted it to do, and I want to take it apart properly before I use it again. It comes up everywhere I read anyway: it's all over EU discourse (their own treaties gesture at decisions being taken as closely as possible to the citizen), it's half the appeal of Swiss cantonal autonomy, and rationalist-adjacent people invoke the same idea when talking about centralization of AI governance or of forum moderation or whatever.

So: what are the distinct precise claims packed into "decisions should be made at the lowest possible level"? I can see at least a few directions but I want the full menu laid out. Like -- is it a burden-of-assignment principle (start at the lowest level and only move up when you've shown the lower level fails), or a comparative-competence principle (for each decision, put it wherever the balance of expertise, externalities, and preference heterogeneity is best, which sometimes means LOWER isn't right at all)? Or is it a claim about who bears the costs of error -- local decisions concentrate harms on the deciders, federal ones disperse them, so lower is safer even when it's less efficient? Or something almost epistemic, that local knowledge is incommunicable so central planners are structurally blind no matter how smart? And there's the version where it's not about decision quality at all but about exit rights and sorting, the Tiebout-ish thing where having many jurisdictions lets people vote with their feet.

For each reading, tell me which are trivial, which are substantive and defensible, which are just false, and -- this is the part I actually care about -- which ones would have supported me in the housing argument, where I wanted to block a federal zoning mandate but was fine with the feds setting, say, building codes. Because it feels like "lowest level" is doing pure vibes work in how people (me) actually deploy it, and I'd rather know which precisification I'm signing up for before I say it again. Also flag if any readings quietly presuppose that jurisdictional levels form a nested hierarchy, because he definitely scored points on me with the observation that river-basin-scale problems and neighborhood-scale problems don't stack into one ladder. Lay it out as a list I can actually think against.
