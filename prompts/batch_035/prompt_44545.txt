My IR professor loves the scorpion-in-a-bottle picture of the cold war: two scorpions dropped into one bottle, either one perfectly capable of killing the other, but any strike means being close enough that you die too. His conclusion is that deterrence was self-stabilizing in a very strong sense — nobody had to be wise or virtuous or even particularly careful, the geometry of the bottle did the work, and the arms control treaties were mostly theater for domestic audiences. He extends it to new nuclear states as well: more scorpions, same bottle, same stability, so we should stop wringing our hands about proliferation in places we don't like.

I want an audit of the scorpion picture, and I want you to actually take a side, not just list considerations. Specifically:

1. Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the bottle, the venom, the strike, the death, and anything else the analogy needs to get off the ground.

2. Say where the mapping breaks. I half-remember from the reading that Wohlstetter argued second-strike survivability is delicate rather than automatic — vulnerable bases, bomber warning times, that kind of thing. There's also the stuff the bottle has no room for: false alarms, misread exercises, delegated launch authority, limited and counterforce strikes that don't look like the all-or-nothing scorpion sting, and the fact that real governments can misjudge what the other side is doing in a way scorpions presumably can't. Sort the disanalogies into the ones that actually matter for his two conclusions (automatic stability, and proliferation-is-fine) versus the ones that are just pedantic.

3. Notice what the bottle smuggles in: the scorpions can't build exit ramps, can't improve their armor, can't make their venom more or less lethal, and there are exactly two of them forever. Do any of these quietly do argumentative work for him?

4. End with a committed verdict: does any version of his conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table — maybe a weaker version, like the bottle being a decent first approximation that policy still needs to correct? Or was the picture carrying the conclusion the whole time? And if there's a better analogy than scorpions (or chicken, or whatever else is floating around) for how deterrence actually kept the peace, name it and say why it's better.

Don't hedge into a both-sides ending, I can get that from the TA. If you think he's basically right, say so.
