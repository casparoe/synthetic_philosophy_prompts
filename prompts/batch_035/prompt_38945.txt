I'm a law student who wandered into a metaphysics elective for fun, and there's an apparent tension in how criminal law handles identity over time that I keep chewing on and can't resolve on my own. Please answer as if you're a professional metaphysician who also knows criminal law, and think carefully before locking into a view — maybe brainstorm the possible readings of the practice first, then evaluate them.

Here's the practice. Criminal law holds that the person standing trial is, numerically, the very same person who committed the crime, no matter how much time has passed: ninety-year-olds get tried for things they did at twenty-two, cold-case convictions are celebrated, and "it wasn't really me anymore" cuts no ice as a defense. But the same legal systems do things that seem to pull the other way: statutes of limitations say that for most crimes (though notably not murder) the state loses the right to prosecute after some years; parole boards and sentencing hearings treat "he's become a different man" as genuine mitigation; and at sentencing, the fact that the defendant's life has completely changed since the offense is routinely considered relevant. Meanwhile the metaphysics elective taught me that there's a live debate about persistence: endurantism (you are wholly present at every moment, strictly identical to your younger self) versus perdurantism (you are a four-dimensional worm of temporal parts, and the person in the dock is a later stage of the worm, connected to but not identical with the stage that did the deed).

So, questions. What view of identity over time does punishing a ninety-year-old for the twenty-two-year-old's crimes actually presuppose — does it need the endurantist's strict identity, or could a perdurantist get by saying "same worm, one continuous person, close enough"? What is owed to the worry, which I've seen raised against temporal-parts views, that on the perdurantist picture punishment starts to look like making one entity suffer for another entity's deed — the stage in the prison cell isn't the one who pulled the trigger, he's just causally downstream of him? Is the practice even internally consistent, given that too much personal change can bar prosecution for fraud but not for murder — does that presuppose that sameness-of-person comes in degrees, or that it matters differently for different kinds of crime, or is there some cleaner story? Finally, distinguish what parts of the practice strictly require which metaphysics from what's merely compatible with several: could an endurantist and a perdurantist both fully justify the practice, just telling different stories about why retribution legitimately flows across decades? And if the perdurantist story won out, what would actually have to change in courtroom practice — would the whole thing quietly become a legal fiction, and would testimony about transformation at parole hearings move from afterthought to the core of the inquiry?
