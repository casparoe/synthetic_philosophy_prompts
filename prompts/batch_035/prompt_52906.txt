I'm a high school junior working on an extended essay for a decision-making seminar, and I've hit a pattern I can't find a name for. I know the sunk cost fallacy — we covered it, along with "throwing good money after bad" — but I keep encountering cases that don't quite fit it, and I suspect they might not be fallacies at all. The pattern is this: a person honors a sunk cost, and the justification, when you dig for it, is not that they hate wasting money, and not that quitting would look bad, and not guilt. It's that the money or time already spent is the only surviving evidence of a judgment they made earlier, under conditions they can no longer reconstruct.

Two instances. My uncle has spent nine years restoring a car he hardly drives. Everyone in the family diagnoses sunk cost fallacy, and when I asked him directly why he doesn't sell it, he couldn't articulate a reason for having started — but he was adamant that there had been one, that at the time the plan had seemed obviously right to him, and that the pile of receipts in the garage was, as he put it, the only proof the plan had ever existed. Second: I myself spent a semester sticking with an elective I'd fought hard to get into and then disliked. I told myself I was just being disciplined. What I realized later is that I could no longer remember a single reason I'd wanted the course — the fight to get in had been so unpleasant that its details crowded everything else out — and the fact that I had once wanted it badly was the only trace left of whatever my reasons were. In both cases, the "irrational" persistence seems to be functioning as a way of deferring to a judgment that was made with information that has since been lost, using the investment itself as the record of that judgment.

Here is what I want to be clear about, because it's the part that makes me think this isn't just the sunk cost fallacy with a sympathetic story attached. It doesn't seem to be reputation: my uncle's car impresses no one. It doesn't seem to be loss aversion or inertia: both of them could quit tomorrow at low emotional cost. And, crucially, the pattern seems to have an edge — it dissolves when the original reasons are recoverable. I noticed that in cases where the person wrote down why they were doing something, or where someone around them remembers the rationale, honoring the sunk cost looks exactly as irrational as the textbooks say. The behavior appears only where the expenditure is the sole surviving trace of a lost deliberation. That makes me think the two things are connected: the "honoring" is really an inference, from "my past self committed resources" to "my past self saw something I can no longer see."

So my questions: Is there an established concept for using one's own past expenditures as evidence about one's own past deliberations — treating the sunk cost as a record rather than as a loss? If something in philosophy or economics covers this, I'd like to know what it's called, but I won't be satisfied with a bare term: I need to know how it differs from the sunk cost fallacy, from escalation of commitment, and from anything like precommitment, since those all seem to be about motivation rather than about lost information. And if nothing established covers it, I'd like you to coin a term for it, give a definition precise enough that I can apply it to new cases, and draw its boundaries — including the failure mode I'm most worried about, which is that this reasoning can retroactively dignify any past spending whatsoever, since we always flatter our past selves by assuming they knew things. A definition that can't distinguish genuine lost deliberation from that flattery is useless to me, so tell me how to tell them apart.
