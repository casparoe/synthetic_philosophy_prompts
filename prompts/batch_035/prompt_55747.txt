I want a dialogue between two friends arguing about the so-called gender-equality paradox — the finding that in countries that score highest on gender-equality indices (think Nordic countries), women make up a *smaller* share of STEM grads than in some less egalitarian countries, and self-reported sex differences in things like personality and interests actually come out larger there. The two friends are Maya and Dev.

Maya's take: this is at least some evidence that parts of the interest gap are biological in origin. Her rough line is "when you take the brakes off, you get to see the engine" — i.e., if you remove the structural constraints, whatever remains and even grows is more plausibly not a product of those constraints. She should acknowledge the usual caveats (correlation across ~a hundred countries, indices are imperfect) but argue the pattern is stubborn.

Dev's take: he's skeptical that the paradox shows much about biology, but for non-boilerplate reasons. His two main moves should be:

1. The equality indices mostly track formal/legal equality and representation in politics, not day-to-day socialization. A country can top the index while its culture is still heavily gendered — kids there grow up with the same cartoons, toys, and pink/blue marketing as everywhere else. So "most egalitarian country" doesn't mean "least gendered culture," and the paradox may partly be an artifact of what the index measures.

2. The affluence/identity story: the countries at the top of the index are also the richest, and in rich welfare states people can choose jobs for identity reasons rather than necessity. When survival isn't on the line, people sort into work that feels like "someone like me," and "someone like me" is still shaped by a gendered upbringing. On this view the wider gaps in egalitarian countries aren't biology showing through as constraints lift — they're identity expression getting *cheaper*. Constraints didn't disappear; the cost of obeying them dropped.

Important: neither of them should be a strawman. Maya needs to actually engage the identity story, not just wave at hormones — e.g., she can press Dev on why identity preferences would amplify rather than shrink as material necessity fades, or point out that his story makes predictions too (gaps should narrow within a country over generations as upbringing changes) and ask whether that's actually observed. Dev should genuinely grapple with her "brakes off, see the engine" logic rather than just reasserting socialization. Give them real back-and-forth — replies that build on or push back on the specific thing the other just said, not parallel monologues with "but" in between.

Don't wrap it up with a tidy compromise where they agree it's "probably a bit of both." It's fine (even good) if they end by spelling out what evidence would actually move one of them — and if they still disagree about what that evidence would even look like, that's the most honest ending.

Keep it conversational, they can be a little tipsy or tired, maybe one of them keeps checking their phone out of habit. Around 900-1400 words for the dialogue itself.
