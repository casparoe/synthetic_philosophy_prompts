I teach ethics to nursing students, and every year I hit the same wall with the care ethics unit: half the class thinks it's obviously right (pay attention to the particular patient, be kind, rules are for people who don't care enough) and half thinks it's just vibes dressed up as moral theory, and neither side can articulate what the disagreement is even about. So instead of another round of this, I want to figure out what people actually believe by sorting them with cases rather than labels.

Write me a "which care-ethics position do you actually hold?" quiz — eight to ten questions, each one a concrete case with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key at the end. The positions I want to be able to sort my students (and myself) into are roughly:

1. Justice-first: care is at best a virtue, not a foundation for ethics — the person whose instinct in any two-patients-one-bed situation is that the whole framework falls apart there.
2. The "two lenses" view: care is a genuine alternative mode of moral reasoning, not reducible to justice, but the two work alongside each other and come apart on different kinds of cases.
3. Relational/dyadic: what matters morally is the actual caring relation, and caring isn't complete until the cared-for receives it somehow — so caring for strangers at a distance, or care the other person refuses, is diminished or different in kind.
4. Political/structural: the interesting unit isn't the nurse-patient dyad at all, it's who gets assigned the caring work — institutions, unpaid labor, dependency — and cases about those carry the real moral weight.
5. Virtue-theoretic: care is best understood as a motivational disposition or character trait that can attach to any framework, so caring shows up the same way whether you're a Kantian or a utilitarian underneath.

Since the whole point is diagnostic, some constraints: no question should be answerable by pattern-matching to a position's name, and no question may be so easy that all five positions give the same answer (so nothing like "should nurses be kind?"). The cases need to split the positions in non-obvious ways — I'm imagining things like the triage situation where caring harder for the patient you know conflicts with the rule; the competent patient who refuses your care outright; the caring work that's invisible because somebody's mother is doing it at home for free; and something where "caring" language is being used as cover for something worse, like controlling a patient's family through devoted attention.

Two more things for the scoring key. First, flag which pairs of positions your questions genuinely can't seperate — I already suspect 2 and 5 will be hard to pull apart with cases, and I want to know if you agree and why. Second, note any answer pattern that comes out of the key but that no actual position occupies, and say in one sentence whether that's because the combo is incoherent or just unexplored. Keep the language at a level my students can read without a philosphy degree — they're smart, but they haven't read any of this stuff.
