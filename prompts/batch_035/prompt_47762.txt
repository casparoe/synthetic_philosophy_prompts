I'm part of a small forecasting club (we predict things like election outcomes, sports, how long our group projects will take, that kind of stuff) and a while back I got interested in the idea that probabilities don't have to be single numbers. I came across the Ellsberg urn stuff, where people refuse to bet on either side of an ambiguous draw, and it made me think most of my own forecasts are kind of like that -- when I say "70% sure this project finishes by June," if I'm honest there's a whole range in there, maybe 60% to 80%, depending on which assumptions I run in my head. So I've been trying to actually run my forecasting with intervals instead of single numbers, and it's been messy. Two things keep biting me. First, updating: when new evidence comes in, my interval sometimes gets WIDER (I think this is called dilation?), which feels insane -- learning is supposed to make me less confused, not more. Second, and worse, I have no clue how to make decisions from an interval. Different rules (the cautious one where you assume the worst number in the range, the one where you take some average) give different answers, and I can't tell if the choice between them is supposed to be another layer of my own uncertainty or just a personality trait.

Here's my question. Since I can't spend my whole life on this, and the club has maybe a season's worth of collective attention to give it, which open problems in this whole imprecise-probability area actually matter most for people like us trying to USE the thing, as opposed to philosophers proving theorems? My amateur shortlist: (a) whether intervals even earn their keep, i.e., whether "I feel 60-80% confident" is really anything more than being risk-averse around a sharp 70% plus being too lazy to settle on an exact number; (b) the dilation thing -- is widening intervals on some evidence a bug that should disqualify the whole approach or a feature we just find psychologically weird; (c) the decision rule question I mentioned (which rule to use to turn a range into a choice); (d) how to combine a bunch of people's intervals into one group forecast without the disagreements just collapsing into an average and losing everything the intervals were for. There's also something I've been privately wondering that I've never seen addressed anywhere: if I treat my future self's opinions as data, does an interval let me "disagree with myself" in a useful way, or does that just recreate all the problems at one level up?

I want a defended ranking, not a survey -- tell me which of these (or something I haven't listed) should get our attention first and why, using whatever criteria make sense (how much depends on the answer, how hard it is for hobbyists rather than mathematicians, whether settling it would actually change our forecasting practice or just be intellectually satisfying). And please push back if my framing is off, like if (a) being settled would make (c) and (d) mostly moot, I'd want to know that. Also happy to hear if you think the whole project is a dead end and we should just go back to single numbers. Answer in American English please -- I'll be sharing this with the club.
