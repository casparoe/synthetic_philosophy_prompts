Here is my problem. My study group has produced an argument about the pessimistic meta-induction that none of us can break, and the trouble is that it seems to cut in every direction at once, including against itself. It runs as follows:

1. Our current mature theories — quantum mechanics, general relativity, evolutionary theory, plate tectonics — enjoy the same marks of success that the best theories of the past enjoyed at their best: novel predictions, consilience across domains, mechanistic explanation, and technological fruit.
2. A rational agent's confidence that a claim is true should be sensitive to the track record of comparably confident agents with comparably impressive evidence who turned out to be wrong.
3. Most past theories that enjoyed those marks of success at the time — phlogiston, caloric, the luminiferous ether, miasma theory of disease — turned out to be false.
4. So, by parity, our current theories are probably false too.
5. So we are not justified in believing our current theories true; at most we are justified in using them.
6. But notice how we know (3). That phlogiston was nothing, that caloric was nothing, that there is no ether — these verdicts come from our current chemistry, thermodynamics, and electrodynamics.
7. If our current theories are probably false in the way phlogiston was false, then the verdicts in (3) are themselves probably verdicts of a false theory, and the judgment that the past theories were flatly false may be an artifact of reading the past through the present.
8. So the premise doing all the pessimistic work, (3), is credible only if our current theories are basically right about exactly the matters in question — which is what the argument was supposed to show we cannot believe.

One member of my group says the argument is fine and simply shows that we can never coherently assert that past theories were false, only that they were superseded. That feels like it saws off the branch the pessimist is sitting on: superseded by what, if not by truer theories? Another says the whole thing equivocates on "false" — that Newtonian gravitation is false the way phlogiston is false only under a strict, all-or-nothing reading that nobody at the seminar table actually uses when they trust Newtonian mechanics to land a probe on a comet. But I cannot see how to make "approximately true" do the work the realist needs without either (a) making it so weak that phlogiston theory comes out approximately true too, or (b) making it so strong that it just restates the realism the argument was challenging. A third member insists the argument was never meant as a standalone argument for skepticism — that it is best read as a defeater for the inference from success to truth, in which case, she says, (7) and (8) miss the target entirely. I do not fully understand what that reading costs, or whether the pessimist can really avoid asserting anything like (3).
