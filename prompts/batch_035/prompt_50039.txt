My book club split into two factions about a year ago over collective memory, and they've finally agreed to stop yelling past each other and write up an adversarial-collaboration style joint statement instead. I've been "volunteered" to draft it, so that's what I want from you.

The setup: one camp is all-in on the duty to remember -- basically Margalit's line that communities can have ethical obligations to remember wrongs done to their members, that memory is part of what holds a community together as an ethical community, and that things like truth commissions and memorials are how those obligations get discharged, at least partly. The other camp has gone full Rieff: too much memory is a load, nations nursing grievances for centuries, history weaponized by whoever needs an enemy this decade, and sometimes forgetting -- or at least letting memories fade instead of curating them -- is the more humane option. Not "amnesia is great", more "the cult of remembrance has costs nobody wants to tally".

Here's what I need in the document:

1. A numbered section of claims both camps could actually sign their names to. About what remembering does for communities and what it does to them, about the difference between remembering and official commemorating, about when something is owed to victims specifically, and whatever else they genuinely share (there should be more than they think).
2. A table of the precise disagreements, each phrased so the side holding the view would accept the wording as fair. No strawmen, and nothing vacuous like "they disagree about the value of memory".
3. The cruxes -- the sub-questions where settling one would actually move at least one camp, not just make them shuffle their feet.
4. For each crux, a concrete case, policy, or piece of evidence both sides would agree bears on it, with each camp's prediction written down in advance. Think stuff like: what happened in Spain after its transition (the deal to just not litigate the civil war for decades), South Africa's truth-and-for-giving amnesty trade, memory laws that mandate how history gets taught in schools, or what a second-generation descendant of victims is actually owed. Both camps should agree the case is relevant before writing down their predictions.

And two extra things. First, they got badly stuck last month on whether "remembering" is an act (something communities deliberately do, like teaching history or building museums) or a state (something that just persists in people's heads whether or not anyone tends to it). If that's a real crux, treat it as one; if you think it's really a verbal dispute about the word, say so and explain which part of their whole fight it drags down with it. Second, if you think other chunks of their disagreement are also just verbal -- e.g. one camp means deliberate suppression when they say "forgetting" and the other means natural fading, so they keep condemning different things -- flag exactly which parts and why, because I want the signed statement to be honest about what's left over once the word games are cleared away.

Keep it in the joint-statement register: constructive, both parties could put their names to it, residual disagreement made exact. It's fine to include short framing notes (like a "status of this document" blurb) if that makes it read like something two camps really signed. And don't turn it into a bibliography -- the whole thing should stand on the arguments.
