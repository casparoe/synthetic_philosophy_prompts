I manage subcontractors for a mid-sized construction firm. I have no academic background — no degree, no training in philosophy, mathematics, or economics — but for the past few years I have been thinking, on my own and mainly from experience, about when it pays to extend trust to people I cannot compel. Suppliers can short me on materials, subcontractors can cut corners I won't discover for months, and contracts are too slow and expensive to referee daily dealings; what actually holds the work together is reputation and reciprocity, and I have been trying to work out the principles behind that for myself. In that spirit I read whatever crosses my desk on cooperation and trust, though I cannot always tell the sound books from the flattering ones.

The latest was a business book my manager lent me. One chapter rests on a piece of research from around 1980, which I have since gathered was conducted by a political scientist named Robert Axelrod. As the chapter tells it: the Prisoner's Dilemma had long seemed to prove that individual rationality ends in collective ruin — where two parties will not meet again, each is better off betraying no matter what the other does, yet both end up worse off than if both had cooperated, so a rational person betrays. Axelrod then invited specialists from several fields to submit strategies as computer programs and set them to play repeated rounds of the game against one another. The winner, in both tournaments he ran, was the shortest and simplest entry, Tit-for-Tat: cooperate on the first move, then do whatever the other side just did. It won, the chapter says, because it is decent — it never strikes first, and it holds no grudges; the moment the other side returns to cooperation, it lets the past go — while the cleverer programs spent the tournament outmaneuvering one another and finished behind it. So, the chapter concludes, the game theorists' pessimism was refuted: cooperation is rational after all, and the proof is that it wins in the long run. Since in real life there is almost always some chance of meeting again, the practical rule follows: be the first to cooperate, return betrayal with betrayal, and forgive the moment the other side does — a rule, the chapter says, as sound for marriages and trade negotiations as it was for the tournament. Finally, the chapter reports that Axelrod replayed the results as an evolutionary simulation in which high-scoring strategies left more descendants, and that the meaner programs died out while Tit-for-Tat spread until it dominated the population, where nothing could dislodge it; cooperation, once established, is permanent, and we should expect trust to become universal as evolution selects for it.

Something in this does not fit, though I cannot locate the fault. Three things bother me in particular. First, the chapter says the game theorists were proven wrong, but it also states as plain fact that a rational person betrays when there is no future; I cannot see how the same theory can have been refuted and still be standing within one chapter. Second, the explanation of the win is that decency triumphed while cunning punished itself, yet the chapter's own rule includes returning betrayal with betrayal, which does not sound like pure goodness — and in my trade, the people who never strike first are also, often, the ones who finish last, which makes me suspect the chapter is flattering its readers rather than reporting. Third, the leap from a simulation of computer programs to the claim that trust will become universal strikes me as too smooth, but I don't know what would make it rougher.

Please take the chapter's account apart claim by claim. For each claim, tell me what a game theorist would accept as written, what they would want qualified or rephrased, and what is flatly wrong. Then answer the question that matters most to me: does the chapter's practical rule follow from whatever parts of the account turn out to be true? If not, state plainly what does follow, in terms I can use in my working life.
