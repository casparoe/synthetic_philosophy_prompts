I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background, but I've gotten weirdly obsessed with what happens to blame when machines make decisions. At work we've been shipping ML systems into things humans used to decide themselves — fraud flags, support triage, pricing — and when one of them causes a mess there's this eerie meeting where everyone technically did their job and yet the harm sits there with no owner. I read that philosophers call these "responsibility gaps" and I've been testing the idea against my own gut reactions. Except my gut reactions don't seem to line up with each other, so I want to know if there's an actual principle hiding in there or if I'm just pattern-matching on vibes. Here are my verdicts on some cases:

- A self-driving car kills a jaywalker at night, and it turns out the system drove exactly as a careful human would have. I feel like this is a genuine gap — nobody was negligent, nobody intended anything, and a person is still dead.
- Same accident, except the manufacturer skipped a cheap sensor to save costs: no gap whatsoever, that's just on the manufacturer.
- A Roomba knocks my tablet off the edge of the stairs: also no gap, and honestly not even really the company's fault. Stuff happens.
- A fraud model at my company silently flags a legit business for months and they nearly go under: I *want* to say no gap, someone at my company should be on the hook, but I genuinely can't point at anyone. This one keeps flipping on me.
- A script I wrote back in 2019 has a bug that deletes a customer's data three years later when some rare input finally triggers it: my fault, no gap — even though there's no realistic sense in which I could've foreseen it. The foreseeable-ness seems identical to the jaywalker case but my verdict is opposite?
- A teammate accepts a diff from an AI coding assistant without reviewing it and the generated race condition takes down prod: teammate's fault. But when I imagine a human writing the same buggy code from scratch, I blame them too, just... less? Not sure why less.

So my rough theory is that I refuse to accept a gap whenever there was some decision point where a human could have done otherwise (skip the sensor, review the diff, write better code), and I only invoke the gap when the system was functioning exactly as designed and everyone behaved reasonably. But that makes the jaywalker case depend on a pretty strict standard of "reasonably," and it makes my own bug case feel like guilt-by-authorship rather than actual responsibility. My friend, who does ML stuff, thinks there are no real gaps at all — just organizations that are bad at tracing responsibility, and the "gap" is a management failure dressed up as metaphysics. Is she right? If she's wrong, what makes the first car case a harm with no responsible party rather than just a harm nobody's gotten around to pinning on anyone? And maybe most useful to me: what does whatever principle you land on say about the case I keep chewing on — a fully autonomous system where the engineers were careful, the operators followed protocol, and the harm still traces back to no decision anyone could have made differently? I'd also like to hear which of my verdicts you think I have to give up, because I suspect at least one of them is doing no work.
