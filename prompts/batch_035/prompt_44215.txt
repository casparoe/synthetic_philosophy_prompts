The question the students answered was:

"In 'Mathematical Truth' (1973), Benacerraf argues that our best account of mathematical truth and our best account of mathematical knowledge cannot both be correct. Explain the two sides of this tension, explain why he thinks they pull apart, and assess one platonist response. Does the dilemma refute platonism?"

And here is the shortlisted essay (I've trimmed it a little but haven't changed any substance):

"Benacerraf's 1973 paper argues that we cannot have both a satisfactory semantics for mathematics and a satisfactory epistemology of mathematics. On the side of truth, he assumes what he calls the standard view: arithmetic sentences have determinate truth-conditions involving abstract objects, in effect a Tarskian account. If '3 is prime' is true, it is true because a particular abstract object has a property, just as 'Jupiter is a planet' is true because Jupiter has a property. On the side of knowledge, he argues for a causal account: a belief counts as knowledge only if the fact believed stands in an appropriate causal relation to the believer. Numbers are causally inert, so no belief about numbers is knowledge. Benacerraf concludes that mathematical knowledge is impossible, and since we plainly do know arithmetic, platonism is false.

The causality point, however, is only the second horn. The deeper horn is set up earlier in the same paper, with the two children who learn arithmetic and set theory together and identify the numbers with the von Neumann ordinals and the Zermelo ordinals respectively. Nothing in either child's practice settles which objects the numbers really are, since any progression would do the work. So there is no fact about which set-theoretic objects the numbers are, and the standard view of truth assigns indeterminate truth-conditions, which it cannot do.

One platonist response, following Gödel, is that we know mathematics by intuition, a faculty analogous to perception, and that intuition puts us in causal contact with abstract objects, which repairs the second horn.

Finally, the dilemma lapses once the causal theory of knowledge is abandoned, which by now it has been. What survives is the identification problem, and structuralism answers that comfortably. So the dilemma does not refute platonism."
