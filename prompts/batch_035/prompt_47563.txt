Among my AI-safety-minded friends, a certain analogy keeps doing heavy lifting in discussions of how artificial agents will bargain with one another. The picture descends from Hofstadter's old idea of superrationality: in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma between ideal reasoners facing symmetric payoffs, each player can note that whatever she decides, her counterpart must decide the same way — the two are effectively twins, and just as I cannot cut my twin's throat without cutting my own, I cannot defect without thereby producing my own defection. My friends then argue as follows: humans are bad twins, since we cannot verify one another's reasoning; but AIs that can inspect each other's source code are good twins. So once agents can show each other their programs — this is the stuff people call program equilibrium or open-source game theory — deals that are impossible between humans, like simultaneous disarmament or irreversible trades with no escrow, become routine, and much of the institutional machinery humans need (contracts, collateral, courts) turns out to be a workaround for our inability to read minds. Audit this analogy carefully. Give me an explicit correspondence table: what plays the role of twinship, of symmetry of payoffs, of deciding the same way, of the throat that cannot be cut without cutting one's own. Then say where the mapping breaks — in particular, whether having one's code read is anything like having one's reasoning duplicated, and whether decision procedures that are merely similar are close enough to identical ones for the twin logic to run; if not, say what the deciding-the-same-way premise actually requires. Consider also whether revealing a program is better seen as a move inside the game rather than a way of stepping outside it. A more mathematical friend mutters about agents proving things about each other's proofs and about fixed points being hard to reach, but you needn't reconstruct that; I want the conceptual audit. Tell me which disanalogies are load-bearing for the conclusion that AI-to-AI deals will be cheap and reliable, and which are merely pedantic. Finally, if the twin frame is the wrong one, propose a better analogy — transparency plus verifiable commitment, perhaps, or something else entirely — and say whether the optimistic conclusion survives the substitution, or whether the twin picture was quietly carrying it the whole way.
