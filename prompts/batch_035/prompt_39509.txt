I produce a small podcast on decision theory and formal epistemology for a technically-minded audience, and next month's episode features a guest known for defending updateless decision theory — the idea that a rational agent should choose as though it had fixed its entire policy in advance of making any observations, so that when it finds itself in a situation it acts on the policy that was optimal from the perspective of the prior, rather than conditioning on the evidence that it is, in fact, in that situation. Draft ten to twelve interview questions that go well beyond having him restate the view. At a minimum I want one question on counterfactual mugging — whether rationality can really require handing over the money when the coin has already come up tails and no deal is on the table — and one on the objection that updatelessness throws away information it has no business throwing away: in ordinary cases where nothing in the world is correlated with my choice, refusing to condition on what I observe just seems like deliberate self-blinding, and I'd like to press him on whether his view collapses into ordinary decision theory precisely when no logical or causal twins exist.

A few more threads worth including. One question should take up the self-locating angle: proponents sometimes suggest that updatelessness dissolves puzzles like Sleeping Beauty by never conditioning on who or where you are, and I'd like to hear him defend that against the reply that self-locating evidence is simply ordinary evidence about the world. Another should probe the reference-class problem from the updateless side: when you ask what policy you would have wanted yourself to commit to in advance, how finely or coarsely do you individuate the observations you pretend not to have? Updateless with respect to the color of a stranger's hat seems absurd; updateless with respect to the outcome of the coin in the mugging seems essential; I want him to give me a principled way of drawing that line, or to admit it's handled case by case. A final one should be more speculative: whether he thinks updatelessness is a recommendation for what agents should literally compute, or an idealized rational reconstruction whose practical upshot might be something much weaker, like a bias toward policies that do well across possible selves.

For each of these, include a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give — for instance, if he answers the mugging question by appeal to identifying with all the agents running the same decision procedure, ask him what individuates that procedure when two agents differ slightly in their inputs or source code; if he says the reference-class issue is still open research, ask him what would count as evidence that one individuation is correct rather than another. Keep the questions pointed but courteous; the audience enjoys a good impasse, not a gotcha, and he is known for being unusually precise about what he takes to be settled versus unfinished, so questions that let him mark that boundary while still feeling pressure are ideal.
