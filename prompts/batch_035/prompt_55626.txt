I have no academic background in philosophy or computer science — I just think about AI risk a lot on my own, mostly on walks, and there's a question I keep going in circles on. The people who write about what goes wrong with advanced AI usually tell two different stories. In one, a system actively goes after influence: it grabs resources, hides what it's doing, resists being shut down. In the other, nothing dramatic ever happens, but our values and our institutions and our AI systems drift by inches — every single step looks defensible, and yet a few decades later we're living in a world nobody ever chose. The more I turn this over, the less these look like two different failure modes to me. In both stories, the engine seems to be the same: a loop in which whatever a system (or a whole society of systems and people) values now gets to shape the environment that everything will learn from later. Today's values quietly vote on tomorrow's. The influence story is that loop running fast and loud; the drift story is the same loop running slow and quiet.

Please argue that this reading is correct: that influence-seeking systems and slow value drift are not two distinct threats but a single mechanism operating at different speeds, and that treating them as separate categories is itself a mistake — for example, it makes us underestimate how much an intervention aimed at one (oversight, alignment techniques, institutional design) is or isn't aimed at the other. You may grant that the two-speed framing is practically useful for prioritizing, but you must defend the stronger claim that at bottom there is one failure mode here, not two. Build the argument from the ideas themselves; don't bring in named authors, papers, or terminology I'd have to look up.
