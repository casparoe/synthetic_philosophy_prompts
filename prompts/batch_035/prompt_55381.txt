I write novels, mostly in the first person, and there is a norm of my trade that I have never managed to state without embarrassment. My narrators may withhold anything at all. They may fall silent at the crucial moment, answer a question with a truth that invites the wrong inference, dwell on the wrong detail so the reader overlooks the right one, and omit the letter that would undo everything; when they do this, my editors call it suspense, and reviewers call it playing fair with the reader. But if a narrator asserts, flatly and in his own voice, something the book knows to be false — if he says he spent that week in Lisbon when he spent it in the house — my editors call it cheating, provided the text has signalled no unreliability beforehand. The distinction my trade draws is, in other respects, oddly placed. It does not track harm: the reader is no more damaged by the withheld letter than by the false alibi. It does not even track deception: both devices get her to believe something false about the narrator. What it tracks, as far as I can see, is only the manner of the deception — what is smuggled by omission and implication versus what is carried by a flat declarative sentence.

So I have two puzzles, and I want them treated as one. First: what theory of the narrator–reader relation does this norm presuppose? Is it simply the ordinary moral distinction between lying and merely misleading, transplanted into craft — in which case the norm is a kind of folk evidence that we do treat those two as different in kind, whatever the philosophers conclude? Or is it a different animal altogether, an aesthetic of fair play, like the old detective writer's pledge never to hide the clue, which would survive intact even if lying and misleading turned out to be morally on a par? Second: the norm sits on top of a book that is, from cover to cover, composed of sentences about people who do not exist. Every sentence in the novel is false in the same way the alibi is false; why do ten thousand falsehoods count as license and one as perjury? Does the norm presuppose that fiction is a shared pretence in which the narrator's sentences carry a game-relative truth — so that the alibi is not a lie about Lisbon but a lie about the game, a refusal to keep the props working — and if so, does the lying/misleading asymmetry even arise inside the game, or only at the level where the game itself is set up?

Complicating all of this, the practice seems to turn on its own practitioners. There are celebrated books in which the first-person narrator's flat assertions turn out, on the final pages, to have been false from the start, with no signal given; readers acclaim the reversal and no one cries cheat. Elsewhere the same device draws the charge. I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory of communication and of fiction that best explains the norm as my editors apply it; to say which parts of the norm strictly presuppose that theory and which merely sit comfortably with it; to tell me whether the theory can draw the line between the praised twist and the condemned cheat, or whether the practice is underdetermined or simply inconsistent there; and to say what would have to change in the workshop if we replaced the implicit view with one on which misleading and lying are equally wrong — whether every misdirection would then become cheating, and whether a novel written under that regime could still do the things I am paid to do.
