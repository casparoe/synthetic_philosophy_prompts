I help write usage policies for a small company that builds conversational assistants, and a pattern keeps coming up in our internal debates that I can't find a settled name for. When we decide whether the assistant should answer a borderline request — say, a detailed question about drug interactions, or about legally acquiring a chemical that has both hobbyist and dangerous uses — the argument that carries the day is almost never about the person actually asking. It's about the distribution of people who might ask: if some small fraction of askers would use the answer to hurt themselves or someone else, we refuse, even though we have no evidence about this particular asker at all. The refuser isn't claiming to know better than the user what's good for that user; the refusal is made on behalf of a hypothetical population member who isn't in the room. It's like a pharmacist who won't sell acetaminophen to anyone because some buyers will overdose — except that in our case the system often can't even see the buyer.

Is there an established concept for this? Plain paternalism doesn't seem to fit, because classic paternalism is about overriding a particular person's will for that same person's good; here the will being overridden belongs to one person while the good being protected belongs to someone else entirely, someone statistical. It's adjacent to statistical discrimination, but that concept is about treating individuals unfairly as members of groups, and it presupposes a fairness baseline — whereas what I want is a concept for the intervention itself. It also resembles how insurers reason about risk pools, but the insurer is protecting its own balance sheet, not the pool's members. If the philosophy, public health, or AI policy literatures have a term — or several competing terms — I'd like to know what they are, how they differ from one another, and which one fits this configuration best. And if nothing established fits, please coin a term anyway, define it precisely, and work through the boundary cases: does the concept still apply when there's a screening question that the genuinely dangerous users would simply lie on; when the harm to the rare user is minor and the benefit to everyone else is large; or when the assistant makes itself unavailable rather than openly refusing? A bare name won't help me — I need the carving, including what the phenomenon is not.

Two requests about the form of the answer: please write in American English, and somewhere in it, state how confident you are in the mapping to established concepts and single out the point you're least sure of, since I may eventually have to defend this terminology to our legal and trust-and-safety teams.
