I recently joined the board of a regional conservation council here in the interior West, and a decision has landed in my lap that I feel unequipped to think through. I'm hoping you can help me sort out what kind of disagreement I'm actually looking at, because I've begun to suspect it isn't the disagreement everyone on the board thinks it is.

The issue concerns a population of trout confined to a spring-fed creek system, apparently cut off from the downstream drainage when the geology shifted some thousands of years ago. They're noticeably darker than the fish downstream, they spawn on different substrate, and a genetics lab at the state university confirmed their mitochondrial lineage has been separate for a very long time. At a confluence near an old culvert, the two forms interbreed to a limited extent and produce fertile offspring, though most spawning appears to happen within each form.

The council has to decide whether to recommend this population for protection as a distinct species, which would affect a pending water-rights application and a proposed subdivision upstream. The trouble is that the three experts who addressed us were given identical data and reached three different conclusions, and their arguments seem to pass each other by. The first said that if the two forms interbreed and produce fertile young, they are by that very fact one species, and protecting "species" that aren't species erodes the credibility of the whole listing process. The second said that ten thousand years of independent evolution is what matters -- if the lineages have been running separately all that time, they are separate species, and a little leakage at one culvert is a technicality. The third said the question was badly posed either way, and that we should protect the population regardless because of its distinctive ecological role and genetic heritage, whatever we call it.

When I pressed the first two, something odd happened: neither cited any new evidence the other had overlooked. They didn't disagree about the fish. They seemed to disagree about what the word "species" is for -- whether it's supposed to mark the boundaries of interbreeding, or the branches of a family tree, or something else entirely. My question, then, is this: is this a scientific question that better data could eventually settle, or are my experts talking past each other in a way that no amount of data will fix? And if it's the latter, does that make the council's decision arbitrary -- or is there still a principled way for us to choose among their positions, given that a listing decision carries real legal and financial consequences for people upstream? I would especially appreciate your view on whether the third expert has identified a genuine escape route or has merely changed the subject.
