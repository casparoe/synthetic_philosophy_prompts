I've been around the rationalist corner of the internet since the Overcoming Bias days, and I've noticed that our recurring argument about wireheading always dies in the same place. Someone describes a rat with an electrode in its reward circuitry pressing the lever until it starves and calls that maximal pleasure; someone else replies that wanting and liking have come apart in that rat — that mesolimbic dopamine is being cranked rather than experienced pleasantness, so the rat is being tortured by compulsion rather than paid in bliss — and a third person mutters that we have no idea what we even mean by experienced pleasantness once it has to cover morphine, orgasm, the flow state of a working mathematician, the relief of a stopped migraine, and the contentment of a fed infant. The first two keep arguing. I want to take the third person seriously for once.

The mutterer is gesturing at the heterogeneity problem for hedonism: the observation that our pleasures seem to share nothing but the name. Before I go back to arguing about wireheaded utopias — and, more importantly, before I trust any cross-species or cross-mind welfare comparison that presupposes a common hedonic currency — I want the full specification first.

So write me the desiderata: what any adequate theory of what pleasures have in common must deliver. Some candidates I can see, which you should feel free to add to, reject, or refine:

1. It must say whether the commonality is introspectively accessible — whether someone who has the pleasures of food and of music could in principle notice what they share — or whether the unity can be purely theoretical, posited against the testimony of how different they feel.
2. It must handle the cases where wanting and liking come apart, and say which of the two the theory is a theory of.
3. It must explain how intensities can be compared — within a modality, across modalities, across minds — or else explain why it is acceptable that they cannot, and what that failure does to aggregation.
4. It must respect the distinction between the glass of wine and the pleasantness of drinking it, without collapsing into the claim that only the latter really exists.
5. It must handle relief — the cessation of a pain, the scratched itch — which behaves like pleasure but seems to lack any positive felt quality of its own.
6. It must tell me whether two beings with radically different phenomenology can both be maximally happy, or whether that phrase can simply fail to make sense.

For each requirement, give the motivating case, and say whether it is a genuine desideratum or a prejudice of one camp — I suspect the first and third pull in opposite directions, since the more the unity of pleasure is something you can feel, the less it resembles a measurable dimension, and if some subset of the list is jointly unsatisfiable, that is the part I most want to see. Then, briefly, how hedonic-tone theories, attitudinal or adverbial accounts, and the deflationary view that pleasures share only a family resemblance do against the list. Finish by telling me which single requirement excludes the most contenders, and whether a theory that satisfied every requirement on the list would thereby be in a position to settle the wireheading question — or whether the unity of pleasure and the value of pleasure are two problems wearing one coat, and no spec of the first will answer the second.
