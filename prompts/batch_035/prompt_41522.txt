The exam question was: "Mencius holds that human nature is good; Xunzi holds that it is bad. Yet Mencius insists that virtue must be cultivated and can be lost, and Xunzi insists that anyone can become a sage through learning. Some scholars have concluded that the two disagree mainly about the word xing (nature) rather than about human beings. Defend or attack that conclusion, drawing on at least one argument from each thinker."

The student wrote: "On the surface Mencius and Xunzi appear opposed, but since both agree that virtue requires sustained effort, the disagreement is largely verbal. Mencius grounds his position in the four sprouts — compassion, shame, deference, and the sense of right and wrong — which he says are in us as surely as our four limbs. His famous case of the child about to fall into the well functions as empirical evidence: he reports having found no one who would not feel a flash of alarm on witnessing it, which establishes that the sprout of compassion is genuinely present in all humans. Xunzi, for his part, is careful to define his terms. Nature is what is inborn and spontaneous; conscious activity is what results from deliberate effort and learning. So when he says that nature is bad, he means only that we do not start out virtuous, not that we are wicked. He compares human nature to water that always seeks the low ground of profit: left to itself it produces strife and disorder, which is why the sage kings invented ritual to channel it in a better direction. And since desire cannot be eliminated, it must be disciplined rather than suppressed. Given all this, both thinkers hold that the raw material of human beings is roughly morally neutral and that the real work lies in cultivation. Calling that nature good just labels the sprouts' potential; calling it bad just labels the absence of virtue at the start. The disagreement, then, is about which word to apply, not about what humans are like."
