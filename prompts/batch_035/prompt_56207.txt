Write the "Exclusions and Limitations of Coverage" section of a homeowner's insurance policy issued by a fictional insurer that, uniquely in the industry, covers only true statements made about the insured property. Each exclusion should quietly take a position on the Liar paradox: include a stratification clause under which no clause may exclude claims at its own level, so that customers who want protection against statements about statements about their roof must buy a separate, more expensive policy one level up; a gap clause that leaves ungrounded statements neither covered nor excluded, with claims held in limbo for as long as it takes the fixed point to settle; and a dialetheist rider (available in select states) under which certain claims are both covered and uncovered, entitling the customer to compensation and to a denial of that same compensation. At least one clause must function as a revenge paradox: an exclusion the policy cannot state without triggering, and which the insurer's legal department handles by simply not mentioning it. Format as numbered clauses, around 600 words, in deadpan legalese. The humor should come from getting the philosophy of truth right — Tarskian hierarchy, Kripkean groundedness, semantic ascent, the strengthened Liar — so that a logician laughs rather than winces, while a reader with no background can still follow the joke.
