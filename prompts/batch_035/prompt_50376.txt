I teach a 300-level philosophy of religion seminar, and for our unit on divine attributes I want a one-page handout: a decision tree that takes a student from the stone question — whether an omnipotent being can create a stone it cannot lift — to a position on the omnipotence paradox. The students have not read the literature, so every branch node must be a question they can answer from their own reactions to concrete cases, not from knowing where the branch leads. Target around ten nodes and at most eight leaves, so it can be worked through in fifteen minutes at the start of class.

Three design requirements, from how I want to run the discussion:

First, build the tree from the logical space, not from a list of famous replies. The nodes should be the live questions in the debate: whether the descriptions of the alleged tasks are coherent in the first place; whether an inability to do something self-contradictory counts as a limitation on power at all; whether omnipotence is essential or merely accidental to God; whether logical impossibility is inside or outside the scope of what omnipotence covers; and whether a being that could do anything, including the contradictory, would thereby dissolve the paradox or merely relocate it. The positions — the Aquinas-style restriction of omnipotence to the logically possible, the pseudo-task reply associated with Mavrodes, its extension by Frankfurt, C. Wade Savage's treatment of the stone, the voluntarist or Cartesian view that God can bring about contradictions, Kenny's degree-theoretic worry that there is no maximal amount of power to have, and reformulation projects in the spirit of Flint and Freddoso's maximal power — should fall out as leaves once the answers are given, with unnamed leaves allowed where nobody occupies a combination.

Second, do not treat the stone in isolation. The handout should sort students by how they treat a whole family of alleged divine incapacities: sinning, lying, forgetting something once known, dying, creating another omnipotent being, drawing a square circle, and making a stone one cannot lift. Some of the best diagnostic questions will come from the cases that are not self-contradictory on their face — if God permanently bound himself never to lie, would that binding cost him power, or display something other than power? — and a node should decide whether an inability can be non-power-limiting without being a pseudo-task. A separate node should ask whether the two-step structure of the stone case (create, then lift) does any work that a single self-contradictory task does not; my suspicion is it does not, but I want the tree to test that rather than assume it. I would also like a node on Kenny's generalization: whether the trouble is specific to power or afflicts every maximal-degree property, including omniscience and greatness itself, such that the right leaf is a reformulation of the doctrine rather than a stance inside the paradox debate.

Third, annotate the tree honestly. Flag any pair of leaves the branch questions cannot genuinely separate — I worry, for example, that the pseudo-task reply and the straightforward logical-possibility restriction end up indistinguishable unless the essential-omnipotence node is doing real work — and for each unoccupied combination of answers, say whether nobody holds it because it is incoherent, because it is theologically unattractive, or simply because no one has bothered.
