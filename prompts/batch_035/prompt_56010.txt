I'm a PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science, and one of the senior members of our consciousness reading group — I'll call her M — is the most consistent Dennettian I know: she holds that a self is a center of narrative gravity, a theoretical posit like a center of mass, something our narratives construct and track rather than something the brain contains as a part. After two years of arguments over coffee I've assembled a fairly reliable map of her verdicts. Normal adult humans: one self apiece. Newborns: no self yet, but selves are acquired gradually over the first couple of years as narratives come to be told about and eventually by them. Dogs: a minimal self, since even a rudimentary center of narrative gravity gets attributed to them and their behavior is weakly self-integrated. Sherlock Holmes: a real fictional character, and in that sense a real pattern, but not a self. A corporation: no self, notwithstanding its brand voice, annual reports, and legal personhood. A split-brain patient after commissurotomy: still just one self, though a strangely fissiparous one, because the narratives about the patient continue to converge on a single center of gravity. Next week the group takes up three new cases, and I'd like to walk in already knowing where she'll land: (i) a hypothetical conversational system with persistent autobiographical memory that maintains a consistent first-person self-description over months and generates its own self-narrative, though M and I agree that attributing consciousness to it would be a stretch; (ii) a second system with no memory at all, starting fresh each conversation, but whose fixed persona prompt leads its millions of users to attribute a stable character to it; and (iii) a human patient with profound amnesia whose moment-to-moment experience and personality are intact but who cannot sustain any autobiographical narrative from day to day. Her track record seems to fit at least two different rules — something like self-narration as the criterion, versus narrated attribution by interpreters, versus a residual requirement of phenomenal consciousness — and I genuinely can't tell which she is running, especially since her Holmes and corporate-personhood verdicts cut in different directions than her dog verdict.

Please respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion. Item one: state the first candidate rule her verdicts are jointly consistent with. Item two: state the second candidate rule, and note whether any third rule also fits. Item three: say what each rule predicts about each of the three new cases. Item four: give your best guess at her actual verdicts, with your reasons for preferring that reading of her. Item five: describe the single further case, not among the three given, that would best discriminate between the candidate rules.
