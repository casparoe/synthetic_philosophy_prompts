I am a graduate student in jurisprudence, and in two weeks I present my workshop paper to a department whose senior members are mostly natural law theorists, plus two Fuller scholars who work on the internal morality of law, one committed Dworkinian, and a visiting legal realist. I want a preparation sheet for the question period, not a defense of the paper itself.

My thesis has three parts. First, on the grudge informer facts — the wife who, in 1943, denounced her husband to Nazi authorities for remarks disparaging the regime, leading to his prosecution under wartime statutes and a death sentence — I hold that the applicable statutes were valid law under the pre-1945 German system, and that the informer's conduct satisfied them. Second, I hold that the postwar German court was right to punish her anyway, but for the wrong reason: the retroactive criminal statute of 1945 was itself an exercise of legislative power whose validity is exactly what a positivist theory of legal validity explains, so the conviction is justified as an act of postwar constitutional politics, not as the discovery that the Nazi statutes were never really law. Third, I argue that Radbruch's extreme-injustice formula is best understood not as a timeless truth about the concept of law but as a postwar change in Germany's rule of recognition — a positivist mechanism doing work that natural law rhetoric takes credit for. I also claim, against Fuller, that the eight desiderata he lists constitute a theory of effective legal craftsmanship rather than criteria of validity, and that reading them as validity conditions collapses the distinction between a badly made legal system and a non-legal one.

For my prep sheet, give me the objections in roughly the order they will actually come, given this audience, each phrased the way its likely questioner would put it. I want the natural lawyers' attack on part one — that I have no principled stopping point between the Nazi statutes and any tyrant's decree, and that my position would instruct officials to apply even grossly unjust law. I want the Fuller scholars' response to my efficacy-versus-validity move, including the likely charge that I have smuggled a moral standard into the rule of recognition and thus conceded their point under another name. I want the Dworkinian's version of the retroactivity problem, which I suspect will come through the later East German border guard prosecutions rather than through the informer case itself. And I want the realist's disruptive point that the rule-of-recognition framing describes what academics do, not what officials do.

For each objection: how damaging it really is, a two-minute reply, what I should concede up front, and where the reply is soft. Also identify the question I am hoping nobody asks — my instinct is that it concerns whether my reading of the 1945 statute as valid-by-mere-pedigree commits me to the validity of any ex post facto law whatever, including vindictive ones, and if that instinct is right, tell me how bad it is and what my least costly answer looks like.
