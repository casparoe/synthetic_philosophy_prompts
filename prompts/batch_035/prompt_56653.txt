Next month I'm recording a long-form interview series for a small philosophy-of-mind channel, and the confirmed guest is a philosopher who has spent her career defending eliminative materialism about beliefs and desires — roughly the Churchland-style claim that propositional attitudes are posits of a primitive folk psychology that mature cognitive science will progressively displace, the way alchemy and phlogiston were displaced by real chemistry. She's an engaging but very practiced interviewee, and past sessions with her have tended to produce a familiar routine: she wheels through the standard examples of past theoretical revolutions, jokes about not believing in beliefs, and then leans on two stock deflections — that the theory is a promissory note payable by future neuroscience, and that anyone who objects simply hasn't grasped how radically our self-conception can be revised.

I want to break that routine. Please draft eight to ten interview questions that press on the actual load-bearing parts of her position. At minimum, include: one question on the self-refutation worry — if she sincerely asserts her own theory, in what does her acceptance of it consist, once belief is eliminated — together with a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give (I anticipate either that "asserting" will be redescribed in terms of some successor state, or that the objection will be dismissed as folk psychology's last stand); one question on whether the analogy to past theoretical eliminations is apt, since folk psychology arguably supports successful prediction and explanation of everyday behavior in a way phlogiston never did, plus a follow-up on what would even count as folk-psychological failure at this point; one on what the successor ontology might look like — neurocomputational states, predictive-processing constructs, or something else — and whether the phenomenon of intersubjective understanding gets explained or merely re-described there; and one asking her to state, concretely, what discovery in the next few decades would count against her view, so that the promissory-note deflection becomes unavailable. The remaining questions can cover whatever you judge most productive — for instance, whether desires can be eliminated separately from beliefs, or how the view handles the apparent persistence of propositional-attitude vocabulary even among working scientists — but each should likewise be paired with a follow-up anticipating her most probable answer. Keep the tone courteous and genuinely curious, as befits the format, but make the sequence progressively harder to deflect.
