I chair the planetary protection advisory panel for a mid-sized space agency, and we're deadlocked on a licensing decision, so I need a written opinion I can circulate before our meeting on the 12th. The case: a private company wants to land a sealed bioreactor payload in a Martian lava tube as a proof-of-concept for what they eventually intend to scale into full-scale terraforming. The tube is one of the few sites where the company's own astrobiologists concede there is a non-trivial chance -- they put it somewhere around 3 to 5 percent -- of an extant chemolithotrophic community, meaning life that may represent a second, fully independent genesis from Earth's. The reactor is designed to stay sealed, but everyone on the panel agrees that its integrity over geological time is basically a hope, and our contamination modeling says a breach means the engineered organisms outcompete anything native within decades, if there is anything native.

The panel has split three ways and it has gotten personal. One faction says microbial life has moral standing if it exists at all: a second genesis might be the only other example of life in the universe, and extinguishing it would be a kind of cosmic vandalism, maybe categorically worse than any terrestrial extinction because it ends a whole separate experiment rather than one more species in a shared tree. A second faction says that is sentimentality run wild -- we sterilize our sinks without grief, nothing without sentience can be wronged -- and the right frame is scientific value: the epistemic loss of a unique data point is real, but it can be weighed against the benefits of proving out the terraforming tech, and if we attach monitoring conditions it comes out in the company's favor. A third faction barely cares about the microbes either way and thinks the whole panel is missing the point: Mars access is finite, whoever demonstrates biological terraforming first shapes whether humanity gets a backup biosphere at all, and delaying a decade for a 3 percent chance of bacteria costs more in expected human flourishing than the bacteria could possibly be worth.

The reason I'm writing to you rather than drafting it myself is one member, who keeps making what I find the most annoying argument because I genuinely can't answer it: if microbes have the kind of standing faction one claims, why is it only extraterrestrial microbes? We kill staggering numbers of terrestrial microorganisms every day without a second thought, and "it's a second genesis" seems to do the work only if the reason we don't count our sinks is that those organisms aren't special enough -- but then the standing isn't really about being alive or even about having interests, it's about being rare and informative, which sounds like faction two's position wearing faction one's clothes. I suspect our decision actually hinges on whether that challenge can be met, so the memo shouldn't dodge it. Please draft the written opinion I'll circulate: it needs to recommend a concrete decision (approve, reject, or approve with specific binding conditions and a time frame), and it needs to seriously engage the strongest version of the faction that loses, not just outvote it. Keep it under 900 words -- the panel stopped reading anything longer months ago.
