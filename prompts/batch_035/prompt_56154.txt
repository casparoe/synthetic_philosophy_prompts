I'm a backend engineer at a studio that runs a long-lived MMO, and I need a philosophical ruling to take into a policy meeting. Last month a data migration bug permanently corrupted part of the character database, destroying a set of items that can never be legitimately recreated: they were awarded at live events a decade ago, the event tooling no longer exists, and the items have become status markers in the community. There's no official real-money market, but these items change hands on gray-market sites for hundreds of dollars apiece. Our VP keeps saying "it's just rows in a database — nothing real was lost," and I half-agree with him, since at the physical level the items are trivially copyable bits. But the analogy that keeps nagging at me is a fire destroying someone's only photos of a late parent: also just patterns in a substrate, and we'd never call that nothing. There's a further wrinkle: we could technically mint bit-for-bit identical replacements, but some affected players have explicitly asked us not to, saying a freshly generated copy wouldn't be *their* item — what mattered was that it came from the 2013 event. Is that sentiment coherent, or is it superstition about indistinguishable data? I have to write a recommendation memo on whether we treat this as routine data loss or something closer to the loss of irreplaceable physical property, and how we should compensate accordingly. Please take a definite position and defend it: tell me what, if anything, was actually lost, whether the copy-worth-differing intuition makes sense, and what a proportionate response looks like. I need a verdict for the memo, not a survey of both sides.
