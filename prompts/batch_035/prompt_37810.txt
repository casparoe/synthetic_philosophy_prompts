I am a high school senior writing an extended essay for my mathematics course, and I have run into a question that seems to have an obvious answer, which makes me suspect I am misunderstanding something. Last term we studied Gödel's incompleteness theorems, and while researching him I learned that late in his life he also wrote down a formal proof of God's existence, usually called his ontological proof. As I understand it, Gödel set out some definitions and axioms in a system of modal logic — a logic for reasoning about necessity and possibility — and then derived the existence of God as a theorem, step by step, the way one derives a theorem in geometry. I have also read that logicians have since checked the derivation with computer proof assistants and confirmed that every inference is valid, so that the argument commits no formal fallacy anywhere.

If all of that is correct, it seems to me that the existence of God has now been established with the same rigor as the Pythagorean theorem. And yet my philosophy textbook still describes the ontological argument as one that most philosophers reject, and I notice that none of my teachers treat Gödel's proof as settling anything. This is where my question comes in: once a theorem has been proven from explicitly stated axioms, and the proof has been mechanically verified, on what rational grounds could anyone continue to reject its conclusion? Isn't a philosopher who dismisses the ontological argument in this situation rather like someone who accepts all the rules of chess but refuses to concede checkmate when it is on the board? If there is something wrong with this comparison, I would like to know what it is, because my essay currently argues that the remaining disagreement is irrational, and I would rather find out now that this is too quick.
