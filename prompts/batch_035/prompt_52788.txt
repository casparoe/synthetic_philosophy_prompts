You are a moral philosopher with expertise in the doctrine of double effect, trolley ethics, and the empirical moral psychology literature on these cases. Please reason carefully, and consider starting with a brief brainstorm of the candidate rules before committing to any predictions.

A friend from my bioethics course has a stable set of verdicts on the classic trolley-style cases, and I want help predicting what she will say about some new ones — and, more importantly, working out what rule she is implicitly running. Her verdicts so far, each given as permissible or impermissible:

1. Bystander at the switch: a runaway trolley is heading toward five workmen; she can divert it onto a side track, where it will kill one workman. Permissible.
2. Footbridge: the only way to stop the trolley is to push a large man off a footbridge into its path with her own hands. Impermissible.
3. Transplant: a surgeon can kill one healthy patient and distribute his organs to save five dying patients. Impermissible.
4. Strategic bombing: bombing an enemy munitions factory while foreseeing, but not aiming at, civilian deaths in the surrounding blocks. Permissible.

The new cases, both described so that no further facts matter:

(a) Loop: like the bystander case, except the side track loops back onto the main track just short of the five; if the lone workman's body were not there, the trolley would continue around and kill them. His body is what stops it. Will she say permissible?

(b) Trapdoor: like footbridge, except that instead of pushing the large man, she pulls a lever that opens a trapdoor in the bridge deck, dropping him in front of the trolley. Will she say permissible?

I am less interested in the bare predictions than in the following: the rules that fit her existing record — as far as I can tell, the doctrine of double effect, the principle against using a person as a means, and the personal/impersonal distinction are all still live candidates; what each rule predicts for (a) and (b), and where the rules agree and disagree; your best guess, with reasons; and, finally, a further case, described concretely, that would best discriminate between whichever rules remain in the running after her answers to (a) and (b).
