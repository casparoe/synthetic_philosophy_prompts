My friend Devi and I run a small philosophy-of-language reading group, and for the past term she has been acting as a one-woman court on which sentences a speaker could sincerely assert without absurdity. The exercise grew out of the old puzzle people usually attach to G. E. Moore, the weather sentence that can be true but cannot be said. Her rulings so far, on a speaker asserting in the first person, present tense, unless noted:

1. "It is raining, but I don't believe that it is" — absurd to assert, even though it could perfectly well be true; she stressed that the speaker might be right about the weather and right about his own disbelief, so the trouble is in the asserting, not the content.
2. "It is raining, but I believe that it isn't" — equally absurd, same flavor.
3. "It isn't raining, but I believe that it is" — she waved this off as "the same thing again": asserting one thing while avowing belief in its opposite.
4. "It is raining, but you don't believe that it is" — perfectly sensible; she pointed out we say this sort of thing to each other all the time.
5. "It was raining all morning, but I didn't believe it at the time" — sensible; a retrospective confession of obliviousness.
6. "It is raining, and I believe that it is" — vacuous but harmless.

At our last session four new cases came up, and I want to predict her rulings before she gives them. The cases:

(a) "It is raining, but I don't know that it is" — knowledge where the earlier cases had belief.
(b) "Either it is raining or I don't believe that it is" — the disjunctive cousin; note that the classic sentence from ruling 1 entails this one.
(c) "It is raining, but I don't believe that I believe it" — the pattern applied one level up, to the speaker's belief about his own belief.
(d) A man standing in front of his burned-out house the morning after the fire says, quite sincerely, with no irony and no change of mind mid-utterance: "The house really is gone, but I don't believe it." Almost everyone finds this a natural, felicitous thing to say.

What I want from you: reconstruct the rule, or rules, Devi seems to be running. Her record is consistent with more than one principle — for instance, something structural about first-person, present-tense assertions that conjoin a content with a self-directed attitude report, versus something pragmatic about what an assertion of the first conjunct presupposes about the speaker's beliefs, versus something that looks at what the utterance implicates rather than what it says. Spell out at least two candidate rules that fit her six rulings equally well, state what each predicts for (a) through (d), and flag any case on which all the candidates agree, since that agreement would itself be informative. Then give me your best-guess ruling for each of the four cases, with your reasons for preferring the rule you favor — I'd like to know where the record genuinely underdetermines the verdict and where it doesn't. Finally, tell me which further sentence, not among the four, would most cleanly separate the candidate rules from one another, so I can spring it on her at the next meeting.
