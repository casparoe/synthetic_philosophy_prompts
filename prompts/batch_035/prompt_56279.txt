I've been circling the dirty hands debate for years — ever since the old LessWrong threads about torture vs. dust specks and whether "shut up and multiply" licenses things decency forbids — and I've noticed the argument never moves past the case du jour. Ticking bomb, drone strike, lying to the electorate, whatever, everyone just re-litigates the case and nothing general gets built. So before I argue another one, I want the spec written down: give me the desiderata for any adequate theory of dirty hands, the "leader does wrong so that worse won't happen" phenomenon. Things that should plausibly be on the list (add or cut as you see fit, with your reasons): it has to face the core paradox head-on — that the act seems genuinely wrong and yet seems like the thing a good person in that office must do — without relabeling one horn away; it has to distinguish real dirty hands from (a) mere tragedy, where every option is bad but none is wrongful, (b) ordinary hard tradeoffs morality already licenses, and (c) self-serving rationalization dressed up as necessity, which I care about a lot because every strongman in history says he had no choice; it has to say what the residue is for — the guilt, the penance, the reparations — and whether that residue is actually the point of insisting the act was "wrong"; it has to handle role morality, why putting on the office changes what you may do, if it does; and it should cope with the successor problem, where I resign rather than dirty my hands and someone crueler takes the job and does it worse — did my clean hands help anyone but me? Maybe throw in a couple of less-worn cases to motivate the criteria, like a public health official who understates a risk because the panic itself would kill people, or a city engineer quietly shifting inspection funds from a probably-fine bridge to a definitely-failing one. Each desideratum should come with the case or argument behind it, not just an assertion.

For each requirement mark it non-negotiable vs. a preference of one camp. My hunch: "the act must come out genuinely wrong, not merely regrettable" is a camp preference that consequentialists will simply deny, while "there must be an account of why we sometimes still demand penance or punishment afterward" is closer to universal — argue with me if you think I have that backwards. And if some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — e.g. if wrongness, all-things-considered ought, and a non-fungible residue can't all three be had without cheating on one of them — say so plainly, because if the spec is impossible that IS the result, and I'd much rather learn that than get handed a theory that quietly drops a criterion when nobody's looking.

Then, briefly, run three contenders against the list: the Walzer-ish view where the necessary act remains wrong and the good leader accepts guilt and punishment; the consequentialist line that if it's really the best available thing to do there's no wrongdoing, just squeamishness we should outgrow; and a role/institutional view on which the office, not the person, both licenses and taints the act. Keep the scorecard short — the thinking should be in the criteria, I can grade the contenders myself. Housekeeping: American English, and no bibliography or reading list, I want your reasoning not your recall of the literature. Also, since I fully intend to quote you back at people in arguments, tell me how confident you are in the load-bearing claims and flag the single point you're least sure of — my money's on either the joint-satisfiability bit or whether the residue requirement is really a requirement and not just inherited piety, but it might be something I haven't thought of.
