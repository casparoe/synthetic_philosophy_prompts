As part of my PhD in philosophy of physics, I run the revision sessions for our department's master's course on scientific realism, and this year I want to replace my usual slide deck with something the students can actually use: a decision-tree handout that takes them from a fairly neutral starting point to a named position in the realism debate. The starting commitment should be something almost everyone in the room accepts, e.g. that the predictive success of mature physical theories calls for some explanation beyond sheer luck or selection of the survivors — and the tree should then funnel them through a sequence of branch questions, each phrased as a concrete case or claim they can answer without knowing which position it is supposed to lead to. So, for instance, a node on the pessimistic induction should be put as a case (something like: when Lavoisier's oxygen theory displaced phlogiston, or when relativity dispensed with the luminiferous ether, was there nonetheless something those abandoned theories had got right about the world?), not as a question naming the induction; and the split between epistemic and ontic structural realism should be posed as a thought experiment about permutable objects — two particles, or two people, swapping every relational property they have — rather than as a request for definitions.

Here are the constraints on the tree. Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first the branch questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the actual positions in that space. The positions that must appear as leaves include: convergent/knee-jerk scientific realism, Hacking-style entity realism, Worrall-style epistemic structural realism, ontic structural realism, Psillos-style selective realism, Chakravartty-style semi-realism, van Fraassen's constructive empiricism, and instrumentalism — roughly ten to twelve leaves from around six to eight branch nodes. Where a combination of answers is unoccupied (nobody holds the view that answers point in), say so in a sentence and diagnose it: is the position incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored? And where the tree cannot genuinely separate two positions — I suspect epistemic structural realism and structural empiricism end up distinguished only by a question students would answer the same way for both — flag that honestly instead of inventing a fake discriminating question; the handout is more useful to students if it tells them where the tree runs out.

Format: one page, suitable for printing, with each internal node a question and each leaf a position name plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment (the objection its defenders find hardest to answer, not a generic description). Please also add a short paragraph at the end, addressed to me rather than the students, on which branch questions you found hardest to phrase neutrally and why, so I know where the tree is doing violence to the actual debate.
