My book club is doing The Remains of the Day (the Ishiguro novel, not the film version, though you can bring that in if its useful) and I've been reading some philosophy of causation on the side, so bear with me. I want a reading of Stevens through the literature on causation by omission, and I want it to take both halves seriously.

The obvious scenes: Lord Darlington decides the Jewish maids have to go, and Stevens carries the instruction out even though he privately thinks its wrong. Stevens stays downstairs serving drinks at the big conference while his father is dying upstairs. Miss Kenton announces her engagement and Stevens basically just keeps polishing silver through it. In each case nothing Stevens *does* is the cause of the bad outcome — the question is whether his not-acting counts as a cause, and the philosophy of omissions is exactly about that. Its a nice fit because the whole novel is narrated by a man describing, in extraordinary detail, things that did not happen.

But heres the philosophical wrinkle I want you to actually engage with. The standard worry about omission-causation is that its profligate: if Stevens caused the maids' dismissal by not quitting, then so did the vicar, and the other staff, and arguably everyone in England. The usual fixes are things like the counterfactual test (would the mauds have kept their jobs if Stevens had refused?), norm-based accounts where you only count as a cause of outcomes you had a duty to prevent, and contrastivist moves where its Stevens-rather-than-the-housekeeper or whatever. Now notice that the counterfactual test might *fail* Stevens — plausibly Darlington would have found another way, some other servant wouldve done it — and yet the novel, and Miss Kenton, and eventually Stevens himself, treat his omission as the thing that matters. So does the novel vindicate the norm-based or contrastivist accounts over the plain counterfactual one? Or does it show that moral responsibility and causal contribution just come apart, and Stevens is a case where the blame floats free of the causation?

And then the deeper bit: a lot of the literature says the reason omissions causally depend on norms is that we cant even individuate an omission without saying what should have happened. Stevens is obsessed with dignity and duty — his own norms are doing the work of deciding which absences in his life count as events at all. Is Ishiguro dramatising that norm-dependence, or is the narration itself Stevens's attempt to have his omissions not individuated at all, to make his life a sequence of things that merely occured around him?

If the club should read one scene aloud and debate it, tell me which. No need for citations, just the ideas. Keep it lively though, this is for a book club not a journal — some of them havent read a word of philosophy since school.
