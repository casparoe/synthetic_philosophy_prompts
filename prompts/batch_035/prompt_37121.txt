My roommate is deep into the big-picture history genre (Guns, Germs and Steel and everything downstream of it) and has been running an argument on me all week that I can't beat but also can't accept. It goes:

(1) Pick any historical outcome you like -- Cortés taking Tenochtitlan works fine. The explanation historians actually give, once you push on it, is mostly not about Cortés: it's steel, horses, and above all smallpox, which emptied the city before the siege was even over. None of those were up to him.
(2) If the factors that fully explain an outcome are all outside a person's control, then the outcome isn't attributable to that person.
(3) The same holds for every historical outcome. Geography, climate, germ pools, volcanic winters -- given enough research, every conquest, collapse, and revolution comes out the other end fully explained by stuff nobody chose.
(4) So no historical outcome is attributable to anyone. Cortés is just a label we put on a conduit; the real actors are the microbe and the mountain.
(5) But if that's right for Cortés it's right for everything: my "deciding" to write this reply is fully explained by my upbringing, my genes, and the temperature of the kitchen, none of which I chose either.
(6) So nobody ever really does anything in the sense history books imply, and "agent" is just the word we use for wherever the causal threads happen to be densest.

He thinks the upshot is liberating -- if germs and volcanoes are the real actors, the triumphalism drains out of history and nobody gets to feel smug about thier civilization. That part I almost want. But (6) seems to prove way too much, and I can't find which step is the trap. The move from (2) to (4) feels fast to me, but I can't say exactly why, since he's careful to keep saying "fully explained." Which premise would you drop, and what do I lose by dropping it? And if you think the argument is actually valid and I have to live with (6), tell me that -- but then explain why (6) feels like a loss and whether it should.
