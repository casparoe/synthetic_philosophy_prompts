I spent the better part of my career as a freshwater ecologist, and I have recently been roped into running a discussion group at the local University of the Third Age: fourteen retirees, one ninety-minute session, no background in economics or statistics beyond what the newspapers supply. Our next topic is Goodhart's law — the thought that when a measure becomes a target it ceases to be a good measure — together with its engineering cousin, specification gaming, where optimising a proxy for what you want leaves you holding the proxy and not the thing. I can lecture about regression to the mean until the cows come home, but I would rather the group feel the mechanism before anyone names it.

Design me an in-class game for that session. I have in mind something with rounds, a legible running score, and a hidden quantity that the score is only a proxy for — something like what I watched with river-restoration targets, where hectares of reedbed planted were easy to count and actual biodiversity was not. Please set out the rules, materials (ideally nothing beyond pens, index cards and a whiteboard), and a payoff scheme that requires no money; specify what participants are told, what they must infer, and the moment at which the hidden quantity is revealed. Then a debrief that maps what happened onto the distinct ways Goodhart's law bites — selection pressure and regression to the mean, severing of the proxy's causal link to the real thing, and outright gaming — and that says where my ecological example fits and where it misleads. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and what I should be ready to say. Please write your answer in British English.
