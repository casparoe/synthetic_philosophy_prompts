I'm a philosophy professor who directs a large undergraduate lecture course, roughly three hundred students a year, mostly first-years from outside the humanities, called Ideas That Shaped the Modern World. During the annual redesign my teaching team — me and two graduate teaching assistants, neither of whom has read a word of Nietzsche — has to settle, unit by unit, a recurring decision we currently make by instinct: how should a given historical figure, episode, or institution be presented? As an exemplar to be admired and, up to a point, emulated; as something to be preserved and cherished, placing students in a continuity they can feel at home in; or as something to be put on trial and condemned, so that they can be delivered from the past rather than burdened by it. I organize my own thinking here around Nietzsche's second untimely meditation on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, and I'd like that essay to govern the course design in a disciplined way rather than as a mood. Its three modes, for your reference: monumental history, which selects and glorifies great individuals and great moments so the present learns that greatness was once possible and may be possible again; antiquarian history, which reveres and preserves what is local and inherited, giving a person or a people a sense of origin and of what they may hold onto; and critical history, which drags everything inherited and customary before the bar and marks it as something we would be delivered from, at the price of acknowledging that we can never fully destroy the past. Nietzsche's criterion throughout is whether the mode serves life — history in the wrong mode, or in the right mode at the wrong dose, produces irony, cynicism, and paralysis; he allows that the modes can blend, and that each is a form of self-treatment of a people or an individual.

What I want from you is a decision procedure the team can run on any proposed unit in under thirty minutes, with no knowledge of the theory behind it: an ordered list of questions about the unit and the students, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates with a recommendation (a single mode or an explicit mixture, with proportions if you can manage that), and — this is essential — where it stops and hands the decision back to a person, because the question is one of values the procedure cannot settle. The most obvious candidate is Nietzsche's standard itself: what counts as serving life, and whose life — the students', the polity's, the discipline's? Please be honest about which steps are genuine tests and which are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and note explicitly where the procedure could misfire, e.g. by letting a TA's politics do the work while the flowchart takes the blame.

Then run the procedure on three units we actually argue about. First, a unit on the founding of our own university, in which students take proprietary pride, and whose founding benefactor turns out, on a colleague's recent archival work, to have made his fortune in ways the department finds it uncomfortable to teach. Second, a unit on Napoleon, taught to a cohort many of whom arrive cynical about the very idea of greatness, where some colleagues want a hero and others want a cautionary tale about ambition. Third, a unit on the scientific revolution, where one faction wants hagiography of Newton and another wants his alchemy and the Leibniz priority dispute front and center from the first slide. Include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer I am likely to dislike, and say plainly that it does so. Finally, state what the procedure cannot do: whether any operationalization can apply a standard like serving life without quietly substituting some contested conception of the good for Nietzsche's, and whether the procedure can even detect the condition he feared most — an excess of history, where the problem is not the mode but the dose.
