I lead the long-range analysis desk at a small geopolitical risk consultancy. Twice a month we review submissions — from our own analysts and from outside commentators — that explain current events by appeal to some putative law of history: empires decline once military spending passes a certain share of revenue, great-power wars recur on a roughly eighty-year rhythm, hegemonies last about a century, and so on. My analysts are split between those who dismiss every such claim as historicist nonsense and those who quietly build client forecasts on them. Please turn Popper's critique of historicism — his argument that there can be no laws of historical development, while historical explanation and conditional forecasting remain possible — into a screening procedure my desk can run on a single submission in under an hour. It should specify: what separates a testable conditional forecast from an unfalsifiable prophecy dressed up as one; how to treat claims that survive every failed prediction by absorbing it ("the cycle was merely delayed"); where the procedure hands the decision back to a human because the disagreement is about values or client relations rather than method; and what the procedure cannot settle. Then run it on three submissions: (i) that great powers which allow military spending to exceed a given share of economic output decline within two generations; (ii) that mature democracies rarely if ever fight one another; and (iii) an internal memo arguing that deep global economic integration must end in either world federation or collapse, since no intermediate arrangement is stable. Include at least one case where the procedure returns a verdict the desk will not like — we have clients who pay for forecasts built on one of these — and be honest about where the procedure is merely a disciplined judgment call. Please answer in British English.
