I'm giving the toast next month at my mentor's retirement dinner. She spent almost thirty years at an anti-hunger NGO, the last twelve as country director, and she is absolutely militant about one thing: famines are not natural disasters. Her whole career was basically built on Amartya Sen's argument that famines are about people losing their entitlement to food (their ability to command it), not about food not existing -- her favorite example is Bengal in 1943, where Sen argued there was no exceptional overall food shortage and yet millions died, which is only explainable politically. She also loved Sen's observation that no substantial famine has occurred in a functioning democracy with a free press, and she repeated it to every minister, donor and journalist who would listen. The dinner crowd will be split: maybe half former field staff and wonky policy people who know the entitlement approach cold, and half donors and family members who, honestly, say things like "you can't fight mother nature" without thinking twice.

Here's my problem. I had a long argument about this with a friend last weekend -- she thinks I'm overthinking it, and that the toast should just be warm and personal and not make it into a lecture. And maybe she's right that nobody wants a seminar at a retirement dinner. But I know my mentor, and if I stand up there and say anything that frames her life's work as heroic disaster relief against the elements, she'll grimace through the whole speech and correct me over dessert. So the toast somehow has to honor her -- warm, funny, personal-feeling, works for the donors in the room -- while everything it says about famine is consistent with her view that these are political failures, things done or allowed by people, and therefore preventable in a way "natural disaster" language quietly denies. Maybe even use the preventability thing as the hopeful note the speech ends on, since that's how she always framed it, but without it turning into a policy paper.

Please write the toast, maybe five minutes when read aloud. Start directly with the toast itself, no preface or commentary before it, and no title. You can put a very brief note after if something needs explaining, but I'd rather you not -- the speech should carry itself. And don't put any actual statistics in it; I can look those up myself, and I don't want to misquote Sen at HIS retirement party, you know what I mean lol.
