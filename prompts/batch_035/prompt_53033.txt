I am a retired neurophysiologist who has spent the last few years reading philosophy in earnest, and I have been working through Avicenna's flying man argument. As I understand the setup, Avicenna asks us to suppose a person created fully formed but suspended in a void: no visual input, no auditory input, no tactile contact with anything, limbs separated so that they never touch one another. Avicenna claims that such a person, if he attended carefully, would still affirm the existence of his self, while affirming nothing whatsoever about his body — no limbs, no extension, no internal organ. The conclusion drawn is that the self is grasped directly, with an immediacy exceeding anything known through the senses, and is therefore distinct from, and for Avicenna immaterial with respect to, the body. I gather there is also a live interpretive debate about what the argument strictly establishes: whether it shows the immateriality of the soul, or only that self-awareness does not require body-awareness, or only that the concept of the self is available independently of any concept of the body.

Restate this argument, and that interpretive debate, in the vocabulary I actually used for forty years: afferent and efferent signals, proprioception and interoception, vestibular input, the distinction between body schema and body image, efference copy and corollary discharge, sensory deprivation, and the well-documented patients who have lost all large-fiber sensory afference below the neck yet remain fully conscious and report a robust sense of existing. Translate as faithfully as you can, and then give me the residue report. Which parts of the claim map cleanly onto questions about which afferent channels carry self-related signals? Which parts have to be distorted to fit — for instance, does the claim that the self is known without any sensory content have any counterpart at all in my vocabulary, or does it collapse into the claim that the system maintains a model of itself even when every error signal is silent? And is there anything in the argument that cannot be expressed at all?

I will tell you where my intuitions sit, so that you can tell me whether they are right or missing something. My instinct is that the argument is an empirical conjecture wearing metaphysical dress: it guesses that self-awareness survives the silencing of all exteroceptive and proprioceptive input, and that guess is in principle testable — total deafferentation, deprivation studies, the residual awareness reported at the edges of sleep. If that is all the argument amounts to, then the inference to an immaterial soul is simply an overreach past the data. But I suspect something is lost in my translation, and I cannot tell whether the residue points to something my vocabulary cannot say, or to something the argument never actually established.

Please write your answer in British English.
