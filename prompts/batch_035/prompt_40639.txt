My housemate works on AI safety and has an argument he deploys whenever I express enthusiasm about any project, professional or personal. (1) Every objective we can give an optimizer — a loss function, a metric, a constitution, a verbal description of human values — is at best a proxy for what we care about, since the true goal is never directly measurable and our values themselves are too fuzzy to be represented exactly anywhere. (2) Goodhart's law, properly understood, has four distinct mechanisms: regressional (selecting hard on a proxy selects partly on noise), extremal (the proxy's correlation with the goal breaks down in the far-off-distribution region a strong optimizer pushes into), causal (interventions that raise the proxy need not raise the goal), and adversarial (other agents redirect your proxy toward their own ends). (3) Each of these applies to every possible proxy, not merely to badly chosen ones, and each bites harder as optimization pressure increases. (4) So strong enough optimization on anything we can specify predictably leaves us worse off than not optimizing at all. (5) Therefore an aligned superintelligence is in principle impossible, and ordinary optimization — science, policy, personal habits — is the same failure on a smaller scale, so the rational response is to cap optimization wherever we find it, starting with compute. I accept that Goodhart effects are real, so I would like to keep (1) and (2). But (5) implies that designing a vaccine, running a clinical trial, and setting a household budget are all mistakes of the same kind, differing only in degree, and that the last century of rising life expectancy is a large-scale failure to take this advice. That I cannot accept. Which step or inference should I give up, what does rejecting it cost, and is there a slide somewhere between the mechanisms listed in (2) and the universal quantifier in (3)?
