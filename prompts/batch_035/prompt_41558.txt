My roommate and I have been going back and forth for weeks about whether being "money-pumpable" is really the mark of irrationality that the textbook argument says it is, and I've started collecting my gut verdicts on cases to see what I actually believe. I'm hoping you can tell me if there's a principle that captures them. Here they are. The classic cyclic agent, who prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A, and who gets walked around the loop by a trader charging a small fee at each step until they're back where they started minus the fees: irrational, obviously, that's the paradigm. The very same agent with the very same cyclic preferences, except nobody ever notices — no traders around, no markets, they live and die contented and unpumped: I still think they're irrational, though I hold it less confidently. An agent with the same cyclic preferences who, when offered the first trade, thinks it through and says "if I take this now I'll just trade my way back down the cycle later and eat the fees, so no thanks": rational, I think, even though their preferences are exactly as cyclic as the victim's. An agent with perfectly transitive preferences at every moment but who is time-inconsistent — at dinner they sincerely prefer to save, at midnight they sincerely prefer to splurge — and a clever bank sells them a commitment account for a fee and then, later, an early-withdrawal option for another fee, so they get pumped despite never having a cycle anywhere in their preferences: irrational, and this one worries me because the money-pump story was supposed to be about cycles. An agent who pays an upfront fee to bind their future self, Ulysses-and-the-sirens style, deliberately buying fewer options: rational, even admirable, though it's structurally paying to be less flexible. And a deeply status-quo-biased agent who demands compensation for any change from their current endowment and is therefore nearly impossible to pump: irrational anyway, in my gut — they leave real gains sitting on the table — even though no trader can touch them.

So here's my problem. My tentative principle was "a preference structure is irrational iff a cunning trader could actually extract money from you," which is roughly the story I was taught, but it can't be right: the contented unpumped agent and the status-quo agent are unexploitable and I still convict them, while the cycle-spotting refuser and the fee-paying self-binder escape the pump and I acquit them. Maybe the principle should be "irrational iff your preferences, taken at face value, permit a pump" — but then the cycle-spotter and the victim in the first case have identical preferences, and I want to call one rational and the other not, which that principle can't do without saying something about how the agent treats their own future decisions, and I don't know what that something is. So: is there one principle that gets all six verdicts, or close, and if it needs clauses, what motivates each one? And what does the resulting principle say about the case I keep flip-flopping on — an agent with cyclic preferences who adopts a strict personal rule at the start of their life, something like "once I've chosen a full plan I never reopen it," so a trader can never get the second trade? That agent is unexploitable and their plan-following looks disciplined rather than arbitrary, but their underlying preferences are still the ones that pumped the victim in case one. Finally, be honest about which of my verdicts the best principle has to overturn — I suspect at least one of them is sentiment I should give up, possibly my soft spot for the contented unpumped agent.
