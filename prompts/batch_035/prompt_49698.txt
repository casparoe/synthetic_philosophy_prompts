I'm a second-year PhD student and in two weeks I'm giving a talk at a small workshop on decision theory and statistics -- the audience is mostly Bayesian statisticians who treat value of information as basically arithmetic, plus two behavioral economists and one old decision theorist who shows up to everything and asks the question that kills grad students. My paper argues that Good's theorem -- the result that free information can never lower your expected utility, since worst case you just ignore it -- fails for what I call "unignorable" information, and fails in an interesting way, not just by violating an assumption someone forgot to write down. I have two running examples: a free test for a condition you can't treat, where knowing changes your utility directly and there's no ignore option because you can't un-know (the updating is involuntary, conditioning isn't a choice); and a negotiation where the other side can observe whether I've gathered information, so my information state is itself a move in the game and inquiring acts as a costly signal even when the information itself is free and worthless to me. My thesis is that in both cases a rational agent would pay to NOT recieve free information, and that this tells us the theorem's ignore-it move quietly assumes the information state is inert and invisible to everyone including yourself.

I ran a pilot version of this talk at my own department and it went badly in a specific way: the formal people said I'd just left a term out of the model and that once you enlarge the action space to include "acquire information or not" and put the info state in everyone's payoff functions, the theorem is restored and there's nothing left to say. The less formal people said the whole thing is just belief-dependent utility, which everyone knows about, and that dressing it up as an objection to Good's theorem is misleading. I have replies to both of these but I don't trust my replies, and I have no idea what the decision theorist will say, which scares me. My advisor just said "the statisticians are right that it's a modeling move, but you might be right that the modeling move is the whole content of the theorem" and then went on sabbatical.
