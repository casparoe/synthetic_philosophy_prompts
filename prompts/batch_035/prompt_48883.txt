I'm the volunteer archivist in a small New England town — call it Millbrook, about 4,000 people — and I've somehow been asked to give the keynote at our tricentennial banquet this fall. So here's my problem. The town's whole identity is the founding legend: in 1724 our founder, Josiah Crane, supposedly hid three families in the mill to keep the sheriff from hauling them off to debtors' prison. There's an annual reenactment, a mural in the elementary school, the high school teams are literally the Keepers. Except, doing the archive work for the anniversary, I found Crane's account book and a letter he wrote the sheriff, and the record is ugly: he took a bounty for turning in two of the men. And it gets worse — I can date the legend itself. The "hiding in the mill" story doesn't appear anywhere before a bicentennial pageant in 1924, which seems to have been built out of a garbled family anecdote. So the community's memory isn't just wrong, it has a birth certificate.

The banquet crowd will be brutal in its own way: Crane descendants front and center, the fourth-grade class that just performed the pageant, and an 80-year-old woman who has played "Mrs. Crane" in the reenactment for forty years and is more or less the keeper of the story. Part of me thinks a birthday party for the town is not the place to detonate all this, and I'm publishing the full findings in the historical society newsletter regardless, right after the anniversary. But part of me thinks standing up in front of those kids and repeating the legend would be lying, and that I'd be choosing the comfortable version becuase it's mine too — I grew up on this story. I also can't shake the feeling that the story isn't nothing: when the mill closed in the eighties and half the town was out of work, the emergency levy passed by a landslide, and people talked about it in exactly the pageant's language, "keeping the keep." A false memory that makes a town act generous is doing something, I just don't know if that buys it anything.

So: write me the keynote. American English please. I have about twelve minutes. It has to comfort and hold that room without me saying a single thing I believe is false — no lawyer's evasion where the audience hears the legend confirmed. And I want the speech to actually take a position on the question, not dodge it: when a community's memory and the record conflict, does the record automatically win, or does a shared memory have some kind of authority of its own that the archive doesn't touch? You can tell me where you drew the lines and why, but put the substance in the speech itself — I'm not going to editorialize on stage after its done. If you think the honest answer is that the banquet is the wrong venue entirely and the speech should say so out loud, make that case by writing it that way, and show me what it costs.
