Hospitals assess pain in patients who cannot speak for themselves — premature infants, heavily sedated ICU patients, people with advanced dementia — using behavioral observation scales that score grimacing, restlessness, guarding, and changes in heart rate and blood pressure, and analgesics are administered on the strength of those scores. Yet for any patient who can speak, the same institutions treat self-report as the gold standard, in line with the familiar clinical slogan that pain is whatever the patient says it is; a nurse who overrides a verbal patient's stated pain level on the grounds that the vital signs look fine is doing something close to misconduct. What view of our knowledge of other minds does this pairing of practices presuppose? Does the behavioral scale commit clinicians to the view that another person's pain is known only by inference from outward signs, and if so, how is that squared with the near-absolute deference to self-report — is first-person authority being treated as constitutive of pain itself, or merely as its most reliable evidence? Reconstruct the implicit epistemology, say whether the two practices presuppose consistent theories or quietly rest on different ones, and describe what pain management would have to look like if the institution adopted a single uniform view instead — for instance, a fully inferential one on which a verbal report is just one more piece of behavioral evidence to be weighed against grimace scores and vital signs, or a fully first-personal one that has no principled way to attribute pain to the nonverbal at all.
