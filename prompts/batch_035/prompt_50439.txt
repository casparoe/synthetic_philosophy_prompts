I'm a PhD student writing on criminal law theory and I've realized I genuinely don't know whether I'm a retributivist or a consequentialist about punishment, so I'm doing the honest thing and just listing my gut verdicts on cases. Here they are. A drunk driver who kills a child deserves far more punishment than a driver who did the exact same reckless thing but whose victim survived by sheer luck -- punish the outcome. A would-be murderer whose victim is saved by an amazing trauma surgeon should get less than the successful one, even though their culpability is identical. I'm fine with day fines, so fining a CEO fifty times what I'd fine a cashier for the same offense. If a city can only enforce speeding against one driver in a thousand, I'm okay with that unlucky driver getting a punishment way out of proportion to the offense, as long as it actually deters everyone else -- this one embarasses me but there it is. An offender who is demonstrably impossible to deter and poses no future danger (he's elderly, his crime was a one-off) still deserves his sentence, in my gut. Framing and executing a totally innocent scapegoat to prevent riots that would kill dozens -- obviously not, no hesitation, and I don't even feel the pull. And here's the pair I can't reconcile: a man who committed a serious assault, fled, and has lived an exemplary life for thirty years before being caught -- I think we should punish him lightly or maybe not at all. But a ninety-year-old camp guard who also lived quietly for decades after committing atrocities -- I think he should be prosecuted to the full extent, no discounts for age or elapsed time, and I'd be disgusted by any discount. Is there one principle that gets all of these, or close? I have a tentative one, that punishment must be proportional to desert at sentencing but that the state may modulate around the desert floor for deterrence, but I suspect it can't survive both my scapegoat verdict and my speeder verdict. So: state the principle or candidate principles precisely, say which clause each case motivates, tell me which of my verdicts you'd overturn and why, and then tell me what the best candidate says about the case I keep going back and forth on -- a jurisdiction that, thanks to near-perfect AI-enforced detection, catches basically every offense and therefore wants to lower all punishments to trivial slaps on the wrist, since even a tiny penalty times a near-certain detection produces the same deterrence. Is that fine by my lights? It feels like it should be, and it feels awful.
