I'm a software engineer trying to get clear on when a person is actually dead, because my verdicts on cases don't seem to line up, and I want to know if there's one principle behind them or whether I'm just being inconsistent. My gut calls: a patient with total brain failure on a ventilator — dead, even though the heart still beats with support. A patient whose heart stopped in the field, declared dead at the scene, who then would have been revivable for another few minutes — dead at the moment the heart stopped, not at the moment the possibility of reversal ended. A patient in a permanent vegetative state with an intact brainstem — not dead, though it feels like the person is already gone. An anencephalic infant with a brainstem but no cortex — also not dead, but I'm less sure. And here's the pair I can't reconcile: we say the person whose heart has stopped and will not be restarted (because they have a DNR) is dead immediately, but we would not say that about a patient whose heart has stopped and could be restarted in thirty seconds but whose doctor happens to be slow to respond — same physical state, different verdict depending on what other people are going to do. Is there a principle — irreversibility of circulation, cessation of brain function, something else — that captures all or most of my verdicts? If one of my verdicts has to go, tell me which and why, and tell me what your principle says about the case I keep going back and forth on: a controlled organ-donation scenario where the heart is deliberately not restarted and the patient is declared dead about two minutes after it stops.
