I'm writing a term paper for a decision theory seminar on extortion between agents who never causally interact, and I've gotten stuck at the stage before the interesting part: deciding what would even count as an adequate response to the problem. The setup, as I'm framing it: an agent (call it the victim) may face an adversary who can predict or simulate how the victim would respond to a blackmail demand before ever issuing one. If the adversary's model says the victim would pay, issuing the threat is profitable and the victim finds itself threatened; if the model says the victim would refuse, the adversary stays home and the victim never knows it was considered. The threatening is acausal in the sense that no actual causal channel is needed for the selection effect — the adversary's simulation does the work. I've come across several proposed defenses: precommitting updatelessly to reject all threats, adopting so-called surrogate goals (deliberately coming to treat attacks on things you don't actually value as if they were attacks on what you do value, so that every threat pattern-matches to one you are already committed to resist), and committing to wildly disproportionate retaliation in order to make any interaction with you unprofitable. But I don't want to grade these against each other's marketing.

What I want first is the full specification. Write the desiderata that any adequate defense against acausal extortion has to meet, with the motivating case behind each. Some requirements I suspect belong on the list: that it actually renders threatening unprofitable given that the adversary models your strategy rather than a fixed behavior; that it distinguish threats from trades, so that rejecting extortion doesn't also destroy profitable offers (the coercer-versus-bargainer distinction); that it be cheap to maintain in worlds where no adversary ever appears, since the typical world may be one where the machinery is dead weight; that it be adoptable by an agent that did not have the foresight to precommit at design time; and that it remain stable when the agent reflects on its own commitments and notices it could profitably abandon them. For each, say whether it is a genuine desideratum or a prejudice of one camp (e.g., the updatelessness people treating precommitment as the only real solution), and flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — I have a suspicion that perfect threat/trade discrimination and robustness against an adversary who models your discriminator are in tension, and I'd like that addressed head on rather than waved off. Then, briefly, score the three defenses above against the list; keep the weight on the criteria, since that's where the thinking is.

Please deliver the core as a table with four columns: "Desideratum" (short name plus one-line gloss), "Motivating case" (the scenario that breaks a defense lacking it), "Non-negotiable or camp preference" (with a sentence why), and "Contenders eliminated" (which of updatelessness, surrogate goals, and disproportionate retaliation each one rules out, if any). Follow it with a brief JSON object having exactly these fields: "jointly_unsatisfiable_subset" (an array of desideratum names, or null if you think the list is satisfiable), "most_excluding_desideratum" (the one requirement that does the most to knock out candidates, with one sentence), "confidence" (high, medium, or low, with one sentence on what would raise it), and "least_sure_point" (the single claim in your answer you'd bet least on, and why). I care about the honesty of those last two fields as much as the analysis itself — if your ranking of the defenses against the criteria leans on contested assumptions about how adversaries actually model commitment devices, say so there rather than in a footnote of the table.
