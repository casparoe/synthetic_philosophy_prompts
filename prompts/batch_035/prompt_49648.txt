You are a political theorist with serious training in both analytic philosophy and the anthropology of exchange, and you have spent more time than is healthy thinking about how concepts travel across societies. I want your careful, considered judgment on a question about the structure of a long-running debate. Before you commit to a diagnosis, brainstorm: sketch at least three candidate diagnoses of the disagreement, test each against what the sides actually say, and only then argue for the one you find best supported. Do not simply survey the positions; the deliverable is a diagnosis of the disagreement itself.

The debate is over whether the concept of political corruption is universal or whether applying it worldwide smuggles in a parochially Western picture of the state. On one side stand the universalists: an official's role everywhere carries duties of impartiality, and diverting public resources for private or factional advantage is a wrong of the same kind whether it happens in Copenhagen or Lagos; they point to the near-universal insider condemnation of corruption, and to the standard one-line NGO definition of corruption as the abuse of entrusted power for private advantage, as showing that the concept is coherent and portable. On the other side stand the culturalists and anthropologists: they argue that the gift/bribe boundary was co-produced with the modern bureaucratic state and its attendant public/private distinction, and that in many societies the things reformers label corrupt — gift-giving along guanxi networks, patronage obligations to one's kin and village, the informal economy of favors around permit offices — are the ordinary and often morally required workings of reciprocity, in the lineage Mauss traced in The Gift. Between the poles sit intermediate views: that corruption is a violation of locally defined institutional rules rather than a universal role-morality; that there is a universal core (naked theft of public funds) surrounded by a culturally local penumbra (gifts, favors, tribute); and that the debate is an artifact of development discourse itself.

The debate has been running in something like this shape since at least the modernization-theory era of the 1960s, and as far as I can tell nobody has moved. Every few years an anti-corruption campaign in some country is offered by one side as decisive evidence, and the other side reads the same campaign as proof of its opposite. Meanwhile the ethnographic record keeps producing awkward facts for everyone: survey respondents in societies with extensive gift economies condemn corruption loudly in the abstract while participating in it eagerly in the particular, which the universalists read as revealed preference for clean institutions and the culturalists read as proof that the survey question is importing the concept it purports to measure.

My working hypothesis is that this is not a factual disagreement at all but a disagreement about what the concept of corruption is for: whether it functions as a term in a universal role-morality of office, or as a term of art within a particular (state-centric, Weberian) theory of institutions — so that the two sides are answering different questions under one name, and each side's favorite cases slide off the other because they are being classified by different criteria. But I hold that only as a hypothesis, and I can see at least two rival diagnoses: that there is a hidden empirical question (about what institutions people in various societies actually rely on to secure cooperation) doing the work beneath the normative surface, or that the impasse rests on bedrock intuitions — about whether role-duties are real moral duties or conventional artifacts — that neither side can argue for without circularity.

Diagnose the disagreement. Is it verbal, substantive, or two questions under one heading? Where exactly do the sides part company — on the facts, on what counts as a case, on standards of evidence, or on starting commitments that don't get argued for? In the course of the diagnosis, say what each side's strongest proponents would actually say about the awkward facts: the insider who condemns corruption and practices gift-giving; the anti-corruption cleanup that visibly delivers better public services; the patron who genuinely feeds his clients with diverted resources and is mourned at his funeral. And be honest about what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: a sharper definition, a natural experiment, a distinction between kinds of societies, a change in the question — or an argument that no progress should be expected, because the disagreement is a stable equilibrium of deep commitments rather than a mistake waiting to be resolved. Reach a conclusion and defend it; do not end on a noncommittal note.
