I've been reading Hofstadter's *Le Ton beau de Marot*, the 1997 book where he takes this tiny poem Clément Marot sent to a sick young noblewoman to cheer her up — *A une Damoyselle malade*, the one Hofstadter calls ma mignonne: twenty-eight lines, three syllables each, every line ending on the same rhyme sound, basically an elaborate Renaissance get-well card — and uses it to think about what translation preserves and what it throws away. One thing the book keeps hitting is that the constraints aren't packaging. A poem that says get well soon, but says it in a shape, isn't just a message plus decoration.

I want you to construct a thought experiment for me that pries that apart, and it has a twist I've never seen used, so no recycled form-versus-content boilerplate. Here's the setup to build on: imagine Marot's poem gets a modern commission. An aging French rock star — famous among his fans for exactly one thing, that he refuses to sing a single word of English and only ever performs in French — is terminally ill. His friends, knowing he loves Marot, want a get-well poem performed for him at a benefit concert, in his style, honoring his French-only rule on stage. So the French really has to be French.

But the benefit is funded by an American committee that speaks no French, and after a previous translation fiasco they've instituted a rule: they will only approve a poem they can verify word by word against the original — each French line has to say roughly what the corresponding line of Marot's says, in an order they can check with a dictionary. Not a beautiful equivalent. A checkable approximation.

Then run the whole thing a second time with the constraint scrambled: the committee now demands genuine artistry — each line a self-contained unit of meaning, roughly the length of Marot's lines, with some strong formal bind running through the set (one rhyme sound, or a fixed cadence) — but they no longer care about fidelity, except the result still has to function as an actual get-well poem for a dying man, not a riff that just name-drops Marot.

Specs for both versions:

- no more than a paragraph each, and no quoting Marot verbatim — describe, don't recite
- realistic — a benefit concert and a committee, no Borgesian narrators, no perfect translators, no demons
- after each scenario, walk through what the artist loses and what the committee loses, and crucially what the dying man has to actually hear for the poem to do its job on him
- then tell me plainly which feature of each setup is doing the work, and what happens to the case if you delete that feature — e.g., if the French-only rule turns out to be benine branding the star himself drops whenever he's tired, or if the committee's word-by-word verification is just a legal fig leaf nobody intends to enforce
- finish with how Hofstadter himself would attack the case if he wanted to hold the line that genuine translation always carries form and meaning together. Is there some version of this setup where the dilemma survives no matter which constraint you strip out, or did I accidentally build a case that smuggles in its own answer? Be honest — if I rigged it, I'd rather know now.
