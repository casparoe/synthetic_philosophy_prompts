So my girlfriend and I rewatched Signs (the 2002 Shyamalan one with Mel Gibson) last weekend and got into a whole argument afterwards that I havent been able to drop. I'm a backend engineer, zero philosophy background, but I went down a rabbit hole afterwards and apparently the problem of evil is an actual huge deal in philosophy, and now I want someone smarter than me to settle the argument.

The way I see it, the whole movie is basically the problem of evil with a resolution bolted on: a priest loses his wife in a completely pointless accident (a guy falls asleep at the wheel, she gets pinned against a tree), he quits God, and then it turns out literally everything about that night was load-bearing — her dying words are the instruction that saves their son, the kid's asthma attack is what keeps the alien's poison out of his lungs, the daughter's weird habit of leaving half-drunk water glasses all over the house is the weapon, the brother's baseball swing is the delivery mechanism. By the end the accident doesn't look pointless anymore, it looks like a setup. My girlfriend says that's exactly the problem: the movie cheats, because a screenwriter wrote the payoffs. In real life nobody shows you the later scenes that justify the earlier ones, so believing it was all part of a plan is just pattern matching on noise. Basically my side of the argument is only winnable inside a movie theater.

So here's what I want: read Signs as a theodicy, seriously. Does the film actually dramatize one of the theodicies philosophers defend (I keep running into free will defenses and this Leibniz best-of-all-possible-worlds thing), or is it something weirder — a story that only works because the camera has access to information a real grieving person would never have? And is my girlfriend right that the honest reading is survivor bias with a happy ending, or is there a version where the film is making a real point, like that the pattern only becomes visible after the fact and that's just what faith is? One detail that might break the tie: the guy whose car kills the wife is played by Shyamalan himself, and the movie goes out of its way to show him wracked with guilt afterwards — no idea if that matters. Lead with your bottom line: is the movie's theodicy something a philosopher could actually endorse, or is it only persuasive because we're the audience? Keep it under 600 words.
