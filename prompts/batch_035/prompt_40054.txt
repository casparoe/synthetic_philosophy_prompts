Please describe the most important implications of the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness — in particular, the fact that a risk model cannot be calibrated within each demographic group while also exhibiting equal false-positive and false-negative rates across those groups, whenever the groups' base rates for the condition differ — for how the two-person data science team at my public hospital should build, monitor, and justify a risk model used to allocate a scarce follow-up intervention that we can offer to only about a tenth of the patients the model scores. I am not asking for a literature survey, a bibliography, or citations. Assume the patient groups we serve differ substantially in the prevalence of the target condition, that referring a false positive carries real costs because the intervention is expensive and clinician time is limited, and that the model is periodically retrained on the outcomes of referred patients only, since we never learn what would have happened to the patients we did not refer. Spell out the practical and conceptual consequences for our situation: what exactly the incompatibility forces us to choose between, how the scarcity of the intervention interacts with that choice, which fairness statistic we could honestly report to the hospital's ethics committee and which we could not, whether there is any coherent way to present different error rates across groups as fair rather than as a defect to be fixed, and what we should say to clinicians who ask whether the model's overall accuracy in the higher-prevalence group is being purchased at the expense of the lower-prevalence group.
