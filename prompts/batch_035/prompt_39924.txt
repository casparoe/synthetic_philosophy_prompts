I got into an argument at a reading group last week about Lewis Carroll's tortoise, and afterwards I tried to actually write down where I stand on the justification of deduction. The result is the five claims below, and now I can't tell whether they add up to a position or just a mood. Something feels off to me somewhere in there, but I genuinely can't locate it, so before I embarrass myself at the next meeting I want someone to audit the whole package. Here's what I've got:

(1) I take the tortoise regress seriously, and the standard lesson from it: no inference gets validated by adding its own rule as a further premise. The tortoise accepts every premise you offer and still refuses to make the move, so rules have to be applied, not believed — the regress is real and not an artifact of how Carroll staged it.

(2) From that I conclude that our title to use basic rules like modus ponens isn't any proposition we know. It's more like a skill or an entitlement, something non-propositional, and I'm fine with that — its not a skeptical concession, it's just what justification of deduction has to look like.

(3) Everywhere else I'm a pretty hard internalist: a belief is justified only if the subject can in principle cite its grounds. A thermometer that reliably tracks the truth isn't justified, and neither is a person whose method works but who couldn't articulate it if their life depended on it. Reliability without access just isn't justification, in any domain I can think of.

(4) In logic and math I accept rule-circular arguments. If you defend classical logic by proving, partly with classical reasoning, a soundness result about classical logic, or you run Gentzen-style arguments about consistency, I think that's legitimate rather than viciously circular. Someone once told me there's a distinction between rule-circular and premise-circular justification and I think I buy it.

(5) But circular arguments are worthless everywhere else: "the book says the book is accurate" gets you literally nothing, and circular testimony is a scam.

One of my seminar mates looked this over and said (4) and (5) can't both stand, which I actually think is wrong — using a rule to run an argument doesn't seem like the same thing as citing your own conclusion as a premise — but I couldn't fully say why, and I half suspect she spotted something real in the wrong place. So don't give me a menu of possible diagnoses with the choice left up to me. Tell me which pairs are actually in tension, which only look like it, and order them by how bad they are rather than flagging everything equally. Then commit: if something has to give, say which one gives, and tell me what it costs me elsewhere in the list; if you think the whole thing survives without dropping anything, own that verdict too and take responsibility for it. I'd much rather you err toward a definite answer than hedge.
