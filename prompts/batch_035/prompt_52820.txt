I teach an upper-level course that mixes epistemology and decision theory, about 18 students, one 80-minute session. Next week we get to the common prior assumption, Aumann's agreement stuff, and the no-trade results around it, and honestly students never feel why any of this is surprising -- on paper "rational agents can't agree to disagree" just reads like a slogan, and they file it away and move on. Design an in-class game where they place bets with each other based on private signals about some unknown quantity, where the trap is that the other side's willingness to accept the bet is itself evidence, so if they reason it through the volume of trades should dry up on its own before the quantity is ever revealed. I want rules, materials, how to seed the private information (asymmetric signals, maybe some noise), and a payoff scheme using play money or candy rather than real stakes. Then a debrief that maps what actually happened onto the distinction between everyone knowing something and something being common knowledge, and that shows where the common prior assumption is doing real work vs. where it's just an idealization we pretend is fine. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about which part of the theorem the game simply cannot reproduce no matter how I run it -- I suspect genuine common knowledge is one of those things, but I'd rather hear it from you than find out in front of the class.
