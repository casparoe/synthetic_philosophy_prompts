I sit on the pharmacy and therapeutics committee of a mid-sized regional health plan, and every few weeks we face a request that turns on the line between treating a condition and enhancing someone who is, clinically speaking, healthy. Our current practice, I have to admit, amounts to pattern matching: if the requester has a diagnosis code, we usually approve; if not, we usually deny, and no one can articulate why that is the right criterion. Last weekend this came to a head in a long argument with a friend of mine, a sports physician who thinks the whole therapy/enhancement distinction is bankrupt. She pressed the objections I half-knew were coming: that the line tracks statistical normality rather than any coherent notion of functioning, and that "normal" is a moving target (normal for whom — the whole species, an age band, a sex, the applicant's own baseline before they started declining?); that ordinary therapy constantly pushes people above species-typical functioning, so the categories bleed into each other; and that in cases like idiopathic short stature the diagnosis seems manufactured by the existence of a treatment, which makes the distinction look circular. I came away still convinced there is something real in it, but I can't run a committee on "something real," and that is what I need your help with.

Please write a decision procedure my committee could actually run when one of these cases lands on our agenda. Our reviewers are clinicians, not philosophers, so it has to be usable in about twenty minutes per case by people who will never read the underlying literature; it has to produce a written rationale we can attach to the decision; and it must say explicitly, at each step, whether the question is one of fact that the procedure answers or one of values that has to be handed to a person, since our appeals process requires us to be honest about which is which. For each question in the sequence, give the criteria for a sufficient answer, and mark the points where the procedure stops with a denial versus where it escalates to judgment. Then work the procedure through three cases in full: a healthy adolescent in the first centile for height requesting growth hormone despite no hormonal deficiency; a surgeon with no ADHD diagnosis requesting stimulants to sustain alertness through long operations; and a patient with entirely normal cholesterol requesting a newly covered gene therapy that would push his LDL well below anything diet or statins achieve. I want to know honestly where the procedure misfires, what it cannot do even when followed perfectly, and specifically whether it yields an answer I won't like — my expectation is that it denies the short-stature case, and if that is what it gives, say so rather than softening it, because my committee will spot the softening even if the appeals panel doesn't.
