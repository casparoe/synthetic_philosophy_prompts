I'm a PhD student in machine learning working on RLHF, and I help run the discussion section for a graduate course on aligning learned systems with human intent. Next week's section covers the distinction between outcome-based supervision (rewarding the final answer or end result) and process-based supervision (rewarding the individual steps of reasoning as they're produced), together with reward hacking and Goodhart-style failures. I want the students to feel the tension rather than just hear about it — most of them have read the standard arguments, so I'd rather the problem emerge from their own incentives.

Design an in-class activity for this setting: 16 students, one 90-minute section, a seminar room with movable desks, no computers needed. The activity should work roughly like this, though you're free to restructure: over several rounds, some students play the role of a learned policy whose only objective is to maximize their score from the supervisors, while other students play supervisors who must award scores under changing information conditions — in early rounds they can only see the final artifact (an answer, a solution, a delivered product), in later rounds they can inspect the intermediate work, and in a final round they can ask questions about the reasoning before committing a score. The tasks the policy-players attempt should be chosen so that there are cheap shortcuts that produce outputs scoring highly under outcome-only inspection while being subtly wrong, defective, or misspecified — I want the policy-players to discover and exploit these on their own, without ever being told that exploits exist or that the topic of the section is reward hacking. The name "reward hacking," and any framing in those terms, should come only in the debrief.

Please specify: the concrete tasks and their built-in shortcuts; the full rules, including how roles rotate between rounds so everyone experiences both sides; a scoring system using tokens or points rather than money, with a payoff structure that makes supervisors want to be accurate and policy-players want to maximize score regardless of genuine quality; a round-by-round timeline fitting the 90 minutes; and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the formal ideas — outcome supervision, process supervision, the tradeoff where step-level feedback constrains exploration and can reward plausible-looking rather than genuinely correct reasoning, and Goodhart's law. I'd especially like the debrief to handle the case where policy-players in the process-supervision rounds start producing fake-but-plausible intermediate steps, since that's the phenomenon the students most need to sit with.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it, and be honest about which features of the real setting the activity cannot reproduce — for instance, that a policy trained by gradient descent doesn't strategically choose to hack the way a student gleefully does, that real optimization pressure is applied over millions of updates rather than one section, and that our student supervisors know an exploit is coming in a way that human raters deployed in production do not. For each of these gaps, say whether I should paper over it in the debrief or explicitly flag it as a disanalogy.
