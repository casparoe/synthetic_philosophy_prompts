Over dinner, two colleagues of mine — a German and an American — got into a sustained argument about how much citizens should let their governments know about them. It was sparked by a mundane anecdote: the American mentioned that he had filed his taxes in twenty minutes, mostly by approving a prefilled return the tax authority had prepared from reports that employers and banks are required to send in. (1) The German said this was precisely what she objects to: not that anyone is hiding wrongdoing, but that the state assembles a picture of a person which that person never sees, cannot check, and cannot correct — the problem, she insisted, is the asymmetry of knowledge, not secrets. (2) The American replied that the asymmetry runs the other way: he knows far more about himself than any file ever will, and every datum in that file exists only because citizens initiated it — you ask your employer to pay you and your bank to hold your money, and the reports are downstream of your own choices; you cannot commission the reports and then complain that they arrived. (3) She answered that this conflates disclosure with inference: each individual disclosure was consented to, but what the state ends up with — conclusions about her health, her income trajectory, her relationships, drawn from combinations she never agreed to as combinations — was not. No single item is sensitive; the profile is. (4) He conceded the inference point but drew a consequence she did not expect: if the real trouble is inference rather than collection, then the German strategy of restricting collection is aimed at the wrong target, and his preferred arrangement — let collection proceed for the sake of convenience, but require the state to show you every inference it draws about you — would protect more actual privacy with less friction. (5) She replied that transparency is toothless: being able to view a profile is not being able to contest it, and what she wants is a line beyond which certain inferences may not be drawn at all, however the underlying data was obtained — a line, she added, that cannot be drawn by listing data types, because the entire issue is that sensitive conclusions follow from individually insensitive inputs.

A third colleague, who grew up in China, listened to all of this and then said they were answering a question that comes after the real one: in his experience, the state's knowledge was never the burden; the burden was having no channel through which to dispute what the state did with that knowledge — and both positions, he observed, quietly assume the state is an adversary to be kept ignorant rather than a party that must remain answerable.

None of the three is a crank, and as far as I can tell each move has something going for it. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each of the five moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating. Then assess the third colleague's remark: does it dissolve the dispute, relocate it, or miss it? Finally, tell me whether the disagreement, on your analysis, ultimately bottoms out in an empirical question, a conceptual one, or a difference in what the two of them take the state to be.
