Write a parody of a "We've updated our Terms of Service" email from a group-decision app — think a shared-calendar-plus-polling product — announcing a new "honest balloting" mode that guarantees no user can ever benefit by misreporting their preferences. The fine print should quietly concede the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem: with three or more options on the ballot, no voting rule that takes everyone's rankings seriously and doesn't anoint one fixed user in advance can be strategy-proof. So the numbered clauses should let the company deliver on its guarantee by the only routes the theorem leaves open: capping ballots at two options, requiring users to certify in advance that their preferences are single-peaked along a left–right axis (with a procedure for what happens when a user turns out not to be single-peaked after all), and appointing a "Decider of Record" whose sincere ranking is, technically, everyone's ranking. Include a dispute-resolution clause that breaks deadlocks by random dictatorship, presented as an exciting feature, and a liability clause limiting the company's guarantees to menus of exactly two alternatives. The humor should come from getting the actual theorem and its genuine escape routes right, so that a social choice theorist recognizes each clause as a real impossibility condition or domain restriction rather than a gag; don't name the theorem or any authors in the email itself. About 600 words, formatted as a breezy corporate preamble followed by numbered clauses. Keep it deadpan throughout: the email never admits there's a problem, only that the product has been "simplified for your convenience."
