I am a retired physical chemist who, since leaving the lab, has taken to attending a philosophy discussion group that meets monthly in the back room of a bookshop. At our most recent meeting, the topic was the Liar paradox, and two of the members became locked in a genuinely interesting dispute that the rest of us could not resolve. I will try to reconstruct the exchange faithfully, in numbered steps, because I would very much like a careful verdict on each move.

The sentence under discussion was, of course, "This sentence is false."

(1) Daniel opened by denying that the sentence says anything at all. He argued that just as the legendary barber who shaves all and only those men who do not shave themselves can be shown never to have existed, the liar sentence can be shown never to constitute a genuine statement: it is grammatical English, certainly, but it fails to express any proposition, because a proposition must have determinate truth conditions, and the liar's truth conditions are circular in a way that leaves them undefined. Paradoxes of this kind, he said, are symptoms that we have been invited to consider a statement that is not really there.

(2) Margit objected that this misdescribes the phenomenology of the case. We understand the liar sentence perfectly well; nothing about it is opaque or confusing the way a meaningless string of words would be. She then argued that Daniel's move proves too much: consider the structurally identical sentence "This sentence is true." It is self-referential in exactly the same manner, yet it is entirely harmless — no contradiction follows from it. If self-reference or circularity destroyed meaning, the harmlessness of the truth-teller sentence would be inexplicable, since on Daniel's diagnosis it ought to be meaningless for precisely the same reasons. Yet we can see that the truth-teller is a perfectly well-formed claim about a sentence, one that simply lacks any way of settling its truth value.

(3) Daniel said he was content to bite that bullet: in his view the truth-teller sentence also fails to express a proposition. Its apparent harmlessness is an illusion of habit; it is not meaningful but merely indeterminate, and indeterminacy of that pathological, self-grounding sort is just what it is for a sentence to fail to say anything.

(4) Margit found this unacceptable, on two grounds. First, Daniel now owes us a principled criterion for which sentences express propositions, and the only one visible would be something like "the ones that don't generate paradox" — which is shamelessly circular, since it explains the paradoxical sentences by the fact that they are paradoxical. Second, she offered her own diagnosis: the liar sentence is meaningful, and the correct response is to keep meaning fixed and adjust our logical background assumptions. Specifically, she suggested that some meaningful sentences simply fail to be true or false — so that the principle of bivalence, and perhaps the law of excluded middle, must be restricted — or alternatively that the liar is both true and false, with classical logic replaced by a paraconsistent logic in which contradictions do not explode into triviality.

(5) Daniel replied that this gets the methodology exactly backwards. Logic, he said, is the instrument by which we assess everything else, including theories of meaning; when an apparent sentence conflicts with logic, it is the sentence that must go, not the logic. And he pressed on the paraconsistent option: if the liar is both true and false, then the law of non-contradiction fails, and that law is, he claimed, the single most secure item in human knowledge — we could not so much as state the paraconsistent position without presupposing it.

(6) Margit answered with what she took to be an appeal to my professional sympathies. She said that Euclidean geometry and Newtonian mechanics were each regarded, for centuries, as the most secure knowledge we possessed, and each was revised when the data demanded it — not by fiat, but because holding onto it cost more than giving it up. The liar sentence, she said, is our anomaly: a stubborn piece of data that any theory of truth must accommodate, and Daniel's theory accommodates it only by declaring the data to be fake.

At that point the moderator called time, and the group dispersed without agreement. Would you adjudicate this exchange move by move? For each of the six steps, please say whether the speaker's reasoning on that step is sound, and explain what is right and what is wrong about it — not merely who wins overall. In particular, I should like your view on whether Daniel's analogy between the barber paradox and the liar holds up, whether Margit's use of the truth-teller sentence against him succeeds, whether Daniel's criterion is really as circular as she claims, and whether the physics analogy in step (6) is a fair model for revising logic or a disanalogy that undermines her case. Finally, if there is some consideration both of them overlooked that would restructure the whole dispute, please say what it is and how it would affect the verdict.
