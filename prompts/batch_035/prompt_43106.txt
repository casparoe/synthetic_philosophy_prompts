I'm writing a short paper on Mill and India and want feedback on the argument before I develop it further. Basically my claim is that there's an underappreciated tension in Mill's defense of the East India Company, and I want to know if it holds up or if I'm strawmanning him somewhere.

Here's the draft outline:

1. Mill spent basically his whole working life (35 years or so) at the East India Company, and he defended Company rule in front of Parliament when the Charter came up for renewal. Famously he never set foot in India and didn't know any Indian languages — everything he knew came through reports, and through his father James Mill's History of British India, which was pretty contemptuous of Indian learning and institutions.

2. My point: Mill is the great champion of the epistemic importance of experience — his empiricism, his "experiments in living" argument for liberty, his insistence in On Liberty that you can't know what's good for someone else without hearing their side. Yet when it comes to India he was apparently fine with a system where the governed never got to speak for themselves, and where the "knowledge" the rulers acted on was secondhand at best. It's not just that he was inconsistent or a hypocrite — lots of people have said that — but that his own epistemology gives us the tools to diagnose exactly what was wrong with benevolent despotism: it's structurally like the silenced opinion in the liberty argument, except applied to a whole civilization.

3. The objection I'm worried about: Mill explicitly says despotism is legitimate for "barbarians" and that this is compatible with the liberty principle because barbarians aren't capable of the kind of reciprocal discussion that liberty presupposes. So maybe there's no real tension — he can consistently deny that Indians were owed the epistemic standing his principles require. But then I want to argue this makes things worse, not better: the barbarism claim was itself the product of the epistemically closed system (James Mill's History and Company records), so the theory ends up laundering ignorance into a principled exemption from the theory. The exclusion is self-insulating.

4. Maybe a paragraph at the end on whether this generalizes — i.e. whether every "improvement" oriented paternalism has this feature, where the judgment that someone can't be reasoned with is itself made without reasoning with them.

So: does this work as an argument? Is the "self-insulating exclusion" move in (3) actually novel or has someone basically made it? And am I being fair to Mill in step 2 — is there anywhere he addresses the secondhand-knowledge problem directly? Also feel free to tell me if part 3 is doing too much work and the paper should just be about that.
