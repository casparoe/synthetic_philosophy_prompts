I run the graduate seminar in environmental ethics, and for the past several weeks the discussion has been stuck on one question: does destroying habitat reduce wild animal suffering? My students divide cleanly into two camps, and neither has moved. On one side are those persuaded by the wild-animal-suffering argument: since most wild animals are r-strategists that produce enormous broods, the overwhelming majority of which die before maturity through starvation, disease, parasitism, or predation, a mature forest plausibly contains more aggregate suffering than the pasture or parking lot that might replace it — so on their view, habitat preservation and rewilding are not obviously good for the animals themselves, and may be bad. On the other side are the conservation-minded students, for whom declining to protect a wetland because of what happens inside it borders on the immoral: they talk about the integrity and stability of ecosystems, the value of species persisting, and a conviction that flourishing and suffering in wild populations are intertwined in a way a ledger of premature deaths cannot capture. Strikingly, both camps accept the ecological facts and both accept that suffering is bad, yet neither can produce the one number that seems to matter — the sign of net welfare in an intact ecosystem — and I have begun to suspect the disagreement was never about that number at all. My working hypothesis is that the real work is being done elsewhere: by unavowed commitments in population ethics (whether adding lives that end badly is worse than not adding them), by totalist aggregation as a silent premise, and by 'naturalness' functioning as a non-instrumental value that neither side will own up to. Is that hypothesis right? Diagnose the disagreement — verbal, empirical, normative at base, or two questions (what to do versus what to value) being run under a single heading — using what each side actually says rather than what the literature says about them, and then tell me honestly what, if anything, would count as progress: a credible estimate of the sign of wild welfare, a settled population ethic, a conceptual distinction no one has yet drawn, or nothing, because the question is ill-posed. I want the diagnosis and its support, not a survey of positions.
