I recently took over as the essays editor of a general-audience science and ideas magazine, and I have discovered that no topic attracts misapplied depth quite like Gödel's incompleteness theorems. Nearly every batch of submissions contains at least one that leans on them, and I am expected to write reasoned acceptances and rejections rather than gut reactions. The trouble is that my gut reactions are not obviously underwritten by any principle I can state, and I would rather find one before I start signing letters. Below are my verdicts on a representative sample of the kinds of arguments that cross my desk; assume each is argued competently, since the question is not about the quality of the prose.

One: because any consistent formal system containing arithmetic leaves some arithmetical truths unprovable, there are truths about the physical world that science can never in principle establish, and scientism is thereby refuted. My verdict: bogus. Two: because the mind can see the truth of the Gödel sentence for any given consistent machine, the mind is not a machine. My verdict: also bogus, though I confess that when I try to say exactly why, my answer sounds uncomfortably like my answer to the next case. Three: within any fixed formal system, one cannot prove that most particular strings are algorithmically incompressible, since proving a string incompressible would let the system establish its own consistency. My verdict: legitimate — this seems to me real mathematics, honestly deployed. Four: no nontrivial program can fully verify its own correctness, so critical software needs independently constructed checkers rather than self-certification, and this is a genuine engineering consequence of incompleteness-adjacent results. My verdict: legitimate, and I would run the essay. Five: any codified legal system, being a formal rulebook of sufficient expressive power, contains statements it can neither establish nor refute, so gaps are unavoidable and judges must inevitably exercise judgment that is not itself codified. My verdict: this one I keep flip-flopping on. The conclusion feels right to me, but I suspect the Gödel machinery is doing no real work — that gaps in law have humbler sources — and I cannot decide whether that suspicion is enough to reject the piece. Six: no sufficiently strong and consistent theory can certify its own consistency, so science as a whole rests on faith rather than proof. My verdict: bogus, and more rhetorically inflated than the first.

What I want from you is a principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely enough that an editor could apply it: something that distinguishes the legitimate transfers from the bogus ones and explains the distinction, rather than merely labeling cases. It should tell me what makes three and four work while one, two, and six fail, and it must confront the pair I find hardest to reconcile — two versus four — since both move from limits on formal systems to limits on actual physical or mental systems, and I accept one move but not the other. I also want to know whether my soft verdict on five survives: does the principle vindicate the legal-systems essay, kill it, or show that its conclusion is true for reasons that have nothing to do with incompleteness, in which case I should ask the author to rewrite around those reasons? Finally, test the principle against a case I have not listed — say, a submission arguing that a sufficiently powerful AI can never fully model itself, and that this limitation is a Gödelian one rather than a garden-variety resource constraint — and tell me which of my verdicts, if any, the principle forces me to give up. I would rather overturn one of my own judgments than publish a profound-sounding essay on the strength of an editorial instinct I cannot articulate.
