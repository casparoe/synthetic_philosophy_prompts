I teach a general-education seminar in which students spend a semester reading biographies — Plutarch's paired Lives, a couple of medieval saints' lives, some modern political and scientific biographies — and I have to justify, in the syllabus I'm writing, what exactly this is supposed to do for them. Before I read any more theory on the subject, I want to know what I should be demanding of one. Please write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of moral learning from lives must deliver — which phenomena it must explain, which cases it must get right, which distinctions it can't afford to collapse — with the case or consideration motivating each requirement. For instance: such a theory presumably has to handle morally mixed lives, since Plutarch expects us to learn from Alexander and Alcibiades while seeing their vices plain; it has to say something about the fact that biographers select and shape — Plutarch announces in the opening of the Life of Alexander, in Bernadotte Perrin's translation, that "it is not Histories that I am writing, but Lives; and in the most illustrious deeds there is not always a manifestation of virtue or vice, nay, a slight thing like a phrase or a jest often makes a greater revelation of character than battles where thousands fall, or the greatest armaments, or sieges of cities," while the hagiographer goes further and simply edits out what doesn't edify, sometimes at the price of inventing what does. Does the theory require historicity, or can a partly false life teach a true virtue? It also needs to distinguish admiring a person from imitating them (a student who imitates Alexander's drinking has learned something, but not what the seminar was for), to say what exactly is acquired — maxims, perceptiveness, motivation, a self-narrative? — and to say whether what's learned generalizes beyond the exemplar or is stuck at the level of particular episodes.

A few things I especially want you to do with the list. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are prejudices of a particular camp — I suspect the historicity requirement is doing controversial work, since the hagiographic tradition would simply reject it and Plutarch's own practice of privileging jests over sieges is not straightforwardly aiming at the historian's kind of truth either. Flag any pair of desiderata you suspect can't be jointly satisfied — I have a feeling that "the life must be shaped so as to edify" and "the life must not pre-form the lesson so completely that the learner has nothing to do" are in tension, but work that out properly, and say whether the tension is fatal or just sets a design space. And tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude candidates, since that's what will decide my syllabus's framing. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases, not on scoring contenders.

That said, once the list is in place, do give a brief scorecard of three approaches against it, so I can see the desiderata working: Plutarch's own practice, as far as it can be reconstructed from his prefaces and his habit of pairing a Greek with a Roman for comparison; the hagiographic template descended from something like Athanasius' Life of Antony, with its removal of flaws and its climactic holy death; and a modern exemplarist view along the lines of Linda Zagzebski's, where admiration of exemplary persons is the basic moral concept and rules are derived from the exemplars rather than vice versa. Where each one fails a desideratum, note whether the failure is internal to the approach or could be patched. And at the end, tell me if there is a case a student of mine might meet in a biography — say, coming to admire someone they have good reason to think never existed in quite the way the book describes — that your best-supported reading of the desiderata can't handle, since that's the case I'd want to hear discussed in class.
