I host a philosophy-of-science podcast aimed at grad students and statistically literate non-specialists, and next month's guest is a philosopher who has spent her career defending the single-case propensity interpretation of probability -- the view that a probability is a real, physical disposition of a particular trial or setup to produce a particular outcome, so that a given radium atom really has, right now, a propensity of such-and-so to decay within the hour, and this is not shorthand for anything about long runs or anyone's degrees of belief. She is allergic to rehashing the standard frequency-versus-subjectivism survey, and she is very good at compressing objections into a friendly-sounding "that's a nice epistemology point, but I'm doing metaphysics" and moving on. I have about 45 minutes. Draft me eight to ten questions that do more than invite a restatement of the view, with at least one question each on the following, and for each of those, a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give.

First, the asymmetry problem: ordinary probabilities invert freely through Bayes' theorem -- the probability of decay given the state is exactly as well-defined as the probability of the state given decay -- but propensities seem to point forward in time, cause-like, so there is no such thing as a propensity of the atom's being in its present state, given that it decayed; I want a question that forces her to say which side she gives up, the dispositional character or the full probability calculus, and a follow-up for whichever half she sacrifices. Second, the bridging problem: why should an ensemble of independent trials with a given single-case propensity produce a stable limiting frequency, given that any argument via the laws of large numbers appears to presuppose probabilities at the level propensities were supposed to explain. Third, the reference-class or trial-conditions problem: a propensity is a disposition of a trial under a description -- the same coin toss has different propensities under different specifications of the setup -- so I want a question about what fixes the relevant description without smuggling in our ignorance (at which point the view risks collapsing into subjectivism about evidence). Fourth, whether the quantum-mechanical motivation actually does the work her tradition claims: does the propensity reading add anything to the Born rule beyond a label, and what happens to the view if deterministic hidden-variable or Everettian physics turns out to be true. Fifth, the dispositions analogy she leans on -- propensities are supposed to be like fragility or solubility, single-case and real -- and where she thinks the analogy is load-bearing, given that familiar dispositions have identifiable causal bases, and it is far from obvious what the causal basis of a 1/3 propensity could be.

A few constraints and requests. Keep the mathematics at the level of conditional probability -- no measure theory on air. For each follow-up, state in a sentence or two what reply you are anticipating and why that's the probable one, since I may need to ad-lib around it. Finally, be candid about the plan's fragility: flag which of your anticipated replies are extrapolations from her tradition's textbook responses rather than from anything she has actually committed to in print, and tell me specifically which question you would cut or restructure if, five minutes in, it turned out she defends a long-run rather than single-case version of the view -- that is, where the list silently depends on a contestable reading of her position, and what signal from her would tell me to abandon that reading.
