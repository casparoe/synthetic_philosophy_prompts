I'm a PhD student in physics with a side interest in philosophy of science, and last weekend two friends of mine got into it at a barbecue about what laws of nature actually are. Maya, who's doing her PhD in philosophy, holds the Humean view: laws are just whatever propositions show up in the systematization of all the particular facts that best balances simplicity against strength, so there's no extra layer of nomic stuff sitting over and above the mosaic of events. Dan, a condensed matter guy, thought this was wrong in a specific way. His moves, roughly in order: (1) he said that on Maya's view the law that nothing travels faster than light is just a very elegant summary of what happens, which means it doesn't forbid anything — if a tachyon showed up tomorrow, the best system would just get worse and eventually change, and Maya has to say the old law was never really a law, wich makes laws useless for understanding why the tachyon would be so surprising. (2) Maya replied that governing laws are a mystery: what would the law of gravitation even be doing, pushing the planets around? The mosaic just is a certain way, and positing some extra entity that constrains it from outside adds nothing and violates Ockham's razor. (3) Dan said it adds explanation: on her view the fact that the glass fell tonight is accounted for by the mosaic, but the mosaic can't account for itself, and its own regularities just being where explanation bottoms out means she's traded a mystery for a circle. (4) Maya answered that every view bottoms out somewhere and hers bottoms out in the cheapest thing; Dan said cheap isn't the same as explanatory, and that physics itself treats laws as things you measure, confirm, and then extrapolate to cases nobody has seen, which only makes sense if the law has some grip on unseen cases — and a summary of seen cases has no such grip. They then drifted into arguing about whether the values of the fundamental constants could have been different, and at that point I lost the thread and just watched. Please act as a professional metaphysician and think carefully before you decide — maybe map out the whole exchange first, since some of the moves are better than they initially sound. Then adjudicate: who's right at each of the four points, what if anything both of them are missing, who's more right overall, and (bonus) whether the constants argument they ended on actually tracks their earlier disagreement or changes the subject. Write your answer as continuous prose, no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, like an email to a colleague, and in American English.
