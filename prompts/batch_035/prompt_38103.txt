I write about science and ideas for a mid-size magazine, mostly neuroscience and AI lately, and a philosopher I profiled last spring has got me stuck on an argument he swears is airtight until it detonates functionalism, which he hates, and I can't find the fuse. Since my editor says I can't run a piece called "I Lost An Argument At A Conference," I'm bringing it here.

The setup: I'd been writing about cerebral organoids and about neuromorphic chips, and I said the usual thing — if you get the causal organization right, the substrate shouldn't matter, pain is pain whether it runs on neurons or silicon. That's when he laid this on me:

(1) Functionalism says a mental state just is whatever state plays the right causal role. Pain is the thing in you that's triggered by tissue damage, that interacts with beliefs about how bad the damage is, that causes avoidance and distress and so on. (2) A causal role can be described at any grain. Coarse: damage in, wincing and withdrawal out. Fine: every internal transition, including ones that only show up in humans, or only in me.

(3) Pick the coarse grain. Then a thermostat wired to a little motor that yanks your hand off a stove realizes damage-in, withdrawal-out, and a worm's escape reflex does too, and a factory line that stamps out recalls of contaminated lettuce realizes the belief that the lettuce is bad. (4) So functionalism is committed to thermostats feeling pain, which literally nobody at the conference would accept, him smirking the whole time.

(5) So go fine instead, fine enough that only human nervous systems realize the role. Then octopuses don't share our pain states, hypothetical AI systems can't either, and mulitple realizability — the entire reason I liked functionalism in the first place, the thing that lets me write that a sufficiently organized silicon system could suffer — is dead. Chauvinism, he calls it.

(6) Therefore functionalism is false either way, or else it's only true at some magical middle grain that nobody ever specifies, and if you can't say what fixes the grain, the view is empty.

Where I smell something: between (3) and (4). The thermostat only realizes a little arc of the role — pain is also supposed to interact with attention, memory, learning, planning, and no thermostat does all of that. So my instinct is the coarse-grain horn is cheating, the role was never meant to be that short. But when I said this, he came back with: fine, take the whole-system role, and now a giant lookup table can implement your entire psychology — one entry per possible input, outputs precomputed — and it realizes every role I care about at whatever grain I pick, so by the view's own lights it suffers, and I don't believe that either. Which makes me worry the middle grain I want doesn't exist, and I'm just pointing at the middle of a spectrum and calling it a place.

So: which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I want to keep the conclusion of (1)/(2)-ish — I want to keep being able to say substrate doesn't matter — and I'm okay paying in whatever other coin that takes. Also tell me whether the lookup-table thing is genuinely a new problem or just the same problem wearing a hat, because if it's new I need to know before I put anything like it in print. And if the answer is that the grain gets fixed by something outside the causal roles themselves — by what our pain concept latches onto, or by whatever science eventually individuates — I want to know whether that's a rescue or basically an admission that he's right.

One more thing, since I'll probably have to compress all this into 800 words with a lot of the scaffolding cut: if you had to bet on which horn actual working functionalists bite, the thermostat-liberalism one or the species-chauvinism one, where does the smart money go? My guy says nobody bites either one because nobody ever thinks about the grain at all, and I suspect he's half right, which annoys me more than the argument does.
