I'm a PhD student working on decision theory stuff (mostly from the agent foundations / ML safety angle, not pure philosophy) and I run a grad reading group on bargaining and commitment -- about 12 people, one 90-minute session. We've done Schelling and the standard credible-commitment literature, and we've all read the arguments that advanced agents might race to commit to things *before* they've finished computing, because the first one to credibly commit grabs the bargaining power even though it's deciding with worse information. Everyone nods along on paper. But in discussion it's obvious nobody has actually felt how ugly that tradeoff is from the inside -- how tempting it is to commit early, and how fast things go bad when everyone reasons the same way.

So: design an in-class game that produces this. The core mechanic has to be some way for participants to publicly lock in a strategy *before* they receive private information, so that each round they choose between (a) committing now and securing first-mover advantage in a subsequent negotiation, or (b) waiting, seeing their card / info / whatever, and entering the negotiation better informed but having conceded the commitment advantage to whoever moved first. Payoffs in points, no real money, and I want the equilibrium-ish outcome to be that everyone is drawn into committing early even though the group as a whole would be better off if everyone waited -- the race dynamic should emerge from the rules, not from me telling them to race.

Also need:

- rules and materials that a TA can run with paper, index cards, and a whiteboard, no software
- a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the parts of the idea -- the first-mover commitment advantage, the value of the foregone information, the point where waiting stopped being rational for anyone
- a prediction of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm guessing someone finds a loophole in what counts as "credible" commitment, or the whole thing collapses into people just chatting and colluding, idk)
- an honest note on which feature of real commitment races the game cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply in the debrief -- my worry is that in the real thing the agents are choosing *while still computing*, so the early commit is made by a dumber version of yourself, and I don't see how a 90-minute classroom game gets at self-modification under incomplete deliberation

No lecture up front, and don't name "commitment races" in the rules themselves -- they should run into the trap before I tell them the term. Extra credit if you can design it so that one participant who figures out the dynamic early can try to talk everyone into a "we all wait" pact, and the rest get to experience whether that pact survives contact with a round of actual payoffs.
