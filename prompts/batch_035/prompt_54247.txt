I am a second-year undergraduate taking a philosophy of mind course, and before university I spent two years working as a software engineer, mostly on quality assurance and test automation. I read the literature on the Turing test with the persistent feeling that this is a debate I have already had, in different words, in sprint retrospectives about flaky test suites and benchmarks that teams learn to game. I would like you to make that feeling precise, or else refute it.

Restate the debate over the Turing test and behavioural criteria for mind in the vocabulary of software testing and evaluation methodology. By that vocabulary I mean roughly: test cases and coverage; oracles, in the tester's sense of a source of expected outputs against which behaviour is checked; ground truth and labelled data; overfitting and benchmark saturation; Goodhart's law, on which a measure that becomes a target ceases to be a good measure; adversarial inputs; and the distinction between a test that certifies a property and a test that merely fails to falsify it. I am fluent in this vocabulary — I have written property-based tests, and I have been blamed for a production incident that every test we had passed — so please do not simplify it for me, and do not smuggle in extra philosophical assumptions while calling them translation. In particular, treat with suspicion any move that quietly renders "thinking" as "satisfying a specification", since whether anything in the vicinity has a specification at all is part of what is in dispute.

Where I think the translation goes, and where I want your corrections:

1. The test itself becomes something like a paired-discrimination protocol: not a threshold a system passes, but a comparison against a human control under the same inputs, with the interrogator as a deliberately fallible oracle. My suspicion is that this translation is faithful to Turing's actual procedure, and that a good deal of the secondary literature treats the test as an absolute benchmark — which the testing vocabulary would expose as a category error. Is that right?

2. Searle's Chinese Room looks like the claim that the suite can pass while the system under test is a lookup table with no internal model — that behavioural coverage constrains implementations without determining them. In testing terms this is familiar, and the familiar reply is that at sufficient coverage the distinction stops mattering operationally. Does the testing vocabulary dissolve the Room, or does it conceal the part Searle actually cared about?

3. The residue I anticipate is a single item: every property a test checks has an oracle or ground truth, and the dispute is over whether "thinking" admits of an oracle at all. If that is the whole residue, then the debate over the test is really a debate over the possibility of a specification, which the testing vocabulary can frame but not settle. But I want to know whether the residue is genuinely that small, or whether there are further leftovers — for instance, anything in the historical and social character of the test, that it requires an interrogator who can be taken in, an imitation rather than an evaluation, that has no expression in a vocabulary built for artefacts.

Then give me the residue report: what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion, what has no expression at all, and, for each item of residue, your view on whether the failure reflects a blind spot in the testing vocabulary or an unclarity in the original philosophical debate. My overall suspicion, which I would like tested, is that the philosophers have been arguing about benchmark design without admitting it, and that the interesting question is not whether machines can pass the test but what any test can be expected to establish in the absence of a specification. If you think that suspicion is itself an artefact of my vocabulary — a defect in the translator rather than in the translated — say so plainly.

Please reply in British English.
