i'm a phd student in cog neuro (philosophy of mind is my minor field) and our consciousness reading group makes everyone write up their "theoretical stance" once a year so the others can poke holes in it. mine is below. the assignment is basically: does this package hang together, or am i holding views whose motivations clash. heres what i've got:

(1) in the commissurotomy patients as tested in the lab — the classic setups where the right hemisphere can point at or grab a stimulus that the speaking left hemisphere flatly denies seeing — there really are two subjects of experience in the room.

(2) outside the lab, the same patient in daily life is one subject.

(3) the way to reconcile (1) and (2): subject-count depends on what's doing the unifying work. pre-surgery it's the corpus callosum, in the lab it's the experimenter's apparatus and testing regime, so the number of subjects can genuinely vary with context without anything weird being true of the patient.

(4) unity of consciousness is anyway graded and multidimensional, not an on/off switch — access unity, phenomenal unity, co-consciousness of perceptions with intentions, these can come apart.

(5) still, for any given case there is a determinate, non-verbal fact about how many subjects there are. its not just a choice about how to talk, the psychologists aren't free to stipulate.

(6) personal identity over time is psychological continuity, and since each hemisphere of a split-brain patient carries enough of the psychology, if you transplanted the two hemispheres into two bodies both recipients would literally be the original patient — and im fine with that because i think identity just isnt always a one-one relation.

(7) the left-hemisphere interpreter findings (where the patient invents a story explaining why the left hand did something the left hemisphere didnt know about) show that ordinary felt unity is partly a narrative construction the left hemisphere confabulates.
