I'm a high school senior in an elective called "Physics and Philosophy," and our teacher, Ms. Adeyemi, ends every unit with a round of "same world or different world?" rulings on pairs of descriptions. I've kept a running list of her rulings, and she has just posted the three new pairs we'll take up next week; I'd like to walk in with a considered view rather than a guess. Her rulings so far: (1) Leibniz's shifted universe, in which everything is moved three feet east with every relative distance, velocity, and timing unchanged — same world; indistinguishable descriptions, she said, are descriptions of one reality. (2) Newton's bucket, the water climbing the sides as it spins versus sitting flat at rest — different worlds; here she sided with Newton against Leibniz, saying the visible difference in the water's surface shows that space itself has structure. (3) The hole argument: two complete solutions of general relativity that agree perfectly everywhere outside a small isolated region, while inside it the second is obtained from the first by a smooth sliding of the gravitational field that nevertheless preserves every measured distance, duration, and coincidence of events; she ruled them the same world, and added that this is why the bare points of space can't be treated as objects with their own identities. (4) In electromagnetism, a description in terms of the vector potential A versus one in terms of A plus the gradient of some function, with the electric and magnetic fields exactly as before — same world; the fields are what's real, and the potential is bookkeeping.

The three new pairs are: (a) a universe just like ours except twice as big, by which I take her to mean that every length and every duration is doubled while all ratios and dimensionless facts stay fixed; (b) a Newtonian universe just like ours except that everything in it has an additional uniform velocity of ten meters per second relative to absolute space; and (c) a universe just like ours except that the gravitational constant is half as strong, with everything else left as it is. Please predict her verdict on each. But I want the analysis more than the answers: which rules actually fit her track record — at least two, please, since I can see how a simple "no observable difference, no difference" rule accommodates everything she has said, but I suspect there is a second rule available, something about when a transformation counts as a mere redundancy of the governing theory, and I don't think the two rules agree everywhere. Say what each candidate rule predicts for (a), (b), and (c), which prediction you would bet on and why, and — since case (c) looks like it may come out the same on every rule — propose the further case she could rule on that would best separate the rules that currently look tied. If her record genuinely underdetermines some verdict, I'd rather hear that than receive a forced guess.

One last request. If you think any of the three cases is ill-posed — for instance, if "twice as big" has no fact of the matter in the absence of an external yardstick, or if case (b) secretly turns on whether her Newtonian space carries rest-structure rather than only the rotational structure the bucket reveals — please tell me so plainly instead of answering around it; I would rather learn that my question is confused than get a confident answer to a confused question. And where you do commit to a prediction, indicate how confident you are, since I will have to defend it in class against students who have read more philosophy than I have.
