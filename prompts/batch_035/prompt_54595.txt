I hold the dialetheist position: some contradictions are true, with the liar sentence as the paradigm case, and classical logic should be replaced by a paraconsistent logic such as Priest's LP, in which explosion fails. What would actually count against this view? I'm not asking for the "a contradiction entails everything" sermon, which just restates the classical rule; I want the real pressure points. For instance, in LP the material conditional has to be defined as the negation of the antecedent or the consequent, and that definition invalidates modus ponens, so the dialetheist owes us a better conditional -- if no workable one exists, how much of the position does that take down? Curry's paradox is often said to be harder for the dialetheist than the liar, since it apparently yields arbitrary conclusions from a conditional rather than from a contradiction; is that right, and what would a solution have to accomplish? Then there is revenge: strengthened liars -- sentences that say of themselves that they are only false -- seem built to be unabsorbable, and I have begun to suspect that the standard move of simply accepting them as further true contradictions has qualified the position into unfalsifiability. Rank the defeaters from fatal through merely awkward, distinguishing what Priest and other defenders would themselves concede as counting against dialetheism from what only critics press, say what the view predicts or enables that rival treatments of the liar -- Kripkean gaps, revision theory, contextualist approaches -- do not, and tell me whether retreating to non-dialetheist paraconsistency, keeping the logic while denying the true contradictions, is a live fallback or a different position entirely. Please write the answer in American English.
