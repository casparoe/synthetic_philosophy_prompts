I run "choice architecture" (ugh, the job title the company gave me) at a mid-size fintech, about 3 million users and a compliance team that mostly cares about what's legally defensible, not what's right. Concretely my group owns defaults, opt-in/opt-out flows, friction choices (extra confirmation screens, cooldown timers), and the wording on stuff like savings round-ups. We ship maybe a dozen of these changes a quarter, so whatever procedure you give me gets run fast and often by people who are product managers, not philosophers.

Here's my problem. Everyone on the exec team read Nudge on a plane once, so "nudging is fine because it's just helping people do what they already want" gets quoted at me like scripture, and then two weeks later someone proposes pre-checking the insurance add-on box because "it's just a default, defaults are neutral." I can't fight every one of these battles ad hoc. I want a written decision procedure my team can actually run on a proposal in like 20 minutes, grounded in whatever the actual philosophy of paternalism and manipulation has worked out -- soft vs hard paternalism, the conditions under which interfering with someone's choice respects them vs treats them like a child, the line between influencing choice and subverting it, all that.

One more thing. I've been toying with my own rule that I want you to evaluate as part of building the procedure, not just swallow. I call it the "grudge test" for lack of a better name: a nudge is OK only if, upon later finding out exactly how it worked, the median user would say "yeah, fair enough" rather than feel suckered. My instinct is this is doing a lot of work -- it feels like it captures the difference between good defaults and dark patterns -- but I half suspect it's too lenient (people forgive all kinds of stuff) or in some cases too strict. You tell me where it fits, whether it needs patching, or whether it should just be one input among several, and be honest if you think it's wrong.

So, the procedure. Please make it:

1. A sequence of questions in running order, each with what counts as a sufficient answer and what counts as a dodge.
2. Clear about the stopping points: where the procedure outputs a straight "no," where it outputs "yes but with conditions," and where it hands the decision to a named human because the question is a values question that a checklist can't settle. Don't hide the judgment calls -- flag them as judgment calls, not as steps that only look like judgment calls.
3. Explicit about the "wants vs. would-want" problem, since that's where all our fights actually happen: who decides what the user "really" wants when the nudge pushes against their revealed behavior.
4. Include a transparency requirement (or explain why you're not including one) and something on the intensity of the intervention -- I want the procedure to distinguish changing a default from adding friction to removing an escape hatch, because those feel very different and I need the procedure to say whether that feeling tracks anything real.

Then run it on three actual proposals I'm sitting on right now:

- Defaulting users into a round-up savings feature that moves spare change into a locked 30-day account on each card purchase (big consumer benefit per the data, but it's money that gets locked, and the unenroll flow is behind two menus).
- Adding a cooldown timer + three confirmation screens when users try to withdraw more than 40% of their balance in one go, framed as "protection from impulsive decisions" -- this one genuinely reduces losses from scams but also from, you know, people's own choices.
- Pre-selecting "tip the platform" at checkout, where the default is 12% and the option to remove it is in small gray text. I already know this one smells, but exec wants numbers on exactly where in the procedure it dies and why the "it's just a default" defense fails.

For at least one of these, the procedure should give an answer I won't like, and you should say so plainly.
