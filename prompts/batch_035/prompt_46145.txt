For the past three years I have kept a meticulous record of everything I eat, my sleep, my digestion, and my daily concentration — several thousand rows in a spreadsheet, with self-ratings each morning. To my eye the pattern is unambiguous: when I eat late, snack between meals, or drink coffee after noon, my mental clarity collapses the following day; when I confine myself to two unhurried meals taken slowly, I feel like a different person. My doctor, reasonably enough, points out that I am a sample of one, that my self-ratings are contaminated by expectation, and that population-level studies support several of the habits my diary appears to condemn. A friend who reads a great deal of Nietzsche takes my side: he tells me that Nietzsche, in his late autobiographical writings, argued that general claims about diet are essentially worthless, because what nourishes one person harms another, and that the only competent authority on what a given person should eat is that person's own carefully observed self — so my diary is precisely the evidence that matters, and my doctor's aggregate studies are, in Nietzsche's vocabulary, a kind of herd morality applied to the stomach.

I want to know who is right about what my diary is worth, and I am not asking for nutrition advice — the question is epistemological. Please address the following: (1) Is there a principled way to weigh prolonged, careful self-observation against aggregate findings whose averages may conceal individual variation, or is that framing confused about what general studies actually claim to establish? (2) What is the strongest thing a defender of the population-level science could say that takes my data seriously rather than dismissing it — for instance, by treating it as a hypothesis about my idiosyncratic physiology that itself needs controls, blinding, or third-party verification before it can override established findings? (3) Which of my inferences would fail to survive if my self-ratings were as expectation-driven as my doctor suspects — and is there any way for me, from the inside, to tell whether they are? (4) Setting aside the polemics, is the Nietzschean position — that dietary knowledge is irreducibly individual and can only be won through disciplined self-observation — a coherent epistemology at all, or does it collapse under the objection that the observing instrument is the very thing whose reliability is in question? In your answer, I would like you to say plainly whether Nietzsche's own practice, insofar as you know it, actually vindicates his principle or instead illustrates its dangers, and to identify the single feature of my situation that you take to be doing most of the philosophical work.
