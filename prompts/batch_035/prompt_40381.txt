I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for over a decade — LessWrong, the Scott Alexander sphere, and various offshoots — and one argument I keep hitting, most recently in a thread about whether the culture-war fixations of online communities are downstream of material conditions, ended with someone waving off a cultural explanation with the slogan: "the base determines the superstructure." The claim being dismissed was roughly that the specific content of a community's norms isn't predictable from its members' economic positions, since communities in near-identical economic circumstances develop wildly different sacred values. The sloganeer seemed to think the one-liner settled the matter, but I've never been able to pin down what precise thesis it's supposed to express, and I suspect the phrase is doing several very different jobs at once.

Please act as a professional philosopher of social science with expertise in theories of historical explanation, and think carefully before answering — ideally sketch a short brainstorm of the candidate readings first, then organize them into a clean list. Lay out a menu of the distinct precise claims "the base determines the superstructure" could be making, ordered from the nearly trivial to the substantive to the false. I can glimpse at least these shapes, though you should refine, split, or add to them: (1) a banal claim that economic conditions influence institutions and prevailing ideas, which no one disputes; (2) a causal-sufficiency claim — that given the state of production relations, the character of the superstructure is fixed, so any cultural explanation is at most a description of the intervening mechanism; (3) an asymmetric-correction claim — that ideas have their own dynamics, but whenever a society's ideas conflict with what the reproduction of its economy requires, the economic requirement eventually wins, so culture explains timing and flavor but not direction; (4) a selectionist or functional claim — that we should expect the superstructure to look as if designed to support the base, the way biologists expect organisms to look as if designed for fitness, so cultural features that persist do so because they serve economic functions, and the appearance of autonomous cultural causation is like the appearance of design in nature; (5) a purely methodological recommendation to prioritize economic variables in explanation, a research heuristic that carries no claim that economic factors always do the explaining.

For each reading, say whether it's a platitude, whether it's plausible but contested, and whether it's false — and, this is the part I most want, whether it would actually support what my forum opponent wanted it to do, namely rule out the possibility that the specific content of a community's norms is culturally rather than economically explained. My working suspicion is that the strong readings that would license the dismissal are the ones least defensible, while the defensible readings wouldn't license the dismissal at all, but I want that demonstrated rather than assumed. Conversely, if one of the middle readings both survives scrutiny and does the dismissive work, say so plainly.

Please write in American English. And since I plan to take this back to the forum, calibrate as you go: indicate how confident you are in each verdict you hand out, and at the end explicitly single out the single point in your analysis you are least sure of, with a sentence about what kind of evidence or argument would move you on it.
