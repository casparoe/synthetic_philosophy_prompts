Eight months ago my business partner of nine years — I'll call him Marcus — falsified a supplier invoice. It surfaced in a routine audit last month. It happened during the quarter when our firm nearly missed payroll, when our credit line was frozen and he was the one fielding the bank's calls. In every other respect, across nine years, he has been the person I would have offered as an example of integrity: he once flagged and returned a five-figure overpayment a client had never noticed, and he has more than once talked me out of a shortcut I later regretted wanting to take. I now have to decide whether to dissolve the partnership, and I find myself torn between two framings that I can't get to agree with each other. On the first, the invoice revealed who Marcus really is, and everything before it was either performance or luck in never being seriously tempted. On the second, almost anyone's honesty is hostage to circumstance, and his behavior that quarter tells me little about him that I wouldn't learn about nearly anyone by putting them under the same pressure — including, presumably, myself.

The second framing has been eating at me because, around the same time, I read some psychology I've never been able to shake: the obedience experiments where ordinary people administered what they believed were painful shocks at an authority's insistence; the one where seminary students, told they were late to give a talk, stepped around a man slumped and groaning in a doorway; and the general finding that how a person behaves in one kind of temptation barely predicts how they behave in a different kind. If behavior is that sensitive to circumstance, then I don't know what I was trusting in when I trusted Marcus, whether that trust was ever warranted, or what it would even mean to pick a replacement partner "for their character." Someone with an unblemished record may simply be someone who has never stood where Marcus stood.

I gather there is a long-running argument about whether results like these undermine the old idea that people have stable virtues at all, but I've only ever encountered it secondhand, and I'd like it worked out on my actual situation rather than in the abstract. Please structure your reply in four sections, in this order. First, "What the experiments actually show" (about 300 words): be careful about what follows from the findings and what merely sounds like it follows — I want to know if my memory of them is even right. Second, "What, if anything, a virtue is" (about 350 words): say what a defensible notion of character would have to look like for trust like mine to be more than a bet on someone's circumstances holding steady. Third, "Applied to Marcus and to me" (about 300 words): address the two framings directly, including the uncomfortable question of what I would have done in his position, and what follows from the honest answer. Fourth, "Your recommendation" (about 200 words): whether to dissolve the partnership, and — more importantly than the verdict itself — the single consideration that should be doing the most work in it.
