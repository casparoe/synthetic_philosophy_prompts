I'm a retired evolutionary ecologist -- spent about thirty years counting beetles and arguing with taxonomists before that, so not exactly a stranger to evolution, but philosophy of biology is new territory for me since I finally cleared out my office and have time on my hands. I've been working my way through the whole units of selection mess lately and honestly it's more tangled than I expected. Everybody seems to agree now that the group selection wars of the 70s-80s were partly just people talking past each other, and that the Price equation can carve up the same data multilevel-style or kin-selection-style and you get the same predictions. But then people keep fighting anyway, and I can't always tell if they're fighting about something real.

Here's my situation. I've got maybe ten good years of reading and writing energy left, and I want to write something worthwhile about this -- maybe a book aimed at biologists like my old colleagues, maybe something more scholarly, I haven't decided. So what I want from you is a prioritization: of the open questions in this debate, which ones actually deserve those ten years, in what order, and why. To give you a sense of what I mean by open questions (and tell me if I'm missing bigger ones):

1. Whether the equivalence between kin selection and multilevel selection approaches is purely bookkeeping or whether the two frameworks genuinely point you toward different causal stories when things get messy -- non-additive interactions, changing group composition, that sort of thing. If it's just bookkeeping, maybe the whole debate dissolves and I should write that book instead.
2. Whether there's a principled, non-question-begging way to individuate the "units" at all -- I gather there's an old distinction between "selection of" and "selection for" that's supposed to matter here, and the whole evolutionary transitions literature where organisms-as-groups get stitched together over time seems like it should force the issue.
3. Whether any of this matters for working biologists or whether it's philosopher turf. I keep hearing cancer biology and microbiology brought up as places where multilevel thinking might actually do real work -- tumors as populations of cells with selection at the cellular level undermining the organismal level, bacterial biofilms as proto-groups. My instinct says this is where the payoff is, but my instinct also said my last grant would get funded.
4. The status of group selection in humans, culture, and gene-culture coevolution, which I gather is where it gets politically loaded and also where the data is hardest to pin down.

For each question you rank, I want the reasoning, not just the label: why it's upstream or downstream of the others, whether it's even answerable with the tools we have versus being a permanent philosophical stalemate, and what would have to happen -- empirically or conceptually -- for you to move it up or down the list. And push back on my framing where it deserves it; if you think one of these is basically settled or basically dead, say so. I'd rather find that out now than eight years in.
