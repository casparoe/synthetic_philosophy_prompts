My sister and her husband got into it at at Thanksgiving dinner and I promised I'd get someone to referee it for them, so here goes. Some context first: I signed up for a ten-day silent meditation retreat in March. I'm going in skeptical — I'm a no-nonsense person and the retreat's website talks about "observing sensations arising and passing" in a way that honestly reads like astrology to me. The question on the table was what they should do with me when I get back, since everyone agrees on one thing: I will almost certainly come back believing the retreat "did something real." That's just what ten days of silence does to people. It's what happens after that prediction that they fought about.

Marcus (my brother-in-law, does stats for a living) opened with van Fraassen's Reflection principle, which he explained to the rest of us roughly as: if you know now that your future self, at some later time, will assign credence p to some claim, then your credence now, conditional on that fact, should also be p. Rationality requires trusting your future opinions, he said, otherwise you're in effect exploiting yourself, and a bookie could Dutch-book you across time. So since we can all predict believer-me ten days from now, they should already now treat my post-retreat self as the authority on whether the retreat worked. He said it would be "epistemicly incoherent" for me to plan to come back saying it worked while also planning for everyone to dismiss what I say when I do.

Dana (my sister, an anthropologist, professionally unimpressed by intense groups) said Reflection assumes your future self is rational and better informed, not just different. If you can predict in advance the mechanism that will produce the belief — sleep deprivation, no talking, no phones, hours of sitting, the whole apparatus — then deferring to future-you is like deferring to drunk-you. The prediction "he'll come back a believer" isn't evidence the retreat works; it's evidence about retreats, which we already had. Van Fraassen, she said, never intended you to trust the opinions you'll hold after a lobotomy, and this is that kind of case, just milder.

Marcus answered: by that standard learning is impossible. Every belief you'll form later comes from an experience you haven't vetted from the outside, and if you only defer to future beliefs whose causes you've pre-approved, you've decided everything in advance and going on the retreat — or anywhere — is theater. The whole point of new experience is that you can't currently evaluate it, and Dana's "cult test" is just a device for freezing current opinions.

Dana replied that we make exactly these distinctions all the time and we're not wrong to. We don't defer to the fresh convert, the person newly in love, or the guy who just came out of the multi-level-marketing seminar, even though each of them is genuinely having a powerful experience. The fact that insiders can't see the problem from the inside isn't a defect of the test — it's the finding.

They ended with both of them agreeing on the prediction (believer-me, ten days from now) and disagreeing completely about what follows from it, and honestly I couldn't follow why the same fact supports opposite conclusions. Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right about which point — not just who wins overall? And I had the nagging feeling they were using "Reflection" as two different things, like one of them was treating it as a claim about evidence and the other as a claim about consistency, but I couldn't of put my finger on it, so if that's right I'd like to hear what the difference is and whether it dissolves the fight or just relocates it.
