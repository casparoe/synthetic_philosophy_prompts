My wife is an ICU nurse, and during the worst of covid her hospital put out a triage protocol — if the ventilators ran out, you were supposed to follow a scoring sheet (age, odds of recovery, that kind of thing) instead of treating whoever was in front of you. Nobody ended up liking that policy, but what's stuck with me for years is a different part of it: under the protocol, a doctor's own patient — someone she's been fighting for all week, maybe promised the family she wouldn't give up on — has no special claim whatsoever. The patient is just a number in the formula. And that seemed right to most people, in the emergency. But outside the emergency we believe the exact opposite. A doctor who was perfectly impartial between her patient and some stranger would strike everyone as missing something important about being a doctor. Meanwhile a doctor who saved her own husband by fudging the score would be seen as having done something unforgivable — and I honestly can't tell whether what's unforgivable is the fudging, or the fact that she put her husband on the scale at all, weighed the way you'd weigh anyone.

I remember reading somewhere that a philosopher once claimed that if a man rescues his own wife and feels the need to add, as a justification, that she's his wife, that's one thought too many — the love is supposed to do the work on its own, without showing up as a line item in some calculation. So we seem to want two contradictory things here: in normal life, your attachments are allowed to just act through you without needing to be justified, but in the emergency every attachment gets stripped out and replaced with a number. And yet, weirdly, the doctors who ran the numbers during the crisis don't come out of the story looking like the most moral people — more like people who had to do something terrible. The whole thing rests on some unspoken assumption I can't put my finger on.

So what I want to know is what this whole setup presupposes about morality. Is the scoring sheet basically admitting that the real, true morality is the cold impartial kind, and the everyday loyalty thing is a luxury we can only afford when there are enough ventilators to go around? Or is it the other way around — the emergency rule is a kind of damage control, a second-best, not the revealed truth of ethics showing through? And where does the doctor's own integrity fit: does the protocol presuppose that her personal commitments can just be suspended for the duration, or does it presuppose that they can't — which would explain why hospitals quietly made sure it was a committee doing the scoring rather than the bedside doctor with the promises to the family? Basically I want the implicit theory behind the practice: which rival views of the relation between a person's commitments and impartial morality it rules out, whether the two halves (loyal in normal times, a number in a crisis) actually hang together or quietly contradict each other, and what would have to change if you rebuilt the whole institution on one of those views instead of the other. Sorry if this is rambling, I've never taken a philosophy class, it just keeps bugging me.
