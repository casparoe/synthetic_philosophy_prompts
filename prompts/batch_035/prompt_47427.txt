I'd like your credence on a specific, empirically entangled version of Molyneux's question, together with the decomposition that produces it.

First, the proposition. Take Molyneux's original setup: a person blind from birth, who has learned by touch to distinguish a globe from a cube (and to name them), then gains sight in adulthood. I want your credence in the *yes* answer to this question: could such a person, before receiving any haptic feedback or correction, identify the globe and the cube by sight alone at above-chance reliability? Note the stipulations, because they matter for the truth value: above-chance means reliably better than the 50% baseline, not certainty; and "before receiving any haptic feedback" is to be read as a window of at least a minute or two, not a split second — I'm not interested in whether they could do it in the first three seconds. A "yes" answer can allow for the first seconds being noisy; what it cannot allow is performance that stays at chance until touch or verbal feedback enters.

My own credence is around 0.3. A friend of mine, who has read more Locke than I have and less cognitive science, sits at about 0.7. So in addition to your own number, I want to know where you think the disagreement between us most likely lives.

Here is the decomposition I want, though I'm open to you adding or reweighting terms:

1. The amodality term: the probability that our concepts of shape (globeness, cubeness) are genuinely modality-independent — the same representational currency deployed by touch and by vision — rather than assembled per-modality and only later coordinated. This is the term Locke's negative answer implicitly denies and, arguably, the term the rationalist tradition asserted outright.

2. The signal-extraction term: the probability that a newly sighted adult can even extract a stable visual shape representation under the conditions of the question — adequately resolved retinal image, usable figure-ground segregation, some grip on depth cues — given no prior visual experience at all. I take it this is where the question stops being purely conceptual: Molyneux's man may have an amodal shape concept and still fail because his visual system hasn't yet learned to see shapes, which would make the empirical "no" the wrong kind of "no" for the philosophical question. Studies of congenital cataract patients treated late (e.g., the Project Prakash cohort in Held and colleagues, 2011) are the obvious evidence here, and they seem to point "no," but I want your estimate of how much of that "no" is signal-extraction failure versus genuine cross-modal failure.

3. The thin/thick distinction, which I'd like you to take seriously rather than dismiss: the probability that *discriminating* that the two objects differ in the relevant way (thin Molyneux) and *matching* the seen object to the previously felt one under its name (thick Molyneux) come apart. My suspicion is that the historical question conflates them, and that the answer might be yes-thin and no-thick — but I don't know how much of my overall 0.3 is really hiding in this redefinition rather than in an honest answer to the original question.

4. The near-miss term: the probability that any initial failure converges to success within minutes to days, and — given that convergence — your estimate of whether the philosophical tradition should count such a case as a yes, a no, or an artifact of asking about a temporally thin slice of a temporally extended process.

Give me a rough probability for each term with one line of justification, state how you combine them (and whether you treat them as independent — I suspect 1 and 2 are not), and produce the number. Then tell me which term your final credence is most sensitive to, and what evidence or argument would move it the most — including whether there is any conceivable experiment, rather than a priori argument, that would resolve the matter. Finally, locate the disagreement: given our 0.3 and 0.7, is my friend most likely wrong about amodality, about how much of the empirical record is signal-extraction noise, or about something in how the question should be parsed?
