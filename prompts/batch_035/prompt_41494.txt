I lead a monthly reading group of motivated non-specialists — a retired cardiologist, a software architect, a high-school history teacher, and an accountant who cheerfully describes herself as a stubborn reductionist. We don't work through primary texts; I present a topic and we argue about it. Next month I have ninety minutes with a whiteboard, and the topic is metaphysical fundamentality and the grounding relation: the thought that some facts hold because other facts do, and that reality may be organized into more and less fundamental levels. The proximate cause is that last month's session on supervenience ended badly. The architect asked, quite reasonably, whether the mental depending on the physical means anything over and above the two always varying together, and I discovered I couldn't say what the missing ingredient was without gesturing at a notion I had never properly introduced. So the group has already been wounded once by this territory, and I want to go back and do it properly.

What I need from you is a teaching plan rather than the explanation itself. First, the order the pieces must go in and why — what depends on what pedagogically, not metaphysically. For instance, does the session need to begin from the failure of supervenience to capture direction — the fact that Socrates and the singleton of Socrates exist or fail to exist in exactly the same worlds, yet one of them seems to depend on the other and not conversely — or is that example too set-theoretic for this crowd, and would a moral or semantic case do the carrying better ('the act is wrong because it causes pain', 'the sentence is true because snow is white')? And if so, what does each candidate example smuggle in that I'd then have to dig out again — the moral case presumably inviting the epistemic-explanation confusion, the truth case dragging in deflationism about truth.

Second, the misconception map: name the misunderstandings this particular audience will predictably form and tell me exactly where in the sequence to head each one off. My guesses — the cardiologist will hear 'grounds' as a causation word and assimilate everything to efficient causation, since dependence-without-causation is precisely what his training never required of him; the architect will treat grounding as a fact about our explanations — that we cite lower levels because they are the useful ones for creatures like us — rather than a feature the facts have independently of us; the history teacher will take it to be the familiar levels-of-science picture under a new name; and the accountant will push the flattening thought that if everything is ultimately physics, then talk of levels is bookkeeping with no worldly backing. Where in the plan does each get absorbed rather than merely dismissed? Third, tell me what to openly refuse to explain at this level and to say so to the group — I'm inclined to exempt the debate over whether grounding is itself a relation or something more like a sentential connective, and the question of whether the ground-floor facts ground themselves or are simply ungrounded, but you should feel free to overrule me. Finally, propose a single closing question I can put to them that would distinguish someone who has genuinely grasped directionality from someone who has merely picked up the vocabulary — ideally built around a pair of facts that necessitate each other perfectly symmetrically, so that the answer cannot be recovered from covariation alone.
