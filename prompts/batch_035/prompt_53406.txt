I'm putting together a term paper for an epistemology seminar that has spent most of the semester on peer disagreement — we've done the conciliationist line that acknowledged disagreement with an epistemic peer rationally requires moving credences toward your opponent's, and the steadfast line that one can permissibly retain one's original confidence, at least when one has already weighed the evidence properly. For the paper I want to work through a real scientific episode rather than the usual stock cases, and I've settled on the 2011 faster-than-light neutrino anomaly. The basic facts as I understand them: in September 2011 the OPERA collaboration at Gran Sasso announced that neutrinos beamed from CERN appeared to arrive roughly sixty nanoseconds earlier than light would over the 730-kilometre baseline, a result they themselves described as anomalous and invited the community to check; several other experiments (ICARUS, and later MINOS) had measured, or soon reported, neutrino speeds consistent with the speed of light; and by mid-2012 the anomaly had been traced to instrumental faults, chiefly a loose fibre-optic cable to the GPS timing receiver and a clock oscillator that ran fast.

Please analyse this episode as a case study in peer disagreement, and make the analysis run in both directions. On the one side: who counts as a "peer" here — OPERA's team had exclusive access to their own apparatus and data, so was this genuinely a case of peers sharing the same evidence, or does the disagreement-theory framework misdescribe what was mostly disagreement about trust in a particular timing chain? What would conciliationism have counselled the OPERA physicists to do once ICARUS contradicted them, and what would the steadfast view permit — and, importantly, was publishing the anomalous result in the first place, with a announced request for checks, the epistemically right move by either standard? It's also worth noting that a minority of OPERA's own members declined to sign the original announcement, so there was internal disagreement too. On the other side: what does the eventual resolution — an equipment fault found only because the anomaly was pursued and scrutinised — show about the theories? Does it vindicate steadfastness because the productive epistemic strategy was to keep looking at one's own apparatus rather than to split the difference between 60 nanoseconds early and on time, or is that a cheap victory, since "splitting the difference" was never on offer and the real disagreement was about error sources, not about what the credence in superluminal neutrinos should be? I'd like you to be disciplined about where the philosophical framework is silent rather than forcing it to speak, and to conclude with a judgement on whether this episode is an argument for conciliationism, for steadfastness, or for the claim that the standard debate simply doesn't anticipate cases of this shape. Please write the whole answer in British English.
