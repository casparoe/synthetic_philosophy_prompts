I'd like your help predicting a friend's verdict on a new case. We've argued for years about fakes, forgeries and the value of originals, and her rulings so far are these:

Van Meegeren's fake Vermeers, though painted with genuine skill, are in her view worth a tiny fraction of what authentic Vermees command — the forger's skill doesn't transfer the value.
A hypothetical molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a Monet would, she grants, possess every aesthetic quality of the original, and yet would be worth far less and would not deserve the wall the original hangs on.
Duchamp's Fountain she accepts without hesitation as a genuine and valuable work, though Duchamp made nothing by hand.
A canvas painted mostly by Vermeer's senior assistant, which Vermeer himself signed and sold as his own, she counts as a fully genuine and fully valuable Vermeer.
A conceptual artist who conceives a work and has assistants fabricate every physical part of it she likewise counts as the work's true author.
But when a painter's studio burns down and the painter herself produces an exact replica of the lost canvas from photographs and her own memory, she calls the replica a copy, not the original, and worth less.

The new case: an artist generates several thousand images with an AI model, curates twelve of them, arranges them into a single installation, and exhibits the result under her own name. Will my friend call it a genuine work of hers, valuable as such? I'm less interested in the verdict itself than in the rule she seems to be running, the other rules that fit her six rulings just as well, and which further case would best tell those rules apart. Please answer in British English.
