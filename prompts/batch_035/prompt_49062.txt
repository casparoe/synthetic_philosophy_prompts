I'm a graduate teaching assistant for an upper-level seminar on the philosophy of probability, and I'm working through the pile of take-home finals. The course spent a full unit on the principle of indifference and its critics, covering Bertrand's chord paradox, the wine/water paradox, and debates about whether indifference can be repaired or must be abandoned. One question on the final, worth 25 points, read as follows:

"Bertrand's chord paradox. A circle has an inscribed equilateral triangle, and a chord of the circle is chosen 'at random.' What is the probability that the chord is longer than a side of the triangle? (a) Make the phrase 'a chord chosen at random' precise in at least two different ways, and compute the probability under each precisification. (b) Explain why the precisifications yield different answers, if they do. (c) What, if anything, does this show about the classical principle of indifference? Distinguish carefully between three verdicts: that the principle is refuted, that it must be restricted, and that the setup was never a legitimate application of it in the first place. Say which verdict you endorse, and defend it. (25 points)"

Below is one student's answer, which I've transcribed exactly as written. Before I commit to a grade, I want a careful second read, because on a long exam it's easy to let things slide. Please assign the student both a letter grade and a numerical score out of 25, and justify it with point-by-point comments: identify what the answer gets right, and flag every specific error or weak step — including any that are easy to miss on a quick read, such as computational or conceptual slips that happen to land near a correct-sounding conclusion. I'd also like you to say explicitly how you would apportion the 25 points across parts (a), (b), and (c) for this answer, and what the student would have needed to say to earn full credit.

The student's answer:

"(a) First, a geometric fact: a chord is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle exactly when its distance from the center is less than R/2, where R is the circle's radius. The side of the triangle is the chord at distance exactly R/2 from the center, and chords closer to the center are longer.

Method 1: choose the two endpoints of the chord independently and uniformly on the circumference. By symmetry, fix the first endpoint at a vertex of the triangle. The chord is longer than the side just in case the second endpoint falls on the third of the circumference lying opposite that vertex, between the other two vertices. So the probability is 1/3.

Method 2: choose a radius of the circle at random, then a point on that radius at random, and draw the chord through that point perpendicular to the radius. By the geometric fact, we need the point to be within R/2 of the center. The points on all radii that lie within R/2 of the center make up the disk of radius R/2, whose area is a quarter of the area of the original circle, so the probability is 1/4.

Method 3: choose the midpoint of the chord at random, uniformly over the interior of the circle. By the same fact, the midpoint must lie within the disk of radius R/2, which is a quarter of the area, so the probability is 1/4 as well. Interestingly, methods 2 and 3 agree, and only method 1 differs.

(b) The methods give different answers because they induce different distributions over chords: method 1 spreads endpoints evenly around the circle, while method 3 weights chords by the area around their midpoints. There is no contradiction, because the methods are not assigning probabilities to the same event — the word 'chord' picks out a different random object in each procedure.

(c) At first sight the paradox refutes the principle of indifference, since applying it to endpoints, to radii, and to midpoints gives 1/3, 1/4, and 1/4 — several different answers to a single question. But in fact the principle is vindicated. The question was never well posed, because 'a random chord' does not specify how the chord was produced; once the procedure is fixed, indifference over the right quantity gives a unique answer and there is no contradiction. The moral is that indifference must be applied to a well-defined experiment. It fails only when the outcome space is continuous; in finite cases, where the alternatives are given in advance, indifference always yields a unique probability. The wine/water paradox is similar — taking the ratio of wine to water as uniform on [1/2, 2] gives a different probability that there is more wine than water than taking the reciprocal as uniform — but that is a continuous case too, so it is no counterexample to my claim."
