I've been trying to write a short essay on the "man of his time" defence for judging historical figures (the Jefferson / Churchill / Columbus genre of arguments), and I've gotten to a draft I half like. Could you read it and tell me what's working and what isn't? Two specific things: (1) does the middle section actually do philosophical work, or is it just relabeling an obvious point; (2) is the analogy near the end worth keeping or is it too cute. Here's the draft (typos included, haven't cleaned it up):

---

The "man of his time" defence: two claims hiding in one phrase

When someone objects that we shouldn't judge historical figures by standards they couldn't have known, they are usually making one argument out of two. The first claim is explanatory: given the moral environment someone grew up in, we should *expect* them to have the views they had. The second claim is exculpatory: because of that environment, they aren't blameworthy for those views. The first claim is nearly undeniable. The second doesn't follow from it, and the defence works by sliding between them.

The slide is visible once you notice that better views were usually available at the time. Jefferson didn't live in a world where no one had questioned slavery — abolitionists existed, some of them Quakers who had been arguing against it for a century before he was born. So the question isn't whether the better view existed, but what it cost to see it and hold it. Call this the difference between availability and salience. A view is available if someone in your milieu has articulated it; it's salient if holding it is a live psychological option for you — something you could arrive at without an act of heroic originality. Most bad historical views fail the availability test for the average person but pass it for elites who read widely and corresponded across borders. Which suggests the defence, even in its best form, applies unevenly: it does more work for a random tenant farmer than for a philosopher who wrote about the wrongs he was committing.

Here's a test case for the structure. Suppose we train a machine learning system on data from 1850 and it confidently outputs racist classifications. Nobody would say the system is blameworthy — there's no one home to blame. But notice what we do instead: we don't display its outputs as-is with a plaque explaining the data limitations. We correct the system, or we don't use it. The interesting thing is that our judgement about the producer (no blame) comes apart entirely from our decision about the output (fix or discard it). I think this is the real shape of the historical debate. Once you seperate the verdict on the man from the decision about his legacy, most of the heat goes out of the argument. A statue is not a verdict on a soul; its a choice about what we, now, want to celebrate. We can grant that Churchill isn't blameworthy in the way a modern holder of his views would be, and still conclude that the commemoration should change — because the commemoration was never about assigning blame in the first place.

So the "man of his time" defence is right that blame doesn't transfer cleanly across moral environments. Its mistake is thinking that the question in front of us was ever just about blame.

---

For what it's worth, my own worry is that the availability/salience distinction is doing less than it pretends to — "salient" might just mean "the views a person would actually have held," which makes the section circular. But maybe you see something in it I don't. And again: point out anywhere I've assumed consensus where there isn't any.
