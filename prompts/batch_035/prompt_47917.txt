I'm writing a paper section defending a responsiveness account of moral worth: what confers moral worth on an action is not the content of one's motive (benevolence, duty, respect for persons) but whether one's motivational structure reliably tracks the right-making features of the situation. Construct a thought experiment engineered to make acting from duty come out with *less* moral worth than acting from a frankly non-moral motive — the reverse of the ordering suggested by the familiar case of the honest shopkeeper who treats customers fairly only because it is prudent, versus the agent who does the same from duty.

Specifications for the case:

1. Two agents, A and B, perform the same act in matching situations. A acts from duty alone — no inclination toward the act, only the conviction that duty requires it — and A's dutifulness is *insensitive* in the counterfactual sense: A would honor any commitment whatever the morally salient features turn out to be, not because A misjudges particular cases, but because duty-observance as such is A's fixation. B acts from vanity or self-interest that remains genuinely non-moral throughout — no covert benevolence, no stipulated hidden warmth — yet is calibrated so that it covaries with the right-making features: B's self-interest pays off exactly where, and only where, help is actually needed and proportionate.

2. Realistic setting (a workplace or caregiving context, not a contrived dilemma), one paragraph.

3. No difference in outward effects: same act, same outcome, same effort, same foreseeability. An observer watching both would see indistinguishable behavior.

4. No stipulated verdicts. The claim that B's act has greater moral worth must be *generated* by the responsiveness criterion the case makes salient, not announced by the narrator. Since my paper argues for that criterion, this fixes what counts as cheating: a case that colors B warmly and A coldly, or quietly smuggles a moral motive into B's psychology, fails.

After the scenario: (a) state what a Kantian about moral worth says and where the pressure on that verdict enters; (b) state what the responsiveness account says, identifying the right-making features and the counterfactual profile of each agent's motive — under what perturbations of the situation each motive would or would not deliver the right act; (c) identify which feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to the reversal if that feature is removed; (d) present the strongest Kantian reply — that acting from duty, properly understood, is acting out of respect for the moral law and therefore *includes* sensitivity to whether the law actually applies, so A is not a genuine Kantian agent — and assess how costly that reply is, noting what it concedes about the felt sense of duty and moral worth coming apart.

Finally, and I want this part honest: tell me where the demonstration is most likely to fail — which premise a critic would reject first — and what consideration would lead you to abandon the verdict that B's act has more moral worth. If you think the specifications cannot be jointly satisfied — for instance, if a genuinely non-moral motive that reliably tracks moral features is impossible without covert moral content — say so and show exactly where the construction breaks, rather than quietly weakening the case to make it work.
