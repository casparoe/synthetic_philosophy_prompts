I am a philosophy professor who has agreed to advise a small family foundation whose staff, none of them philosophically trained, distribute roughly forty grants a year across global health, farmed-animal welfare, and education research. The trustees disagree deeply about morality — one is a utilitarian, another a contractualist who doubts animals matter much, another holds that present people come first — and they have asked me for a procedure that makes their grantmaking principled without requiring them to resolve their disagreement. The obvious theoretical resource is the literature on decision-making under moral uncertainty, but I do not want to hand them a lecture; I want something their program officer can run in under an hour per proposal, with the trustees signing off on anything above a threshold. Please write that procedure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, how to handle the problem of comparing the value of outcomes across theories with different currencies (and whether to use expected choiceworthiness, a bargaining or moral-parliament scheme, or something else), where the procedure should stop with a no, and where it must honestly hand the decision back to the trustees because the value comparison is unsolved. Then run it on two proposals: a campaign to eliminate battery cages in a large egg-producing country, and a speculative project aimed at reducing risks to the very long-run future of humanity. I am particularly interested in whether it gives the committee an answer it will not like, and what it cannot do.
