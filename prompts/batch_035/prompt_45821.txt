I am a retired biochemist — thirty-five years working on enzyme mechanisms — who has spent the last few years reading philosophy seriously for the first time, and next month I have agreed to give a talk to a university philosophy department's colloquium. The thesis I will defend is one I have slowly come to hold and would genuinely like to survive the afternoon: that philosophy makes progress, and that it does so by a mechanism I recognize from my own field — the slow, unglamorous weeding-out of dead ends.

My argument, in sketch: chemistry did not progress only by accumulating findings; it progressed by eliminating theories (phlogiston, the vital force) until what remained became difficult to deny. Philosophy, I want to claim, does the same. Logical positivism's verificationism, behaviorism about pain, interactionist dualism, absolute space, sense-data theories of perception — these are philosophy's phlogistons: not refuted by experiment, but hollowed out over generations until hardly anyone can defend them with a straight face. The surviving positions are fewer, sharper, and better articulated, and that refinement is progress even where disagreement persists. Call it eliminative progress. I further want to say that persistent disagreement among the survivors is no more damning than persistent disagreement among rival mechanistic proposals in enzymology circa 1980 — that is simply what live inquiry looks like from the inside.

The audience: a philosophy department, roughly half analytic, with a couple of historians of philosophy, one quietist who suspects the entire framing of philosophy in terms of progress rests on a scientistic picture of what philosophy is for, one metaphysician who insists metaphysics has made genuine positive progress and will resent my implying that the only progress on offer is killing things off, and at least one graduate student drawn to experimental philosophy who holds that philosophical intuitions never converge and never will. Nobody in the room is anti-science; several will suspect that my analogy with scientific practice is doing sneaky work.

Please prepare my question-period sheet. I want the objections I will actually get from this particular room, in roughly the order they are likely to arrive, phrased the way philosophers phrase them rather than the way my former colleagues in the lab would. Rate each by how much damage it would do to the thesis. Give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, with a candid note on where that reply is weakest and what a persistent questioner could say next that should make me concede rather than double down. Include the one objection I am quietly hoping nobody raises. Two further requests on how you should handle yourself. First, do not soften the serious objections to spare my feelings: tell me plainly if any of them cannot be answered, only mitigated, and say so. Second, at the end, identify the single weakest joint in the whole argument — the place where, if I am honest with myself, some consideration would most plausibly force me to give up eliminative progress as an account of how philosophy moves — and sketch what evidence or argument that would have to be. And where you are unsure whether a reply to my reply would in fact be standard in the philosophical literature rather than something you are inventing, flag that uncertainty rather than presenting it as settled doctrine.
