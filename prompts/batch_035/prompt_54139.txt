My roommate Maya has this whole spreadsheet brain about when you're allowed to count sunk costs and when you have to ignore them, and after a long dinner argument last week I've started collecting her verdicts to figure out what her actual rule is. So far: she slogged through the last 150 pages of a 600-page novel she openly hated, because she was "this close" to done. She told our friend on the city council that the half-built bridge that's way over budget should just be cancelled, and that the millions already poured into the river don't count for anything. She's in the final year of a PhD she's realized she doesn't even want to use, and she's finishing it anyway. She prepays for season tickets, loses all interest in the team by January, and still goes to every single home game. But her cousin, two years into a PhD program his parents basically pushed him into, she told flat-out to quit now and not look back.

Here's the thing: when I try to extract the rule, I can get at least two that fit all five verdicts equally well. One is something like "honor sunk costs when you're near the end" — the novel, her final PhD year, each game being its own little completed purchase, versus the cousin being years from done and the bridge being half-built with a mountain of remaining cost. Another is more like "honor sunk costs only when the person deliberately chose the project themselves" — she chose the book, the program, the tickets, whereas the cousin's program was his parents' project and the bridge was some earlier administration's. There might be a third rule in there about whose money it is, I honestly can't tell.

So now two new cases are landing this month. Her friend Daniel is one semester from finishing an engineering degree that his father arranged and paid for — near the end, but not his own choice. And Maya herself just started a 16-week marathon training plan that she designed and announced to literally everyone, she's two weeks in, and she's alreadyhinting she wants out — fully self-chosen, but nowhere near the end. What is she going to tell Daniel to do, and what is she going to do about the marathon? Which rule is she really running, which of the other rules that fit her track record would give different answers on these two cases, and what case could I spring on her that would actually settle which rule it is? I'm not trying to win the argument anymore, I just want to know how her mind files this stuff.
