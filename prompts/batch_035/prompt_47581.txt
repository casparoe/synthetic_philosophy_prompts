I'm a PhD student in particle theory, currently avoiding my actual work by arguing with a philosophy-of-science friend about whether anything other than prediction can confirm a theory, and I've been keeping a list of my gut reactions so I can figure out if they add up to anything. The so-called no-alternatives argument: after forty years, nobody in the community has managed to construct any framework that quantizes gravity, unifies the gauge forces, and keeps the symmetries we think we need, other than string theory. My gut says this raises my credence in string theory a bit, maybe more than I'm comfortable admitting, since it's clearly not evidence in the ordinary sense. Unexpected explanatory coherence: when string theorists count the microstates of certain extremal black holes and the Bekenstein-Hawking entropy falls out of a calculation that was never reverse-engineered for it, my gut says that's genuine confirmation, and a lot of it. The meta-inductive argument: whenever physics has previously produced a unique mathematically consistent framework of this kind, it turned out to be right, so this one probably is too -- my gut says that's way weaker, almost gambler's reasoning, though I can't articulate why it's weaker than the no-alternatives one, since they look like cousins. And then the anthropic multiverse: explaining the tiny cosmological constant by saying we're a randomly selected vacuum in a landscape of many, with selection doing the work. My gut says that's basically cheating, it confirms nothing, which is awkward because structurally it resembles selection-effect explanations in evolutionary biology that I'm completely fine with.

One more wrinkle, which is the one that keeps me up at night: the landscape itself. String theory was originally sold on eventual uniqueness, that turned out to fail, and the community then reinterpreted the huge number of vacua not as a bug but as an anthropic feature, and my gut reaction to that maneuver is that it's ad hoc rescuing, confirmation laundering, basically. But if I take that reaction seriously, I feel like it should also make me more suspicious of the no-alternatives argument, since the no-alternatives claim and the landscape claim can't both be doing the work the community wants from them -- the theory is supposed to be unique enough to be confirmed by our inability to find rivals, and vast enough to explain away every unexplained parameter.

So: is there a single principle behind these verdicts of mine -- something like a Bayesian reconstruction where each argument is a likelihood-ratio story -- or am I just inconsistently rewarding arguments whose conclusion I already wanted? If there's a principle, I want it stated precisely, with each of my gut reactions traced to a clause, and I want to know which of my verdicts it forces me to give up (I suspect the meta-inductive one, but maybe it's the anthropic one, or maybe even my enthusiasm about the black hole entropy calculation). And then I'd want to know what the principle says about a case I haven't listed: cosmologists who claim that the flatness and horizon problems confirm inflation, not because inflation was tested by a novel prediction exactly, but because every alternative mechanism anyone proposed either failed or collapsed into inflation plus extra steps.
