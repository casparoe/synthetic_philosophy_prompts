At work I keep winning arguments with an analogy and I want to know if it actually holds up. I'm a software engineer, and in my world defaults are everything: there's the famous organ-donation thing where switching a country from opt-in to opt-out enrollment massively changed consent rates, and I've watched a single preselected checkbox flip behavior by double digits in my own products. Policy people seem to make the exact same move: a government nudge is just a UI default. There's no neutral design — any way you present a choice already tilts it, every form has something preselected, so the question was never whether to influence people but only how. And since a designer who refuses to pick a default hasn't avoided influencing the user, a state that refuses to nudge hasn't avoided paternalism either — it's just doing the design badly, like shipping a signup form with no defaults at all. Nudge proponents lean on this to say nudging isn't really paternalsim, it's just good design. Lay out what maps onto what — what plays the role of the user, the designer, the A/B test, the dark pattern, the competitor you can switch to — then where the mapping breaks. I can already spot the obvious one (I can uninstall an app; I can't uninstall my country's tax authority, and I never clicked 'sign up' to be governed by anyone), but I suspect the real problems are elsewhere, like who the designer is optimizing for and what happens when the "user" never consented to being designed at. Then give me a verdict: does the "a nudge is just a default, so it's design not paternalism" conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? And if it's a bad analogy, what would a better one for state nudging look like?
