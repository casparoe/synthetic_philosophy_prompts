I'm a PhD student in philosophy of physics, and next month I'm presenting a paper at our program's joint seminar to a mixed physics/philosophy crowd. The thesis I'm defending: the standard slogan that Bell's theorem proves nature is nonlocal is sloppier than it looks. What the experiments actually rule out is a conjunction — local causality (in roughly Bell's own later sense, factorization of the joint probabilities), plus auxiliary assumptions like measurement independence — and once you see that, the interesting question becomes where you place the failure. If you go the Jarrett route and split factorization into parameter independence and outcome independence, then orthodox collapse theories violate outcome independence while keeping parameter independence, and there's a real (and I think underappreciated) argument that outcome dependence together with no-signalling is a much weaker breach of locality than the pop-science version suggests — more like familiar common-cause failure than an action-at-a-distance mechanism. My paper pushes that line: where the violation lands matters, and nonlocality is an umbrella term covering several very different escapes, including superdeterminism and Everett, which don't give up anything in the same place at all.

The audience: roughly half physicists — a quantum optics postdoc who actually runs loophole-free Bell tests with entangled photons and thinks this is all settled lab fact, plus two or three quantum information grad students who know Bell only as the CHSH game and Tsirelson bounds — and half philosophers, including one senior person who knows the Jarrett–Shimony decomposition cold and has published on whether outcome dependence can salvage a weaker locality, plus a couple of agnostic first-years who'll mostly be lost in the jargon.
