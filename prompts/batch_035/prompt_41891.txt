I've never taken a philosophy course in my life, worked in logistics for 20 years, but for the last couple of years this has been the thing I chew on during commutes and boring meetings: the idea that the "hard problem" of consciousness isn't a fact about the universe that science hasn't cracked yet, it's a fact about us. The way I've come to see it, the reason it seems like something is left over after you've explained all the seeing and reacting and reporting — the "ok but why does red FEEL like anything" move — is just another thing our brains do, on par with any other quirk of our wiring. Our introspection can't see the machinery that generates the judgment "there's an unexplained residue here," so the residue looks like it's out there in the world instead of in the observer. On this picture, if you could fully explain why humans (and maybe some animals, and weirdly maybe even chatbots trained on our text, since they parrot the same complaints) insist there's a hard problem, you'd have explained everything there is to explain, and the hard problem just... evaporates. I watched some old talks by Dennett years ago and I think I've basically ended up on his team without meaning to: the feeling that qualia are something extra is the illusion, and the real research program is explaining why we can't stop making that feeling.

I want to stress-test this properly instead of just marinating in it, which is honestly what I've been doing. So: what would actually count against it? I don't mean the reflexive "but I directly experience my qualia, so there" — I want to know which arguments, thought experiments, or possible empirical findings would genuinely worry someone who already holds this deflationist view, and I'd like them ranked from fatal through serious to merely annoying. Stuff like: could findings about anesthesia, or blindsight, or how the intuition behaves in split-brain patients, cut one way or the other? Which of these would a committed deflationist themself concede as counting against the view, versus which ones would they absorb by reinterpreting — and at what point does the absorbing mean the view can't lose no matter what, it just bends? Related: is there anything this view predicts that its rivals don't, so it's actually sticking its neck out rather than playing it safe? And here's the one that keeps me up a bit: suppose we one day had the complete story of why we judge there's a hard problem, laid out in full, and I understood every step — but I still couldn't stop seeing the residue. Does that sink the view, vindicate it, or neither? For what it's worth, that scenario is the thing that would move ME the most, so tell me straight whether it should.
