I'm a PhD student in health policy, and I've been stuck for a while on what our allocation institutions actually presuppose about distributive justice, because the pieces don't seem to fit together. Consider three strands of practice. First, solid-organ allocation: liver transplant systems rank candidates largely by severity of illness (in the US, roughly by the MELD score), which measures how close to death you are, not how much good a transplant would do you — so a sicker patient with a modest chance of benefit can be ranked ahead of a healthier candidate who would gain decades from the same organ. Second, coverage decisions by health technology assessment bodies: drugs and devices are judged by cost per QALY, which simply adds up benefits across patients and gives no extra weight to treating the worse off; severity enters, if at all, as a modifier bolted on afterward. Third, the rhetoric that surrounds the first strand: sickest-first rules are defended in the name of equality and equal chances, and there is the rule of rescue — systems spend lavishly on identifiable patients in front of them (the US Medicare entitlement for dialysis being the classic case) while declining far cheaper interventions that would benefit mere statistical lives. My question is what theory of distributive justice this pattern presupposes. Is the severity weighting prioritarian — the worse off count for more, but there is no independent value in reducing inequality as such — or genuinely egalitarian, and if egalitarian, must it face the leveling-down objection: could the practice justify a policy that benefits no one and merely narrows the gap between the sick and the well? Where is the pattern genuinely inconsistent, as opposed to merely underdetermined — could several theories at once license severity priority in transplantation and pure aggregation in drug coverage, or does the asymmetry require some further premise, such as identifiability, rescue, or procedural fairness? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, distinguish what these practices strictly presuppose from what they merely fit well with, and tell me what would have to change if a health system made the prioritarian view explicit — in particular, whether an explicit severity weighting in QALY thresholds would simply reproduce sickest-first transplantation, or would come apart from it.
