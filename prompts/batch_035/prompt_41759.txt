I'm running a session for my department's epistemology reading group — analytic philosophers plus a few sharp people from linguistics and law, none with any Sanskrit — on the Nyāya case for treating testimony, śabda, as an independent pramāṇa rather than a species of inference. Forty-five minutes and a whiteboard. Plan the explanation rather than give it: what must be established first about what a pramāṇa is before the testimony question even arises, and why the standard gloss "reliable source of knowledge" actively misleads here; which everyday example should carry the session instead of scripture, and what that example must show that a train timetable cannot; where in the sequence to introduce the reductionist challenge — that hearing a speaker just gives you grounds to infer their reliability, the way you'd infer it for any other sign — and where to bring in the Nyāya reply via the trustworthy speaker, āpta, so the reply lands as more than a relabeling; the misconceptions this audience will predictably form, namely that the pramāṇa list is an arbitrary menu that could just as well be lengthened, that trusting the āpta is epistemic blind faith dressed up, and that the whole debate is just the Hume–Reid dispute over testimony transposed into Sanskrit and therefore nothing new; the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off; one question I can ask at the end that would show whether they've genuinely understood why Nyāya took testimonial knowledge to be immediate rather than inferred; and what I should openly refuse to get into at this level — say, the Navya-Nyāya refinements or the longer pramāṇa lists of other schools — and how to say so without making it sound like dodge.
