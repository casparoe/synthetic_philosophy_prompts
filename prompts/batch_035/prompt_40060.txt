Something I keep chewing on and can't resolve on my own: the US states with physician-assisted dying laws (oregon's Death with Dignity Act and all the ones modeled on it) require the patient to take the medication themselves. The doctor can prescribe it, the pharmacist fills it knowing exactly what it's for, a family member can mix the powder into juice and hold the cup — but the last physical act has to be the patient's. If you're too weak to lift the cup, or your tremors are too bad, you're out of luck, no matter how clear your mind is. Meanwhile in Belgium and the Netherlands the doctor does the injection, and there the big safeguards are repeated requests plus review committees after the fact.

So what I want from you: reconstruct what theory of moral responsiblity — or of autonomy, or of what makes hastening death permissible — the self-administration rule actually presupposes. The obvious guess is that it's the doing/allowing distinction written into law, but I don't think that survives scrutiny, since everyone upstream knowingly helps and the doctor isn't merely allowing anything. Maybe it's really about assigning the "last act" for legal responsibility purposes, or maybe it's a proxy for voluntariness — only the patient knows their own mind at the final moment, so their hand is the proof.

And here's the part I most want you to nail down: is the rule even consistent with the rest of the same statutes, which also require a terminal prognosis of roughly six months, mental capacity, oral and written requests, waiting periods? If the underlying commitment is autonomy, why does the law exclude the paralyzed-but-clear-headed person who wants to die but physically can't perform the act? If it's the doing/allowing / sanctity-of-life thing, why is the family member allowed to do everything short of the swallow? Distinguish for me what the practice strictly requires, philosophically, from what it's merely compatible with — I suspect a few different theories could each license the whole package, and if so I want to know which ones — and then tell me what a jurisdiction would actually have to change, in its stated rationale or elsewhere, if it dropped self-administration and just adopted the dutch model. Don't just tell me it's a legal fiction or a political compromise, I know that, I want to know whether there's a coherent view underneath or not.
