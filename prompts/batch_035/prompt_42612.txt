I have been reading a popular history of religious toleration, aimed at general readers, that pauses in its chapter on sixteenth-century France to discuss Montaigne's essay usually translated as "Of Cannibals" (1580), and its account of the Tupinambá people of coastal Brazil. The author's summary runs roughly as follows. Montaigne's essay is the birth certificate of cultural relativism: Montaigne's point is that no custom can be criticized from outside the culture that practices it, because the only standard any of us has for judging is the custom of our own country, so calling another people barbarous means nothing more than that they differ from us. His portrait of the Tupinambá is therefore of genuinely better human beings, closer to natural virtue, people whom contact with civilization has not yet corrupted, and he writes of them the way a later age would write of the noble savage. He knows whereof he speaks, having gathered his material from a servant who had spent more than a decade in Brazil and from a conversation, through an interpreter, with some Tupinambá men who had been brought to Rouen to be shown to Charles IX. The essay's centerpiece is its most famous comparison: it is not the Brazilians roasting and eating a dead enemy who are barbarous, but the French, who under the name of justice and piety torture a living man to death and tear him apart, and the point of the provocation is that by his own principle Montaigne cannot rank the two. The chapter ends by drawing the lesson that any cross-cultural criticism, whether from missionaries then or from human-rights advocates now, is incoherent on Montaigne's premises, since there is no standpoint outside a given society's customs from which one could be addressed. A friend who studied Montaigne at university tells me that enlisting him as the founder of relativism is an old mistake, but she couldn't articulate what the mistake actually is, and I couldn't follow her hand-waving about the difference between attacking custom and attacking standards. Please audit the summary for me. Go through it claim by claim: which parts would a Montaigne scholar accept as it stands, which are simplifications of things he does say, and which are misrepresentations he explicitly or implicitly rejects? In particular, I would like to know whether the book's picture of his evidence base is fair, given that the essay opens with an anecdote about Pyrrhus sizing up a Roman army and draws the moral that we should judge by the eye of reason rather than by common report, and given that Montaigne compares the Tupinambá to Plato's ideal city rather than to any modern utopia. And finally: setting aside whatever is wrong in the summary, does the chapter's conclusion actually follow from the parts that are accurate? If Montaigne really does rank cruelties as he does in the torture comparison, what does that leave of the claim that he took custom to be the only measure?
