I've been reading about bargaining between AI systems — the sort of scenario where two agents that will have to divide a surplus can each publicly bind themselves in advance to a demand, and once bound cannot back down. What caught my attention is what people in that discussion call a commitment race: each agent wants to bind itself before the other does, because the first credible commitment sets the terms of the whole encounter, and this pushes both to bind themselves very early, possibly before either knows much about the situation it is committing about. I don't want to argue about AI, though; the pattern shows up in ordinary life, and my gut verdicts on the ordinary cases are a mess I'd like help untangling.

Here are cases where I have firm feelings. In a salary negotiation, an engineer tells the recruiter up front, truthfully and with a track record of actually doing it, that she will walk away from any offer below a certain number — I think that's smart; she sets the floor, and the negotiation happens above it. A friend buying a used car tells the dealer that his absolute ceiling is $9,000 when his true ceiling is $9,500, and the dealer, who has seen him walk out of a dealership before, takes the number at face value — I think he's played it well. But: a founder who announces a hard public launch date months before knowing whether the product will be ready, and then ships something broken rather than eat his words — I think that's a mistake, and not just in hindsight; he committed before the information was in. Same for a politician who pledges during a campaign never to raise a certain tax, locking in a position that looks worse with every year of governing — a commitment made under ignorance, and I hold it against him. And a pair of friends I actually watched do this: each one, separately but more or less simultaneously, told the other that if they cancelled plans one more time the friendship was over — both meant it, both had reasons for the past cancellations they never got to explain, and it ended over a conflict neither of them wanted. Individually each move looks rational; jointly it was a disaster, and I can't say either of them blundered.

The tension I can't resolve: my verdicts on the salary and car cases say commit early, because being first and credible is what wins; my verdicts on the founder, the politician, and the friends say commit only once you know enough, which usually means committing later, which is exactly how you lose to whoever commits ignorant-but-fast. If the principle is commit only when informed, it condemns cases I called smart; if it's commit whenever committing first pays off, it has to bless the founder and my ex-friends. Is there a single principle about when to make a binding commitment that captures most or all of these — and if there isn't, tell me which of my verdicts you'd overturn and why. Please state the principle precisely, tie each of its clauses to the case that motivates it, and apply it to the case I keep flip-flopping on: an agent that can bind itself at any moment but can also pay a small cost to wait a bit longer, in a situation where it expects the other side to be deliberating on the same timetable. Finally, be honest about which of my verdicts your best principle cannot save.
