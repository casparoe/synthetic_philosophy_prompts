my uncle Harlan is retiring after 37 years as a high school history teacher, and the family joke -- except it's not really a joke -- is that he has run his whole life on a system. He read Franklin's Autobiography as a teenager and took the "bold and arduous project of arriving at moral perfection" completely to heart: the little book with the virtues down the side and the days of the week across, one virtue per week, the cycle repeating four times a year, marking his faults like Franklin marked his black spots. He actually did this. We've all seen the little grid booklets, decades of them, boxed in the closet. And like Franklin, he'll cheerfully tell you his results: temperance, industry, frugality -- clean columns. Order -- the one Franklin himself never conquered, the virtue where Franklin basically gave up and decided a man with a speckled axe, where the edge was sacrificed to the bright face, was being a little too proud of his polish. And like Franklin, uncle Harlan added humility to the list late, only after his best friend (a Mennonite, which is our version of Franklin's Quaker) told him flat out he was generally thought proud. His version of imitating Franklin's move of never using certain words and never contradicting anybody directly, I should mention, made him an absolutely beloved teacher and a genuinely maddening brother, son, and uncle, in ways I'm still sorting out whether the poem -- no, sorry, the speech -- should touch.

Write his retirement toast, which I'm giving at a party where half the room is family (we all know the system, the booklets, the decades) and half is fellow teachers and old students who only know the beloved-classroom version of him. It should be about five minutes. Things it needs to do: it should honor the project without sneering at it -- Franklin's own tone toward his own project is the model here, half proud and half laughing, a man who concludes that tho he never arrived at perfection, he became a better and happier man for the attempt, and that a character of a faults-and-virtues grid got him the way a gardener gets rid of weeds by aiming at a garden worth weeding. It should land on what a system of virtue tracking is FOR once the career that justified the discipline is over -- the retirement is the actual occasion, not just the backdrop, so the end of the speech should say something true and specific about whether a man who has finished his apprenticeship keeps carrying the grid, or whether that was always confusing the instrument for the workshop. Family in-jokes fine in small doses but keep it warm. One typo-ish looseness in delivery is fine, since I'll be reading it off cards after two glasses of wine anyway. Do not make it a sermon and do not make Franklin a punchline -- uncle Harlan has heard every "early to bed" joke ever made and rates them all.
