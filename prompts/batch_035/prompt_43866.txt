You are a professional epistemologist, comfortable with both mainstream and formal work, who also thinks honestly about the sociology of your field. A debate has been running since the 1990s and, as far as you can tell, has reached a stable stalemate: epistemic contextualism (roughly, the view that the truth conditions of knowledge *attributions* shift with the conversational context) versus interest-relative invariantism, better known as pragmatic encroachment (roughly, the view that whether a given subject knows a given proposition is a stable, context-independent fact, but one that is partly fixed by that subject's practical stakes). Frustratingly, the two camps largely share their favorite data — the low-stakes/high-stakes bank cases, where the same depositor plausibly counts as knowing the bank is open on Saturday when nothing rides on it, yet seems not to when the deposit must be made today — and each theory accommodates those cases about as well as the other. Meanwhile, most of each camp's argumentative energy goes toward classical invariantists further out, not toward each other, and a third group has quietly stopped caring about "knows" attributions altogether, asking instead whether practical stakes can change what one is warranted in *believing*, not merely what one may assert or claim to know.

Diagnose the stall. Is this a substantive disagreement about a single epistemological fact, or are contextualists answering a semantic question about the word "knows" while encroachment theorists answer a genuinely epistemological question about what knowledge (or belief) itself consists in, with shared vocabulary making two questions look like one? Or is the dependency deeper — for instance, if belief itself is interest-relative, is contextualism about "knows" merely a shadow cast by a pragmatics of belief, so that the semantic debate should be deferred rather than settled on its own terms? Before committing to a diagnosis, consider at least three candidates (verbal dispute, different questions under one name, or a real substantive conflict over the same fact), and assess what each camp would have to concede for each to be correct. Then say what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: a new class of cases — say, third-person attributions where the speaker's and subject's stakes come apart, or apparent disagreements between two participants whose stakes differ — experimental findings about how ordinary speakers actually evaluate stakes-shifted cases, a theoretical result about whether encroachment generalizes beyond knowledge to belief and other attitudes, or an argument that the question is ill-posed as posed. Reason carefully and be candid about whether your diagnosis is one the field should already have accepted, and why it hasn't.
