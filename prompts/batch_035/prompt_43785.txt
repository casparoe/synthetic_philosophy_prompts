I came out of a graduate course on decoherence a convinced Everettian, and next month I have to defend that conversion in my qualifying exam; the examiner on my committee is known for pressing that Everettians cannot so much as make sense of expecting anything, since every outcome of every measurement happens to someone. Before I rehearse the standard replies, I want to know what would actually count as a reply. Write the desiderata that any account of probability in a theory where every outcome occurs must meet: why it must single out the Born weights rather than some other measure of the branches; whether it may help itself to probabilistic or decision-theoretic assumptions en route or must instead justify them; what it owes agents facing one-shot events, where there is no long-run frequency to lean on; what it says to someone who asks what to expect when each future successor of her will see a different result; whether the account may depend on how finely branches are individuated, given that decoherence supplies no privileged grain; and what it must preserve empirically so that no experiment could favor collapse theories. For each requirement, give the case behind it, mark which are genuinely non-negotiable and which merely smuggle in a single-outcome picture of measurement, and say whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable — and if one has to be surrendered, which is cheapest to give up. Please do not score the existing decision-theoretic or self-locating-uncertainty derivations against the list; it is the list itself I will be examined on.
