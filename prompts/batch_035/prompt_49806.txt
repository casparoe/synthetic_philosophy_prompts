In a philosophy-of-mind reading group I stumbled into (I'm a backend engineer by trade, my exposure to philosophy is mostly whatever leaks into tech blogs), we've been discussing Daniel Dennett's idea that the self is a "center of narrative gravity." The picture, as I understand it from his Consciousness Explained and a follow-up essay: a physicist's center of gravity is a fantastically useful posit -- you can calculate where it is, predict tipping and falling with it, design bridges around it -- but nobody thinks there's a little heavy dot located somewhere inside the chair. It's an abstractum, a theorist's fiction that does real explanatory work without being a thing in inventory. Dennett's claim is that the self is like that: an abstract center around which the narratives we spin get organized, useful for predicting and explaining a person's behavior, but equally not a thing -- no inner audience, no Cartesian theater, no pearl at the center of the onion. A couple of group members lean on this to argue the self is some kind of illusion in the strong sense, and that worrying about personal identity, death ("the center of gravity just stops being a useful posit"), or blame is like worrying about the fate of a center of gravity.

I'd like you to audit this analogy properly rather than just nod along. Please lay out the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of the mass distribution (is it the brain? the stream of narrative-spinning?), what corresponds to the physicist/theorist who posits the center, and what corresponds to the predictive success that makes centers of gravity worth positing in the first place. Then identify where the mapping breaks: centers of gravity are fixed by the physics of the situation and any competent calculator agrees on them, whereas my "center" seems to depend on who's telling the story and to what end; centers of gravity don't care how they're described, but self-narratives seem to feed back into behavior (placebo beliefs, identity-driven choices) in a way gravity centers never do. Crucially, tell me which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusions my group is drawing and which are merely surface-level. Does the "self as illusion" conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or does it need something stronger than the analogy delivers? And what would a better analogy be, if there is one -- a fictional character in a novel, an operating system's process abstraction, something else entirely -- and what would it get right that the gravity picture gets wrong? For context, I keep thinking about how in distributed systems we routinely reason about "the service" as a single logical entity even though it's a dozen stateless replicas behind a load balancer, and I can't decide whether that's the same move Dennett is making or a disanalogy in itself, so I'd be curious whether that helps or muddies things.
