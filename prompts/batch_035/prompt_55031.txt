In the spring I am expected to convert to my fiancée's faith before our wedding, and the officiant could not have been clearer: in matters of religion the commitment comes first and the conviction arrives afterward, so demanding evidence in advance would get the whole thing backwards. This has made the old dispute between Clifford and James personally urgent for me, and I would rather treat it as a question with a truth value than as a mood I happen to be in.

Please give me your credence that Clifford's principle — that it is "wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone to believe anything on insufficient evidence" — admits of genuine exceptions, so that there are cases in which believing ahead of the evidence is permissible. But I want the derivation, not just the number. Decompose it into terms such as: (1) the probability that belief is ever sufficiently under voluntary control, even indirectly — through what James called our passional nature tipping the scales — for a question of permission to arise at all; (2) the probability that evidentialism gives way precisely where an option is live, forced, and momentous and the evidence is genuinely silent rather than merely unwelcome; (3) the probability that a religious conversion of the kind I face actually satisfies those conditions, since I could convert without believing, or believe without converting, which suggests the option may not be forced in James's strict sense; and (4) any further terms you think belong in the calculation. Assign each term a number with one line of justification, combine them, and then tell me which term the final figure is most sensitive to, and what facts about a case like mine would move that term the most.
