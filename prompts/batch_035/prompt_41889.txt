Last year my city ran a citizens' assembly on housing policy — about 80 people picked by civic lottery, six weekends, expert testimony, and a set of recommendations handed to the council at the end. I know two people who get into it every time it comes up: Dan, who was one of the 80, and Priya, who thinks the whole format is a well-funded scam. I mostly stayed out of it but I'll admit they each landed real punches, and I can't score it myself.

Round 1 — Priya opened with the focus group charge. She said: you 80 didn't really decide anything. You were handed a pre-set agenda, a facilitator ran every session, the expert list was picked by the secretariat, and at the end you voted on options that staff had drafted. That's a focus group with extra steps, and calling it democracy-by-lottery is PR. Real democracy has accountability — you answer to nobody: no election, no recall, nothing.

Dan's reply: the experts don't vote, we do — and everything you just said applies to elected officials with the knobs turned worse, not better. MPs get briefed by civil servants, their agendas are set by whips and leadership, and their options are drafted by staffers. At least in the assembly the agenda documents were published, and every session was minuted. If steering is the charge, the assembly is the more auditable of the two formats.

Round 2 — Priya: fine, but an MP can be punished. If the briefers systematically slant the testimony, voters can throw the government out — that's the backstop. What's yours? If the secretariat quietly stacked the expert list, none of you would ever know you'd been steered, and there is no mechanism that would surface that bias, let alone correct it.

Dan: voters can't detect steering either — that's exactly why money and media can move elections. Our backstop is different: nobody could target us. You can't run a persuasion campaign against a group that was random and unknown until it finished. And because we had no re-election to chase, we had no incentive to pander or trim recommendations toward whatever polls well. Elections give politicians a reason to please the majority that shows up; the absence of that pressure gave us room to actually weigh tradeoffs.

Round 3 — Priya called that romantic. No accountability, she said, also means no discipline. Some of you no-showed after weekend two; some treated it as a nice civic holiday. Elections at least filter for people who want the job and will pay a price for doing it badly. Also — and she said this was the part that mattered — the sample isn't a sample. The 80 who accepted aren't the 80 who were drawn: the people who agree to six weekends of that work are disproportionately the civic-minded, the retired, the talkative. So the "descriptive microcosm" story is inflated — you're a microcosm of people who say yes, and saying yes is itself a biased trait. You can say alot about Athens, and Athens drew the lot for offices, but Athens also had slavery, so citing Athens settles nothing.

Dan: self-selection and dropout are real but they're design problems, not fate — pay a real wage, chase the no-shows hard, replace decliners by redrawing from the same strata. And on discipline: the filter you like selects for people who want power, and wanting power is not obviously the virtue you want in someone deliberating about housing. We didn't invent the lot, and the reason it keeps getting reinvented is that elections drift toward the ambitious.

Round 4 — Priya's closing was: even in the best case, the assembly is advisory. The council took your recommendations, kept the ones it already liked, and binned the rest, so the real decision stayed exactly where it was, with the people who already had it. That's the tell — the lottery decorates a decision that was never yours.

Dan closed: that's a complaint about the council, not about the lot. If the assembly's power is weak, the fix is more power, not less — and by her own logic, an elected body that ignores a representative sample is more exposed, not less.
