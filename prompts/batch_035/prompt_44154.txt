In our reading group we have spent months on the debate over whether satisficing is a genuine alternative to optimizing in theories of rational choice, and the discussion has deadlocked in a way I want diagnosed rather than adjudicated. One side holds, following the spirit of Herbert Simon, that a rational agent can permissibly stop searching once an option clears an aspiration level, and that this is not merely a pragmatic concession to limited minds: it is a different picture of what rational choice is, on which the agent is not trying to pick the best element of a set but to find a good-enough element of a world whose options are only ever partially enumerated. The other side replies that satisficing is optimization wearing a costume: once you count the costs of search, the disvalue of delay, and the diminishing returns of further comparison, the satisficer is simply maximizing expected utility net of deliberation costs, and the aspiration level is just where the marginal cost of looking further crosses the marginal expected gain. On this view the two frameworks are empirically equivalent as descriptions of behavior and normatively equivalent as well, so the disagreement is empty.

What I want is a diagnosis of what the disagreement actually is, not a verdict on which side is right. My own working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that it turns on the individuation of options: the critic assumes that continue searching is itself an option in the choice set, so that stopping at the first good-enough option can be redescribed as an optimal choice among extended acts, while the defender either denies that the agent has access to a well-defined space of extended acts in the first place or insists that rationality is a property of procedures rather than of final selections, in which case the redescriptions change the object being evaluated. But I am not confident that this is the right diagnosis, or the only one. Is the dispute really verbal, hinging on whether costs of deliberation are counted as inside the choice problem or as part of the background? Is it a substantive but untestable difference about whether rationality is outcome-directed or process-directed? Does the psychological literature on maximizers versus satisficers bear on it at all, or does it import a different notion of satisficing -- one about dispositions and well-being rather than single choices -- that has been illicitly used as evidence for the normative claim? And what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress? A formal result showing the frameworks make different predictions, a principled criterion for when two decision theories are equivalent, or an argument that the question of what counts as rational is itself framework-relative and the impasse should be expected to persist? Please answer in American English, engage both sides as their proponents actually argue, and be explicit about whether your diagnosis leaves the dispute substantive.
