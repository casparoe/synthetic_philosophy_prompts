I'm an honours statistics student taking a seminar on the history of economic thought, and I've been assigned to write a term paper on Keynes's views on probability and uncertainty. The seminar has us reading the Treatise on Probability alongside the relevant chapters of the General Theory, but my own training is entirely in modern Bayesian statistics and machine learning. That's the vocabulary I actually think in: priors, likelihoods, posteriors, calibration, coverage, model averaging, the distinction between aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty, misspecification, distribution shift. I would like you to restate Keynes's position in that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, and then report what doesn't survive the translation.

To be concrete about the parts of Keynes's view I most need translated:

1. His claim that probability is a logical relation between a set of premises and a conclusion, rather than a degree of belief, a frequency, or a property of an event. Is there anything in my vocabulary that plays this role, or does Bayesian statistics simply not have logical relations of this kind?

2. His claims that some probabilities are non-numerical and that some pairs of probabilities are incomparable -- not just unknown, but not ordered. In my world, every prior and posterior is a distribution on the reals, so everything is comparable up to sampling error. What would I have to do to my machinery to say what he is saying?

3. His distinction between the probability of an argument and the weight of an argument, where weight tracks how much relevant evidence we have, and can rise while probability itself stays fixed or even becomes less favorable. Is this anything more than what I'd call epistemic uncertainty, or posterior variance, or disagreement across an ensemble? Keynes seems to think weight is a genuinely separate quantity that practical reason should respond to independently, and I'm not sure my framework has room for that.

4. The later economic material in the General Theory and his subsequent clarifications: radical uncertainty that is not risk, the impossibility of assigning probabilities to some future outcomes at all, and his account of what people do instead -- convention, confidence, and animal spirits. My instinct is to translate all of this as model misspecification plus distribution shift, and his conventions as heuristics for acting under it, but I suspect that flattens something.

For each item, tell me what translates cleanly, what can only be translated with distortion (say exactly where the distortion enters), and what has no expression in the target vocabulary at all. Keep the translation honest: don't smuggle in the Bayesian framework's own assumptions -- coherence axioms, betting interpretations, the claim that degrees of belief must be representable by a single probability function -- and present them as if they were part of Keynes's view rather than commitments he explicitly rejected. Then give me your overall residue report: does the leftover point to something Bayesian statistics is genuinely missing, or does it show that Keynes's claims were less clear, or less substantive, than they initially look to someone trained like me? Along the way, please tell me whether the half-measures I've heard of -- imprecise probabilities, robust Bayes, sets of priors, hierarchical uncertainty about priors -- actually capture the parts of Keynes I'm worried about losing, or just rename the problem. I want to be able to argue in my term paper that the translation either succeeds or fails in a specific, checkable way, not just to gesture at Keynes as a patron saint of anti-Bayesian caution.
