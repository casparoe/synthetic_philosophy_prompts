I am a senior program officer at a mid-sized private foundation that makes about seventy grants a year across three portfolios: global health, domestic policy reform, and community organizing. Two years ago the foundation reorganized its grantmaking around effective-altruist-style cost-effectiveness reasoning, and the result was predictable: interventions with clean, measurable outcomes — deworming, insecticide-treated nets, direct cash transfers — now dominate the portfolio, while proposals like tenant organizing or civil-service reform can never clear the bar, because no one can attach a credible number to them. Our new director accepts the standard critique that this reflects a built-in measurability bias: cost-effectiveness reasoning does not merely prefer what can be measured, it gradually rewrites the whole portfolio so that what cannot be measured stops competing at all. But she explicitly rejects the lazy version of the institutional critique — the one that simply declares systemic change more important and thereby exempts every systemic-change proposal from scrutiny. Both errors, she says, are the same error: letting the ease of measurement stand in for the size of the effect, in opposite directions.

She has asked me to draft a screening procedure that program staff can actually run in the forty-five minutes we realistically have per letter of inquiry, before anything goes to a full proposal. Please write that procedure. It must include the following:

1. An ordered list of questions, each with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer and what counts as a failure. The order matters: early questions should be cheap and knock out weak proposals fast.

2. At least one question addressing the two distinct forms the institutional critique takes, and keeping them distinct, because they call for different responses: the causal form (a targeted intervention may prop up the very system that generates the problem it treats, so the measured benefit is partly an illusion — a food program that stabilizes the policies causing the hunger) and the epistemic form (over time, the funding landscape itself reshapes so that unmeasurable approaches stop being proposed, which means the absence of competing applications is not evidence of anything). The procedure should say what an applicant could plausibly show that would answer each form, and what to do when, as usual, nothing can be shown.

3. At least one question on counterfactual fungibility: if we decline, does someone else fund it, and does that make our money marginal or moot? The procedure should specify how this interacts differently with a saturated intervention area and a neglected one, and it should be honest that staff will rarely know the funding landscape well enough to answer.

4. Explicit marks on the two places judgment cannot be eliminated: where the question is one of values rather than facts and must be handed to a person with a name attached to the decision, and where the procedure should stop and reject rather than hand upward. These must be visibly distinguished, so that a rejection never masquerades as a technical finding when it is really a values call.

Then run the procedure on three cases and report its verdicts:

- A deworming program in a region where two large funders already operate, with solid trial evidence behind it, requesting expansion into a third country.
- A state-level campaign to reform public-housing law, run by a coalition with a credible track record of past legislative wins, whose central outcomes (tenants' bargaining power, the credibility of future threats) are in principle unmeasurable and whose proposal says so plainly.
- A well-run, honest community organization whose theory of change amounts to organizing builds organizing — capacity now, campaigns later, with no specified later target. Design this case so that the procedure's verdict is one my staff will dislike, and then say plainly whether that verdict is a feature of the procedure or a misfire, and how you can tell the difference in general.

Finally, include a section stating what the procedure cannot do: where it will systematically misfire, which of its steps are judgment calls dressed up as arithmetic, and what it would take — in staff time or data we will never have — to do better. The deliverable is a working document for non-philosophers, so it should be written in their language, but the distinctions that matter (causal vs. epistemic institutional critique, measurement vs. value, marginal vs. replaceable funding) must survive the simplification, because a program officer who cannot state them is exactly the officer who will apply the procedure as a mechanical scorecard, which is the failure mode we are trying to escape.
