I do demand forecasting for a regional grocery chain — mostly statistical extrapolation from sales history, with manager judgment layered on top — and last week, in a meeting reviewing our holiday-season model, a new colleague with a philosophy background argued that the entire exercise rests on nothing. His argument has bothered me since, because each step seemed fine when he said it and the conclusion is one my job commits me to rejecting. As he laid it out: (1) Any forecast is justified only by the assumption that patterns observed in the past persist into the future. (2) Goodman's grue paradox shows that any body of past observations confirming a hypothesis H equally confirms a grue-like variant H*, which agrees with H on everything observed so far and disagrees about everything after some cutoff. (3) Our sales data through mid-January are, by construction, consistent both with the hypothesis that the February pattern resembles previous Februaries and with an unlimited family of grue-like rivals that match the hypothesis on all data to date and then diverge after a chosen date. (4) Nothing in the data can distinguish these hypotheses, since they entail exactly the same things about every observed case. (5) So no possible evidence, however extensive, makes one forecast more reasonable than any rival. (6) Therefore our forecasts — and all induction, from grocery demand to the expectation that the sun will rise tomorrow — are epistemically on a par with coin flips, and the only intellectually honest move is to stop pretending the backtest means anything. He concluded that we might as well hand seasonal planning to intuition and say so openly. The room found this amusing; I found it unsettling, because I could not point to the false step.

He has since told me, in a friendly way, that the conclusion is just Goodman's own, so if I don't like it I owe him a premise number — and he's genuinely curious whether I'd bite the bullet on (6) or object earlier. For my own peace of mind as much as for the follow-up meeting: which step should I reject, why did that step look fine when stated, and what does rejecting it cost? I dimly recall that Goodman himself thought there was a principled way to break such ties — something about favoring predicates that have a track record of being successfully projected in past inductions — but I don't see how that helps rather than just pushing the problem back a level, since that track record is itself known only inductively, and one can cook up grue-like predicates with distorted "track records" too. Is he right that the response is circular, or does the regress stop somewhere honest? And if the regress does stop, I'd like to know what, if anything, that implies for what my backtest can and cannot be said to show — a modest conclusion I could actually defend in the meeting without claiming more than induction can deliver.
