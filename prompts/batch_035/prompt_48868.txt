A book I'm reading for a work discussion group — a popular treatment of behavioral economics and rationality — has a chapter on decision-making under uncertainty that summarizes the Ellsberg paradox roughly as follows. In 1961, Daniel Ellsberg, later famous for other reasons, ran experiments in which people were offered bets on draws from an urn with a known mix of red and black balls versus an urn with an unknown mix, and found that they consistently preferred the known urn: they would rather bet on red from the known urn than red from the unknown one, and would rather bet on black from the known urn than black from the unknown one. The book says this pattern cannot be squared with expected utility theory, because it violates independence, the theory's central axiom, which in this setting says that if you prefer betting on red from the known urn you must prefer betting on black from it too, since the two bets have the same odds. It concludes that, since expected utility theory is the benchmark of rationality, ambiguity aversion is straightforwardly a bias — on a par with the certainty effect — and that later work such as maxmin expected utility was an attempt to describe the bias formally rather than to excuse it. The chapter closes by suggesting that regulators should treat ambiguity aversion the way they treat other biases: nudge people back toward the known odds, for instance by requiring firms to disclose point estimates rather than ranges.

I have a feeling the chapter is directionally right but cutting corners, and I don't know the literature well enough to locate the corners. Please audit the summary: go claim by claim and tell me which parts a decision theorist would accept, which they'd treat as a defensible simplification, and which are genuine conflations or overstatements. In particular, I'd like to know whether the setup Ellsberg actually used matches what the book describes, whether the axiom it names is the one the standard arguments actually target, and whether the equation of ambiguity aversion with an ordinary bias is something the field settled or something it still argues about. And separately: once you've corrected the missteps, does the book's closing policy recommendation — nudging people back to the known odds, preferring point estimates to ranges — still follow, or was it being carried by the parts that were wrong?
