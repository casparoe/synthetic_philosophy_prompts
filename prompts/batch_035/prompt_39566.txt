I lead evals at a company that ships ML features into a medical scheduling product (nothing clinical, just booking, rescheduling, triaging which queue a request goes to, stuff like that). We have a recurring fight I'm tired of adjudicating by vibes: when one of our models does surprisingly well on a task, half the team says it "understands" what patients want and we can generalize from that, the other half says it's pattern matching that will fall apart the first time the world shifts. I've been reading about Dennett's idea of competence without comprehension -- the "strange inversion of reasoning" he attributes to Darwin and Turing, where you explain a system's intelligent-looking performance by going bottom-up from design/selection pressure instead of top-down from an intelligent designer or inner understander. I want that idea turned into something my team can actually run on a concrete result, like a written procedure with steps, rather than a philosophy of mind position paper.

Specifically, write me a decision procedure an eval engineer could follow when a model clears a benchmark or does well in offline testing, that tells them how much they're licensed to conclude about how the model will behave in new situations, based on the competence/comprehension distinction. Things it should cover:

- What questions to ask about the result in some sensible order -- e.g., whether the evaluation distribution differs from deployment in ways that matter, whether the successful behavior was selectable or trainble for in the way the deployment behavior would need to be, whether performance survives perturbations that break surface pattern-matching, whatever you think the distinction actually requires -- and what counts as a good enough answer at each step
- Where the procedure stops with "you're not licensed to conclude anything" vs "you're licensed to generalize cautiously" vs "this question can't be settled by procedure and an engineer has to make a judgment call," and I want you to be honest about which of your steps are just judgment wearing a labcoat
- The procedure has to handle the case where the model was trained on data from the very process it's now predicting (our booking data is generated by our old routing rules), because I suspect competence that comes from mimicking the output of a rule-following process isn't the same thing as competence that will hold up when the rules change -- tell me if that's right or if I'm overcomplicating it

Then run it on three cases from my actual backlog: (1) a fine-tuned model that routes patient messages to queues and hit 97% agreement with the current human router on historical data, (2) a model that explains to patients why a requested slot isn't available, which does great on the slots from our test set but we've never seen it on a slot type that was added after training, and (3) a case where the procedure's verdict should annoy someone -- maybe where it tells us we can't make the "our AI understands patient needs" claim that marketing already put in the deck, or where it says the safest-looking model is the one we should trust least. Your pick, but the procedure should give an answer the team won't like at least once.

Also tell me plainly what the procedure can't do. Like, I take it the whole Darwin/Turing point is that "does it really understand" might not be the useful question at all, and if that's true then a procedure built on this distinction inherits that problem -- I'd rather know that up front than discover it in a postmortem. Keep it practical enough that a new hire could run it in an afternoon without having read anything by Dennett, and dont make it so long nobody uses it.
