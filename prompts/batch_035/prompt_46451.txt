I've been reading LessWrong and the EA Forum since the early 2010s, and I now sit on the grant-evaluation panel of a small longtermist-aligned fund. We make roughly thirty funding decisions a year, each involving a few weeks of part-time review by panelists who are smart generalists but not domain experts, and the stakes — by our own lights — are about as high as stakes get. Our recurring problem is that an awful lot of applications bottom out, sooner or later, in the hinge of history hypothesis: the claim that our era is unusually influential relative to the entire future, so that leverage available now will not be available later. Sometimes the applicant says this explicitly, citing the usual longtermist sources; sometimes it does quiet work under the surface, in any intervention whose value depends on locking in gains rather than on compounding them. Panel discussion keeps stalling because we also routinely conflate two claims the blogs have taught me to distinguish: the strong hinge claim that tractability is decreasing from here, so acting now beats acting later all else equal; and the weaker 'time of perils' claim that existential risk is concentrated in a window which, one way or another, will close.

I'd like a decision procedure the panel can actually run on an application: a fixed order of questions, criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer to each, a point at which the procedure returns 'do not rest this grant on a hinge claim' and hands the decision back to ordinary cost-effectiveness reasoning, and a point at which it concedes the question is one of values or decision-making under deep uncertainty that the panel must vote on, not something a checklist can settle. The procedure should be honest that the empirical core of the hinge hypothesis is not something five generalists can resolve in a meeting; its job is to locate applications on a map — how much of their case survives if this turns out to be an ordinary time, and how much only works if it isn't — rather than to adjudicate the hypothesis itself. It should also tell us how to handle applicants who have learned the procedure and optimise against it.

Then run the procedure on three applications we have actually agonised over: a technical AI safety research group whose case is that if transformative AI arrives within decades, alignment work done now is decisive, and if it doesn't, the work was mostly wasted but cheap; an archival project that wants to preserve records useful for rebuilding after a civilisational catastrophe, whose case explicitly disclaims any claim about timing — it is valuable whenever it is done, merely more urgent if the risk window is now; and a values-spreading grant whose proponents argue we should shape the long-run future by instilling better values now, before values get locked in. Include at least one case where the procedure delivers a verdict I won't like — I confess a soft spot for the archivists — and state plainly what the procedure cannot do: which questions it routes to human judgment, and where the simplifications will misfire. Please answer in British English.
