So I'm a novelist -- four books out, all quiet domestic stuff, nothing that would prepare me for this -- and I'm three drafts deep into a novel set on a small cattle ranch in eastern Oregon. The narrator is the rancher's daughter, moved back home, recently quit meat, and constitutionally incapable of explaining why. The problem is I keep losing arguments to her, in my own head, because it turns out I don't know what I believe about eating animals either. So I've been writing down my gut reactions to cases as they come up, mostly so I stop contradicting myself in her dialogue. Here they are, as honest as I can make them:

- Eating a chicken sandwich feels clearly worse to me than eating a steak. There's what, two hundred chickens in a cow, and their lives are so much worse.
- Steak from a pasture-raised cow that had a genuinely decent life on grass: fine. I'd serve it to my vegetarian-leaning friends with only a small apology.
- And yet -- I know the soy that feeds those chickens gets harvested with field mice and god knows what else in the way, and I feel literally nothing about that. Not a twinge. Even though the mouse count is probably in the same ballpark as the bird count.
- Venison from a deer my neighbor shot in a cull of an overpopulated, starving herd: fine, maybe even a small positive.
- My cat eats factory-farmed chicken and I buy it weekly without a flicker of guilt. I would not eat that chicken myself.
- A fledgling fell out of a nest in my yard last spring and I spent twenty minutes on a ladder getting it back in because I felt genuinely obligated to. The woods behind my house are full of birds slowly dying of parasites and starvation at a scale I can't even picture, and my verdict there is that I have no obligation at all, and honestly I get a little suspicious of people who think we should intervene in wild suffering. I cannot make that feel consistent with the fledgling. The fledgling feels like the more honest one.
- Eating eggs from my sister's backyard hens: so obviously fine it barely registers as a decision.

My working theory so far is something like: what makes eating an animal wrong is how bad its life was, not how many animals are involved. That handles steak vs chicken, venison, probably even the harvest mice if a quick death during a life that would've been worse in the wild counts as okay? But it does nothing for the cat food (bad lives, zero guilt) and the fledgling points the opposite way entirely -- one tiny bird, massive felt obligation.

So, what principle actually captures all this, or close to it? Does my "badness of the life" theory get repaired or abandoned? What does whatever survives say about the case I keep going back and forth on -- oysters, which I gather might not feel anything at all, so is farming them fine, or is something else doing the work there? And please be honest about which of my verdicts has to go, because something clearly does. I need to know before I write chapter eleven, where she has to defend herself at thanksgiving.
