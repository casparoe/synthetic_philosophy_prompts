I have been recording my own verdicts about how blame should respond to luck, in the hope that they add up to a coherent principle, and I would like you to tell me whether they do, help me repair my tentative statement of it, or else tell me which verdicts have to go. Here are the verdicts.

(1) Two drivers leave the same party equally drunk, having made equally careless decisions to drive home. A child runs into the street in front of one of them, and he kills her; the other encounters no one at all. My verdict: the man who killed the child is far more blameworthy and deserves severe punishment, while the other is guilty of little more than a serious traffic offense, even though the only difference between them is where a child happened to be.

(2) Two nurses give identical injections without checking the label as they were trained to. One patient dies from the error; the other, by pure coincidence, needed exactly the drug that was mislabeled. Again I blame the nurse with the dead patient considerably more.

(3) Two men run identical investment frauds with identical victims. One is exposed because a random clerical error triggers an audit; the other's victims never discover what happened, and he retires wealthy. Here my intuition reverses direction: I regard the successful fraudster as at least as bad as, and possibly worse than, the one who was caught, even though everything under each man's control was the same, and the second man's luck was entirely better.

(4) My brother-in-law was born with a ferocious temper and fights it daily with genuine effort; perhaps once a year he loses the fight and shouts something cruel. A friend of mine is naturally placid and has never lost his temper in his life; on the rare occasion he says something cutting, it is cold and deliberate. I blame my friend far more for one such remark than I blame my brother-in-law for his annual explosion, even though my friend's starting point was luckier and my brother-in-law's outburst was less under his control.

(5) A colleague of mine had an easy opportunity to trade on inside information and took it. I am fairly confident that, seated in his chair, I would have taken it too. Yet I do not conclude that I am nearly as blameworthy as he is; the bare fact that he had the chance and I did not seems to me genuinely relevant to how much each of us deserves.

My tentative principle is that blame should track only what was under the person's control — except that outcomes matter when — and there I stall. Verdicts (4) fits the control part nicely, but (1) and (2) let worse luck increase blame, while (3) and (5) seem to suggest that better luck (escaping detection; having the opportunity at all) either increases blame or at least does not reduce it. Is there a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures all five verdicts or nearly all of them? Please state the principle precisely, note which of my cases motivates each clause, and say honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why that verdict rather than the clause should be surrendered. Please also tell me what the resulting principle predicts for a case I have not resolved: two soldiers each throw themselves onto what they believe is a live grenade to shield their squad; one grenade is live and one is a dud. Should the survivor's medal, or our admiration, be any smaller than the dead soldier's — and does that verdict follow from the principle you derive, or is it a further departure from it that I should refuse?
