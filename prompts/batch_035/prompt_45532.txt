**The exam question** (take-home, 400–600 words):

"Standard Bayesian decision theory assumes that a rational agent's credence in any proposition is a single precise real number. Explain how the imprecise probability approach relaxes this assumption, and show how the resulting framework accommodates the pattern of preferences Ellsberg observed in his famous urn experiments. **The student's answer:**

"Standard decision theory assumes that a rational agent's credence in any proposition is a single real number between 0 and 1. The imprecise probability approach relaxes this: an agent's credal state is represented not by one probability function but by a whole set of them, sometimes called a representor. Her credence in a proposition is then read off as the interval spanned by the lowest and highest probabilities assigned to that proposition anywhere in the set. An agent who knows only that a coin lands heads somewhere between 40% and 60% of the time can be represented by the set of all probability functions consistent with that constraint, rather than being forced to invent a single sharp value.

The framework is motivated by Ellsberg's two-urn experiment. One urn contains exactly 30 red balls and 60 balls that are black or yellow in unknown proportion. Subjects typically prefer betting on red (a known 1/3 chance) to betting on black, but prefer betting on black-or-yellow (a known 2/3 chance) to betting on red-or-yellow. No single precise expected-utility maximizer can exhibit both preferences, since together they contradict the sure-thing principle, which is just the independence axiom of von Neumann–Morgenstern utility theory under another name. The imprecise framework handles the pattern neatly: the red bets have sharp expected values of 1/3, while the black bet's expectation ranges over a wide interval and the red-or-yellow bet's expectation ranges over an interval starting at 1/3. An agent who weighs options by their worst-case expected value across the representor will strictly prefer red to black and black-or-yellow to red-or-yellow, exactly matching the observed choices. So imprecise decision theory proves that Ellsberg's subjects were rational all along; the precise theory simply could not represent their reasoning. This connects to Joyce's point that forcing an agent to commit to a sharp credence makes her fabricate information she does not actually have.

Several decision rules have been proposed for imprecise credences. Levi's maximality rule says that an act is admissible when no competing act has a strictly higher expected utility on every probability function in the representor. Walley's E-admissibility rule, by contrast, admits an act just in case it maximizes expected utility relative to at least one probability function in the set. Maximality is the more permissive of the two, and it is the most conservative of the standard rules — more cautious even than the Γ-maximin rule, which scores each act by its minimum expected utility over the representor and chooses the act with the best minimum. Because maximality works by pairwise comparison against every alternative, it always returns at least one admissible act, so the agent is never left with nothing to choose.

The strongest objection concerns dilation. When an imprecise agent conditions on new evidence, the representor can only grow wider: conditioning on a proposition never shrinks the set of probability functions. Learning therefore makes the agent less, rather than more, precise — the more evidence she acquires, the vaguer her credences become. Since rational agents are supposed to converge toward sharper opinions as evidence accumulates, this is usually taken as a decisive refutation of the whole framework."
