I've been asked to state my position on the precautionary principle for a panel on the regulation of emerging risks, and before I do I want a consistency audit of it. Something in the package feels unstable to me, but I can't locate the instability: each item seems right to me on its own, and I don't know which of them, if any, I'm prepared to give up. Here is the package.

(1) The precautionary principle is a principle of practical rationality and not merely a policy slogan. Where a possible harm is grave and effectively irreversible, it can be rational to restrict an activity, or to intervene, without established proof of harm; demanding proof of harm before acting is a mistake in exactly the cases the principle is about.

(2) What triggers the principle is the severity and irreversibility of the possible harm, not its probability. A very small probability of an irreversible catastrophe can demand action that a much larger probability of a comparable but reversible harm would not. That probabilities cannot yet be estimated is not a reason for the principle to fall silent; it is the condition under which it is supposed to do its work.

(3) The burden of justification falls on whoever introduces a new risk. Regulators do not have to prove harm before restricting; the introducer has to show safety before introducing.

(4) The principle is neutral between intervening and refraining. Not acting is itself a choice for which someone is answerable, and a restriction is itself an intervention that can carry grave and irreversible risks. So precaution cuts against inaction as readily as against action, and the objection that a proposed remedy carries risks of its own is not, by itself, a good reply to a precautionary argument.

(5) Non-paralysis is a constraint on any acceptable formulation. A version of the principle under which, in some choice situation, every option is forbidden is a reductio of that version, not an application of it. In every situation the principle must leave a permissible option; it cannot simply go silent when things get hard.

(6) The principle's scope is restricted. Where the relevant harms are reversible and the probabilities can be estimated, ordinary cost-benefit analysis should decide, and precaution has nothing to add.

(7) On cases, I hold: that the case for aggressive climate action is a precautionary case and a sound one; that the course actually taken on DDT — restricting agricultural use while preserving public-health use — was roughly right, and that I would defend the restriction even if it had raised the cost of malaria control; that when suit was brought to stop a new heavy-ion collider on the theory that the collisions might initiate a world-destroying outcome, refusing to halt it was right and the demand was unreasonable; and that research into solar geoengineering ought not to be shut down on precautionary grounds, though I make no claim about deployment.

Please go through the pairs and tell me which are genuinely in tension and how badly, and which only look to be. For each real tension, say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the list. Rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I would not accept a dissolution that quietly hollows out one of the commitments while leaving it officially on the books — for instance, if the principle is said to trigger only on serious possibilities, I want to know what makes a possibility serious without collapsing back into probability, which (2) says is beside the point. Don't assume I have noticed where the problem is; I haven't. And don't soften the audit on the assumption that I have a favorite item — I don't think I do, but I would rather find out from you than from the panel.
