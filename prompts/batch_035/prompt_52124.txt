You are a professional philosopher specializing in the ethics of technology and collective-action problems, and I'd like you to reason with me with unusual care. Here is the background: my roommate is a third-year PhD student in machine learning, and after a departmental seminar on AI governance we got into a long argument about whether frontier models should be open-sourced. He is strongly pro-release, and by the end of the evening he had assembled the following argument, which I have not been able to break. It seems to me to prove far too much, but every time I try to say where it slips, my objection dissolves once he restates the step. As he wrote it:

(1) Releasing information is wrong only if the release makes things worse than they would otherwise be — only if it raises expected harm. (2) The danger in a model's weights is fixed by what is in the weights; open-sourcing copies existing knowledge to more people, it does not create any new dangerous knowledge. (3) Determined actors — actors willing to steal, leak, or buy their way to the weights — will get them whether or not they are open-sourced: closed models leak and get stolen, and determined actors are precisely the ones with the patience for that. (4) Everyone else — casual, opportunistic actors — cannot do serious harm with weights in hand, because serious harm requires a persistence and follow-through that casual actors, by definition, lack. (5) So open-sourcing changes nothing for determined actors (they obtain the weights either way) and nothing for casual actors (they cannot use them), and therefore it raises expected harm for no one. (6) Hence it is never wrong to open-source a frontier model, however capable — even one whose weights would cut the time and expertise needed to engineer a pathogen from years and a specialized team to weeks and a laptop.

I cannot accept the conclusion: he is committed to saying that it is morally fine to hand out step-by-step bioweapon instructions to anyone who asks. Yet when I go through the premises one at a time, each of them looks defensible on its own, and the inferences between them look valid. My instinct is that the trouble lies somewhere between (3) and (5), but whenever I try to make the objection precise, the words fall apart. Before you answer, please brainstorm the candidate failure points; then tell me exactly which step or inference to reject, explain why that step looked fine to me when I considered it in isolation, and — this is the part I most want — price the rejection: what my roommate gets to say in reply, what his best response would be, and what I have to live with if I instead give up a different premise to save his conclusion. Please take your time and be exact; a vague gesture at "it's more complicated than that" will not help me.
