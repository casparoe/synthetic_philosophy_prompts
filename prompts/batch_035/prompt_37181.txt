I work in compliance at a mid-sized multinational, and something about our gift-and-hospitality policy has started to bother me philosophically, though I can't quite articulate why. The policy, like most corporate codes and government ethics rules, draws a bright line: employees may accept gifts from vendors and clients below a certain dollar threshold (ours is $50; some companies use $25, some $100) without any paperwork, must report gifts above it, and must decline gifts above a higher cap or above a cumulative annual limit. There are carve-outs: logo-branded promotional items of "nominal value" are always fine, and a nice dinner or event tickets get their own separate (more generous) treatment, while cash or cash-equivalents like gift cards are prohibited at any amount. Meanwhile, the criminal law our policy is supposed to keep us clear of has no threshold at all — a $10 payment made with a corrupt quid pro quo intent is a bribe just as much as a $10,000 one, and intent is doing all the work.

So my question is: what theory of corruption does the threshold system presuppose? It seems to treat corruption as a matter of degree rather than kind — as if there is a de minimis level of gift-giving below which influence on judgment is either impossible or acceptable — which sits oddly with the criminal law's all-or-nothing view, and also with the intuition many of us have that a steady drip of small favors might corrupt someone more effectively than one big gift ever could. And the dinner-vs-cash asymmetry looks like it isn't tracking influence at all, but something about verifiability and deniability: a shared meal is visible and discursive, cash is private, so maybe the policy presupposes that corruption is less about the effect on the recipient than about the character of the transaction — its transparency or its ability to be audited later. Is that a coherent implicit theory, or several theories stitched together?

There's also a cross-cultural dimension that makes me more uncomfortable. We apply the same thresholds and the same cash prohibition everywhere we operate, including in countries where exchanging substantial gifts is the ordinary grammar of a business relationship and refusing one is an insult. A colleague from our Singapore office argued that the policy quietly presupposes an arms-length, impersonal model of market relations — in which any personalistic exchange between counterparties is presumptively suspect — and treats that model as universal, when it's really a specific (arguably Western, arguably modern) conception of how business should be done. I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory (or theories) of corruption that a threshold-and-reporting system needs in order to make sense, identify where the parts of the practice presuppose inconsistent things (e.g., magnitude-based thresholds vs. intent-based criminal law vs. form-based distinctions like cash vs. dinner), say where the practice is actually philosophically underdetermined — i.e., which rival theories of corruption would license the very same rules — and tell me what a gift policy would have to look like if it were built instead on the assumption that the gift/bribe line is partly culturally constituted rather than fixed by dollar amounts.
