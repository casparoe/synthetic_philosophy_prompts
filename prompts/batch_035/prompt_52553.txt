The exam question, from a final in a course on twentieth-century epistemology, was:

"G. E. Moore claimed to prove the existence of things outside the mind by raising his hands and asserting, of each, that it is a hand. In the notes published after his death as *On Certainty*, Wittgenstein engages with Moore's procedure at length. Explain Wittgenstein's diagnosis of what goes wrong. In your answer: say what hinge propositions are, why they are not established by evidence, what status Wittgenstein assigns them instead, and how this bears on the possibility of doubting everything at once. Be explicit about whether, for Wittgenstein, hinges are known, justified, or neither."

The student's answer, reproduced in full:

"Moore held up his hands and asserted of each that it was a hand, claiming thereby to prove that there is an external world. Wittgenstein's diagnosis in On Certainty is that the assertions are not false and not nonsense; the mistake lies elsewhere. Some propositions, Wittgenstein says, are hinge propositions: they are not ordinarily tested against experience but function as the fixed framework within which testing goes on. Hinges cannot be arrived at by evidence, because any evidence offered for them would already presuppose them. Wittgenstein therefore holds that Moore's claims are certain, and certainty here means that they are self-evident: the epistemic agent grasps them directly, without inference or support. In this respect On Certainty is a foundationalist work — hinges are the basic justified beliefs on which all other knowledge rests, and once they are in place the structure is secured against skepticism. Doubting everything at once is impossible because each doubt needs grounds, and those grounds must themselves stand fast, being more certain than the proposition doubted. But Wittgenstein also allows that hinges are not permanently fixed: the riverbed of thought can shift, and a proposition that once functioned as a hinge can come to be tested and treated as an ordinary empirical claim. So global doubt fails not because the skeptical arguments are bad, but because we could never bring all hinges into doubt at the same time."
