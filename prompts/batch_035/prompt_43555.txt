I'm a student in a graduate seminar on theories of truth, and two of my classmates have spent the whole term arguing past each other. One is a convinced dialetheist, in roughly the Priest tradition: the Liar shows that some contradictions are true, and we need a paraconsistent logic to say so coherently. The other is a committed classicist: classical logic is non-negotiable, explosion is a feature rather than a bug, and any willingness to assert a sentence and its negation just signals that you haven't thought hard enough about the revenge paradoxes. Our professor has assigned us to attempt an adversarial collaboration, and I've been tasked with drafting the joint statement. I'd like you to write the document both of them could actually put their names to.

The statement should have three parts.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims. These should include, at minimum: that the T-schema instances for the Liar are individually compelling and can't all be abandoned without cost; that naive truth theory is inconsistent by classical lights; that explosion is a genuine theorem of classical logic rather than a prejudice someone made up; and that classical logic is the right instrument for plenty of consistent subject matters, ordinary mathematics included. Add whatever else both parties could honestly accept about the surface data — what we pretheoretically say and do with sentences like the Liar, and about what would count as a solution rather than a restatement of the problem.

Second, a disagreements table. Each row should state one disagreement twice: once in wording the dialetheist would accept as a fair statement of her own view, and once in wording the classicist would accept as a fair statement of his. Neither side should be able to complain that the other side's formulation smuggles in a victory — so, for example, the classicist's wording may not define a contradiction as something that cannot be true, and the dialetheist's wording may not describe classical logic as having been refuted rather than as non-universal.

Third, the cruxes: the subquestions such that settling one would move at least one party, each paired with a specific argument, case, or phenomenon that both agree bears on it, and with each side's prediction about that item recorded in advance. I have my own suspicions about what some of the cruxes are, but I want you to evaluate them and add any I've missed. My candidates: whether the revenge paradoxes are a principled objection to dialetheism or just more of the same handled by the same machinery; whether the failure of modus ponens for the material conditional in logics like LP is a serious cost, or an artifact of conflating that conditional with one designed for detachment; whether the putative ordinary contradictions — inconsistent laws, conflicting databases, borderline-vagueness cases — are really contradictions in the dialeist's sense or something weaker that both theories can classify differently; and whether the Liar and the material-conditional paradoxes like Curry's show that dialetheism localizes the problem or merely relocates it.

Finally, I want an explicit verdict on whether any part of this dispute is merely verbal. For instance: both of them will say the Liar is true in some sense; is "true" doing the same work in their mouths, or is a deflationist going to tell them they're fighting about a label? If part of the disagreement dissolves under careful formulation, say which part and why; if something survives as substantive, say what it is, so that the two of them know what to spend next semester arguing about.
