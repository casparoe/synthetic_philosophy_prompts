I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy — my day job involves compilers, build systems, and test infrastructure — so please keep the terminology as accessible as the subject allows, and please write your answer in American English.

Here is my situation. In my world, bootstrapping is routine and considered perfectly legitimate. A compiler for a language is typically written in that language and compiled by an earlier build of itself; we test our test frameworks with the same test framework; our CI pipelines run on the infrastructure they validate. There's a well-known cautionary line of thought in systems circles about a malicious self-hosting compiler that silently injects a backdoor into every compiler it builds, including future versions of itself, so the source code can look clean forever — and the standard defenses are things like reproducible builds, compiling the same source with several unrelated toolchains, and watching independent chains converge on the same binary. The notable thing is that nobody concludes that the trustworthiness of compilers is unknowable; we just accept that some self-referential trust is load-bearing and add independent cross-checks where we can.

I've recently learned that epistemologists have a problem they call bootstrapping, or "easy knowledge," and it looks to me like the same structure with the opposite verdict. As I understand it, a famous proof attributed to G. E. Moore claimed to establish the existence of the external world by holding up a hand and arguing that a hand is an object existing independently of one's mind; and the bootstrapping reasoning that gets attacked goes roughly as follows: on the basis of vision, I believe the table is red; if the table is red, my visual experience of its being red is accurate; so, on the basis of vision, my visual experience is accurate. Critics apparently regard this as letting one know, far too easily, that one's senses are reliable and that there is an external world, because the reasoning presupposes the very conclusion it draws.
