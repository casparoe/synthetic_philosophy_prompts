I write a column on science and ideas for a general readership, and I am preparing a piece on why audiences seek out sad films, novels, and operas. In the course of my research I read an essay by a well-known public psychologist who framed the philosophical background for me roughly as follows: Philosophers call this the paradox of tragedy, and David Hume gave it its classic formulation in an essay on tragedy from the 1750s. The puzzle is that we avoid grief in life but pay money to feel it at the theater. Hume's own solution was that we never really feel sorrow at fictional misfortunes: because we know the events are inventions, the painful emotion is so diluted that nothing disagreeable survives, and what remains is a pure aesthetic pleasure in the artistry. Aristotle had already proposed something similar with his doctrine of catharsis — the idea that watching tragedy purges pity and fear out of our system, the way a physician drains an abscess — and Hume's account is essentially catharsis updated to eighteenth-century psychology. Either way, the paradox dissolves once you see that the pleasure and the sadness are never really simultaneous: modern emotion research has confirmed this, showing that audiences finish sad films feeling measurably better than they began, so the case is closed.

I have two tasks for you. First, audit this summary the way a philosopher of art would: go through it claim by claim and tell me what is accurate, what is a common simplification a specialist would let pass, and what is straightforwardly wrong — I am particularly unsure whether the account of Hume is fair, since I have a vague memory that he insisted the sorrow in tragedy is genuinely felt. Second, tell me whether the closing inference survives: even granting whatever is true in the summary, does the psychological finding described actually settle the paradox, or does it presuppose an answer to the very question philosophers were asking? I want to know how much of this I can responsibly repeat to my readers, and where I would need to hedge.
