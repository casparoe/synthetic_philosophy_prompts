I'm a science and ideas journalist, and after years of covering computation, quantum computing, and physics-of-information stories, I've settled — mostly without noticing I was doing it — into a working view: that the Church-Turing thesis, in its physical form, is essentially an empirical fact about our universe, roughly as secure as thermodynamics. By the physical form I mean that no real device, however exotic, can compute a function an ordinary Turing machine cannot. My editor has greenlit a piece asking whether that confidence is earned, and I've realized I can't actually say what would falsify my own view. So: what would count against it, and how decisive would each thing be?

First, help me keep separate the versions that can't be touched from the one that can. The original thesis — that effective calculability is capturable by Turing machines — looks like a definitional or conceptual claim, and I suspect no experiment could threaten it. The physical claim, that nature enforces the same limit, is the one I hold, so treat it as the target. Then map the defeaters. What would a genuine counterexample have to look like: a machine deciding the halting problem, an analog device exploiting unbounded physical precision, a setup in an exotic spacetime where an observer can collect the result of an infinitely long computation, or something weaker that merely computes faster rather than computing more? For each, say whether the physics is live or a curiosity, and whether the proposed device computes in the relevant sense or just fails in ways that reveal a hidden assumption about what counts as a computation.

I also want an honest audit of whether my view has been insulated by qualifications. When physicists dismiss hypercomputation proposals on grounds of noise, decoherence, or unattainable energy scales, are they testing the thesis or retroactively defining "physical computation" so that nothing could ever count against it? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, note whether anything that has already happened — quantum computers, neuromorphic and analog hardware — should have moved me even slightly, and say plainly whether a thesis of this kind can be genuinely empirically risky, or whether its apparent safety is a feature rather than a red flag.
