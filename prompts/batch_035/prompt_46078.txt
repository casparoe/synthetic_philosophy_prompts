I've been going back and forth with a friend about whether suffering is the only thing that morally counts, and instead of reading yet more papers I want to just get clear on what my own gut actually commits me to. So here are my verdicts on a bunch of cases, and I want to know what principle, if any, captures them.

1. A completely empty world vs. a world containing one person with decades of bliss and a single pinprick somewhere in the middle. I say the pinprick world is better. If a view says otherwise, that's a problem for the view, not for me.
2. I can either cure one person's decade of grinding chronic pain, or instead bring about a million new lives that are genuinely wonderful but come with the ordinary aches and stubbed toes, which in aggregate add up to more raw discomfort than that one chronic pain. Gut says bring about the million lives.
3. Someone offers to painlessly, instantly and painlessly end all life at once, so that no one ever suffers again and nobody even notices it coming. Obviously monstrous. I don't care how painless it is.
4. A hundred people each get one day of a nasty migraine, versus one unlucky person getting a hundred days of it. Everything in me says spread it out, even though the total is the same either way.
5. A coin flip: heads a year of the worst suffering imaginable, tails ten years of the deepest joy you can picture. I wouldn't take it, and I don't think I'm being irrational.
6. Also, a life that's mostly ecstatic but contains real stretches of grief and pain, versus a life of flat mild contentment with almost no suffering. I pick the ecstatic one, for myself and for other people.

So is there a principle that saves all of these? My own tentative attempt was something like "add it all up but give suffering a big multiplier" — but the multiplier either comes out small enough that verdict 4 dies (the total's the same, so no multiplier helps), or big enough that verdict 2 dies. And verdict 5 looks like it's about risk rather than about any multiplier. Fix my principle, replace it, or tell me honestly which verdicts have to go.

Give me exactly five candidate principles, numbered 1-5, each one or two sentences, no introduction or conclusion. For each one, note which of my verdicts it kills, if any. If one of the five is the repaired version of my multiplier idea, say so. And if the best candidate implies something surprising about a case I haven't mentioned, like whether it's worth preventing one stubbed toe versus creating a very mildly happy life, flag that within the same sentence or two.
