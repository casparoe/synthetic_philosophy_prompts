I am a third-year Philosophy student and the student representative on my university's Estates and Environment Committee. At next month's meeting the committee must decide on a proposal from the grounds team to convert roughly eight hectares of regularly mown amenity grassland — lawns, verges, and the margins of two sports pitches — into wildflower meadow and seasonal wetland scrapes, including three new wildlife ponds, as part of the university's biodiversity action plan. Most members expect an easy yes on ecological grounds.

Our student Animal Welfare Society, however, has submitted an objection that has genuinely divided the committee. Their argument runs as follows: the overwhelming majority of individual animals in such habitats are small, short-lived creatures — insects and their larvae, aquatic invertebrates in the new ponds, young amphibians, small mammals — and the typical life among these ends early through predation, starvation, parasitism, desiccation, or freezing. If welfare is aggregated over individuals without weighting by body size or lifespan, then habitat creation may be net bad: we would be manufacturing enormous numbers of lives that are plausibly miserable on average, whereas close-mown grassland, though ecologically impoverished, supports far fewer sufferers. They add the mirror-image point that the same reasoning seems to imply that some habitat destruction would count as a good thing, which they intend as a reductio; other members take it to follow quite straightforwardly. A second faction argues the reverse — that mature, biodiverse habitat supports longer-lived animals that fare better, and that the correct comparison is not 'many small lives versus few' but the net welfare of the whole ecosystem under each management regime, which nobody can currently estimate with any confidence.

The grounds team needs a decision before the end of term, since the meadow must be seeded by early autumn and the mowing contract is up for renewal or termination.

Please act as a professional philosopher specialising in animal ethics and environmental ethics, with experience drafting advisory documents for non-specialist committees. Think carefully before you write, and begin with some brainstorming: set out the live positions (including any I have not stated), the value assumptions each smuggles in — particularly whether insects and other invertebrates have welfare states at all, how one might aggregate welfare across vastly different numbers, sizes, and lifespans of animals, and whether the value of an ecosystem or of biodiversity counts independently of the experiences of the animals in it — and what a committee like this can sensibly do under that kind of uncertainty. Then draft the decision memo I will circulate before the meeting.

The memo should recommend a concrete course of action — approve, approve with conditions, reject, or approve a modified proposal — together with whatever time frame, conditions, or monitoring commitments the recommendation needs to be actionable. It must engage the Animal Welfare Society's objection on its merits rather than dismissing it, give an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on whichever side loses, and be candid about the extent to which the recommendation ultimately rests on a value judgement that no amount of ecological data could settle. Please keep the memo under 1,200 words, and write it so that a facilities manager with no philosophical training can follow every step of the reasoning.
