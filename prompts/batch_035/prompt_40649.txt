By conviction I'm an eliminative materialist about propositional attitudes, and I mean the strong version: when cognitive science matures, the vocabulary that replaces belief/desire talk will not re-describe the same states under new names — nothing in the finished science will occupy the belief role, not even as a rough approximation. What would count against this? I am not much interested in the self-refutation objection, and I know the standard replies to it; I want the things that should actually move me. Three candidates worry me, though assess any others you think matter: (i) representation-theorem results in decision-theoretic foundations, which seem to show that belief/desire attribution works more like a coordinate system imposed on any sufficiently regular behavior than like a theory positing inner states — in which case there may be nothing for the mature science to eliminate; (ii) the possibility that the mature science itself turns out propositional, as suggested by predictive-processing accounts in which expectation stretches from whole organisms down to synaptic plasticity, so that eliminativism would have refuted folk psychology only by vindicating it at a different grain; and (iii) the psychopathologies of belief — delusion, confabulation, akrasia — which look native to doxastic vocabulary and merely gerrymandered in any replacement scheme. For each defeater, say whether it would be decisive or merely awkward, and whether a committed eliminativist would concede it as counting against the view or instead explain it away. Then give me an honest verdict on whether my strong version — which refuses to let the coordinate-system reading rescue the view — has been qualified so far that no possible discovery could count against it, and whether that is a defect in an ostensibly empirical thesis.
