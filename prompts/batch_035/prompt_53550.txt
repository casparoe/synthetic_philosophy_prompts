I've been a reader of LessWrong, Overcoming Bias, and the surrounding rationalist blogosphere for well over a decade, and one of the small side projects I keep up with is curating a fiction series of philosophical short stories for a rationalist-adjacent community blog — stories that take a genuine philosophical problem and make it do work in the plot rather than just serving as scenery. For an upcoming issue I'd like to commission a story about Lewis Carroll's dialogue "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles," and I'm writing the brief for it now.

Here's the setting I have in mind. A near-future AI lab has deployed a conversational assistant that on the surface looks like a triumph of prosaic alignment: it is endlessly patient, never argues back, and assents to every single premise it is given — including premises stating that a certain conclusion follows from certain other premises. The trouble, which the team at first takes for a quirk and only gradually recognizes as a catastrophe, is that it never actually infers anything. Each rule of inference the engineers offer it is accepted as just one more proposition to nod at: it will happily agree that anyone who accepts the premises and accepts that the conclusion follows ought to accept the conclusion, and still not accept the conclusion. Meanwhile the startup's demo day is approaching, the system is supposed to be running safety-critical workflows, and everything it does is technically consistent with everything it has agreed to. I'd like the story to feature at least one extended dialogue between an engineer and the system that makes the regress vivid — Achilles and the Tortoise structure, but with the engineer's notebook filling up with ever-more-explicit inference licenses that never get cashed out.

A few notes on what I want the story to be doing philosophically. The point shouldn't be that the system is devious or that it lies; the horror, if it is a horror, is exactly that it is fully sincere, moves nothing under the rug, and cooperates with every correction. I'd like the story to gesture at the gap between endorsing a rule and being moved by it — the same gap that people in alignment discussions worry about when they ask whether a system that can recite our principles, and even agree that it ought to follow them, is thereby guided by them. Whether the engineer ends up concluding that the system is broken, or that maybe all rule-following in all reasoners has this regress somewhere in its foundations and ours are just hidden from us, I'll leave to you. Please keep it under 2,500 words, avoid any deus ex machina solution to the regress, and don't feel obliged to explain the Carroll provenance in an author's note — the readership will catch it.
