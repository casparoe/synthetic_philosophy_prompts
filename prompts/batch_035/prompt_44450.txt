I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm putting together an interactive feature for our website to accompany a longer essay on moral intuitions. The conceit: most readers arrive with a self-assigned label — utilitarian, deontologist, whatever — but balk at the very cases their label is supposed to bless. Rather than lecturing them about the doctrine of double effect, I want a diagnostic quiz titled something like "Which trolley are you?": the reader answers a series of hypotheticals, and only at the end discovers which philosophical position on redirecting threats, killing, and letting die their verdicts actually cluster into.

Please write me eight to ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options expressed purely as verdicts (permissible, impermissible, obligatory, or a plain "I'd do it but couldn't say it's allowed"). The cases should go well beyond the two classics: include the original driver-who-can-divert setup, the footbridge push, but also the loop variant where the trolley would circle back onto the five unless the one's body stops it, a case where diverting the trolley onto the one person standing on the side track is a matter of using their body to physically stop the trolley rather than merely rerouting the threat past them, a costless-avoidance variant (a switch that slows the trolley so everyone survives), the transplant surgeon harvesting one for five, and at least one case involving killing one to let five die where the distinction between doing and allowing is strained — say, flipping a switch that simultaneously removes a protective barrier from one and extends it to the other five. Every question must be phrased so that someone could answer it without knowing which position it is diagnosing: never mention intention, means versus side effect, rights, or any philosopher's name in the questions themselves.

Then supply the scoring key. It should map answer patterns onto the live positions: the straight consequentialist; the defender of the doctrine of double effect as the discriminating criterion; the later Thomson-style rights view on which what matters is whether the one is being used rather than whether the harm is intended; Kamm's more fine-grained constraint structure, on which harm as a means, harm as a side effect of aid, and harm permitted by the distribution of an existing threat come apart; the contractualist treatment; and the debunking position that the intuitions themselves are noise our evolutionary and emotional wiring explains rather than justifies. Be explicit about which pairs of positions the instrument cannot genuinely separate given your questions, and add a question or two specifically designed to pull apart the double-effect view from the means-based view, if that's possible — the loop case and its relatives are the natural place to look. Finally, an epistemic note that will go into my editor's brief: please mark in the key what is settled background (which cases are standard in the literature and which variants are my own constructions) versus what is genuinely contested among specialists — including the standing disagreement over whether the double-effect and the means-based readings are even distinct doctrines, and over whether the footbridge intuition tracks anything principled — and make sure the quiz never presents one scoring outcome as the correct answer to the cases themselves.
