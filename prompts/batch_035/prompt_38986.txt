I am a retired condensed-matter physicist who has spent the past few years reading systematically in analytic metaphysics, and there is a distinction I keep tripping over. In physics we had a serviceable notion of one theory reducing to another — usually cashed out via derivations, limit procedures, and bridge principles connecting the vocabularies. In philosophy, I gather, there is a separate relation, grounding, said to hold not between theories but between facts or entities, and to be distinct from reduction, causation, and mere entailment. Please explain, in terms a natural scientist can evaluate, what work grounding is supposed to do that reduction, as physicists understand it, does not. In particular:

1. Reduction appears to be a relation between theories — their laws, their vocabularies, the mappings between them — whereas grounding is claimed to relate facts or entities themselves. Why is that shift from the representational to the ontological supposed to matter, and what goes wrong if I simply treat talk of grounding as shorthand for talk of reduction plus a claim about which theory is more fundamental?

2. Reductions in physics can be direction-neutral: Newtonian and Hamiltonian mechanics are intertranslatable, and we would not comfortably say that either grounds the other, even though each is plausibly more fundamental than, say, an effective hydrodynamic description. Grounding, by contrast, is advertised as asymmetric and irreflexive. What is supposed to break the tie in cases where the formal relationship runs equally well both ways, and is the asymmetry a discovered feature of the world or a stipulation built into the relation?

3. I understand that grounding is characterized as hyperintensional: two claims that are necessarily equivalent can nevertheless differ in what grounds them. Necessary equivalence is not a notion I handled in the laboratory, so give me a workaday example that shows why entailment is too coarse a relation for the job, and explain why this coarse-grainedness is supposed to be a defect rather than a virtue — after all, a relation defined over coarser equivalence classes is easier to establish.

4. Finally, situate my own field. When condensed-matter physicists say that macroscopic behavior emerges from, and is in principle derivable from, the microscopic theory, is that a grounding claim in the philosophers' sense, a reduction in the physicists' sense, both, or neither? If you think the distinction collapses or becomes unstable for cases like the relationship between thermodynamics and statistical mechanics, say so plainly and explain where the friction lies. I am not asking for a tour of the literature; I am asking for an account pitched at someone who knows what a bridge law is, has no attachment to any school, and has little patience for obscurity dressed as depth.
