I've been reading about Arrow's impossibility theorem and I want to test it against a real case rather than the toy examples in textbooks, because I genuinely can't tell whether the theorem actually *explains* anything that happened or whether it's just a nice way of redescribing it afterwards. The case I have in mind is the 2009 Burlington, Vermont mayoral election, and I'd like a proper two-way analysis: theory illuminating case, case testing theory.

Let me lay out the facts as I understand them so we're on the same page (correct me if I get something wrong): Burlington had adopted instant-runoff voting in 2005. In 2009 the incumbent Bob Kiss, a Progressive, ran against Democrat Andy Montroll, Republican Kurt Wright, and a few minor candidates. Wright got the most first-choice votes, but nobody had a majority, so the lower candidates were eliminated and their votes transferred; eventually Montroll was eliminated and his transfers put Kiss over the top, so Kiss won. The kicker is that in the ballot data Montroll was the Condorcet winner — head-to-head, he beat both Kiss and Wright. And a lot of voters apparently concluded the system had produced a "wrong winner," because the next year Burlington repealed IRV by referendum (by a few points, if I remember right) and went back to plurality.

So here's what I want. I take it the natural read is an independence-of-irrelevant-alternatives violation — the presence of Wright (or the eliminated minor candidates) in the race changed whether Kiss or Montroll won — but I want you to be precise about which comparisons break IIA and whether Arrow's setup even applies directly, since IRV selects a single winner rather than producing a full social ranking. If you think the honest mapping goes through a cousin theorem instead of Arrow itself, say so and explain the difference.

Second, tell me what the theorem genuinely explains about the public reaction. Burlington voters repealed the system after seeing the Condorcet winner eliminated — does Arrow (or the surrounding social choice literature) actually predict or account for that reaction, or does it turn out that the impossibility result was mostly irrelevant to why people were mad? Was the outrage really about a violated axiom, or about something the axioms don't capture?

Arrow's theorem is silent about a lot of things that mattered here: procedures, runoffs, campaigning, the fact that the electorate then got to *vote on the voting rule itself*. Does the theorem say anything that helps explain that choice, or does the episode show that the popular lesson people draw from Arrow ("every voting system is broken, so nothing matters") is exactly wrong — that the axioms were doing real work in shaping which failure voters could see and which they couldn't?
