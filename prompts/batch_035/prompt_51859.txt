My dept colleague keeps running this one on me and I can't find the exit. It's about judicial review:

(1) A political practice is democratically legitimate as long as the people living under it could get rid of it, through the constituion's own amendment procedure, whenever they decide it's doing more harm than good.
(2) Judicial review is set up by the constitution, and like everything else in there, it's formally amendable.
(3) So if the people ever concluded that judges striking down laws does more harm than good, they could end it by amendment.
(4) So judicial review is democratically legitimate. Not just tolerable — legitimate.

Then he leans on it harder and this is where I lose the thread: the same test would bless anything that's formally amendable. So a constitution that gave lifetime-tenured ex-generals a veto over every defense bill would be democratically legitimate too, just because a future supermajority could repeal it once it got bad enough. That seems to prove way too much. And it gets worse — if the country passes one extra clause making judicial review unamendable, his test says it flips from legitimate to illegitimate overnight. Same practice, one line of text. So legitimacy would also vary with amendment thresholds: the identical practice would be legitimate in a country with easy amendments and illegitimate in one with near-impossible ones, which feels arbitrary. Something slips between (1) and (4) but I can't name it. And I can't shake the feeling his test would let a dictator say if the people really wanted me gone they could remove me, which is the same move with violence swapped in for procedure.

Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd honestly like to keep (3), it just seems true.
