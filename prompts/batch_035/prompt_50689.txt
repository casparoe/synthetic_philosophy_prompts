My roommate (stats PhD, insufferable) has gotten me interested in the Condorcet jury theorem — if each voter independently answers a yes/no question correctly with probablity above one half, then as you add voters the chance the majority is correct approaches 1. Fine, textbook stuff, and I'm taking a social choice course this semester so I even needed to know it. But last night she used it to walk me into an argument I can't get out of, and the conclusion is nuts. Here's the chain as she laid it out: (1) the theorem says independent voters each with competence p > 0.5 make the majority verdict correct with probability approaching 1 as the electorate grows; (2) competence is just a probability of getting it right, and probabilities don't care about labels — a test that's right 40% of the time is right 60% of the time if you read its outputs backwards; (3) so for any voter whatever, there is a way of counting their ballot — as cast, or flipped — on which they come out competent, and flipping doesn't break independence either; (4) whether we count ballots as cast or flipped looks like pure bookkeeping, a convention we adopt, not a further empirical discovery about the world; (5) so take a million voters who are each right only 40% of the time, count their ballots flipped, apply the theorem, and their flipped-majority verdicts are correct with probability as close to 1 as you like; (6) but a million people who are wrong 60% of the time can't be turned into a nearly infallible oracle by a bookkeeping convention — truth doesn't come free with headcount, even inverted headcount; (7) so something in the setup is broken, and she says the obvious suspect is the theorem itself, since the competence condition apparently has the answer smuggled into it from the start.

She even doubled down and said the flipped version shows the truth is still "in the room" with a bunch of bad but independent voters, since their minority is nearly always right, which made my head hurt more, not less. I have a midterm in a week so I'd obviously love to keep (1) if that's at all defensible, but I honestly can't see where else to jump — every step still looks okay to me when I read them one at a time. Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked fine to begin with, and what giving it up actually costs, including what the theorem is then really telling us about a room full of people who are mostly wrong.
