My ethics reading group has been going round and round on internal vs external reasons basically forever, and I've hit the point where I can predict everyones moves. The internalists say a reason has to connect up to something in the agent's motivational set, otherwise it's just not a reason *for them* — at best it's advice the agent has no way to care about. The externalists keep insisting that the guy who genuinely doesn't care about his family any more still has a reason to call his mother, and that this isn't a prediction about what will move him, it's a claim about what counts in favor of acting. All the arguments on both sides have been on the table for ages and nobody has budged in two years.

I've got a hypothesis I want pressure-tested, not a survey of the literature. I've been rereading Geach on 'good' — his point that 'good' is attributive, so a good thief needn't be a good man, and that arguments which trade on sliding between 'good' and 'good F' fail. What if 'a reason' works the same way? Maybe the externalist is reading 'X has a reason' predicatively — there's a reason there, full stop — while the internalist is reading it attributively: there's a reason-for-X, relative to X's motivational set in something like the way a good thief is relative to thief-ing. On that story the whole forty-year fight is two people pointing at different grammars, each correctly saying that the other's sentence, read their way, is false.

But I'm not sure this dissolves as much as it looks like it does. There's a residue: even if the readings come apart, the externalist wants to say the predicative reading is the one that matters for justifying criticism of the agent (you're being irrational, not just unlucky in your desires), and the internalist wants the attributive reading to be all there is. That second fight doesn't look merely verbal — it looks like a genuine disagreement about whether deliberation can be criticized from nowhere, or only from within some concern or other.

So, three questions. Is my Geach-style diagnosis right, partly right, or wrong — and what in what the sides actually say supports it or cuts against it? Once you strip off the verbal part, what's left: a substantive dispute about whether practical justification is standpoint-independent, or just each side caring about its own question, one about what can move a person, one about what ought to? And given your diagnosis, is there anything — a case, a distinction, a way of carving up 'reason' — that would actually count as progress here, or should my group accept this one is bedrock and stop scheduling it?
