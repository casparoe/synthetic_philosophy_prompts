Retired chemist here — 35 years in materials science, only got serious about philosophy after retiring. I'm working through modal metaphysics at the moment, partly because I like Lewis's physics-flavored way of doing things. My book club friend, who did a philosophy degree decades ago, tried to sort out the possible worlds business for me like this:

"Lewis's view is that each thing exists in only one possible world, and in the other worlds there are things resembling it. When you say you could have gone into academia instead, what makes that true on his theory is just that some other world contains a person who resembles you and did go into academia. So strictly speaking you were never really capable of it yourself — your 'possibilities' are really facts about the lives of other, similar people. That's also why, on Lewis's picture, counterfactual talk about you ends up being about your counterparts rather than about you: there's no you in those other situations for it to be about. And Kripke's older picture, where the very same you exists in many worlds, isn't really a rival to Lewis's — it's the same story in different notation, so the two camps can't actually disagree about anything."

Something about this feels off. I'd have though that a theory whose whole purpose is to analyze "could have been" wouldn't turn around and deny that I could have been otherwise — as stated, my friend's version seems to collapse modal facts into a kind of fatalism, and I can't believe that's what Lewis was after. Though I'll admit my physicist instincts balk at Kripke's side too: the same numerically identical me literally inhabiting many different worlds sounds to me like saying one electron is in many different experimental runs at once. So I'm inclined toward Lewis, but I don't trust my instincts yet, and I definitely don't trust my friend's summary. Could you go through it bit by bit and tell me which parts a Lewisian would accept as stated, which are standard simplifications, which are quietly wrong, and whether his closing remark — that the two views are just notational variants that can't disagree about anything — actually holds up, or whether that's one of the quietly wrong bits?
