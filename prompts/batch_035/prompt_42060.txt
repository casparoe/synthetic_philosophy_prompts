I'm putting together teaching material on whether knowing-how can be Gettiered. The thought is: if the intellectualists are right that knowing how to do something is just knowing a proposition — roughly, knowing of some way w that it is a way of doing the thing — then there should be Gettier cases for skills, not just for knowledge-that. So construct one: a subject who genuinely succeeds at a skilled task, who you'd have counted as knowing how to do it beforehand by ordinary standards, but whose beliefs about how to do it are only luckily true in the classic Gettier way. Constraints: one paragraph, realistic setting (sailing, bread baking, plumbing — no demons or fake-barn-style outlandishness), and the luck can't come form an incompetent instructor lying to them, since that's just a standard testimonial Gettier with a skill garnish. The luck has to come from something about the practice itself: misleading feedback, equipment quirks, conditions that mask errors. After the scenario, walk through what each side says: does the intellectualist bite the bullet and deny the subject know-how, or find some extra condition to patch it? And how would a Rylean use the case the other way — as evidence that know-how was never propositional to begin with, so it can't be Gettiered in the first place? Finish by telling me which feature of the case is doing the work, and whether an anti-intellectualist who's willing to admit Gettier-style luck for abilities (like the environmental luck literature on skills) still has anywhere to hide. The point of the case is to test the reduction, so a scenario that just stipulates "she doesn't really know how" instead of letting the luck emerge from the setup counts as cheating.
