Below is an argument, due to a friend, that taxation is theft; the trouble is that it seems to prove a great deal more than that, and I want to know where it breaks.

(1) Theft is taking what belongs to another without that other's consent. (2) Consent is genuine only where refusal is a real option: what is handed over under a credible threat of penalty is extracted, not consented to — the mugger's victim who surrenders the wallet has not made a gift. (3) One cannot refuse to pay taxes without penalty; refusal ends in fines, seizure, imprisonment. (4) So taxes are not paid by genuine consent. (5) So taxation takes what belongs to another without consent: taxation is theft.

My friend is happy with the conclusion. But nothing in the argument restricts step (3) to taxes. One equally cannot refuse to return borrowed property, honor a signed contract, or pay court-ordered restitution to one's victim without penalty. Run (2) through (5) on these cases and the argument proves that returning what you borrowed is theft, that paying restitution is theft — indeed that nobody genuinely consents to the property regime at all, since it too is enforced by penalties, in which case nothing belongs to anyone and nothing can be stolen. The argument devours the very concept of theft it relies on. My friend says the extra conclusions are a feature, not a bug: he denies any duty to obey law as such and holds that all enforcement-backed obligations are of a piece. My own suspicion is that something slips between (2) and (3) — perhaps an equivocation between being unable to avoid a penalty and being unable to avoid a transfer, or between refusing a demand and refusing a duty — but I cannot make it precise, and I would rather keep (2), since the mugger case seems to me decisive.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost — in particular, does anything survive of the thought that extraction under threat is not consent? Reply as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: (i) the step you reject; (ii) why that step looked unimpeachable; (iii) the precise flaw in it, naming any equivocation or scope shift; (iv) the cheapest alternative rejection and its price; (v) what, if anything, remains of my friend's conclusion once the argument is repaired.
