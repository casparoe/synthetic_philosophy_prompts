For years now, my brother-in-law and I have had the same argument every Thanksgiving. He read a book about how societies choose to fail, and he holds that civilizations are like businesses that go bankrupt: they make bad long-term decisions, ignore warnings, and get the collapse they earned. I've come to suspect he's wrong, but not for the reasons I first thought. I've noticed that when we argue about the Maya, we agree on nearly all the facts — the big southern cities stopped raising stelae and building monuments by around 900, the population of the lowlands dropped, and yet millions of Maya are alive today, speaking Maya languages, in the same region. He calls that a collapse; I call it the political class dissolving while the people carried on. Neither of us can prove the other wrong, and I've realized that's because we aren't actually disagreeing about facts. We're answering different questions and calling both answers "collapse."

I have no academic training — I run a heating and cooling business and read about this stuff at night — so I want to lay out the raw material plainly and see if you can build something out of it. The cases I keep circling are these. The Bronze Age: the Mycenaean palaces burned, the bureaucracy with its clay tablets vanished, literacy was lost for centuries, but the farmers kept farming, and within a few centuries alphabetic writing, which the palace scribes never had, was spreading around the Mediterranean — so was that a failure or a messy transition to something the old system couldn't have produced? Easter Island: I grew up on the story of the islanders who cut down every tree and destroyed themselves, but I've since read that much of the damage is now blamed on Polynesian rats eating the palm seeds, and that the real population crash came much later, from slave raids and disease; if that's right, the story stops being a morality tale about a society condemning itself — or does it? And the Maya again: if a society's institutions die but its people, languages, and gods persist, in what sense has a society failed at all? My working theory is that two questions are hiding underneath all of this. First: can a society genuinely fail, or does it only ever reorganize, with "failure" being a word that the literate minority (the only people who could write) used for the end of their own paperwork? Second: when failure is real, is it something a society does to itself, or something done to it — droughts, migrations, strangers with guns? My hunch is that the real positions in this debate are just the different ways of answering those two questions, plus possibly a fifth held by people who think the whole concept of civilizational failure is confused and should be dropped.

So here is what I'd like from you. Build me a field guide to the positions: exactly five numbered items, no introduction, no conclusion, and nothing after the fifth. Each item should be one or two sentences and should do two things: give the position a name (or say honestly that it has none), and give its tell — what a holder of that position has to say about at least one of my three cases (the Maya monuments, the palace burnings, the Easter Island palms) that a holder of the neighboring positions would not say. Derive the five positions from the two questions I described rather than from any list of authors, and if it turns out that two of the five can't actually be separated in practice — if no answer to any real case distinguishes them — then say so inside the item rather than hiding it. I intend to print this out and read it across the table next Thanksgiving, so every word has to earn its place.
