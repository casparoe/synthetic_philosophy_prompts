so I write novels — literary fiction, morally gray people doing slightly-too-much for strangers, the usual — and I'm stuck on a question that turns out to be a whole thing in ethics. My current manuscript has a woman who donates a kidney to a stranger she met once, and my editor keeps marking up those scenes with things like "is she a saint here or just a decent person who did the math?" and I realized I honestly don't know whether that distinction carves anything real.

so here's what I want a number on. Give me your credence that supererogation is a genuine, coherent category: that there can be acts that are (a) permissible, (b) better than what duty requires, and (c) not themselves required. The part that keeps me up is the transmission problem — if the kidney donation is better than not donating, and not donating is obviously fine, why doesn't the "better" push the obligation upward until everything above the minimum becomes required too? Something has to break that transmission, and everyone seems to disagree about what the breaker is.

Decompose it for me. I'd want at least: the probability that consequentialism of the maximizing sort is true, since if maximizing is the whole story the category looks like it evaporates — though I gather the satisficing and scalar consequentialists have escape routes, so maybe tell me how much those routes actually rescue supererogation. The probability that the demandingness objection works somewhere, i.e. that ought-implies-can style limits on obligation are real and not just a comfort blanket for readers who want to feel okay about not donating kidneys. The probability that the three conditions (permissible, better, not required) are jointly satisfiable in a non-consequentialist framework without smuggling the problem in elsewhere — stuff like threshold views where you're required to hit the threshold but praiseworthy for overshooting. And any other terms you think belong, with a number and one line of justification each, then the combination.

Then tell me which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, and what argument or kind of case would move it the most.

And if you're feeling generous, one line at the end on my actual problem: if your number is low, is my editor right that the character is just doing what she ought and the "saintly" framing is sentimentality, and if it's high, does that make heroism cheap in a way I should be writing against? sorry this ran long, I've been arguing with the editor for three weeks
