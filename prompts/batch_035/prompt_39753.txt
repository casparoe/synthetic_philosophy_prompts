I'm a patient safety officer at a mid-size regional hospital, and I'm the one who signs off on root cause analyses (RCAs) after serious adverse events — wrong-site surgery, medication errors, a patient who fell three times in one weekend, that sort of thing. We run maybe eight to ten of these a year, each with a small team, and the stakes are real: whatever we call a "root cause" is what gets a corrective action and budget attached to it. Over the years I've noticed that our teams basically cannot agree on what makes something a cause. The nurse gave the wrong dose, but also the pharmacy stocked two nearly identical vials next to each other in the same bin, and also the ordering physician was on hour 14 of a shift. Every RCA ends with a laundry list of "contributing factors" and then somebody's gut decides which one becomes the official finding.

I took a philosophy course ages ago and remember there's this whole fight between counterfactual theories of causation (Lewis: the but-for test, would the effect have happened if the cause hadn't) and interventionist ones (Woodward: a variable is a cause if wiggling it, while holding other things fixed in the right way, changes the outcome). My suspicion is that the interventionist picture is actually the one we implicitly need — because an RCA finding is really a claim about what intervention we should buy — but I also suspect the counterfactual test is what my teams are doing instinctively whenever they ask "would she have died if X hadn't happened?" And I don't know which parts of that theoretical fight actually matter at the bedside and which parts are artifacts of puzzle cases cooked up in seminar rooms.

You are a professional philosopher specializing in causation who has also, improbably, spent time embedded in hospital quality departments. Think carefully before answering — start by brainstorming which of the genuine differences between the counterfactual and interventionist pictures are load-bearing for something like an RCA, and be honest about where the theory is just too messy to operationalize and you're going to have to make an executive decision.

What I want is a decision procedure my RCA teams can actually follow, in plain language, that tells them:

1. How to decide whether a factor is a cause versus merely background (the classic struck-match-in-a-room-full-of-oxygen problem), with tests a non-philosopher can run in the moment;
2. How to handle the case where the outcome would have happened anyway — overdetermination and preemption — e.g. the overdose came from both a mislabeled vial AND an incorrectly programmed pump, and either one alone would have killed the patient;
3. How to handle absences — nobody did the required safety check, so did the not-checking cause the death?
4. And, crucially, how to keep straight the difference between what caused this event and what we should intervene on, since those come apart: some causes are unfixable, some fixes would create new risks, and some things we could fix wouldn't have prevented this event but might prevent future ones. Our lawyers and our board are not going to be happy if a procedure confuses causal responsibility with where to spend money.

Mark clearly the points where the procedure has to hand the decision back to human judgment because the philosophy genuinely doesn't settle it, and tell me where the procedure will misfire — the kinds of cases where following it faithfully produces an answer the team, or I, won't like. Then run the procedure on two cases: (a) a patient recieved ten times the ordered heparin dose because two look-alike vials were stored in the same bin and the nurse was covering two units during a staffing crunch; and (b) a patient died of sepsis caught late, where the late catch traces to an alarm that had been disabled — by policy, for that unit, by a committee two years earlier — plus a covering physician who never got the handoff page. Make sure at least one of these worked cases produces the unwelcome answer, for instance that the thing we can most easily and cheaply fix is not properly a cause of this event at all, so the procedure has to route it somewhere other than the "root cause" line of the report.

Constraints: the team has maybe two hours total for this part of the analysis, they have no philosophy background, and whatever we call a cause has to be defensible to a hospital board and occasionally to lawyers. Keep the procedure itself short enough to fit on two pages once the worked cases are pulled out. Please answer in American English.
