So: the situationist critique of virtue ethics. By my read the debate went hot in the late 90s/early 2000s, peaked maybe around Doris's book on lack of character and Harman's papers claiming there are no character traits, and then... froze. Nobody has moved in like fifteen years, people just restate their opening positions with new footnotes. The situationists lean on Milgram, Asch's line experiments, the Good Samaritan study where semarians in a hurry stepped over a guy slumped in a doorway, and further back on Hartshorne and May's honesty studies from the 1920s, where a kid's cheating on one test barely predicted his cheating on the next. Their line: cross-situational consistency is close to zero, traits explain little variance, so the robust stable traits virtue ethics is built on mostly don't exist, and a theory about cultivating them loses its explanatory and prescriptive footing.

The defenders have a standard menu of replies and they've had it forever: the experiments are one-shot lab slices where the trait barely gets a chance to show; nobody ever demanded perfect consistency, only something weakly above zero, which the data don't rule out; the situationists are measuring the wrong thing since an Aristotelian virtue manifests in how you deliberate over time, not in single behavioral frequencies (I think this is roughly Kamtekar's line, that character shows up in second-order reflection, though don't quote me); virtue was always supposed to be rare, a lifetime achievement, so ordinary people being flaky in a lab proves nothing; and Miller's move where you just give up on widespread virtues and vices and posit "mixed traits" instead. The situationists answer every one of these the same way: unfalsifiable, moving the goalposts, and if virtues are that rare then fine, but then virtue ethics is useless as a guide for actual humans, which was supposedly the point of it. Then the defenders say the critique was always aimed at a predictive theory virtue ethics never claimed to be. Then the situationists point out that the defenders keep publishing empirical replies, so they must secretly think it is predictive. And so on, in a circle, since roughly 2005.

My working hypothesis, take it or leave it: this isn't really an empirical dispute at all. The two sides never agreed on an operationalization of what a trait even is -- what counts as "the same situation," what counts as "the same behavior," what frequency of manifestation a trait requires -- so each side's evidence doesn't even register as evidence for the other. It's a verbal or methodological disagreement dressed up as a fight about data. But a friend of mine in a psych department is having none of it. She says it's substantive all the way down: the real fight is over what virtue ethics needs to be true to survive as a theory (must traits be common, or is a rare-ideal picture enough? must ethics explain behavior, or only guide it?), and it stalls because the ethics side can't say which of those it's committed to without giving something up -- if traits are rare, they concede practical uselessness; if they're common, they walk back into the data. On her story it's not that the question is ill-posed, it's that one side hasn't decided what its own theory is for.
