For my next novel I have been interviewing my brother, a philosopher of physics, so that my narrator — a retired theorist — can hold her views with something like conviction. Over coffee he made the case for his position, and I have transcribed it as five numbered steps, since that is how he paced it. (1) Our best physical theories say of an electron only how it relates: charge, mass, position in the equations; there is no further fact about what it intrinsently is. (2) Permuting the objects of a structure, while leaving the structure intact, leaves every truth of the theory intact — so two such permuted configurations are not two possibilities but one. (3) Hence there is no fact of the matter about the identities of the relata, nor even whether there are relata beyond their roles; objects are, as he puts it, nothing over and above nodes in a web of relations. (4) By parity of reasoning the same holds of the table and the chair, each known to us only through its relations. (5) The conclusion generalizes without limit — to tables, to persons, and to the relations themselves, each fixed only by its role among the rest — so that in the end there is nothing for anyone's novel to be about. I have a deadline, a suspicion that something slips between (2) and (3), and no name for it. I would like to keep (1), which gives my narrator her voice, and to reject (5), since my plot needs a son-in-law. Since I work in five-point outlines, tell me, in exactly five numbered items and nothing else — no preamble, no closing remark: the step you reject, why that step seemed safe when my brother asserted it, and what it costs to hold onto the steps that remain.
