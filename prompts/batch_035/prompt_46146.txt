I'm taking a course on transitional justice, and I keep running into a move that I don't have a name for. The setting is always roughly the same: an authoritarian regime falls, and the new democratic government wants to punish people who did awful things under it — informed on their neighbours to the secret police, ran the deportation paperwork, whatever — but at the time those acts were legal, sometimes even rewarded. Passing an openly retroactive statute looks bad and courts hate it, so instead I keep seeing this: the court (or a commentator) says the old regime's rules were so wicked that they were never really law at all, and therefore prosecuting people now isn't retroactive — there's no valid old law for the new prosecution to conflict with. It's a way of having your cake and eating it: you get the conviction while insisting the rules never changed, because the wicked rules never counted as rules. My professor mentioned in passing that this move split the Anglophone legal philosophers down the middle in the late fifties, and I've also half-remembered a line attributed to some mid-century German legal philosopher about intolerable injustice not counting as law, but noone ever gives the general move itself a label.

So, is there an established term for this specific step — the "declare the old rules void ab initio so you're not retroactive" bit? I want to know how it differs from the nearby things I do know the names of: ordinary judicial review striking a statute down (which normally operates going forward, not as if the statute never bound anyone), and plain ex post facto legislation (which at least admits what it's doing). And if there's no established term, coin one and define it, because I especially want to know where the edges are: does the move only work for genuinely genocidal regimes, or can it stretch to merely bad ones — and if the old regime's law was mostly ordinary with a few wicked provisions mixed in, does the whole legal order get declared void or just the wicked parts, and who decides which is which? That stretching question is basically what my term paper is supposed to be about, so a bare name won't help me — I need the carving.
