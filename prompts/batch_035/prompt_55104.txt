I write psychological fiction — my last two novels basically consist of people talking each other into and out of things — and I've become slightly obsessed with the line between persuasion and manipulation, mostly because my characters cross it constantly and I never quite know where it is myself. In November I'm running a weekend retreat for about a dozen fellow novelists, and I want one three-hour session that doesn't lecture at anyone about ethics but instead makes us feel where that line sits in our own bodies. My idea (and you can improve on it or replace bits) is something like: participants get influenced for real, in the room, by methods they don't know about in advance — some techniques that engage their judgement, some that sidestep it — and only afterwards do they try to sort which was which and discover they can't quite agree. I'm imagining the facilitator (or a scripted actor, or a confederate) running something like a book-club deliberation where the fix is in: maybe pressure to decide before people have thought, maybe manufactured warmth, maybe framing that makes one option feel like the default, maybe just a genuinely good argument delivered well. Then the big reveal and the debrief.

What I want from you is a full design: the rules and materials, the exact influence techniques planted in each round (mix subtle with a bit obvious, and include at least two that sit right on the contested boundary rather than being clear-cut), a payoff or stakes scheme that works without real money, and a debrief that maps each moment of the exercise onto a candidate criterion for the persuasion/manipulation line — the "bypassing your judgement" idea vs the "would survive reflection" idea vs the "who benefits" idea — and gets participants to test the criteria against what actually moved them. Please also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (my guess: someone spots the con early and doesn't say anything — how do I handle that?), and be honest about which feature of the real philosophical problem no classroom exercise can reproduce, so I know what I'll have to just assert in the debrief.

Please answer in British English, and structure it as follows, with these exact section headings in this order: "The con" — the activity itself, rules, roles and techniques, roughly 500–700 words; "The reveal" — how to run the unmasking and the debrief, 300–400 words; "Where it'll break" — what goes wrong on first run and the fixes, 150–200 words; "What I can't stage" — the honest note on the limits of the exercise, 100–150 words. No reading lists or citations — I just want the design and the reasoning. Ta!
