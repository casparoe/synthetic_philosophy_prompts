I'm giving a talk next month to my department's weekly ethics seminar where I defend a version of Confucian role ethics as a standalone moral framework — not a historical curiosity, and not just virtue ethics with the serial numbers filed off. My line, roughly: moral deliberation properly starts from the relation, not from the choosing individual. The question isn't "what would a good person do here" in the abstract, it's "what does being a parent come to in this situation," or "what does a teacher owe this student" — and you can go all the way down like that without needing some theory of human nature or a universal principle hiding behind the roles to ground them. The roles carry the normative weight themselves, or at least that's the claim I want to make.

The audience, so you can calibrate: mostly analytic ethicists who think in terms of Kant and Mill. One very serious Kantian who suspects anything relational is just imperfect duties wearing a costume. One virtue ethicist who's going to tell me I reinvented Aristotle. Two metaethicists who will want to know what makes a role obligation binding rather than merely customary. Plus a couple of grad students who've actually read Ames and Rosemont and will push me on the antitheoretical side of that program — which is honestly where Im least sure of myself, since if no theory sits behind the roles I owe them an account of what to do when the tradition runs out.

Prepare my Q&A sheet. Give me the objections in rough order of how much damage they'd do to the paper, each phrased the way this particular crowd would actually phrase it, with a two-minute reply I can deliver and a note on where the reply is soft. Cover at least: the conformism worry (what happens when the role itself is rotten — the filial son of a cruel father, or the conscientious official inside a corrupt bureaucracy), the normativity question from the metaethicists, and the "this is just virtue ethics in different vocabulary" move. Tell me what I should concede up front so I come off honest rather than cornered. And then tell me the question I'm quietly hopeing nobody asks.
