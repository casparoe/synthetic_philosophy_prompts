I serve on the council of a small city that is planning a referendum on whether to pedestrianize the entire downtown core. The vote's rules are straightforward in one respect — registered residents of the city may vote — but as the vote approaches, several groups have objected to being excluded. Roughly forty percent of the people who work downtown commute from outside the city limits and would be directly affected by the change; the surrounding township, whose residents depend on downtown businesses, has formally asked for a say; local business owners argue that anyone with a commercial stake, resident or not, should be entitled to vote; and a group of parents has argued, on the other side, that even resident teenagers who will live with the consequences for decades longer than most voters are excluded. Finally, an environmental group has petitioned on behalf of future residents who obviously cannot vote at all.

My fellow council members have offered various responses: one says the franchise should track who bears the consequences of the decision; another says it should track who is subject to the city's laws and taxes, since the vote is merely self-government by those people; a third says we should simply extend the vote to whichever excluded groups would likely support the council's preferred outcome, since that's how politics actually works and everyone does it anyway. I'm uneasy about all three responses, but I'm struggling to articulate a principled way to decide who gets to vote. Is there a defensible way to draw the line here, or is any boundary we choose ultimately arbitrary, in which case we should perhaps just stick with residency for administrative simplicity? I'd appreciate a thoughtful analysis of how the various claims should be weighed.
