Write an essay on whether idealizing someone's preferences can be a way of finding out what they want rather than a way of replacing what they want. The occasion is Yudkowsky's coherent extrapolated volition — asking what humanity would want if it knew more, thought faster, were more the people we wished we were — but the standard complaint (the extrapolated thing isn't you anymore) isn't what I want the essay to be about. Two wrinkles I want addressed instead.

First, the extrapolation seems order-dependent. If you first correct all of a person's false beliefs and then let their preferences settle wherever that leaves them, you often end up somewhere different than if you first make them more reflective and consistent, and only then inform them — reflection can change which corrections they'd even accept. So which is the extrapolated volition? CEV-type views lean on the hope that if the result is coherent, the extrapolations from different starting points will converge, but it's never clear to me whether convergence across extrapolation methods is supposed to be evidence that we've found something real about the person, or whether it's just an artifact of the extrapolation procedure — the methods share assumptions, so of course they agree. Address that.

Second, Bernard Williams has this old example of Paul, who arranges in advance for his future self to live by a certain policy, knowing that the future self would choose differently if left alone. We seem fine saying the arrangement respects Paul's will even though it overrides the future person's preferences. So is extrapolation just Paul's case at the scale of a whole life or a whole species — present us laying down the law for extrapolated us — or is there a real difference, since present us hasn't actually made any commitment the way Paul did?

Reach a verdict: is extrapolation discovery, replacement, or something in between, and what exactly survives the process? Its fine if the answer is messy, just make sure its defended.
