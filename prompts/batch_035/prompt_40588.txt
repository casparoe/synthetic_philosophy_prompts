so a wierd thing happened: I got roped onto the resource-allocation subcommittee of our county health board (long story — someone dropped out last minute and apparently I'm the "philosophy guy" everyone knows). we have a fixed pot of money and have to rank programs that mostly benefit different groups, and while prepping for the first meeting I realized I have a pile of principles floating around in my head that I've never actually checked against each other. before I say any of this out loud in a room full of doctors and accountants, I want to know if my own position is even consistent. here's what I wrote down:

(1) I'm basically a prioritarian: the moral value of an outcome is a weighted sum of how everyone's life goes, where a benefit counts for more the worse off the recipient is — and it's the person's *absolute* level that matters, with smooth trade-offs, so a big enough benefit to a well-off person can outweigh a modest benefit to a badly off one. (2) I take the leveling-down objection as a knockdown argument against egalitarianism: the mere fact that an outcome is more equal is never even a weak pro tanto reason to prefer it — making the better off worse off when nobody ends up better off gains nothing morally, period. (3) but here's where my gut starts acting up. compare two towns: in Alphaville everyone is equally badly off (poor, isolated, glum); in Betaville one person sits at exactly that same level while everyone else is doing fine. we can build the same small clinic for one person in either town, and suppose everything else is held equal — the Betaville person is not humiliated or stigmatized, their actual quality of life is just the same as everyone's in Alphaville. my gut insists the clinic matters *more*, is somehow more urgent, in Betaville — that the person there has a stronger claim. (4) and in the actual committee votes my gut goes even further: when the choice is between a modest benefit to someone very badly off and a large benefit to someone well off, I find I just can't vote for the well-off one, no matter how large — as if the badly off come first, full stop, which I'm aware is not what (1) says. (5) one more: I also think inequalities that come from a rigged system are worse, and demand more of a response, than the exact same inequalities arising from people's own choices — though I couldn't tell you whether that's supposed to be part of the value of outcomes or something else entirely.

so here's the ask: go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like it — for instance, is (2) even compatible with the weighting in (1), or is weighting the worse off just caring about equality in disguise? rank whatever tensions you find by severity rather than listing them as all equally bad, and for each one say what the cheapest fix would be and what that fix costs me elsewhere. don't assume I know where the problem is — I honestly don't, and I'd rather be told the truth before the meeting than after. end with the single revision that would clean up the most, and then give me a final machine-readable line of the form \answer{N — short fix}, where N is the number of the commitment you'd change first and the fix is ten words or fewer.
