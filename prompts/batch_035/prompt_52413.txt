I'm a postdoc in legal philosophy, and next month I'm presenting at a faculty workshop at a US law school a paper defending a narrow, European-style hate speech statute for democracies. The proposal is roughly the German model of criminal incitement to hatred, scaled down by importing the six-factor severity threshold of the UN's Rabat Plan of Action — intent, content, context, speaker status, likelihood of dissemination, and imminence of harm — so that only speech that scores severely on all dimensions loses protection. The philosophical core is Waldron's assurance argument: the statute doesn't regulate opinion but protects a public good, namely the baseline assurance each member of a vulnerable group needs that they can live in society as equals in good standing. On that framing, the right comparison isn't censorship of dissent but environmental regulation of the public sphere, or group libel law. I concede up front that any such statute is content- and viewpoint-based; my claim is that viewpoint neutrality is not an end in itself but a defeasible means to a well-ordered public sphere, and that for this narrow class of speech the presumption is defeated.

The audience is the hard part. It's mostly American First Amendment specialists: two doctrinalists who think Brandenburg settled the outer boundary of incitement regulation and who will read my proposal through R.A.V. v. St. Paul and its ban on viewpoint discrimination within unprotected categories; one scholar who works on counterspeech theory and thinks the remedy for hateful speech is always more speech; a clinician who has defended unpopular speakers and worries about selective enforcement; a visiting German constitutional scholar who will think my watered-down Volksverhetzung doesn't go far enough and will invoke militant democracy at me; and a couple of critical theorists who will push on whose speech actually gets prosecuted in Europe. My thesis defense instincts are calibrated for philosophers, not for this room.

Please take the role of a comparative constitutional law scholar who knows both the American doctrine and the European landscape cold, and who has sat through enough faculty workshops to know how these questions actually land. Before drafting anything, sketch what this particular audience's priors are and how they differ from each other, because the same argument draws different fire from a doctrinalist and a clinician. Then produce my Q&A prep sheet: roughly eight objections in the order they're likely to come and ordered by how much damage each would do, each phrased the way that specific person in the room would put it (in their vocabulary, with their cases and their precedents), a two-minute reply to each, and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front, what I must hold at all costs, and which single reply has to land for the paper to survive the room. Finally, include the question I'm hoping nobody asks — the one that, if asked by the right person in the first ten minutes, reframes the whole session against me.
