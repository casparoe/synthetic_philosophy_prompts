Write a dialogue between a maximizing consequentialist and a defender of supererogation. The consequentialist holds that if an act would produce more good than any available alternative, then one ought to perform it — so the very notion of an act that is praiseworthy but not obligatory is incoherent: either the supposedly supererogatory act was actually required, or it was not better than the alternatives and the praise is misplaced. The defender takes the defining mark of supererogation to be its dual character — performing the act merits moral credit, yet omitting it incurs no blame — and argues this is best explained by a satisficing structure in which duty demands only that one act well enough, while credit tracks excellence above that threshold. Have the consequentialist press the arbitrariness of any such threshold and offer the alternative diagnosis that praising beyond-duty acts is simply a useful incentive tool, with no distinctive realm of obligation behind it. Have the defender respond that this diagnosis makes praise look manipulative in a way it does not when we admire, say, someone who donates a kidney to a stranger, and note that the consequentialist's monistic ought struggles to distinguish the wrongness of betraying a friend from the wrongness of merely failing to squeeze more good out of one's afternoon. Both characters should be equally competent, and each should engage the other's strongest point rather than talking past it.
