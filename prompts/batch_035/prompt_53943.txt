Two members of my formal epistemology reading group, Daniel and Elena, got into a long argument about whether the logical omniscience problem is a real problem or a philosopher's manufactured one, and I want an expert's read on it. The context: in the standard possible-worlds treatment of knowledge, an agent knows a proposition iff it holds at every world compatible with what the agent knows, and because any logical consequence of what she knows holds at all those worlds, she thereby knows every consequence of her knowledge — every theorem, every entailment, however remote. That is the target of the argument.

Move 1 — Daniel claimed the framework is thereby broken as a theory of knowledge: a model that entails that every actual agent knows all of mathematics and every chess endgame has simply missed the phenomenon, the way a theory of temperature that entails all metals are liquid would have.

Move 2 — Elena replied that this is just idealisation, which is ubiquitous and benign: frictionless planes in mechanics, ideal gases in thermodynamics. The model deliberately brackets the agent's computational limitations in order to isolate something else — the contribution of her information to what follows from it. Complaining that real agents don't know all consequences, she said, is like complaining that real engines have friction.

Move 3 — Daniel pressed a disanalogy: idealised models earn their keep because real systems approach them along some parameter — a real gas approximates an ideal gas as pressure drops. But there is no parameter along which a finite agent approaches omniscience; an agent that is 'less and less' limited computationally is still nowhere near knowing all consequences of its evidence, and the omniscient limit is not a knower of the same kind at all but a different sort of thing. So the idealisation does not simplify a phenomenon; it replaces it.

Move 4 — Elena accused Daniel of conflating two distinct problems: a descriptive one (modelling what actual agents believe, which is psychology, not epistemology) and a normative one (what an agent is entitled to believe given what she knows). On the normative side she proposed that closure still holds of what the agent is entitled to believe — she is entitled to all consequences, and the fact that exercising the entitlement takes work is a matter of resources, not of epistemic standing, rather as one can be entitled to a fortune one has not yet bothered to collect.

Move 5 — Daniel's rejoinder was that the entitlement picture either collapses back into omniscience or does no work: if entitlement is unrestricted, then a bounded agent who must choose which of infinitely many derivations to actually perform fails almost every obligation the theory sets, violating ought-implies-can; and if entitlement is restricted to consequences derivable within the agent's actual resources, then it no longer follows from knowledge itself but from facts about her computational budget, which looks like a different and vaguer theory.

You are a professional philosopher working in epistemic logic. Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: for each of the five moves, say whether it is basically right, basically mistaken, or right about something smaller than its author claims, with brief justification. Then say who is more right overall, and whether there is some distinction or consideration that would dissolve the disagreement that neither of them brought up. Please think carefully before committing to verdicts, and note explicitly where you think an idealisation analogy is doing legitimate work versus where it is papering over a change of subject.
