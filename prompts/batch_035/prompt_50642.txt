Two research groups have been publishing past each other for decades on the philosophical use of Gödel's incompleteness theorems. Group A defends the line running from Lucas's 1961 paper "Minds, Machines and Gödel" through Penrose's The Emperor's New Mind and Shadows of the Mind: the incompleteness theorems show that human mathematical understanding cannot be the output of any formal system or Turing machine, because for any such system there is a true arithmetical sentence it cannot certify, while a human mathematician can see that the sentence is true. Group B, the mechanist critics, holds that this argument fails: it illicitly requires that the human mathematician's output be consistent and sound as a totality; it requires that the human be a definite formal system whose identity could in principle be fixed; and it trades on confusing the ability to verify a machine's soundness from outside with the ability to do so from inside. Suppose the two groups have finally agreed to an adversarial collaboration, with a neutral moderator, and have committed to producing a joint statement. Draft that statement. It must contain the following four parts.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims — at least six — covering at minimum: what the incompleteness theorems literally establish, including their conditional character (they concern formal systems meeting representability and soundness or consistency conditions, and by themselves say nothing about minds); that the passage from self-referential formulas to self-aware minds is an interpretation added on top of the mathematics rather than part of it; and that in Gödel, Escher, Bach the same materials are put to a mechanism-friendly use — its author draws on self-reference and strange loops to explain how self-aware minds could arise within mechanical systems, and has publicly protested the anti-mechanist reading of his book. These claims must be non-vacuous: things a third party could actually learn from.

Second, a disagreements table. Each row states one residual disagreement in wording that both groups would accept as a fair statement of the views in question. The table must cover at least: whether the totality of human mathematical judgment is sound or consistent in the sense the Gödelian argument needs; whether that totality forms a definite system at all, or is open-ended and revisable in a way that blocks ever fixing which machine to compare the human against; and whether a human can in principle come to know the soundness of any machine that exactly models them — including the mechanist reply that one is some machine but cannot know which one, and the question of whether that reply renders the mechanist position untestable or is instead exactly what the second incompleteness theorem should lead one to expect.

Third, a cruxes section: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one group, with, for each crux, a concrete case, argument, or thought experiment both groups agree bears on it, and each group's prediction about that case recorded in advance. One crux must be the external-perspective case: a machine M whose axioms and inference rules are fully displayed, and which was built without consulting the human subject. Both groups accept that competent mathematicians examining M can come to accept M's Gödel sentence as true. The live question is whether the asymmetry Group A needs — that the human could do the same when M is the machine that exactly models that very human — survives the observation that the external verification of M did not, and could not, proceed by reflecting on M's axioms from within M. For this crux, have each group write down in advance what it would conclude if the external case proves disanalogous to the self-application case. A second crux must be Gödel's own disjunctive conclusion from his 1951 Gibbs lecture: either the human mind surpasses the powers of any finite machine, or there exist absolutely undecidable mathematical problems. Each group should state where it stands on each horn, and the statement should record whether Group B's position — accepting the second horn while rejecting the first — is stable, or whether it is a hostage to future mathematics, for instance to the emergence of a problem widely regarded as absolutely undecidable, or to the persistent failure of any candidate to be so regarded.

Fourth, a closing section identifying which of the residual disagreements are substantive and which are verbal — in particular, whether the dispute over whether the mind "surpasses" machines trades on a conflation of classifying more arithmetical truths, reflecting on more of one's own procedures, and computing faster — followed by one short paragraph, in language both groups can sign, about what development would genuinely move the debate.

Throughout, no appeals to authority: the statement may not rest on who said what in the secondary literature, only on what can be stated and assessed on its face. And every row of the disagreements table must be phrased so that the group it describes would put its name to the wording.
