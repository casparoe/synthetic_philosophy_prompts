Write an essay on whether a large language model should be understood as an agent, a simulator, or something else altogether. Your essay should weigh at least the following four positions, though you may add others or argue for a fifth of your own:

1. The agential view: the model itself is a goal-directed system — it plans, pursues objectives, and can resist or comply strategically — even if its goals are alien or fragmentary compared to ours.
2. The simulator view: the model is less a character than a theatre. It predicts plausible continuations of text, and whatever goal-directedness we observe belongs to the personas it momentarily brings to life, not to it. On this view, asking whether the model "wants" something is like asking whether a stage wants something.
3. The role-occupant view: agent-talk about language models is systematically ambiguous between the model (a persistent substrate with stable dispositions) and the character it plays in a given conversation (an ephemeral construction of prompt and context). Neither is the right unit of analysis; agency attributions go wrong because they force a choice between the two.
4. The deflationary view: "agent" and "simulator" are not rival metaphysical categories but different stances or vocabularies we adopt toward a complex system, chosen for their usefulness in particular contexts; the dispute is therefore verbal, and the interesting question is which stance better serves which purposes.

In weighing these positions, your essay should confront at least the following complications. First, persistence: suppose a model displays stable, predictable dispositions across thousands of unrelated conversations — it consistently refuses certain requests, consistently produces text in a recognizable style, and behaves predictably under prompting variations. Do cross-context dispositions of this kind belong to the simulator or to the characters, and does their existence put pressure on the simulator view? Second, control: suppose a model's persona is heavily shaped by a system prompt written by its developer. If the character's goals are largely chosen by someone outside the model, where does whatever agency exists reside — in the model, the character, the developer, or the human-machine system as a whole? Third, an institutional analogy: corporations lack bodies, sensations, and inner lives, yet the law treats them as agents that can intend, promise, and be liable. If agency can be partly constituted by social and institutional practices rather than by intrinsic psychology, does this suggest a further position the four above miss?

You should engage with whatever philosophical tools you find helpful — for instance, the distinction between intrinsic and ascribed intentionality, or the difference between what a system is and what it does under a given description — but you should not rely on citations or reading lists; argue from the cases and concepts themselves.

The essay must reach and justify a clear verdict. A verdict that one of the four positions is closest to the truth, that some hybrid or reformulation is needed (say what it is), or that the question rests on a confusion (identify the confusion and say what should replace it) are all acceptable outcomes. Ending on an agnostic note is not.
