Two members of my moral philosophy seminar have been arguing past each other for a year about whether the numbers count in rescue cases, in the tradition of John Taurek's paper "Should the Numbers Count?". One — call her the aggregator — holds that when a rescuer can save either a larger or a smaller group of strangers, and nothing else differs between the groups, she ought to save the larger group, precisely because more lives would be lost otherwise. The other — call him the Taurekian — holds that the bare number of persons at stake gives the rescuer no reason of its own to prefer the many; each person's claim on the rescue is equally strong, and nothing in the situation sums those claims into a stronger one. Neither is a utilitarian about everything, and neither thinks rescuers may permissibly favour people they love in these cases — they have stipulated that much already, and it should be treated as settled.

Draft the joint statement these two should have produced from a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Please write your answer in British English, and structure it as follows.

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. These should be neither vacuous nor slanted: both must be able to sign each one without hesitation. Include the symmetry assumptions they share (the cases involve strangers, equal ages, equal prospects, no special relations), their shared view that each person's death would be equally bad for that person, and whatever they can agree on about the fairness of lotteries as a way of giving each victim an equal chance when a lottery is available.

2. A disagreements table. State each remaining disagreement in wording that its own side would accept as a fair statement of its view — the aggregator on why five deaths is worse than one death and why that fact matters to the rescuer's reasons; the Taurekian on why there is, in his view, no standpoint from which the five losses are totalled, and on what the rescuer may permissibly do when no lottery is available. Flag any disagreement that turns out to be merely verbal, and say which part it is and why.

3. A cruxes section. Identify the two or three sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party, and for each, describe a case or argument that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. In constructing these, use the following three cases, which you should treat as fully specified rather than interpreting with further background assumptions:

- The Cascade Rescue. Five miners are trapped in sequence along a single passage; each extraction takes an hour, and at each stage there is a known one-in-ten chance that the passage collapses and no further miners can be reached. A sixth miner, trapped separately, could instead be rescued by a route that is certain to succeed but takes the same five hours in total. Each side must record, in advance, what the rescuer ought to do, and at what point — if any — the numbers begin to count for the Taurekian.
- The Denied Lottery. The choice between saving five and saving one must be made instantly, with no device or method available for randomising and no way to let the victims choose. The aggregator must state whether the impossibility of a lottery changes what ought to be done; the Taurekian must state whether anything at all is now impermissible, or whether either option is permitted, and if the latter, whether he accepts that his view leaves the rescuer with no guidance and whether that is a cost.
- The Downstream Single. The rescuer can save either five strangers or one stranger who, if saved, will certainly go on to save three further people. The question for both sides is whether the consideration the Taurekian refuses to apply to simultaneous victims re-enters when the further lives arrive through the saved person's agency, and each must say exactly why or why not, in wording the other accepts as capturing a genuine difference between the cases.

Where a side's prediction commits it to something it has previously resisted, note this explicitly. End with a short paragraph, in both voices, on what each thinks would have to be true for the other side to be right, and what each concedes the other has established over the year.
