In a seminar on the causes of the First World War, a classmate dismissed our structural readings — alliance commitments, mobilization timetables, the July Crisis dynamics — by invoking the old proverb: for want of a nail the shoe was lost, for want of a shoe the horse was lost, and so on up to the kingdom. His point seemed to be that since a single assassination, or even one chauffeur's wrong turn, could have derailed the whole thing, structural explanations are at best after-the-fact rationalizations: whatever happened, something small could have prevented it, so nothing deep was 'driving' events. I suspect the proverb is packaging several very different claims, but I can't yet separate them. Please lay out the distinct precise theses 'for want of a nail' could be expressing — as a menu, from the near-trivial to the genuinely radical: e.g. an event-level counterfactual claim about what would have happened had some detail gone otherwise; a claim about the sensitivity or instability of historical outcomes to initial conditions; a claim about which causes are explanatorily privileged; a claim about the asymmetry between how history unfolds forward and how it looks reconstructed backward; a claim about indeterminacy or the impossibility of explanation itself. For each reading, say whether it is plausible, and — the part I most need — whether it would actually support my classmate's use of the proverb to dismiss structural explanation, or whether it is compatible with structural explanation, or too weak to do any work at all. Include your verdict on whether the strongest defensible reading of the proverb really is in tension with the strongest structural account of the war's origins, or whether the two can simply be true at different levels of description.
