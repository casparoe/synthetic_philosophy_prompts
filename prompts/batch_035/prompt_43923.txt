I teach a graduate seminar on the ethics of assertion, and for our unit on lying versus misleading I want a written exercise that goes beyond the usual case wrangling. To fix the background for you: the standard analysis treats lying as asserting something you believe to be false, with the intent that your audience believe it; misleading, by contrast, is getting someone to a false belief by way of assertions you believe to be true, exploiting the gap between what is strictly said and what is conversationally implicated — the hearer does the inferential work themselves. On one prominent position in this literature, the wrong of lying is exhausted by the false assertion: the speaker violates the norm of assertion at the level of what is said, and that is the whole of the wrong. On that view, technically true statements are always above board, however predictable the false belief they induce; the competing thought is that a speaker's responsibility doesn't stop at the boundary of the proposition expressed but extends to what she foreseeably gets her audience to believe. The piece I'm commissioning should put real pressure on exactly that fault line.

What I'd like is a satirical corporate policy document: a "Communications and Truthfulness Standards Manual," in numbered clauses, roughly 700 words, issued by the compliance office of an invented firm — a consultancy, say, "Meridian Vale Advisory" — whose official doctrine is that the truth of what is asserted is necessary and sufficient for permissible speech. The tone must stay deadpan throughout: no winking, no editorializing, the irony legible only from the content. The jokes should come from getting the philosophy right rather than from slapstick, and the manual should contain at least the following machinery. First, a definitions clause that carefully distinguishes what is said from what is merely communicated, and explicitly places the latter out of scope ("the Company is responsible for its statements, not for its audiences' inferences"). Second, two or three worked examples in boxes — something like a truthful "this facility has never failed a safety audit" where no audit has ever been conducted, or "no customer complaints have been received" in a context where the complaint line was quietly disconnected — each resolved in favor of the speaker. Third, a legacy-scenario clause handling the classic case of the inquirer at the door demanding to know the whereabouts of someone you are sheltering, with an approved truthful-but-diverting formulation supplied. Fourth, an incident-response protocol for the case in which the audience works out the implicature after all, the only harm the manual recognizes being the reputational one of having been found out. And fifth, a staff training requirement: any employee who catches herself about to utter a falsehood must be able, on the spot, to convert it into a compliant truth.

Two constraints on execution. The target is the position, not any person: attribute nothing to named living philosophers, and do not have the manual's authors stumble into self-contradiction — they must be perfectly consistent within their own doctrine, since the satire is a reductio of the doctrine, not of the drafters' competence. A specialist reading the piece should recognize the view actually defended in the literature — that the wrong of lying lies entirely in the false assertion — and should judge the satire fair, an exaggeration of a real feature rather than an invention. And by the final clause, a reader who has never heard of the lying/misleading distinction should nonetheless come away genuinely unsettled about the question underneath the comedy: whether moral responsibility tracks what a speaker asserts or what she foreseeably causes her audience to believe. If the piece can leave that question live without ever stating it, it will have done the work I want.
