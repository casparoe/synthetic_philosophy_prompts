In the hospital where my mother worked as a social worker, the same institution does three things I cannot reconcile. When a patient with capacity refuses a feeding tube, her present word is final and no one may question it. But when a patient with advanced dementia pulls at the tube and asks to go home, and her advance directive from years earlier says no artificial nutrition, the staff honor the paper over the person in the bed. And in a third kind of case the institution behaves differently again: a patient who, while competent, tried to irrevocably bind his future self — telling his treatment team in writing that he wanted no antipsychotic medication ever, even if he later demanded it during a psychotic episode — was medicated anyway, on the reasoning that his past instruction carries weight only when the present patient is judged unable to decide. So present competent wishes always win; past wishes win only when the present self is disqualified; and even past wishes apparently give way when the later self's distress is severe enough.

What theory of the self, or of autonomy, does this layered practice presuppose? One possibility is that autonomy belongs to the whole life: the earlier, competent self speaks for the person because a life is the unit whose interests matter, and the directive executes the project that person set for herself. Another is that the directive works by identity loss: the later, demented self is in some relevant sense no longer the one who made the promise, so following the directive is not self-rule at all but the carrying out of a bequest. A third is that the directive is merely evidence of what is best for the patient, defeasible like any evidence, with no authority over anything. The practice does not treat these as equivalent: an authority reading should honor the directive even when the later patient is content and resistant to treatment, whereas an evidential reading should give way when the patient's current state is itself the better guide to her interests. And the psychiatric case suggests the operative principle may be none of the three — perhaps something closer to honoring the past self only when compliance is cheap, or only when the later self is not suffering.

Then commit: which reading of what the practice is doing do you actually endorse, and what specifically should change in how advance directives are drafted, interpreted, or overridden if your reading is right?
