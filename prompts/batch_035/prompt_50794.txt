My wife and I have spent the past year trying to decide whether to have a child, and last weekend's dinner with her sister Dana and my old roommate Marcus turned into a full argument over Benatar's asymmetry, which Dana had been reading about and now cannot stop citing. Dana laid it out: the presence of suffering is bad and its absence is good, but the presence of happiness is good while its absence is not bad — so a never-conceived child loses nothing it could miss and is spared everything bad, which makes non-creation at least as good as creation and arguably better, and that was supposed to settle our question. Marcus answered that the comparison is rigged: in one column there is a subject with interests, in the other there is no one at all, and a better-than relation cannot reach across that gap — what Dana calls an asymmetry, he said, is just the grammar of benefit breaking down when there is no bearer. Dana replied that we make such cross-gap judgments fluently all the same: we say it was a mercy that a child who would have had a short and agonizing life was never conceived, and if Marcus's gap objection worked, that ordinary verdict would be nonsense rather than obviously correct. Marcus conceded the mercy judgment but said it is best explained as concern for people who exist or will — the grieving parents, the counterfactual child we cannot help picturing — and then pivoted: even granting the asymmetry, it only forbids creating lives whose suffering outweighs their happiness, so to get a verdict about an ordinary prospective child Dana needs Benatar's further claim that everyone systematically overrates the quality of their own life — which Marcus found self-undermining, since it was asserted by someone apparently confident in the accuracy of his own gloomy audit. Dana said the bias claim is an empirical one about known asymmetries in how people weigh and recall suffering versus satisfaction, not a blanket skepticism that would eat its own tail. They called a truce before dessert. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: who is right on each point, whether the gap objection or the asymmetry comes out of it alive, and what both of them left out that bears on the decision my wife and I actually have to make.
