My reading group in cognitive science has spent the last few sessions on the language of thought hypothesis, and this week two members got into an argument about whether modern deep learning has finally settled it. Marianne, who is writing a philosophy thesis on mental representation, and Keith, who trains neural networks for a living, have been circling this dispute for a month. Their exchange this week went in rounds, and since I'm supposed to moderate the group next session, I'd like some help refereeing it.

Round one: Keith opened by claiming the empirical side is decided. Large networks trained on nothing but text already exhibit systematic behavior — a model that can process "the dog chased the cat" handles the roles reversed without being separately trained — so the old demand for an inbuilt, symbol-manipulating architecture was a bet about what learning systems can do, and the bet has come in; the LOT's inner symbols now look like epicycles. Round two: Marianne replied that this mislocates the argument, because the classic systematicity challenge never claimed networks couldn't behave systematically; it asked what explains that behavior. If a network generalizes systematically, either it has internally learned something compositional — in which case the classical story is vindicated at the level that actually matters — or the generalization is shallow and will break under pressure; either way, "look, it works" is a success report, not an explanation. Round three: Keith said that makes the hypothesis unfalsifiable, since whenever networks succeed she redescribes them as "really implementing a LOT underneath," and when they fail she blames scale rather than the architecture; he added that the brain itself is continuous and noisy, with nothing resembling discrete symbols anywhere, yet on her picture the real cognition of real brains always conveniently lives at some higher "level," which looks like moving the goalposts to wherever the data aren't. He then tried to force the dispute to go empirical by proposing a diagnostic: train a network from scratch only on sentences with one thematic-role pattern — always "A loves B," never "B loves A" — and check whether it generalizes, treating the outcome as a verdict either way. Round four: Marianne answered that levels of explanation are perfectly respectable — a laptop runs a symbolic program even though its underlying physics is continuous — and that the "no discrete symbols in neurons" objection proves too much, since by that reasoning nothing physical could ever implement software at all. On the diagnostic she liked the spirit but refused the design: you can never exclude subtle exposure to the missing pattern during training, and if the network does generalize, that's a fact about learning, not evidence about architecture, so the experiment would measure the wrong thing.

They asked me to referee, and I honestly couldn't. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the four rounds, say who is right about what, and where either of them overreaches or quietly concedes something. In particular, assess Keith's charge of unfalsifiability in round three — is it a genuine problem for Marianne's position, or does her laptop analogy answer it? — and judge whether her reply to his diagnostic, that it would measure generalization rather than architecture, is a fair methodological point or a deflection from a test she should welcome. Finally, tell me who is more right overall, and what, if anything, both of them are missing about what the systematicity challenge was originally supposed to show.
