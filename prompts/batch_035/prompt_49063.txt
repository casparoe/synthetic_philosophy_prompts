I'm teaching an upper-level seminar on vagueness and putting together the final. I want one essay question on the sorites paradox that students can't get through by reciting the standard catalog (no "how many grains make a heap" narrative, no list of responses with a paragraph each). Here's the angle I want to force them into. A colleague of mine defends what she calls a "threshold-choice" view of vague predicates: individual speakers have no sharp cutoff in mind for 'tall', but the aggregate pattern of use settles one anyway, the way a market price settles without anyone choosing it — and on her story the sorites paradox is basically an optical illusion, generated by asking a question ("is there a last bald man in the sequence?") that our classificatory practices were simply never built to answer. Write the exam question so it makes students test this view against the sorites reasoning itself: is "our practices weren't designed to answer that" an answer, or just a polite refusal of one? And the fun bit — the view weirdly resembles epistemicism (there *is* a sharp cutoff, we just can't know it) and supervaluationism (the cutoff question lacks a determinate answer) at the same time, and students have to say which resemblance is deeper and why, or argue the view collapses into one of them. Along with the question, give me a model answer of about 600 words and a marking scheme worth 20 marks total, with the must-hit points and how much each is worth. Two more things. The marking scheme should explicitly separate an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific move that distinguishes them (my guess is it's whether they notice that "practices settle a threshold" and "no determinate threshold" can't both be said without argument, but you tell me). And please have the model answer flag which claims are actually settled in the literature versus genuinely contested among people who work on this — e.g. don't present "the conditional premises fail" or epistemicism as consensus, and don't have the model answer pretend the colleague's view has a standard refutation when it's really an open question how it stacks up. Students lose marks for presenting contested stuff as consensus and so would the model answer.
