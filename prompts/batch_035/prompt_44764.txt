I've been recording my gut reactions to cases where someone learns of an artwork's value secondhand rather than firsthand, and I want to know what principle, if anything, captures them, because I suspect my verdicts are not jointly consistent.

My friend's taste has tracked mine for a decade; when she says a film is extraordinary, I tell a third friend it's extraordinary before I've seen it. That feels fine — I'd even say I know it. Picking a restaurant from hundreds of strangers' reviews: fine. An acclaimed sommelier tells me a wine is superb; though I cannot taste any difference between it and a much cheaper bottle, I serve it at celebrations and call it superb: fine, or close to it. But after reading that a certain painting is overwhelming, I stand in front of it, feel nothing, and am asked by a companion whether it's as powerful as they say — I balk at saying yes in my own voice, and I don't think I'm entitled to. Relatedly, I'll take my friend's word that a novel is moving, but I refuse to laugh at a joke on testimony: someone recounting a bit and insisting it's hilarious leaves me cold in a way that being told a film is excellent does not. My tentative principle was that testimony transmits aesthetic knowledge when the hearer could in principle come to detect the relevant value themselves, with training or exposure; but I can't tell whether that makes my sommelier verdict and my Rothko-in-front-of-me verdict come out consistently, and I don't know what it says about someone congenitally deaf taking a friend's word that a performance of a symphony was shattering.

Please give me a definite answer: commit to one principle, state it precisely enough to be applied mechanically, show what it says about the cases above and about the deaf listener, and tell me plainly which of my verdicts, if any, you think has to be abandoned rather than saved by complication. Use American English.
