I was laid off last month when my company shut down my entire division, and as part of the separation package they offered me a choice that has turned into a running argument with my best friend. Option one is a flat severance of $25,000, paid on my last day. Option two is tied to the division's final client contract: if it collects, I get $100,000; if it doesn't, I get $8,000; the company says each outcome is about 50% likely. My friend, who is an engineer and thinks in expected values, calculated that the gamble averages out to $54,000 — more than double the sure thing — and says that refusing it is not just irrational but numerically illiterate, that I would be leaving money on the table out of sheer timidity. I haven't signed anything yet; I have ten days.

I want to be clear about what the money is for, since my friend thinks it matters and I half agree. I'm 34, and the plan is to use whatever I get to fund a twelve-month nursing program I've been admitted to; tuition plus living expenses come to roughly $24,000, so the low branch would mean student loans and probably delaying a year. So there's a threshold nearby, and I recognize that a certain kind of risk aversion is trivially defensible when losing the gamble means losing the program. But that isn't really what's bothering me. When I imagine having no particular plans for the money, I still prefer the $25,000, strongly, and I would feel sick choosing the coin flip. My friend's response was to lecture me about diminishing marginal utility, and to his credit we actually sat down and tried it: at the suggestion of a former classmate, we modeled my utility as the logarithm of my total wealth, and even under that model the gamble comes out comfortably ahead of the sure thing. So the standard explanation — that the eightieth thousand matters less than the eighth — apparently doesn't capture my preference either. And I have a nagging sense that the one-shot character of the decision matters: this is not a choice I get to repeat, there is no averaging over many trials, and the good branch and the bad branch land on the same single life.

So my question is this: is there a principled way to defend preferring the sure $25,000 here, or should I concede that my risk aversion is irrational, however understandable? I'm not asking for reassurance. I'm asking whether there's a serious framework under which weighting the bad branch of a one-shot gamble more heavily than its probability counts as reasoning well rather than reasoning badly — whether my preference could be reconstructed as a coherent policy rather than a psychological quirk — and if such a framework exists, whether it can explain what's going on in my head better than the logarithm did. If it helps, my friend's strongest point, as I understand it, is that once you admit probabilities, you've already committed to some way of aggregating outcomes, and any deviation from weighting them by their probabilities looks like double-counting or superstition. I'd like to know whether that point is as airtight as he thinks.
