I'm a third-year mathematics major, currently enrolled in a philosophy of mathematics course, and I've just been asked to serve as TA for our first-year discrete mathematics course. In two weeks I'll lead my first solo discussion section — fifty minutes, chalkboard only — on Russell's paradox. The preceding lecture will have introduced naive set theory as a foundation, presenting the comprehension principle informally as the claim that for any property there is a set of exactly the things having that property. My job is to walk the students through the paradox and its immediate aftermath. I'm not asking you to explain the paradox to me; I understand it. I'm asking for a plan of the explanation itself.

Specifically:

1. What has to be in place before the construction is even shown? My worry is that students will treat the comprehension principle as a notational convenience rather than a genuine foundational commitment, in which case the contradiction registers as a party trick rather than a crisis. How do I get them to feel the weight of the principle they're about to lose — and does that require spending real time on what set theory was supposed to be doing for mathematics in the first place, or does that overextend a fifty-minute section?

2. What should the carrying example be? The barber version is the famous one, but I suspect it actively misleads: students conclude the paradox is just an impossible premise (the barber turns out to be a woman, or lives in the next town) rather than a proof that a seemingly innocent principle is inconsistent — closer in spirit to the "smallest positive integer not nameable in fewer than sixty words" trick than to the real thing. If you agree it's the wrong carrier, tell me what should replace it, and what the replacement must have and must lack.

3. Map the misconceptions I should expect. My guesses: that the paradox shows all self-reference is incoherent; that it is simply the liar paradox transplanted into mathematics and therefore a problem about language, not about sets; that it refutes the existence of infinite collections specifically; and that ZFC "solves" it by banning self-reference wholesale. For each, tell me where in the sequence it should be headed off rather than left to fester until the exam.

4. Whether the Russell–Frege correspondence belongs in a mathematics section at all. My philosophy of mathematics course dwells on it, and I'm tempted to include it for the drama, but I'd like a judgment about whether it illuminates the mathematical point for this audience or merely decorates it.

5. What I should openly refuse to cover — type theory, proper classes, the cumulative hierarchy — and how to acknowledge those exclusions honestly so the students know the story continues without pretending I've told it.

6. One question I can ask in the final five minutes, or put on an exit slip, that distinguishes students who understood the structure of the argument from students who memorized the punchline.
