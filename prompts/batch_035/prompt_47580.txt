A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about AI risk, and I came away unsure whether my own position is even internally consistent. Here it is, as fairly as I can state it: (1) I put the chance that AI development this century ends in human extinction or irreversible disempowerment at a few percent at least, absent major changes in how things are going. (2) I think capability progress is broadly smooth and driven by scale, and I am skeptical of stories in which a lab system goes overnight from mediocre to overwhelming. (3) I think the core alignment problem is that we can only train on measurable proxies for what we want, and that the gap between proxy and goal gets exploited more effectively as systems grow more capable — so by the time systems are dangerous, they will tend to look good on every test we have. (4) I think commercial and geopolitical competition means systems get deployed at the edge of demonstrated safety, so society learns about risks mainly from near-misses. (5) I am basically an optimist about institutions: faced with a visible, attributable threat, humanity has a decent record of muddling through, and I expect AI to become politically salient the way nuclear power and pandemics did, after the first serious incidents.

My friend claims the package contradicts itself: that I cannot both expect failures to stay invisible until they are catastrophic (3) and count on being saved by visible incidents (5), and that if I really believed (1) I would not be so relaxed about everything else. He also said gradualism (2) does not fit with a few percent doom. I do not trust my own judgment here, since I was the one defending it. Please respond as a philosopher with a background in decision theory and the study of technological risk, and before writing, work through the pairwise relations between the five commitments. Then give me an audit rather than a lecture: which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly; which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection; and, for each real tension, what would have to give and roughly what it costs. If any tension turns on the number in (1) being too high or too low to cohere with the rest, say which way it pulls. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them flatly, and do not flag everything — part of what I need is discrimination. Finally, tell me how confident you are in each of your verdicts, and single out the one point in your analysis you are least sure of.
