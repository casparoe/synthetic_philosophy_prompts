I work on distributive justice, and I have slowly come to the view that the "equality of what?" debate — the question posed by Amartya Sen's 1979 Tanner Lecture about the right metric of egalitarian concern — was never settled, only abandoned. The history is familiar. Rawls measured egalitarian concern in primary goods; Dworkin defended resources, with the hypothetical auction and the envy test meant to show that the egalitarian need not measure anything so private as welfare; Arneson and Cohen argued that the currency should be welfare after all, once properly qualified — Cohen pressing that the right metric is opportunity for advantage, cleaned of the familiar counterexamples about expensive and cheap tastes; and Sen held that the whole dispute was on the wrong foot, because both sides measured holdings rather than what a person can actually do or be: the same bundle of resources converts into very different capabilities depending on disability, health, social norms, environment. Then Elizabeth Anderson's "What Is the Point of Equality?" shifted the question from the metric to the relationship — democratic equality against luck egalitarianism — and after that the field quietly self-sorted. Capability measurement became an industry in development economics; resource and primary-goods theorizing continued within its own literature; relational equality generated its own sub-debates; and almost nobody argues across the old lines anymore. When someone does, the arguments are recognizably the ones from the 1980s: the expensive-taste cases, the disabled person who needs far more resources to reach the same level of functioning, Dworkin's complaint that any metric reaching into people's preferences forces the state to intrude on the very persons egalitarianism is meant to protect.

I have a hypothesis about what happened, and I want it diagnosed rather than confirmed. My guess is that the metric question was never one question. Part of it is an engineering question — what should institutions actually measure, given limited information and administrative capacity, and a question whose answer may legitimately differ by context: a disability-adjustment policy, a school-funding formula, a cross-country development index. Part of it is a philosophical question: what feature of a person's condition grounds an egalitarian complaint at all — what makes a condition an injustice rather than a misfortune. My suspicion is that resource theorists were largely answering the first question while capability theorists, and welfare egalitarians before them, were answering the second, and that Anderson's reframing let everyone leave the table without conceding anything, since each side could take its preferred question and treat the other's literature as beside the point. But I cannot tell whether that means the disagreement was verbal all along, or whether there is a genuine substantive dispute still standing — about, say, whether a theory of justice requires a single currency at all, or only whether this or that institutional metric is good enough — that everyone simply got tired of having.

So diagnose the disagreement for me. Is it verbal, substantive, ill-posed, or a case of several legitimate questions wrongly run together under one heading? Support the diagnosis with what the sides actually claimed, not a survey of positions. I want you to take seriously one piece of evidence in particular: the dialectic of counterexample and refinement. Welfare egalitarianism gave way to opportunity for welfare and then to responsibility-sensitive variants; equality of resources gave way to the hypothetical insurance scheme for talents and handicaps. Each refinement absorbed the previous round of cases while leaving the objection's structure intact. Does that history show the positions were converging on something real, or that the metric question had degenerated into a game of absorbing counterexamples without ever touching the underlying disagreement? And given your diagnosis, tell me honestly what would count as progress: a distinction, a shared test case the sides could agree in advance would be decisive, or the admission that the question is answerable only piecemeal and locally — and that there was never anything general to find.
