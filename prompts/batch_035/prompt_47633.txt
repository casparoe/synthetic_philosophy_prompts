My sister and I just sent off the page proofs for a reference book we co-wrote — roughly 1,000 individual claims, each of which we checked as carefully as we know how. She's a statistician, and she has now ruined my sense of accomplishment with an argument I can't get out of my head (she keeps citing the literature at me too, Makinson this, Kyburg that, but the argument itself is hers). It goes:

(1) For each claim in the book, given everything we know, we're justified in believing it. She grants this freely — she says denying it would prove too much (no testimony, no science, no memory, nothing left).

(2) We're also justified in believing our book contains at least one substantive error somewhere. Her basis: she went through the track record — of the last 40 comparable reference volumes she could check, 20 had an error caught within two years. So call it a coin flip. Which means the preface I insisted on writting, the one taking responsibility for the errors that remain, is honest in a weird way: I believe it.

(3) If you're justified in believing each member of a set of claims, you're justified in believing their conjunction. (Agglomeration — her word.)

(4) So by (1) and (3) we're justified in believing the book contains no errors, which flatly contradicts (2).

(5) A rational agent can't be justified in believing outright contradictions.

(6) So (3) has to go, since (1) and (2) are staying.

Her conclusion, stated cheerfully: nobody is ever justified in believing the conjunction of a large number of fallibly established claims. That's the part that seems to me to prove too much: it would mean no mathematician is ever justified in believing a theorem (a proof is a conjunction of lemmas, each resting on fallible checking), no jury is ever justified in its verdict, no engineer should ever sign off on a design, and she's never justified in believing her own book is finished. She says fine, that's just honest fallibilism. I think it's nuts, but I honestly can't see which premise to give up, or whether something quietly slid between (1) and (4).

What bugs me almost as much: she treats the lottery version differently. For a million-ticket lottery she'll say you are NOT justified in believing your ticket will lose, because "some ticket wins" is part of your evidence and is certain. For the book, the error is only 50/50, so each claim keeps something like 99.9% and (1) survives. But that looks like a difference of degree, not kind — if the track record for books like ours were 999 errors per 1,000 volumes, the per-claim probability would still sit above 99%, and the book case would have smoothly turned into the lottery case somewhere along the way. Is there a principled line there, or is she just resolving each case wherever her intuition is loudest?

For what it's worth, I'd rather keep (1) than (3) — I can live with agglomeration failing long before I can live with never being justified in any individually checked claim. But tell me straight what rejecting (3) actually costs: does mathematics really collapse, or is that rhetoric?
