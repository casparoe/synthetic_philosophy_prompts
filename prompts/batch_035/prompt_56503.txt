My essay idea is basically: everyone argues about whether a maximally good person (a "moral saint") is desirable or not, but almost nobody asks whether the LAST bit of goodness is the same kind of thing as the first bit. Here's my draft:

Title: "Moral Tax Brackets"

So imagine moral goodness works like income tax, but in reverse. Going from being a pretty bad person to a decent person is cheap: stop lying, apologize sometimes, give a bit to charity. Going from decent to very good costs more but is still worth it. But going from very good to perfect? That's a different product entirely. It's not more of the same goodness, it's a different substance. The last 5% of sainthood costs you your sense of humor, your pottery, your friendships that exist for no reason -- all the stuff that makes someone a person instead of a moral vending machine. I call this the "marginal cost explosion," and here's the kicker: if the last 5% is different in kind, then asking "is the moral saint ideal?" is a trick question, because the saint isn't at the top of the same scale we're all on. It's like asking if the hottest temperature is a good temperature for swimming -- the scale stopped being about swimming somewhere before the end. Also, maybe people who complain that moral saints are boring have noticed the tax bracket thing without knowing it, they just described it as a personality complaint instead of a structural one.

P.S. there is definately a counterexample I'm ignoring involving someone who is naturally, effortlessly perfect, and I know it, I just don't know what to do about it.
