I am a novelist finishing a book in which the narrator commits a betrayal that readers are meant to hold against her, and the question of whether she deserves that blame has begun to feel like the book's real subject. Free will has thus become a professional problem for me. Every one of my character's choices is fixed in advance by me, and yet while writing I often experience her as resisting her fate — an experience common enough among novelists that I suspect it quietly shapes how people ordinarily think about agency. So here is my question: what is your credence that a person can be genuinely morally responsible for a choice even if determinism is true and that choice was fully fixed by prior causes? Please decompose the number rather than giving it bare: the probability that determinism actually holds of our world; the probability that responsibility survives determinism when the fixing is done impersonally by character plus circumstance rather than by any agent; the probability that manipulation arguments succeed — the claim that a puppeteer's causation of a puppet's choices is relevantly like determinism's causation of ours, and thus that compatibilism about the second collapses under our clear judgment about the first; and any other terms you think belong, such as whether "could have done otherwise" is genuinely required for blame. Give a number and a line of reasoning for each, combine them into your final credence, and identify which term the result is most sensitive to. Finally, tell me whether my authorial situation — an agent whose every choice is literally scripted by another mind — is an aid to the compatibilist or an embarrassment to them, and what would most shift your number in either direction.
