I'm taking a course on rational choice theory, and I've gotten stuck on a distinction I can't make precise. On the one hand there is the Kelly criterion: when a bet is offered repeatedly and you may choose what fraction of your bankroll to stake, you maximize long-run wealth growth by maximizing the expected logarithm of wealth. For example, in a bet that triples your stake with probability 1/2 and loses it with probability 1/2, the Kelly rule says to stake a quarter of your bankroll — staking everything maximizes expected wealth after one round, but repeated full staking almost surely ends in ruin. On the other hand, there is a more radical idea I've come across under the name of geometric rationality: that a rational agent should maximize the geometric mean of utility across possibilities in general — not just wealth across a long run of repeated bets, and even in a one-shot decision among possible worlds, where there is no actual sequence of trials for any averaging-over-time to occur. Since the geometric mean never exceeds the arithmetic mean, the two rules come apart precisely where rare catastrophic or astronomically good outcomes dominate the arithmetic expectation.

Please compare these two ideas carefully. First, in which ways is the Kelly view special? That is, which features of its setting — repetition, a bankroll that persists between bets, the absorbing barrier of ruin, the multiplicative dynamics of wealth — are doing the real justificatory work, and which of those features does geometric rationality deliberately generalize away? Second, address the crux: does anything like the Kelly justification survive the move from wealth to utility, and from repeated trials to a single decision? Maximizing the arithmetic mean of utility is the orthodox theory of rational choice, so explain exactly what the geometric rationalist claims is wrong with it — for instance, what the two camps would each say about a one-off bet that offers a minuscule probability of a payoff so vast that the arithmetic expectation is enormous while the geometric mean is not. I'd like a verdict, too: is geometric rationality a natural extension of Kelly's logic, or does it borrow the prestige of a theorem whose assumptions it has dropped?
