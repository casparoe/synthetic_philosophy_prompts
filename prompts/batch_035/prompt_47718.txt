hey, so I'm writing the take-home final for my mid-level phil of mind course. We spent two weeks on Dennett's kinds of creatures — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian — and I'm tired of recieving essays that just recite the ladder with the stock examples (the bacterium, the pigeon, the chess player, the map user). I want one question, answerable in about an hour, that forces students to use the taxonomy as a tool instead of a list to memorize.

Here's the case I want the question built around. A chatbot is trained with RLHF: during training it generates lots of candidate responses that get scored by a reward model, and its weights change in response. Then it's deployed as a single forward pass — it just outputs its best guess, no runtime testing of alternatives. Meanwhile the company keeps running A/B tests on the deployed system, quietly killing prompt variants that users dislike.

The question should make students classify each piece of this setup and then, crucially, generalize. The wrinkle I want in there: they'll probably reach for "Darwinian" for training (variation + selection), but then have to notice the model's weights are changing during its own "lifetime", which sounds Skinnerian — so which is it, and what decides? Then the deployed model: does a single forward pass count as Popperian hypothesis-testing (something simulation-like going on in the layers?) or not even that? And the A/B testing seems to make the *company* the Popperian creature, with the chatbot as part of its environment — which is weird, since the chatbot was supposed to be the creature in this story. The deepest part of the question: does this show the hierarchy is really about kinds of creatures at all, or about kinds of *processes* (variation-generation, selection, simulation, cultural inheritance) that can be layered onto the same substrate at different timescales? Students have to take a position on that and defend it, not just go case by case.

Can you put together:

1. **The question** — about 120–150 words, written so it can't be answered by reciting definitions; the case description can be compressed, students have read the chapters;
2. **Model answer** — 600ish words, taking a clear position on the creatures-vs-processes issue rather than hedging;
3. **Marking scheme** — five must-hit points, with mark values out of 20 total, plus a one-line note on each saying how students typically flub it;
4. **"Why the B answers go wrong"** — a short closing section, ~100 words, so I can calibrate when grading.

One more thing: please don't lean on exact quotes from Dennett or page numbers anywhere — paraphrase freely. I don't want the model answer to hinge on anything my students would have to have underlined.
