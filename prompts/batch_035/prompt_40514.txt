I'm a retired developmental biologist (thirty-five odd years, mostly early embryology in mice and humans) and since leaving the lab I've gotten well and truly hooked on philosophy — I go to a continuing-ed discussion group on applied ethics, and next month I'm supposed to submit a one-page "position statement" on abortion so the rest of the group can poke holes in it. Before I hand it in I'd like a sanity check on whether it's even internally consistant, because a friend in the group insists it isn't and refuses to tell me which part to cut, which was annoying and unhelpful in equal measure. Here is what I hold:

(1) I buy the future-like-ours argument: the reason killing anyone is wrong is that it robs them of a future of experiences, projects, relationships and so on that they would otherwise have had, and a fetus has a future of that kind from very early on — so early abortion is in itself a serious wrong. I do NOT buy any line based on consciousness, pain, or viability, and here's where I'll pull rank: I spent decades watching development unfold and there is no bright line anywhere. It's gradients and gradual acquisition of competence all the way down, so every threshold anyone proposes (implantation, quickening, brainstem maturity, viability) is arbitrary in exactly the way sorites thresholds always are. The twinning objection people sometimes raise never bothered me either — if the embryo splits, each twin ends up with her own future-like-ours, so what.

(2) I also buy the violinist point from Thomson: a right to life is not a right to somebody else's body, and nobody is morally required to serve as a life-support system for a stranger. So even granting (1) in full, I think an abortion can be morally permissable — the fetus's future makes the killing a serious, regrettable thing, but it does not saddle the woman with a duty to gestate.

(3) That said, I hold — and this is where my friend started shaking her head — that the circumstances of conception matter. A woman pregnant from rape is in a categorically different position from one who had unprotected sex while not wanting a child, and I think that difference is morally real, not just an emotional residue. In the second case I'd say she took on a special responsibility for a needy situation she herself created, and her claim to be free of the burden is weaker. I don't think of it as punishing carelessness — it feels like the same reason a parent has duties toward a child she created that a random bystander lacks.

(4) On the legal side, I'd vote to ban elective third-trimester abortions while leaving the first two trimesters free of restriction, and I don't feel the slightest awkwardness about drawing a legal line at viability even after everything I said in (1) — legal systems need lines, and rough ones have always done the job.

So: is this a consistant package? My friend says at least two of the four are fighting each other but she can't or won't say which, and honestly, laid out like this, I can't see it myself. Please go through it properly, in this order:

- "Summary" — two or three sentences on whether the set hangs together.
- "The real tensions" — ordered worst first, one short paragraph each, saying exactly which commitments clash and why.
- "Tensions that only look real" — anything that seems to conflict but dissolves on inspection; if there are none, say so rather than padding.
- "The cheapest fix, ranked" — for each real tension, what I'd have to give up and what that costs me elsewhere, then which single revision would clean up the most.
- "Verdict for the group" — three or four sentences I could more or less read out loud at the meeting.

Don't go easy on me because the topic is touchy, and don't relitigate whether abortion is wrong in general — I've told you exactly what I believe; the question is whether it all fits together.
