The moderation policy of the platform I work for says a post is removed for "stating or implying" prohibited content — harassment, threats, dehumanizing stereotypes — and in two years nobody on my team has been told what "implying" is supposed to mean. I review escalated reports: a few dozen a week, about ten minutes each, with my written reasoning attached and appeals going to a senior reviewer who can overturn me. An increasing share of the reports don't claim the poster asserted anything. They claim the post communicated it anyway: a "question" everyone reads as a threat, a compliment everyone reads as a slur-adjacent dig, a banned slogan deployed as a joke that half the audience takes straight.

I've read enough to believe the Gricean framework is the right tool here. Readers assume a cooperative author, and when a post conspicuously fails one of the cooperative expectations — truthfulness, adequate informativeness, relevance, clarity — the audience works out what the author must have meant for the post to make sense at all, and that recovered content is the implicature. What I need is that theory turned into a procedure a reviewer with no background in philosophy can run in ten minutes on a real post under appeal.

The procedure should settle, in order, the things we currently argue about:

1. Said versus implied. What the post's words commit the author to — including, I'm told, content carried by the wording itself, by words like "but," "even," "still," which can commit the author to something over and above the literal claim — versus what the author meant beyond the words. For removal decisions we care about implicated content only when it is the author's, not something an uncharitable reader projected onto her.

2. The cancelability line. As I understand it, an implicature can be denied without contradiction — "she'll do great — not that I think she's qualified, I just mean I like her" — whereas what is said cannot be denied without plain contradiction. I want this as a test the reviewer applies, and I want to know where it fails. My guesses: sarcasm, and authors whose denial is technically consistent with their words but whom no reasonable reader believes.

3. Audience-relative meaning. Some posts implicate one thing to readers who share background knowledge and something anodyne to everyone else — in-jokes, code words, threads where the violating content is visible only to its target. When the content is audience-relative like this, is it the author's meaning, the audience's inference, or neither? And which of those does our policy have to be about for the word "implying" to pick out anything determinate?

So: give me the questions to ask about a post, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure returns a no-remove; and where it stops and hands the case to the senior reviewer because what remains is a judgment about our community rather than a fact about the post. Add a short set of test posts a new reviewer could practice on. Then run the procedure on three cases of the kind we actually see:

A. In a thread about a new housing development, a user writes: "I'm sure the longtime residents will be thrilled about who's moving in." Reported as implying that the newcomers are an undesirable class of people. Asked, the author says she meant the residents hate density and traffic, and points out that the sentence says nothing about any group.

B. A user with a months-long history of hostility toward a particular streamer posts during her broadcast: "Wouldn't it be a shame if her home address stopped being a secret." The words are a question about a hypothetical; the report calls it an implied threat.

C. A user recycles a slogan associated in our region with a banned movement, framed as a joke about shipping costs — something on the order of: they can pry the free shipping from our cold dead wallets. Users who read it straight filed the report; the author's friends insist the ironic framing is obvious to anyone who follows him.

At least one of the three should come out in a way I won't like, and you should say so plainly rather than tuning the procedure to my instincts. At the end, tell me how confident you are in the procedure as a whole, name the step you would trust least and why, and be explicit about what the procedure cannot do: where it will misfire, and which decisions it can only route to a human being rather than make.
