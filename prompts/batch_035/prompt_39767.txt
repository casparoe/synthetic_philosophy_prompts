Background: I'm 71, spent my whole career as an evolutionary biologist (mostly working on decapod morphology, crabs and their relatives, so bear with me), and since retiring I've gone down the philosophy rabbit hole. Reading about philosophy of history lately and I've drafted the notes below for a short essay. I'd like your comments — not a rewrite, comments.

The idea: everyone who argues about whether history was "locked in" or whether the same outcomes would've arrived anyway seems to talk past each other, and I think I see why, and it's something I can explain with the math I used my whole career.

1. Gould famously said that if you rewound the tape of life back to the Cambrian and played it again, you'd get a completely different world — contingency all the way down. His Burgess Shale stuff. The counterattack (Conway Morris and others) was convergence: eyes evolved independently dozens of times, streamlined body plans keep showing up in the ocean, and my own favorite example, carcinization — the fact that crab-like body plans evolved separately in several lineages of anomurans, critters I spent decades on. If the tape keeps producing the same shapes, how contingent can it be?

2. Here's my claim: both sides are conflating two different objects. Convergence tells you about the *geometry of the attractor landscape* — where the stable states are. Contingency tells you about *which trajectory you're on*. These are simply different questions. A trait can be a deep attractor and still never be reached, if no historical path ever wanders into its basin. The attractor doesn't reach out and grab you; the path has to come to it. Dynamical systems people would never confuse a phase portrait with a single solution curve, but that's exactly what this debate keeps doing.

3. So when people ask "was industrialization inevitable?", the question decomposes. Maybe industrialization is an attractor (any sufficiently large interconnected society drifts there). That tells you nothing about whether the actual path — the one through 18th-century Britain with cheap coal and specific institutions — was one narrow corridor among many, or whether most rewound tapes never get anywhere near the basin at all. The inevitability people cite the basin, the contingency people cite the path, and nobody notices they're answering different questions.

4. Spicy conclusion I want to land: "would it have happened anyway" is not one question, it's two — one about the landscape and one about the trajectory — and a lot of the philosophy of history literature looks incoherent to me because it treats them as one.
