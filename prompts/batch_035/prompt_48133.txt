I'm working on an essay about the problem of logical omniscience in epistemic logic and I want comments on the draft before I commit to writing the back half. First the setup, then what I want from you.

The essay's thesis, roughly: the standard statement of the problem conflates two different complaints about the ideal agent, and separating them changes what a solution would even look like.

The usual story: epistemic logic has closure under known implication — if the agent knows phi, and knows phi entails psi, then the agent knows psi. Iterated, this makes the agent logically omniscient: it knows every consequence of what it knows, including every tautology and every valid formula of the system. Real agents obviously don't, so (the story goes) epistemic logic can't be a logic of real knowers.

My claim is that this bundles together two complaints that should be pulled apart. Complaint one: the agent knows too much. This is an idealization like the frictionless plane — harmless in principle, as long as there's some story about de-idealizing. Complaint two: the agent knows everything *immediately*, with no derivation labor. My hunch is that almost all of the paradoxical flavor of the problem — the reason it *feels* like a contradiction rather than just a simplification — comes from complaint two, but that the literature mostly argues about complaint one.

Once you see that, the fix I propose for the essay is this: model an agent with a derivation budget n. Its knowledge set is closed only under consequences derivable in at most n steps of some fixed proof system. n at infinity gives you back the ideal agent, tiny n gives you something like the awareness-style frameworks people already use, and in between you get a spectrum, which I think is the interesting part. Two claims I want to defend: (a) most of the puzzle cases dissolve in a boring way once you have budgets — the agent doesn't fail to know the hard tautology for some mysterious reason, it fails because that tautology costs more than its budget; (b) the budget framing lets you ask quantitative questions the binary framing can't even express, e.g., how large does a budget have to be before backward induction arguments in games become applicable to the agents in question.

The worry I'm stuck on: to know whether it knows something, a budgeted agent seemingly needs to know the length of the shortest derivation — and knowing *that* looks like it requires an idealized meta-level, so the omniscience just gets pushed up a floor rather than removed.

So, comments please, in this order:

1. Does the two-complaints split hold up, or is immediate-knowing not actually separable from knowing-too-much in the formalism? If you think the split collapses, say why — that would gut the essay.
2. Is the budget idea doing real work, or is it just awareness logic with extra machinery I'd have to justify? Be honest about whether my claim (b) is a genuine advance in expressiveness or a rephrasing.
3. Does the regress worry in my last paragraph actually bite, and is there a standard response to that kind of move that I'd be embarrassed to not know about?
4. Where in my presentation of the "standard story" am I saying something that's actually contested rather than settled? I want to know specifically which bits people who work on this would push back on, not just a general hedge — and in your comments, please keep track of what's genuinely agreed versus genuinely disputed in this area, and flag any spot where I seem to be passing off a contested reading as the consensus one. I'd rather fix that now than in review.

Don't worry about giving me exact references for anything — describe positions and moves in your own words, I can chase citations later. If you think some version of the budget idea has already been done to death somewhere, just tell me roughly what it looks like and how mine differs (if it does).
