So heres my situation. I'm a science-and-ideas journalist, and I'm writing a piece about panpsychism that actually gets past the part everyone quotes (conciousness in everything, ha ha, rocks) and into the part philosophers themselves treat as the real obstacle: the combination problem. My editor has one rule for this piece — every abstract point has to ride on a concrete case, no floating thought experiments that only work if you've already read five papers of background.

Here's what I need: one thought experiment, built to spec.

The case has to do this. Take two things that are uncontroversially subjects — ideally the two hemispheres of one person's brain, on the model of split-brain patients — and vary exactly one thing: the degree of communication between them. The dial is the corpus callosum, and I want to run it from fully severed, through partial connection, to fully intact. No sci-fi: callosotomy is real surgery, partial sections happen, and gradual recovery of the connection happens too. Everything else stays fixed — the neurons, the local dynamics, the person's memories, their abilities. And here's the crucial constraint: the case can't smuggle in any assumption about which configurations are conscious. The setup has to generate the trouble, not stipulate it. If your case only works because you whispered "and obviously each isolated hemisphere is a subject of its own," that's cheating, and worse, my readers will catch it.

So: describe the scenario in one paragraph, plain enough that a reader who has never heard the word panpsychism can follow it. Then walk me through three stations.

1. What the constitutive panpsychist has to say at each setting of the dial — how many subjects there are, where the new one comes from, where the old one goes.
2. What the emergentist says, why this case is supposed to be easier for them, and whether it actually is.
3. The trap I'm actually trying to spring in the article. The case should force a dilemma: either there's a sharp threshold in the degree of communication, below which there are two subjects and above which there is one — which looks like magic, and I want you to tell me which feature of the case, if any, makes the threshold look non-arbitrary — or there's no fact of the matter about how many subjects there are in the middle of the dial. And my angle is this: if it's the second horn, then the combination problem was never really about combining micro-experiences into macro ones. It's the boundary problem (some call it the decombination or subject-summing problem) wearing a different hat — the hard part isn't getting little feelings to add up, it's that nobody can say what counts as one feeler.

Constraints on your answer: no demons, no perfect predictors, and no stipulated consciousness-detecting devices — a scanner that "shows" consciousness in a disconnected hemisphere is exactly the smuggling I'm banning. Don't lean on real patient names or specific papers either; my fact-checker owns that side, and I don't want you guessing at citations.

Finish by telling me straight: does the case actually meet the spec, where is it softest, and what would a panpsychist who thinks the combination problem is overstated say to try to defuse it? I need to know that before I file, not after.

(Also if you can keep the whole thing under ~700 words that helps — the draft section is budgeted at 900 and I need room for the the obligatory "Galileo of panpsychism" joke.)
