I'm on the advisory board of a mid-sized Holocaust museum, where I mostly handle the boring stuff (I'm the one with the nonprofit accounting background), and we're stuck on one gallery in the permanent exhibit redesign. One curator wants the gallery to argue, roughly along Zygmunt Bauman's lines, that the Holocaust was not some breakdown of civilization but a product of modernity itself — bureaucracy, the division of labor, instrumental rationality, railways running on schedule — with a wall panel drawing out how an ordinary modern state apparatus made the machinery of extermination possible. A second curator thinks this flattens the Holocaust into just one instance of a generic modern phenomenon, and gets uneasy because she hears an echo of the Historikerstreit in it: Nolte was ruined for less, and she worries that pushed far enough the "product of modernity" line ends up doing the same excusing work, making the perpetrators look like cogs anyone could have been. A third curator wants an explicit comparison panel on colonial genocides, especially the Herero and Nama war, on the theory that visitors can't evaluate a claim of uniqueness without seeing what the nearby alternatives actually look like. The current draft panel text says the Holocaust "could not have occurred without the administrative machinery of the modern state," and honestly half the board can't agree on whether that sentence is a defensible claim or a provocation.

I also suspect the three of them keep talking past each other because "unique" is doing two different jobs in our meetings: sometimes they mean nothing else is comparable to it in any respect, sometimes they mean nothing else was the same in kind. Getting that distinction on the table feels like it might dissolve part of the fight, but I'm not sure how much of it.

Please draft the written recommendation I'll circulate before the board meeting: a concrete decision about what the gallery should claim, what the comparative panel (if we keep one) does and does not assert, and how the framing relates to the uniqueness debate — with reasoning each curator could actually engage with rather than a verdict from on high, and a fair treatment of the strongest point on the losing side. Since I'm circulating this to people who know this literature far better than I do, I need you to say how confident you are in the main claims, and flag the single point you'd least want to defend to a room full of historians. Keep it under two pages.
