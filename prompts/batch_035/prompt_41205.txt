I write a column on science and ideas for a general readership, and I'm at work on a piece about the quiet moralization of everyday conventions. In the United States, servers depend on tips and failing to tip is treated as a genuine wrong rather than a lapse in taste; the pre-set tip screens now common at coffee-shop counters, which suggest twenty or twenty-five percent at the turn of a tablet, show how quickly a norm can be engineered by companies and how quickly customers begin to feel they are doing something bad in declining it. In Japan, by contrast, tipping is not merely optional but can cause embarrassment or even read as an insult. Britain treats queue-jumping as a serious breach of decorum while jaywalking is broadly legal there; Germany treats crossing against a red pedestrian light as seriously wrong even on an empty street in the middle of the night, and strangers will scold you for it. Guidebooks, travel writers, and most of us tell visitors that they ought to follow the local conventions — not that it is merely prudent or convenient, but that it is somehow owed.

What view of moral obligation does this whole practice presuppose? I can see how a local's obligation might be explained on a conventionalist theory: the duty arises from participation in a mutually reinforcing system of expectations, the way the conventions of a language bind those who have grown up speaking it. But visitors never opted in; they arrive as adults with fully formed habits from another system, sometimes one that directly conflicts — the American who must suppress the tipping reflex in Osaka, or the Berliner who must override the reflex to wait at a deserted red light while visiting London. So does the when-in-Rome norm presuppose that obligations can arise unilaterally, from benefiting from everyone else's compliance? That one acquires duties simply by physical presence in a community of expectation? That it's a matter of not treating locals as means to one's own convenience? And is the practice internally consistent at all, given that we simultaneously insist these conventions are arbitrary — we tell travelers on both sides of the Atlantic not to moralize about each other's tipping habits — and yet treat violations by visitors as real wrongs, not just faux pas?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory as best you can, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined — that is, which rival accounts of convention and obligation would license the very same pattern of attitudes. Then tell me what would have to change, in how we instruct travelers or in how the tipping screens themselves are framed, if we replaced the implicit view with a purely instrumental one, on which etiquette is nothing more than a tool for reducing friction. As you go, indicate how confident you are in each major claim, and single out the point you are least sure of — I would rather know where the reconstruction gets shaky than receive a seamless account.
