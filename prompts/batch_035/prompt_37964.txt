Longtime LessWrong/EA Forum reader here — been around since the Sequences era — which means FTX hit my corner of the internet about as hard as anything could, and I've read my share of the post-mortems (the EV-reasoning takes, the deferring-to-impressive-people takes, the incentive-gradient takes). But it only recently struck me that the oldest version of this question is sitting right there in Republic Book II, and almost nobody in those threads reached for it: Glaucon's challenge and the ring of Gyges. Glaucon wants Socrates to prove that justice belongs in the class of goods worth having for their own sake, not merely for their consequences, and he sets the bar with the ring: an invisible man, immune to consequence, would behave however he actually wants, so prove the just man with the ring stays just anyway.

Run the FTX collapse through that. Be disciplined about the mapping: who plays the Gyges role — SBF specifically, or the whole structure (Bahamas incorporation, a lightly regulated exchange, customer deposits quietly shunted over to Alameda)? What exactly was the ring, i.e., what made him effectively invisible — the absence of regulators, or something more interesting, the EA status network that functioned as a substitute reputation he could spend? What does "being seen" even mean when your peer group has a norm of taking impressive people's stated commitments at face value? And what is the "justice" whose loss the story is about — the fiduciary duty to customers, something stronger, something weaker?

Then take seriously the part where the mapping strains, because it does, hard. Glaucon's ring-bearer uses impunity to grab ordinary goods — the queen, the kingdom. SBF's stated project was maximally not that: earning to give, pandemic preparedness, and if you take his self-description even slightly seriously, personal luxury was barely the point. So is he a confirmation of Glaucon — people are only just under compulsion, he had the compulsion removed, and there you go — or is he a case Glaucon never anticipated: someone who agrees with Glaucon's own metaethics, that justice is purely instrumental, and simply acted on it consistently? That's the bit that spooks me about my own community. Glaucon's challenge is supposed to be answered by showing justice is intrinsically good, and the effective-altruist critique of common-sense morality is... suspiciously close to Glaucon's premise, just with the sign flipped.

A couple more things I want handled. The ring only came off because of a market crash — absent the run, the hole presumably never becomes visible, he maybe pays everyone back, and he's a hero who funded bednets. Does that contingency do anything to the the analysis, or is it just Glaucon's point about reputation in modern dress? And where is Glaucon simply silent here — his setup has nothing to say about a man who launders injustice as long-run justice and gives the proceeds away.

Dont give me reading lists, I've read them. I want the actual case analysis, run in both directions, and I want you to be honest about whether the ancient frame earns its keep or whether the modern post-mortem frame (incentives, expected value, status games) just does the job better.
