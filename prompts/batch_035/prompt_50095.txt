I am putting together a critical survey of the debate over origin essentialism in the metaphysics of personal identity — the thesis that a particular person could not have originated from a different pair of gametes than the pair from which that person actually originated, so that a slightly different sperm fertilizing the same egg would have produced someone else, not you in different circumstances. Please rank the following six considerations into tiers according to how dialectically strong you judge them to be (use whatever tier scheme you like, but make it explicit), give a short justification for each placement, and close with a note on which single consideration an opponent of origin essentialism should take most seriously.

1. The sibling intuition, often associated with Saul Kripke's remarks on necessity: if a different sperm from the same father had fertilized your mother's egg, the natural description of the resulting person is a sibling of yours rather than you, which suggests the actual gametes are somehow essential to you.

2. The counterpart-theoretic deflation: claims about what you could have been are settled by which counterpart relations are relevant in the context, so the "necessity of origin" reflects a somewhat arbitrary choice of counterpart relation rather than a deep metaphysical fact about persons.

3. A gradual-modification sorites argument I want to press: suppose geneticists intercept the zygote immediately after fertilization and alter its genome one base pair at a time across successive possible scenarios. The opponent of origin essentialism seems to owe us a sharp cutoff — the last modified genome that still yields you — which is implausible; the essentialist can say that every altered genome yields someone else, but at the cost of denying that there is any nearby possible person who is very much like you except for one pre-conception nucleotide change. Does the sorites pressure favor essentialism, mere vague identity, or neither?

4. The granularity problem posed by mitochondrial replacement ("three-parent") techniques: a child may have nuclear DNA from two gamete providers and mitochondrial DNA from a third woman's donated cytoplasm. If origins are essential, it is unclear whether the essential origin is the nuclear pair alone, the cytoplasmic contribution, or some larger whole — and disagreement about the granularity seems to undercut the idea that there is a determinate fact of origin to be essential to.

5. The monozygotic twin puzzle: identical twins originate from the very same zygote and, a fortiori, the same gamete pair. If the twins could have swapped places in the sense that each could have developed from the other's portion of the split embryo, then the gamete pair does not by itself individuate persons; if they could not, then persons have modal features over and above their origins, and origin essentialism is not the whole story about what makes someone who they are.

6. The parity argument from post-origin replacement: over roughly a decade, nearly every molecule in your body is replaced, yet you plainly persist through that turnover. The opponent of origin essentialism asks why the pre-zygotic material that gave rise to you could not have been similarly, gradually replaced just before and during conception, so that you could have come, at the margin, from partly different material.

Please be explicit about your tier assignments, keep each justification to a few sentences, and treat the considerations as interacting where appropriate (for example, where the gradual-modification argument in item 3 and the replacement argument in item 6 pull in opposite directions).
