I'm a PhD student in philosophy, mostly formal epistemology adjacent, and this semester I'm TAing a course where we spend the last two weeks on Sleeping Beauty and self-locating belief. The students have read the classic halfer/thirder exchanges but what I keep noticing in section is that they basically pick a team by vibes and then can't say what actually separates the teams. So what I want is a diagnostic handout: a decision tree that takes a student from the plain setup (fair coin Sunday night; Monday awakening either way; on tails a Tuesday awakening too, with the amnesia drug in between) to a named position, where every branch node is a question about concrete cases that someone could answer without knowing which leaf it feeds into. The point is to sort them by what they say about cases, not which label they like.

Some cases I want in the tree, since I think these are where the positions genuinely come apart: (1) the experimenter truthfully tells Beauty it's Monday — does her credence in heads move, and if not, what happens to standard conditioning when she later learns something she was pretty sure she'd learn anyway? (2) she's told it's Tuesday, where everyone agrees heads is out — so ask what happened there, did she gain information, or (as at least one classic halfer move has it) lose some, and whether that's even coherent; (3) a mirrored version where heads gets two awakenings and tails one, because I want to check whether people are answering about the coin or just about awakening-counts; (4) a version where tails leads to, say, six awakenings instead of two, since I privately suspect some halfers are really reacting to two feeling close enough to one that it seems like no new info; and (5) a card variant where the room is red on Monday and blue on Tuesday under tails, but under heads there's a single awakening with a randomly chosen color — I forget who this one is due to and don't care, describe it fresh, it's a good probe of how people handle self-locating vs ordinary evidence when they're tangled together.

Leaves should at minimum include the halfer, the thirder, the double-halfer (happy to be corrected if that label or its attribution is off), some version of the view that the problem is ill-posed or that there's no determinate fact of the matter, and the view that it's secretly a decision problem about betting odds rather than an epistemic one at all. Where the tree genuinely can't separate two leaves — e.g. whether a plain halfer and a double-halfer come out different anywhere except on the day-revelation cases — flag it rather than inventing a fake branch. I also want you to say, per branch question, how confident you are that it actually carves the joint and doesn't collapse under a student pushing back, and single out the part of the tree you'd trust least. And if some leaf is only there because somebody once wrote a paper defending it rather than because the cases force it, be upfront about that. Keep it to something that fits on two sides of a page.
