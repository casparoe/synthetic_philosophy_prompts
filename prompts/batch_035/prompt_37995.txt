I do one-on-one advising for people standing at big life forks — mostly whether to have a first kid, whether to emigrate, whether to leave a stable career for something unknown. My background is coaching, not philosophy, but a philosopher friend told me about the idea of transformative experiences: choices where you can't know what the outcome will be like from the inside until you've actually had it, so the normal picture of rationally weighing options kind of breaks down, since the weighing is supposed to be done by a "you" who can project forward and feel out each outcome, and for these choices that self doesn't exist yet — the experience creates the person who'd have the opinion. That matches what I see. Clients who've read every testimonial on the internet and are still stuck, because the parents saying "it's wonderful, I couldn't imagine my life without them" and the childfree-by-choice folks saying "best decision I ever made" are both reporting from the far side of a change they can't undo, and its not obvious their testimony counts as evidence about my client's future at all. So build me a decision procedure I can actually run over a session or two: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a real answer to each one, where the procedure should stop and hand things back to the client because it's a values question that's hers to settle and not mine, and — this is the part I care most about — what to do with testimony from people who've already had the experience, since that's normally my best tool and it's exactly the one this theory poisons. Be honest about where the procedure will misfire, e.g. when the client has already basically decided and is using me for permission. Then run it on three clients: a 34-year-old deciding whether to have a first child, a mid-career accountant who wants to quit and retrain as a paramedic, and a woman deciding whether to leave the religious community she grew up in. At least one of the three should come out with the procedure's honest output being "there's no answer to be had here, only the choice" — even though that's the last thing that client wants to hear from me.
