I've been having the same argument with my friend Priya for the better part of a decade — a standing fixture of our rationalist meetup, in the way that definitional disputes tend to become standing fixtures on the forums. The subject is democratic backsliding: she has crisp verdicts on whether a given country counts, and I've come to suspect she's running two or three different rules under one label, while I keep telling her to taboo the term and she keeps replying that some terms are load-bearing and earn their keep. What I want is a prediction about her next verdict, and since she refuses to state operationalized criteria ("I'm not pre-registering my priors so you can score me," she says), you'll have to reverse-engineer them from her track record.

The track record, as faithfully as I can reconstruct it:

- Hungary under Orbán after 2010: yes, backsliding. Fidesz took a two-thirds supermajority, rewrote the constitution, packed the Constitutional Court, and put loyalists on the media authority.
- Poland under PiS from 2015: yes. They captured the Constitutional Tribunal first, then went after the ordinary courts and public broadcasters.
- Turkey, especially from the 2017 constitutional referendum onward: yes.
- Venezuela from Chávez on: yes.
- The US during Trump's first term: no. Her reasoning was that the referees held, and she wants the term reserved for cases where they go.
- New Zealand and Australia during the pandemic: no. Emergency powers, honestly accounted for, mostly handed back when the emergency passed — ugly, but not backsliding.
- South Korea in December 2024, when Yoon declared martial law and the National Assembly voted it down within hours: no. A failed burglary, she said, doesn't change the neighborhood's burglary rate — and he was impeached.

The new case is India under Modi through the present: governors in opposition-run states sitting indefinitely on duly passed bills; the Enforcement Directorate's corruption cases landing overwhelmingly on opposition politicians; the 2023 law that replaced the Chief Justice of India with a government minister on the committee selecting election commissioners; and yet elections at the national level remain genuinely competitive — the BJP lost its outright majority in 2024 and had to govern in coalition, and opposition parties keep winning and holding states.

So: will she say India is backsliding? I care less about the yes-or-no than about what rules actually fit every verdict above, which of those rules disagree about India, and which further case would best tell the rules apart. She's threatening to weigh in next on Israel's 2023 judicial overhaul, and possibly on Mexico's switch to electing its judges, so feel free to consider those as candidate discriminators alongside anything else you'd propose.

Please structure your reply as follows:

4. "The discriminating case" (roughly 100 words) — the single further case that would most cleanly split the candidate rules, and what each rule would say about it.
