In my reading group on David Lewis's modal realism, one member keeps defending it by analogy with the physicists' multiverse: serious cosmologists posit countless other universes that we can never causally reach, without anyone accusing them of medieval metaphysics, so Lewis's vast plurality of causally isolated concrete worlds deserves the same courtesy — the stare of incredulity Lewis reports receiving is, on this telling, just physics envy, and his ontology is a scientific commonplace in philosophy-of-language dress. Please audit the analogy. First lay out the correspondence: what in the multiverse case plays the role of a possible world, of actuality, of isolation between worlds, and of the inference from theoretical virtue to existence. Then show where the mapping breaks — for example, on whether the other universes are alternate ways our world could have been or merely other regions of all there is, and on whether the physicists' posits are meant to do the job Lewis built his worlds to do, namely grounding modal notions like possibility and counterfactual dependence. Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the intended use — making Lewis's ontology scientifically respectable — from those that don't, and say whether the respectability conclusion survives once they're on the table, or whether the analogy was only ever carrying it rhetorically. If there is a better analogy available for what Lewis's worlds are like, say what it is and what it gets right that the multiverse comparison misses.
