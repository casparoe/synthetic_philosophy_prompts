For a while now I've been sorting evolutionary-psychology claims into "I believe it" and "that's a just-so story" by gut, and I'd like to know whether my gut is tracking a principle that could actually be stated. My verdicts so far: I believe fear of snakes is evolved — people and lab-reared monkeys acquire fear of snakes far more readily than fear of guns or electrical outlets, and lab-reared monkeys learn it from a single viewing of another monkey reacting fearfully to a snake but not to a rabbit, an asymmetry that seems too systematic to be culture. I believe the cheater-detection finding: people who fail the abstract Wason selection task solve it when the cards are social contracts and violators are cheaters. I believe the finding that men's jealousy is triggered more by sexual infidelity and women's more by emotional infidelity, roughly as parental-investment reasoning predicted in advance. But I don't believe the theory that morning sickness is an embryo-protecting adaptation steering mothers away from teratogens. I don't believe a waist-to-hip ratio of about 0.7 is a cross-cultural constant of male preference. And I don't believe people choose mates by smell in order to get immunologically unlike offspring.

The pair I can't reconcile is the snake fear and the morning sickness. Both were devised after the facts, both are stories about the ancestral environment, and both are backed by a scattering of supporting observations — the morning-sickness theory's defender really did assemble material on which foods carry teratogens and on when in pregnancy the nausea peaks and subsides. Yet my gut acquits the snake story and condemns the pregnancy one, and I can't say why.

So what principle captures these verdicts? Candidates I half see: that the claim must be backed by an unexpected finding in a neighbouring domain (the cheater-detection result might be that); that the story must identify a mechanism, not just a function; that the claim must not be explainable as cultural transmission (which, worryingly, would threaten my snake verdict, since a snake-fearing parent is a very good teacher). State the principle, or the small set of candidates, as precisely as you can; say which of my verdicts it cannot save and which should be overturned; and tell me what it predicts about two cases I haven't decided: whether depression is best understood as an adaptation for analytical rumination, and whether moral disgust began as a parasite-avoidance system later co-opted for ethics.
