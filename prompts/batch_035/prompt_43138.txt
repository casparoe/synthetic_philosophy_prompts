ok so bit of background: I teach an upper-level seminar on philosophy of technology at a state school, and I've been around the rationalist blogosphere long enough to remember when superintelligence arguments were mostly a LessWrong thing before they went mainstream and got weird about it. now I have to write the final.

I want ONE essay question on the case for and against AI existential risk, 90 minutes in class, and I'm begging you: make it unanswerable by reciting the litany. You know the litany — orthogonality thesis, instrumental convergence, recursive self-improvement, the bit about the AI not hating you but your atoms being useful for something else. Any student who's read one summary can barf that back at me. What I actually want to test is whether they can see the dependency structure of the argument — which premises are load-bearing and what survives when you knock each one out individually.

so build the question around counterfactual knockouts. like: suppose the orthogonality thesis is false — suppose intelligence and the right values come packaged together somehow, so a sufficiently smart system just couldn't be a paperclipper. Does the case for x-risk collapse, or does it limp along some other route (competitive dynamics between individually-fine systems, lock-in of values that looked good enough at deployment time, the steering problem where nobody can steer the joint human-machine system even when every piece is benign)? and then the mirror image: suppose orthogonality holds but instrumental convergence is wrong — what's left of the case then, and is what's left enough to still worry about?

after the question, give me a model answer around 600 words and a marking scheme that seperates an A from a B by naming the specific moves each has to contain. my prediction is the B students write "if orthogonality is false the risk mostly goes away" and stop there, and the A students see why that's way too fast — but encode that into the scheme explicitly so my graders actually enforce it.
