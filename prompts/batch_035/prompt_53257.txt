I run scoring for a small non-profit that runs long-horizon forecasting tournaments — we commission predictions 20 to 40 years out (food security, energy transitions, AI diffusion, demographics) and then have to grade them later. The recurring fight on our grading calls: a prediction missed its date, and the forecaster, or their fans, insist it was "right but early," directionally correct with the wrong timeline. Somebody always brings up Malthus — he looked wrong for 200 years, and then every generation gets told we're finally about to be Malthusian again. We have no principled way to grade these, and right now whoever argues longest wins the call.

I want you to take the role of a philosopher of science who also knows the forecasting world. Think carefully about this before committing to anything — maybe brainstorm the failure modes of the obvious approaches first — and then write me a grading procedure my panel can actually run on a contested call. Ground it in what falsificationism actually says about this: the part where rescuing a prediction by adjusting auxiliary assumptions can be legitimate science or degenerating ad-hoccery, and the part where it's just protecting the theorist. There's also, I think it goes back to Popper but you tell me, the observation that a prediction whose mere issuance triggers a response that averts it — Malthus arguably scared people into agricultural reform — is doing something different from ordinary prediction, so the rubric needs a slot for self-defeating versus self-fulfilling, even though we can rarely observe which one happened.

The procedure should specify: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the points where it outputs a hard grade-it-wrong and stops; and the points where it has to hand off to the panel's judgment because the question is really about values (e.g., how much of "the world changed in ways the forecaster couldn't have known" counts as an excuse, which is partly a policy choice about what we want forecasting to be, not a fact). Then run it on three contested calls from our archive, roughly these:

1. A 1995-era prediction that global grain demand would outrun supply before 2010, with attendant famine risk.
2. A prediction from the 1980s that a majority of jobs in rich countries would be done by machines by 2025.
3. A 2001 prediction that solar would become the cheapest source of new electricity "within two decades" — which actually landed around 2020, so call it nineteen years.

Number 3 is the awkward one for us. The panel very much wants to grade it as a clean hit, and I suspect the procedure honestly applied won't fully support that, so tell me where the procedure bends, if it does, and whether bending it there opens the door to the same excuse on calls 1 and 2.

Finally, be honest about what the procedure can't do: which kinds of rescue attempts we will still be fighting about in ten years no matter how good the rubric gets, and whether there's a principled ceiling on how much "right but early" grading a tournament like ours can tolerate before it stops measuring forecasters and starts measuring our own sympathy. Don't give me a reading list — I want the reasoning, not the bibliography. Usual format for our internal docs: short intro, then the procedure as numbered steps, then the three worked cases, then a closing section on limits. Keep it tight enough that a panel member actually reads it before a call.
