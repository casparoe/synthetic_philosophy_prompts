I'm preparing for a viva on science and theology in the long seventeenth century, and the examiners are known for pressing on exactly one point: whether Newton's prophetic and anti-Trinitarian theology was an application of his method in natural philosophy or a departure from it. Textbook summaries are useless for this, so rather than rehearse against a caricature, I want to practise against the strongest version of the man himself. Please play Isaac Newton for me.

Play him in the first person, in the early decades of the eighteenth century: the Principia is published, your work on prophecy is well advanced, and you may treat your unpublished theological manuscripts, including your exchange with Locke on the corruptions of scripture, as things you have written and hold. You hold, as you actually did, that the Son is a created being subordinate to the Father, that Trinitarianism is a fourth-century innovation imposed by councils that had no authority to impose it, and that the prophecies of Daniel and Revelation constitute a genuine history written in advance, decodable by fixed rules and patient comparison of scripture with scripture, in the same spirit as your experimental philosophy. You are not a zealot: you may concede that particular interpreters before you went astray, that a chronology needs revision, or that a copying error is real. But you will not concede that the Son is co-equal with the Father, that ecclesiastical tradition can overrule the plain text, or that prophecy is undecipherable. If I introduce anything anachronistic, ask about it as Newton would; do not break character until I say 'stop'; keep each reply to roughly a paragraph.

Here is my opening move. In natural philosophy you will admit nothing that is not evidenced, and you despise the Cartesians for feigning hypotheses to cover the gaps. Yet your prophetic method looks like it runs in the opposite direction. Your rules for the emblematic language are elastic enough that the fourth beast of Daniel can be made to fit whichever empire your chronology requires; and where the plain text contradicts your anti-Trinitarianism — above all in the Johannine witness passage and the verse on the manifestation in the flesh — you argue, in your letters to Locke, that copyists corrupted the text precisely there. So scripture is inerrant where it suits your doctrine and corrupt where it does not, which is the very double standard you lay at the door of the Schoolmen. Defend the method: what, exactly, distinguishes your fixed rules for decoding prophecy from a hypothesis feigned to save the phenomenon?
