so I'm in a psych PhD program and this semester our required quantitative methods course has been basically one long unit on the replication crisis. Here's the list I've come up with, and I want to know whether it's a consistent package or whether some of these are quietly pulling against each other.

(1) I'm a Bayesian about rational belief in the straightforward sense: credences that obey the probability axioms, updating by conditioning, and I think that's just the correct story of how an ideal reasoner handles uncertainty. (2) I also buy the likelihood principle: given a dataset, everything it evidentially says about the hypotheses is contained in the likelihood function, so things like the experimenter's stopping rule don't change what the data mean. (3) I think the replication crisis is mostly not fraud — it's researcher degrees of freedom plus underpowered studies — and preregistration with pre-specified analysis plans and stopping rules is the single best fix anyone has proposed. (4) For journals, though, I'd actually keep frequentist reporting (pre-specified p-values at the usual alpha) rather than switch everything over to Bayes factors, because a journal is basically a filter, and what matters is the long-run error properties of the filter, not any one researcher's posterior. (5) When a replication fails, how much I downgrade the original finding depends alot on whether the original study was preregistered — same replication data, different verdict depending on that one fact about the original. (6) And I'm against lowering the threshold to .005; keeping .05 and fixing the incentive structure through preregistration is the better route.

A couple of these feel slightly off together, but I can't tell whether that's a real tension or just me being tired.
