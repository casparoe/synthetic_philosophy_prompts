My old friend from grad school — a logician, now retired, and absolutely insufferable about it — spent a long weekend at my place walking me through Gödel's ontological argument, the one from his notes with the positive properties. I teach phil of religion most springs, so I figured I'd be able to poke holes. Instead he walked me into a wall. He reconstructed it like this:

(1) For any property, either it or its negation is positive, and never both.
(2) Whatever is necessarily entailed by a positive property is itself positive.
(3) Necessary existence is positive.
(4) Positivity is no accident: if a property is positive, it's necessarily positive.
(5) From (1)-(4), in S5, there necessarily exists a being having every positive property essentially.

He insists the derivation is airtight — claims poeple have even verified it with automated theorem provers, which honestly sounds like exactly the thing somebody would do — and each axiom did look innocent when he said it: (1) just says value is everywhere defined, (2) says excellence can't entail shabbiness, (3) and (4) are the ontological-argumenty bits. Fine. Here's my problem. While he was napping I kept playing with the axioms and got this out of them:

(6) Take any truth whatever — say, that I had coffee this morning. Consider the property that everything has if I had coffee, and nothing has otherwise.
(7) By (1), either that property or its negation is positive.
(8) The being from (5) essentially has every positive property and exists necessarily.
(9) So either it's necessary that I had coffee, or it's impossible. Since I did, it's necessary. And every truth goes the same way.

So the axioms hand me Spinoza: nothing contingent, no open futures, my coffee as fated as the theorems of arithmetic. Dave's response when I showed him: a shrug, and "maybe thats just true — and anyway, doesn't some famous Oxford logician already think everything exists necessarily? How expensive can the neighborhood be?"

I don't want to live there, but I also can't find the crooked step. Tell me which one to give up — (1), (2), (3), (4), or the move from the axioms to (5) — why that axiom looked fine when Dave said it, and what giving it up costs: does rejecting it just break my collapse argument, or does it take the main argument down with it? I'll confess (4) is the one I trust least, though I can't articulate why it should be the culprit. And separately — is Dave's fallback as cheap as he thinks, or is "every truth is necessary" a much stronger claim than "everything exists necessarily"? He'd notice the difference if anyone pointed it out; I'd just like to be the one pointing.
