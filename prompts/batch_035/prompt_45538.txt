I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background -- my exposure to these questions comes from data work and from a career spent on multinational teams. Lately I've been drafted into writing an internal post about "culture fit" and the claims about national character that keep surfacing at work: that engineers from certain countries won't push back in code review, that others treat deadlines as aspirations, that whole offices in one region are consensus-driven while another's are conflict-driven. Some of these claims are obviously lazy stereotyping. But some seem to be backed by real survey data -- the Hofstede-style dimension scores HR keeps circulating, for instance -- and I can't find a clean way to say what separates the legitimate versions from the ugly ones. Before I argue about any particular claim, I want the specification: what an account of national character would actually have to get right to be both coherent and something a decent person could act on.

So write me the desiderata list. To make sure we're in the weeds rather than at the level of "stereotypes bad," here are the candidate requirements I'd want you to evaluate as genuine desiderata or discard as prejudice, plus the ones you think I've missed:

1. It has to settle what national character is a property *of*. To use an analogy from my own work: a codebase can be fragile even though no individual commit is fragile, because fragidity lives at the level of the whole. Can a nation have a character in that distributional or emergent sense -- a trait that literally no member has, or that only exists relative to a population-level statistic -- or does every legitimate claim about national character have to bottom out in claims about individuals?

2. It has to specify persistence conditions. Nations outlive their members many times over, and immigrant-heavy populations turn over within a couple of generations. If national character is real, what is it that stays the same through that turnover -- and if the honest answer is "nothing, it's a moving average," the account should say what work the word "national" is still doing.

3. It has to draw the line on the statistical reading. As someone who works with aggregate data for a living, I know how easy it is for a real distributional difference to be useless or misleading in practice: small mean differences between groups with huge within-group variance, Simpson's-paradox-style reversals when you condition on the right variables, confounding by institutions rather than culture. Does a defensible account need a threshold or a variance condition, or does it just need honesty about effect sizes?

4. It has to handle the normative question separately from the accuracy question. Is a *true* generalization automatically permissible to use -- in a hiring decision, a team assignment, a casual remark -- or are there true things it's still wrong to say or act on? And conversely, are there contexts (epidemiology, cross-cultural UX, diplomacy) where even a rough generalization is fine? My instinct is that permissibility tracks something like the reliability of the inference at the individual level plus the costs of being wrong, but I want to know if that's a real requirement or my engineer's bias.

5. It has to avoid essentialism without becoming vacuous. If the account is so hedged and statistical that it applies to anything whatsoever about any grouping, it arguably hasn't earned the word "character" -- it's just demographics. Is there a formulation that keeps the notion non-essentialist and still substantial enough to be worth having?

For each requirement you keep: the motivating case, why it's a requirement rather than a preference, and whether it's non-negotiable. Then the part I most want: are any pairs on the final list jointly unsatisfiable? In particular, I suspect something like requirement 3 (variance-aware statistics) and requirement 5 (substantial, character-like content) pull against each other -- the more statistically careful the claims get, the less they seem to be about "character" at all -- and if that tension is real, say so plainly, because it may be the actual argument of my post. Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most to rule out the accounts people actually use, from the pop-stereotype version to the survey-dimension version to whatever the sophisticated social-science version is. Don't bother scoring every contender against the full list; the criteria are what I need, and I'll do the applications myself.
