I'm a history PhD student who got roped into running the Q&A for our department's colloquium series, and next month's speaker works on nationalism. Her manuscript chapter argues roughly this: the modernist picture — nations are recent constructions, their "traditions" were consciously invented over the last two and a half centuries — is correct as history, but the debunking tone everyone takes from it is a mistake. On her view, the fact that a tradition has a documented moment of invention, sometimes even a named inventor, is normatively irrelevant: a tradition's authority doesn't depend on being ancient, and knowing something was invented should make us treat it the way we treat other young-but-functioning institutions, like a central bank or a labor union. She apparently also thinks the modernists smuggle in a romantic assumption — that only unbroken, unplanned, organically grown practices are the real thing — and she wants that assumption out in the open and rejected.

She's reportedly great at deflecting normative questions with something like "I'm doing history, take it up with the philosophers," and I want to make that deflection progressively harder to use. Draft me a sequence of ten or so questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. At minimum I want one question pressing on the tartan-kilt and royal-ceremony examples — if the inventors were deliberately passing the thing off as ancient, doesn't the deception matter even if the age doesn't; one on whether "invention" is even a coherent category, since every tradition had a first performance somewhere, and what the difference is supposed to be between inventing a tradition and gradually reshaping one; one raising the objection (I think this one is Anthony Smith's?) that invented national traditions only caught on because they latched onto pre-existing ethnic myths, symbols, and memories, which would make the modernist story parasitic on something much older; and one asking whether her argument generalizes to deliberately invented traditions people dislike — say, a government commission launching a "national dish" next year and promoting it as what the people have always eaten — or whether there's a disanalogy she can point to. Also one question about what evidence would count against the modernist origin story itself, not just against the moral she draws from it. Keep the phrasing conversational enough that I can actually ask it outloud without sounding like I'm reading a journal article at her.
