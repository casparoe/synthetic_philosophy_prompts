I've been arguing about AI takeoff speeds with a housemate who works on ML benchmarks, and he has an argument for a fast, abrupt takeoff that I can feel pushing me around even though I can't quite accept it, because it seems to prove too much. The chain runs as follows. (1) Sometime in the next couple of decades we will plausibly build systems that match or exceed human researchers at every cognitive task involved in AI development — generating hypotheses, designing experiments, writing the code, interpreting the results. (2) Software, unlike people, can be copied: the first system that matches a human researcher gives you, in principle, as many researchers as you have chips to run them on, operating at whatever serial speed the hardware permits. (3) The rate of progress in AI has historically been driven by the effective quantity and quality of research labor applied to it; more capable researchers have meant faster progress. (4) So from the moment the first human-level AI researcher exists, the effective research workforce jumps by orders of magnitude within weeks — you just spin up more copies — and the rate of progress jumps with it. (5) Those improved systems are then even better researchers, the effect compounds, and the transition from "matching humans" to "wildly superhuman" is a matter of months, not decades. (6) Therefore a slow, gradual takeoff — a decade in which institutions notice the change and adapt while systems get steadily but visibly better — is essentially impossible, and anyone planning policy around that scenario is planning for a world that won't exist.

What bothers me is that the shape of the argument seems to prove too much. The same chain would apparently show that any lab with a large budget could have bought decades of progress simply by hiring every graduate student in the field, or that the moment chess engines passed human level they should immediately have been arbitrarily strong rather than improving along a curve. I suspect the slippage is somewhere between (3) and (5) — perhaps a conflation of "the number of researchers you could in principle run" with "the number of researchers that advances the frontier," or a slide from "can do each component task" to "doing the tasks yields proportionate progress" — but I can't name the precise step, and I'd rather understand it than wave at it.

So here is what I'd like from you. Tell me which premise or inference in the chain I should reject, explain why that step looked innocuous when my housemate stated it, and then price the rejection: what does giving up that step cost, and does the rejection leave him with a fast takeoff that is merely less instantaneous — or does it genuinely open the door to a gradual scenario? One constraint on your diagnosis: I'd rather keep (1) and locate the trouble in the middle of the chain than rest everything on hardware, because he has already told me he thinks "we won't have the chips" is the weak reply, and I'd like to know whether he's right about that or whether the chip constraint is actually doing more work than either of us is giving it credit for.
