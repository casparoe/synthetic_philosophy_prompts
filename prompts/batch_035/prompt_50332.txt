I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher -- my exposure to formal philosophy ended with a half-remembered elective -- but I run a recurring brown-bag "math lunch" at my company, forty-five minutes, whiteboard plus a laptop I can project live code on, for an audience of other engineers who are comfortable with probability as we use it (distributions, sampling, Monte Carlo) but have never thought about what "picked at random" means. Next session I want to tackle what I've gathered is called Bertrand's paradox and the principle of indifference, and I'd like a teaching plan rather than an explanation, because I've already fumbled this once in conversation and I want the sequencing right this time.

Here's the situation that hooked me, and I'd like the plan to build around it. A while back I thought I could settle the chord question empirically: I wrote three Monte Carlo routines, each a perfectly defensible implementation of "generate a random chord of a unit circle," to estimate the probability that such a chord is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. Routine A sampled two independent uniform points on the circumference and connected them; routine B sampled a direction, picked a uniform point along that radius, and drew the chord perpendicular to it through that point; routine C sampled a uniform point in the disk and made it the chord's midpoint. They converged, cleanly and reproducibly, on 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4. I've stared at all three and cannot find the bug, and I've come to suspect there isn't one -- that "a random chord" is underspecified in a way that "a random number between 0 and 1" isn't, and that the principle of indifference (assign equal probability to the possibilities when you have no reason to distinguish them) is what breaks here. My audience, though, will walk in believing that if three programs disagree, one of them is wrong, and that randomness is a solved engineering problem once you're off the bad PRNGs.

So: plan the session. What has to be established first before the three-answers result can land as a paradox rather than as a bug report -- should I show the simulation disagreement cold at the start, or build up to it, and why? What should the live code be doing at each stage of the arc, and is there a fourth or fifth sampling routine worth writing on the spot that I should anticipate? Which misconceptions should I expect from engineers specifically (my guesses: that there's a canonical uniform measure over chords we're all implicitly using, that indifference only fails for infinite sample spaces so finite cases are safe, that the disagreement is an artifact of pseudorandomness or insufficient samples) and at exactly what point in the sequence should each one be headed off? Where should I land on the question of whether the principle of indifference is salvageable with some invariance or symmetry criterion, or is that a rabbit hole to explicitly decline at this level, and what should I say I'm declining? And what one question could I pose at the end -- ideally something they'd answer wrong if they've merely been entertained rather than changed their minds about when "at random" is a complete specification?
