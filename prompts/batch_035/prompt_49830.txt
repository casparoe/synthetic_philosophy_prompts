Longtime lurker here — been reading LessWrong and the EA Forum since roughly 2014, so I've watched the "should we be fanatics about the far future" debate go many, many rounds. A friend who's deep into Bostrom hit me with an argument last weekend that I can't get out of my head, and it ends somewhere I don't want to end up. Here it is as best I can reconstruct it:

1. The case for x-risk fanaticism runs through astronomical expected value: if there's even a tiny chance our descendants colonize the visible universe and run something like 10^50 worthwhile future lives, then a small reduction in extinction risk beats every near-term cause by orders of magnitude.
2. That 10^50 number is computed using our physics — how much computable matter there is, how long stars last, etc.
3. The simulation argument: conditional on mature civilizations running huge numbers of ancestor simulations, we are almost certainly inside one, because the sims would vastly outnumber the originals.
4. If we're inside one, our physics is just whatever the sim renders, and "the far future" only exists for as long as whoever is running us keeps paying the compute bill. Ancestor sims are presumably run for research or entertainment, which plausibly mean decades of simulated time, not billions of years. Nobody keeps a save file running forever.
5. So conditional on being simulated, the realistic ceiling on future value isn't 10^50 lives, it's maybe 10^12, whatever the simulators' attention span allows.
6. Give it even a few percent credence that we're in a sim — the trilemma makes this hard to push much lower unless you think we almost certainly go extinct or almost certainly lose interest in running sims.
7. Therefore the astronomical EV that fanaticism is built on should be discounted by orders of magnitude, the "far future trumps everything" picture is heavily damped, and x-risk work might still beat mosquito nets but no longer swamps it the way people claim.

Now here's why I'm suspicious of my friend's argument rather than just depressed by it. It seems to prove too much. If the discounting move in (5) and (7) works, it should work recursively: our simulators presumably face their own simulation argument, and so do theirs, turtles most of the way down, so expected-value reasoning about the long run collapses at every level, and nobody anywhere — not even the basement-level civilization, if there is one — is allowed to be a fanatic about anything. Also, and this nags at me, the argument smells like ordinary risk aversion wearing a credence update as a disguise: a few percent chance that the upside is capped doesn't obviously dampen anything for a *true* fanatic, since "tiny probability of a huge payoff dominates" is the entire fanatic position to begin with — you can't borrow modest conclusions from a view you're trying to defeat. But I can't actually point at the broken step, and I'd genuinely like to keep EV reasoning in the style of (1) (I've donated real money on the strength of it) without signing on to (7).

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Is the flaw in how the simulation credence interacts with the expected value calculation (some scope thing about conditioning on a branch where the payoff is capped), in the assumption that sims get shut off early, or somewhere deeper in the anthropics? And if the argument somehow survives, tell me honestly whether it damps the far future enough to change where a donor like me should send money, or whether it's philosophically interesting but practically irrelevant.
