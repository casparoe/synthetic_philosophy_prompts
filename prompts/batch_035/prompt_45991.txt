I work on optimization infrastructure, so I spend a lot of time near NP-hard problems and a lot of time around people who are confident that some future machine — quantum annealers, analog or thermodynamic hardware, "AI" in some loose sense — will make the hardness go away. I want to stop hand-waving. Give me your credence that no physically realizable device can solve an NP-complete problem (take exact solutions to large random 3-SAT instances as the benchmark) in time polynomial in instance size, and decompose it into roughly these terms, plus any you think belong: (a) the probability that P ≠ NP as a mathematical fact; (b) the probability that some version of the physical Church-Turing thesis holds, so that "physically realizable computation" roughly tracks Turing computation and the asymptotics transfer to hardware; (c) the probability that resources outside that thesis — closed timelike curves, genuine analogue precision that isn't secretly doing error correction, enormous luck in prepared initial conditions — do not offer an end run; and (d) anything about whether "polynomial time on a physical device" is even well defined once you let the hardware scale with the instance. For each term give a number and one line of justification, then show the combination. Two things beyond the bare number. First, there's a meta-question I keep hitting: does it make sense to have a credence at all about a mathematical statement whose truth is settled independent of anything we build, and if you think it doesn't, say which of the terms that objection actually damages rather than the whole exercise. Second, tell me which single term the final answer is most sensitive to, and what evidence or argument — mathematical, physical, or philosophical — would move that term the most.
