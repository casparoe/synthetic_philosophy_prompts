i teach an intro-level moral theory class and every year we do the aggregation unit and every year it turns into a total mess of people just picking teams ("utilitarian!" "no, taurek!") without anyone actually locating where they disagree. so this semester I want a decision tree handout, one page-ish, that takes a student from the rescue case where numbers obviously seem to matter (100 stranded vs 1 stranded, one helicopter) out to a named position on whether and how competing claims can be summed across people.

Build the tree from questions a student can answer before they know any of the labels -- concrete cases or claims, not "do you believe in contractualism." The first few splits should probably be things like: does the number of people affected ever by itself change what you owe (taurek's case: you can save one of five dying strangers, or give a small benefit to each of the five before they die -- does five outweigh one)? do claims of different strengths interact at all, or only compete claim against claim (parfit's billion mild headaches versus one death)? does it matter whether the good at stake can be given to whoever you choose versus being tied to particular people (think the world cup broadcast/transmitter room style case vs saving one specific person)? and somewhere in there: what role does a lottery play -- is it second-best fairness or does it capture something about what the losers can actually reject? I'll let you pick the rest of the splits but each one should be a real fork where the standard positions actually diverge, not a cosmetic question.

Leaves should include at least: straightforward utilitarian aggregation, scanlon-style contractualism with the individualist restriction, taurek's numbers-don't-count view, prioritarianism, some limited-aggregation middle position (there's a family of these and you can pick a representative, name it as "limited aggregation" if that's easier), and an egalitarian leaf. If a leaf has no famous defender, say "unnamed" and one sentence on why nobody's camped there -- incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored? Also please flag honestly where the tree fails to separate positions, like whether prioritarianism ever comes out distinguishable from utilitarianism with diminishing marginal utility, or whether taurek and a crude fairness view end up in the same place for different reasons.

Audience is undergrads who've read the big excerpts but argue in slogans, so keep the case descriptions self-contained (no "as we saw in the reading"). And format the diagnostic questions so someone could literally fill it out alone before class and land somewhere -- the whole point is sorting by what they actually think about cases, not which name they like. If you can add a couple lines at the end noting the single hardest branch to answer honestly, that'd be a nice touch for kicking off discussion.
