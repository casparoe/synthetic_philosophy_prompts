I'm teaching a philosophy-through-film seminar and next month we do the Matrix trilogy alongside the Grand Inquisitor episode from The Brothers of Karamazov, and I want the students to get past the obvious Neo-as-Christ mapping. Here is the reading I'd like you to develop.

The Inquisitor's case is that Christ overestimated human beings: freedom is a burden the masses cannot carry, and the Church — by accepting the three temptations, bread, miracle, and authority — corrected his work and gave people what they actually need: someone to whom they can surrender their conscience, and bread. Map that onto the trilogy's machinery. The first Matrix, as the Architect explains it in the sequel, was designed as a paradise and was rejected wholesale: is that an empirical confirmation of the Inquisitor's anthropology, or something importantly different, since the Architect's subjects never enjoyed the Eden the paradise-Matrix was modeled on, whereas the Inquisitor's masses had literally been offered freedom and turned it in? Where do the three temptations show up — bread as the sensory feed (Cypher's steak is literally fake food doing real emotional work), authority as the enforcement apparatus, and miracle as the management of the occasional anomaly? And then the question I most want pursued: who is the trilogy's Inquisitor? My instinct is that it is not the Architect, who is a cold engineer with no love for his flock, but the Oracle — the pitying deceiver who cheats at the right moments, tells people comforting half-truths about their choices, works to keep the herd inside a system she knows is false, and does it, she insists, for their sake — which makes her the closest thing the films have to the old cardinal's claim that the corrector takes on the sin of deception out of compassion and expects to pay for it. Neo, meanwhile, offers no miracle to compel assent: the red pill is offered, not imposed, which is exactly the behavior the Inquisitor says was Christ's fatal mistake. So does the truce that ends the third film — freedom preserved for the few who want out, happiness preserved for the many who stay plugged in — amount to the Inquisitor's correction arrived at from the other direction, or does it actually answer him?

I want a reading that takes both texts seriously and doesn't force the fit. Say plainly where the trilogy resists the Inquisitor gloss: the machines' original motive is energy and control rather than pastoral care, the Oracle's deceptions serve ending the war rather than managing humanity's happiness, the films never actually show us the plugged-in masses being asked to choose, and the whole dramatic weight rests on exceptional individuals where the Inquisitor's argument is precisely about the unexceptional many. If the reading only works by treating the Oracle as an autonomous agent with her own ends rather than a program of the system, say so and defend or limit the move.

Finally, add a short closing note on the reading's fragility: identify the one or two interpretive commitments that carry the most weight, and say what — in the films or in Dostoevsky's text — would have to come out differently for your verdict to flip. If there's a scene you'd have students rewatch because it cuts against your own reading, name it.
