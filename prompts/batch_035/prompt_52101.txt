I'm preparing a piece for a discussion group on practical rationality, and before I present it I'd like a consistency audit of my own position. The topic is sunk costs and, more broadly, the bearing of the past on present choice. Below is the full set of things I believe. Tell me which of them hang together and which don't.

1. The orthodoxy. I accept the standard decision-theoretic principle that the past is fixed and can confer no reason on present choice: rational choice attends only to the prospective costs and benefits of the available options. I accept the familiar corollaries — that one should not finish a bad movie because the ticket was paid for, and that a failing project should not be continued merely because money has already gone into it.

2. Symmetry. I believe decisions to quit and decisions to persist should face the same justificatory burden. Where an agent lands should not depend on whether the question is framed as "keep going?" or "stop?".

3. Fickleness. Nevertheless, I think an agent who abandons her projects at the first setback exhibits a rational defect — not merely a practical liability but a failing of reason — in a way that an agent who revises her plans coolly on new evidence does not. Steadfastness is partly a rational virtue, not just a useful trait.

4. Debts to the dead. When a leader argues that a war must continue because soldiers have already died, I find I cannot dismiss this as a textbook fallacy. Something in the neighborhood of "we owe it to the fallen" strikes me as a genuine consideration bearing on what to do now — and I do not think it can be fully explained away as morale management or as signaling resolve to the enemy.

5. Identity. I believe that twenty years spent on a research program can give its author a reason to continue that a newcomer with identical prospects does not have — a reason flowing from the project's being hers and long-standing, not from reputation, not from any payoff available to either party, and not from anything a fresh chooser would count.

6. Mechanism. I've read the claim that nonhuman animals, unlike adult humans, show little or no sunk-cost sensitivity, and I find it persuasive. I take this to suggest that the human effect is a misfiring of learned rules of the form "don't waste" and "persevere," rather than an adaptive commitment device. On this basis I reject the rationalization on which being vulnerable to sunk costs is rational ex ante because it makes one's commitments credible to others.

7. Final arbiter. I take decision theory to be the final tribunal of practical rationality: any intuition it cannot capture must either be explained away or recast in its terms.

What I want from you: identify which pairs or subsets of these are genuinely in tension and which only look to be; for each genuine tension, rank it by severity, say what the cheapest repair would be, and note what that repair costs elsewhere in the set; and tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Two constraints on the audit. First, do not let me launder backward-looking reasons as prospective ones without argument: "reputation," "signaling resolve," and "self-knowledge about one's future interests" are the standard reconciliations, and I need to know when they actually do the work and when they merely restate the tension in different words. Second, do not resolve a tension by adding a further commitment I did not state. Note that I have no access to sources while reading your reply, so argue the positions on their merits; if a view has a name, you may name it, but do not rely on my being able to look anything up or supply references.

End your reply with a final line in exactly this form: \answer{...}, where the brackets contain the number of the single commitment that has to go, or "none" if you judge the set consistent as written.
