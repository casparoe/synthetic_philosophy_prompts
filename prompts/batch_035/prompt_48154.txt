So I've got a bit of a thing for art-law crossovers, and there's one episode I keep chewing on that I want you to take apart properly: the Brancusi customs case. Quick facts so we're working from the same page — take these as given, don't go hunting for corrections: in 1926 Brancusi shipped his bronze Bird in Space to New York for an exhibition, the photographer Edward Steichen bought it and was the importer, and US customs refused to treat it as sculpture. Instead they assessed it at 40% ad valorem as a manufacture of metal, on the logic that to qualify as sculpture for duty-free entry a work had to be a carved or cast imitation of natural objects, chiefly the human form. Steichen/Brancusi appealed, and in 1928 the Customs Court (Judge Waite) ruled for Brancusi: it's a work of art even though it bears no resemblance to a bird.

What I want is the episode analyzed through theories of what makes something art. Mainly the institutional theory — art as a status conferred on an object by the right people within the artworld — but also its rivals, like the aesthetic/functional family of definitions where art is about the object being made to afford some aesthetic character. And I want you to be disciplined about the mapping, not hand-wavy. Who exactly is doing the conferring here? On its face it's a US customs court, which is about as far outside the artworld as an institution gets — but the artworld was obviously in the room the whole time: Brancusi himself testified, other artists were called as witnesses, and the piece already had exhibitions and a sale behind it. So does the ruling show the court merely ratifying a status the artworld had already bestowed, or does it show a state body can itself do the conferring? And here's the wrinkle I can't get straight: the court's actual reasoning seems to have been aesthetic — roughly, it's beautiful and expresses an idea — which looks like the court doing aesthetics rather than any procedure of recognition. Where does that leave the institutional story?

Then be honest about where the theory breaks. What can't the institutional account explain here — for instance, why the customs officials' initial refusal reads as a mistake about art rather than mere papperwork, and whether either theory actually predicted the outcome in advance or only accommodated it after the fact. And run it in the other direction too: what does the episode show about the theories? Is it a confirmation of the institutional view (a status got conferred by an act of recognition, just in a weird venue), a confirmation of the aesthetic view (the court won the argument by pointing at qualities of the object), or a case neither anticipated — a situation where the definition of art had to be settled as a matter of law, for tariff purposes, which is not a use any of these definitions were built for? And if you think some third model does better — say, art status as something negotiated between several publics, the artworld and the state and the market each with a say — then say so and argue for it, don't just gesture at it.

One last thing: skip the art-historical trivia, whether this influenced later movements etc. I want the conceptual analysis.
