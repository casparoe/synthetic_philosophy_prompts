Take the long-running debate over the Great Divergence — the question, in Joseph Needham's famous version, of why modern science and industrial takeoff happened in Western Europe rather than in China, given China's long record of technological superiority — and analyze it as a case study in contrastive explanation. On the contrastive view associated with van Fraassen and developed by Lipton, a request for explanation is never a bare "why P?" but a "why P rather than Q?", and what counts as an adequate answer depends on the foil. Map the historiography onto this: Pomeranz's argument that England and the Yangzi delta were broadly comparable in living standards and market development until around 1800, so that the divergence turns on contingent access to coal and New World resources, versus older accounts whose implicit contrast class was France, the Netherlands, or Europe as a whole, which make the divergence look early and deep-rooted in institutions or culture. Show concretely how the apparent disagreement between these camps tracks the choice of foil, and what changes when the foil becomes "Britain rather than Britain-in-1700" or "the Yangzi delta rather than the Po valley." Then say where the theory runs out: can contrastivism say anything principled about which foil is the right one, or does it merely re-describe the dispute rather than adjudicate it? Does it treat the historians' disagreement as substantive or as partly verbal? And how does it handle the fact that Needham's question about the rise of modern science and the economists' question about industrialization seem to demand different contrast classes, so that "the" divergence question may not be one question at all? Finally, run the analysis in the other direction: does this literature vindicate contrastivism as a correct account of what the debate was really about, or does the Great Divergence controversy expose its silence on foil selection as a fatal gap?
