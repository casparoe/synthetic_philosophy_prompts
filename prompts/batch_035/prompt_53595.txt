I am a PhD student in philosophy, serving as the graduate student representative on my department's faculty search committee, and I need a written opinion to circulate before our vote next week.

The situation: we have a tenure-track line in ethics and practical rationality. We have reviewed a first round of applications (roughly 120) and have a clear front-runner who satisfies every criterion in our posted ad: strong research record, defensible research agenda, adequate teaching experience, good job talks. Two committee members are ready to vote an immediate offer. Our chair, however, wants to re-advertise the position and continue searching for another two months, on two grounds: (1) the search budget can absorb it, and (2) he believes we have an institutional duty not merely to find a good hire but to make a serious effort to find the best possible hire, and he suspects our early consensus reflects decision fatigue more than the actual merits. A third member has floated a formal compromise: we pre-commit, before seeing any further applications, to a reservation profile -- a fixed list of non-negotiable criteria plus a quota of desirable-but-optional ones -- and we terminate the search as soon as any candidate meets it, rather than deliberating afresh each round. A fourth member keeps invoking the classical secretary-problem result, claiming it shows that an optimal stopping rule should be derived mathematically rather than left to committee judgment.

The philosophical substance, which the memo should engage rather than merely gesture at: this is in effect a live dispute about satisficing versus optimizing under bounded rationality, in the lineage of Herbert Simon, and about whether satisficing is a defensible normative ideal in its own right or merely a concession to our computational limits. It also raises the question of when a formal stopping rule (like the third member's pre-commitment scheme or the fourth member's secretary-problem math) helps and when it merely launders arbitrary assumptions into an appearance of rigor. The memo should note, where relevant, where the secretary-problem analogy fails for our situation -- e.g., that we can often return to earlier candidates, that we cannot rank applicants on a common scale, and that our costs of further search are borne by people other than the search committee. But it should also treat the chair's position with real seriousness: he may be right that "good enough" talk can rationalize premature closure, and that our all-things-considered judgment after weeks of reviewing files is exactly the kind of judgment satisficing theory was invented to distrust.

The deliverable: a formal decision memo I can circulate to the committee, recommending a concrete course of action (immediate offer, continued search, or continued search under specified termination conditions), with a clear rationale, an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the losing side, and a direct assessment of the two formal-rule proposals -- whether either should be adopted, adopted in modified form, or rejected. Assume the role of an expert in decision theory and the philosophy of rational choice. Before drafting the memo, think carefully about the considerations at stake and sketch a brief brainstorm of the main arguments for each available option, including any options the committee has not yet considered (for example, an immediate offer with a re-advertisement contingency, or a bounded second round with a hard stopping date). Then write the memo itself.

One epistemic requirement: throughout the memo, keep separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this literature. For example, whether satisficing is normatively respectable in its own right, or only a pragmatic second-best under bounded rationality, is a real dispute -- do not present either side as the consensus. Likewise, if you draw on empirical work on maximizing versus satisficing dispositions (e.g., the psychological literature on whether maximizers fare worse than satisficers), treat its status as contested and do not overstate its strength. Where you flag something as settled, make sure it actually is.
