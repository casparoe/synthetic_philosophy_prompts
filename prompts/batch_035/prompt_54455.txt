I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. The vocabulary I actually use daily is the one I want you to work in: random variables in the Monte Carlo sense, expectations as running averages over trials, variable scoping and shadowing, type systems, loop invariants, and the habit of asking what a computation actually returns as opposed to what its comments claim it returns.

Restate the two-envelope paradox in that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. By the two-envelope paradox I mean the family of arguments in which one of two envelopes contains twice what the other contains, you pick one at random, and a short expected-value calculation seems to prove that switching gains you 25 percent in expectation — after which running the same calculation from the other envelope's perspective seems to prove you should switch back, so a program that swaps envelopes forever appears to have positive drift. I want each member of the family translated separately: the naive argument that the other envelope holds twice your amount or half of it with equal probability, so the expected ratio is five to four; the version where you open your envelope first and condition on the amount you see; and the genuinely unsettling result that there exist concrete probability distributions over the pair of amounts for which switching is favorable at every amount you could observe, even though the envelopes were indistinguishable at the start.

Do the translation the way I would debug it: treat each step of the arithmetic as a line of code and tell me which lines don't type-check. Candidates I suspect: the argument reusing the symbol for the amount in one envelope to stand for two different random variables, one conditional and one not — variable shadowing, in my terms; computing the expectation of a ratio as a ratio of expectations; conditioning on an observed amount without renormalizing; treating a quantity that may not exist as a finite float. Then tell me what a simulator can and cannot reproduce. For ordinary finite distributions, the Monte Carlo average converges and the paradox evaporates; the cases that sustain the paradox seem to involve infinite expected values that no finite machine can instantiate. Which parts of the paradox are artifacts of that infinitude, and is that a defect of the paradox or of my simulation-centric vocabulary — is there something in the infinite case that my engineering framing dismisses too quickly as a non-reproducible corner case?

Finally, the residue report, which is the part I care about most. What in the philosophical debate translates cleanly into my vocabulary, what translates only with distortion, and what has no counterpart at all? In particular, the philosophers argue about what a claim like "the other envelope is equally likely to hold twice as much as half as much" commits you to before any distribution over amounts has been fixed, and about whether the paradox is a lesson about decision theory or just about careless algebra. I want to know whether the parts I cannot express are things my vocabulary is missing or things that were never well-defined in the original debate. If your honest conclusion is that the paradox is, in the end, an unhandled edge case in the formalism rather than a deep problem, say so plainly — but show your work on the way there.
