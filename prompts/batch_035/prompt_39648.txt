My oldest friend and I have been arguing about the nature of time for the better part of a decade, and last night's installment — six hours, two pots of coffee — ended exactly where it started, except that we finally mapped our disagreement instead of talking past each other. She is an eternalist: the past and the future are just as real as the present, and the present's apparent specialness is like the specialness of here — it marks where the speaker happens to stand, not a feature of reality itself. I am a presentist: only present things exist, and Caesar is no more out there somewhere than tomorrow's rainstorm is. By the end of the night we had written down six candidate points of contention, and we now suspect that some of them are merely verbal while others are substantive — while also suspecting that we disagree about which are which. Rather than schedule year seven of the argument, I want the document we should have produced long ago: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between the two of us.

The material the collaboration has to work with:

Her side. Her strongest card is an argument from special relativity: simultaneity is relative to an observer's state of motion, so an observer passing me at high speed has a simultaneity slice that intersects events I must call future; if what is real relative to their frame is really real, then events I call future are real, and it is a short step from there to the whole four-dimensional block. Her second card is semantic: past-tensed truths need something in the world to make them true, and it is a mystery, she says, what makes it true that there were dinosaurs if no dinosaurs exist anywhere in my ontology.

My side. My strongest card is the relief argument: when a painful ordeal ends, what I am relieved about seems to be precisely that it is over — not that it sits at an earlier location within a block where, on her view, it exists in the fullest sense and is arguably still being endured. My second card is change: genuine change seems to require that things become, not merely that later stages of a thing differ from its earlier stages the way one stretch of a road differs from another.

The six candidate disagreements we wrote down: (1) plain ontology — do non-present things exist at all?; (2) whether her demand for truthmakers for past truths is a genuine constraint on my view or a partisan rule she invented, given that I can treat past-tensed truths as primitive or ground them in presently existing traces and abstract surrogates; (3) whether the relativity argument actually delivers eternalism, given that frame-relative simultaneity may be an artifact of coordinate choice rather than a guide to what exists; (4) whether cross-temporal relations — causation, my owing a debt to a past loan, a table's outlasting its original legs — can be understood without non-present relata; (5) whether her block-plus-indexical account captures change at all; and (6) the suspicion that the entire dispute is verbal, with each of us free to reinterpret the other's quantifiers as restricted to a different domain — a suspicion each of us directs at the other.

Draft the joint statement. I want: (a) a numbered section of claims both of us could sign without wincing — about experience, about physics, about language — being careful that nothing in it is vacuous; (b) for each of the six candidate disagreements, a statement of it twice, once in wording she would accept as a fair statement of eternalism and once in wording I would accept as a fair statement of presentism, with a note on whether the two wordings actually disagree about anything; (c) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of us — with, for each crux, a concrete argument, thought experiment, or agreed counterexample that both of us would accept as bearing on it, plus our written-down predictions about how it would come out; and (d) for each candidate disagreement, a verdict of verbal or substantive, with your reasoning. Where you think part of our decade-long argument was about words, say which part and why; where you think we were genuinely disagreeing, do not paper over it.

Constraints: do not cite, name, or allude to any specific paper, book, or author; everything must be self-contained so that the statement could be read by someone who has never studied philosophy of time. Write in American English.
