My philosophy of mind / logic reading group has spent what feels like an entire semester on the surprise exam paradox and I'm starting to think we're just re-enacting the literature at each other. You know the one: teacher announces there will be an exam next week (say Monday through Friday) and that students won't be able to know, in advance, which day it's on. The student runs the elimination: it can't be Friday, because Thursday night we'd know it's the only day left, contradicting the announcement; it can't be Thursday given it can't be Friday, and so on down to no day at all. So no exam possible — and then the teacher holds the exam on, say, Wednesday, and everyone is genuinely surprised. One person in our group is convinced the announcement is just self-refuting if sincere, so no sincere announcement like this can ever be made (and the teacher who makes it is bluffing); one takes basically the Quinean line that the student's mistake is assuming they'd *know* the announcement is true even late in the week; one of us keeps pushing the idea that it's a blindspot proposition, something true but structurally unknowable for the student in the situation. Nobody has budged in months and worse, we can't agree on what budging would even look like.

My own working hypothesis — presented as just a hypothesis — is that the whole fight bottoms out in an ambiguity nobody in the room is stating cleanly: whether "you won't know in advance" is part of the content the student may legitimately feed into their own reasoning, or a claim about what the student can know that *cannot* coherently be fed into that reasoning without generating the iteration that produces the paradox. If it's the first, the student's argument looks valid and the exam shouldn't surprise anyone; if it's the second, the argument looks like a cheap trick. But I can't tell whether that's a real diagnosis or just a restatement of the puzzle. We've also been fighting about a variant where the teacher is known to lie, say, 10% of the time about there being an exam at all — the elimination seems to sort of work in a watered-down way and I genuinely don't know if that variant makes the underlying issue clearer or just muddier.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Verbal, substantive about the logic of epistemic self-reference, or are the two camps answering different questions under one heading — say, "is the student's argument valid?" versus "can an exam announced this way actually go on surprising students?" — without noticing? Use what the actual positions involve (the self-refutation reading, the assumption-of-knowledge reading, the blindspot reading) rather than just surveying them. And tell me honestly what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a sharper formalization, some new variant, a distinction nobody's drawn, or an argument that this is one of those debates that stays put for a structural reason and why. Please say how confident you are in the diagnosis and flag the part of it you're least sure of; my own hypothesis feels wobbly to me and I'd rather you didn't inherit my wobble by accident.
