I teach an intro philosophy of mind course — about 25 students, one 75-minute session. Design an in-class activity that makes them distrust their own introspective reports, not because I tell them to in a debrief lecture afterwards, but because they catch themselves doing it live.

The rough shape I want: a series of fast snap judgments (aesthetic or moral, either is fine) where they commit to a choice, immediately write down why they chose it, and then the setup guarantees that at least some of those written reasons can't have been the actual cause. Some possible mechanisms, but you work out the details: the two options are secretly identical; or the "winner" was fixed by me in advance and they just followed a planted cue; or the reason they report was never information they actually had at decision time. I want rules, cheap materials, a timing plan, and a way to get everyone to commit publicly before any reveal so nobody can later claim they knew all along.

Then a debrief that sorts what happened into three bins: confabulated reasons (a cause invented after the fact), misattributed reasons (real cause, wrong label), and blindness to the stimulus itself (they never even registered what moved them). Ideally the students propose which bin each case goes in, not me.

Two more things: tell me what will predicatably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which part of "introspection is unreliable" a classroom demo genuinely cannot show — I don't want to walk out of there thinking we proved more than we did.
