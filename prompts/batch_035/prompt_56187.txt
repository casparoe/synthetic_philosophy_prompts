I co-run a graduate reading group in epistemology, and at last week's session two of the other members — call them Amara and Ben — argued for over an hour about whether testimony is a basic source of knowledge. They eventually asked me to referee, but I found each of them convincing in turn, so I would rather hand the exchange to someone outside the room. Here it is, in six numbered moves as best I recorded them.

(1) Amara opened with the reductionist position: testimony cannot be a basic source of justification or knowledge, because whatever warrant a report has must ultimately derive from perception, memory, and induction — the sources we already accept. She conceded she had never personally conducted the global induction showing that testimonies are more often true than false, but said that was a practical limitation, not a concession of principle: a sufficiently long-lived investigator with good records could run it.

(2) Ben replied that this concedes too much: if no one has ever run the global induction, then on Amara's own view all actual testimonial knowledge is unjustified — yet we plainly know, through being told, the dates of our own birth, the existence of cities we have never visited, and the results of experiments we could never perform. Children, he added, acquire testimonial knowledge before they could possibly possess the inductive evidence the reductionist demands, and if Amara insists their beliefs are not knowledge, she is proving too much.

(3) Amara then retreated to a more refined position, which she called local reductionism: I need neither global statistics nor any positive reason for a particular act of trust; my justification rests on my background evidence together with ongoing monitoring of the speaker for signs of incompetence, insincerity, and incoherence with what I already know. Justification to believe is the absence of defeaters, not the presence of supporting reasons, and that keeps testimony evidentially grounded without absurdly requiring the global induction.

(4) Ben pressed the gullibility objection: if positive reasons are never required, the view licenses trusting anyone about anything until a red flag appears, which seems far too credulous; a source of justification should not tell me that the absence of grounds to distrust a stranger's medical advice is itself a warrant to act on it.

(5) Amara answered with the technical case: for expert testimony — the climate scientist, the radiologist — the monitoring is second-order. I monitor not the expert's inner competence but the credentialing institutions, publication and replication norms, and the fact that expert rivals are motivated to expose frauds; second-order monitoring makes the trust earned rather than blind, so her view generalizes after all.

(6) Ben's last word was that this converts trust in experts into a chain of further testimonies — I trust the credentialing institutions on testimony, and I trust the exposés of frauds on testimony — so Amara's justification is either circular or regressive; the anti-reductionist never faces the regress because she never claimed that testimonial justification was inferential in the first place.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the six moves, say whether it succeeds, fails, or succeeds only against a limited target, with your reasons. Then say who, if either, is more right overall, and identify the strongest point each side never addressed. Lead with your overall verdict in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restatement of the exchange back to me.
