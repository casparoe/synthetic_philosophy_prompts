I've been reading pop history of science books lately (mostly at my girlfriend's dad's place, he leaves them in the bathroom), and my roommate and I keep getting into it because he's one of those "they'll laugh at our physics in 300 years the way we laugh at the four humors" guys. To figure out if he has a point I started keeping a list of my own gut verdicts about which dead theories were false-but-innocent vs. false-all-the-way-down. Here's the list. Phlogiston: false, there was no stuff locked in metals that escapes when you burn them. Caloric (heat as an invisible fluid flowing from hot things to cold things): false, even though I read that Carnot got real, still-taught results about engines out of it. The luminiferous ether: the ether itself obviously doesn't exist, but I feel like the wave theory of light was onto something real — light really is a wave, they just dragged in a fake medium for it to wave in. Dalton's atoms: true, atoms exist, even though Dalton got the weights wrong and drew them with little hooks and shapes that are nonsense. Mendel's genes: true in a strong way, the genes were really down there in the peas, molecular biology just filled in what they're made of. Ptolemy's epicycles: not even false in an interesting way, it was bookkeeping for predicting where planets show up in the sky, wierdly accurate bookkeeping but not about anything. And one live one: I'm confident electrons exist but honestly less sure about quarks, they give me an epicycle feeling, though I can't articulate why.

So is there a principle that sorts these the way my gut does? The obvious candidate — a theory is true iff the central theoretical stuff it posits actually exists — dies immediately, because the ether doesn't exist but I want to say the wave theory of light was substantially true, while Dalton's atoms existed but not with hooks, so I can't call Dalton false either. My roommate's rule — every successful theory so far turned out false, so ours will too — I can't actually refute, but it feels like it lumps together theories that were wrong about everything with theories that were right about the important thing and just wrong about the packaging. The problem is that when I try to say which part of a dead theory was the "important part," it feels like cheating: am I not just looking at what survived and retroactively calling that the theory, the way you could say phlogiston theory was "really about" oxygen because combustion involves gas exchange?

Is there a non-cheaty way to draw the line between a theory being false and a theory being true-modulo-its-mistakes? If there is, does it give me the electron/quark distinction I want, or is that distinction just vibes I should drop? And separately, if the principle works, does it actually answer my roommate, or does it just concede his point with extra steps — i.e., if "true" theories have always turned out to be true-ish with the central posits swapped out, isn't that just his claim wearing a nicer outfit? I'd rather overturn one of my verdicts (the Mendel one is the one I'm least attached to) than keep a principle that secretly reduces to "whatever survived was the real theory all along."
