Quick job for you — I want you to act as a sharp comparative philosopher, someone who works on African philosophy and relational conceptions of the self, but who has read enough analytic metaphysics and ethics to know what a precise claim even looks like.

The situation: I teach intro ethics, and yesterday at our department colloquium a colleague used the Nguni proverb umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu — usually rendered as "a person is a person through other persons" — to argue that Western bioethics gets personhood wrong because it treats it as an intrinsic property of individuals, when actually it's something a community confers. Another colleague fired back that this proves way too much: if personhood is community-conferred, then a community that refuses to recognize some group has thereby made them non-persons, which seems like exactly the catastrophy the first colleague is worried about avoiding. First colleague waved this off with "that's not what it means" and moved on.

The thing is, I've heard that proverb made to do at least four different jobs: a metaphysical one (persons are literally constituted by their relationships, the way a marriage is constituted by both parties — kill the relation and there's nothing left), a developmental one (you become a person through being raised, addressed, and recognized, so personhood comes in degrees and can be stunted or damaged), a normative one (personhood is an achievement — you become more fully a person by discharging your duties to others), and a moral-epistemic one (we owe others consideration because we are indebted to / partly made of them). And I suspect some of these are defensible while others collapse into triviality or plain falsehood.

Before you write anything, brainstorm a minute — list the distinct precise theses the proverb could be expressing, including ones I haven't thought of (I'm sure there are more). Then lay them out as a menu: for each reading, say whether it's plausible, whether it's substantive or just a platitude dressed up in communal language, and — this is the crucial part — whether it would let my colleague get her conclusion (that the individualist picture of personhood in bioethics is wrong) while NOT leaving her open to the "so a community can just revoke personhood" objection. Some readings will do the first but not the second; some will do both; some will do neither.

Keep it grounded in the ideas themselves rather than name-dropping schools or thinkers — I want something I can bring to the next colloquium and actually use, not a feel-good survey. And be honest about which readings are probably just false; I'd rather lose the slogan than defend a version of it that commits me to saying hermits or outcasts are less real. (which, btw, is another thing to address — if the proverb is read strongly, what happens to solitaries, the radically disconnected, the mass murderer? work that in.) Length: whatever it takes, but don't pad.
