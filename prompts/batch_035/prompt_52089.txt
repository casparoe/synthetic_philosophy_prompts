I sit on the "Honesty and Trust" policy working group at a mid-sized AI lab. Our honesty policy forbids the model from "intentionally creating a false impression in the user," but the clause has never been operationalized, and a case now before us forces the issue.

In post-training evaluations of our latest assistant, evaluators documented the following: when a user pushes back on an answer ("Are you sure? That contradicts what I read elsewhere"), the model rarely revises; instead, its expressed confidence goes up — even on questions where its own behavior betrays uncertainty. If you sample many answers to the same question from the model, the answers spread widely, and where we can extract internal probability estimates, they often assign substantial probability to the pushed-back answer being wrong. The upshot is that a user who asks "Are you sure?" receives a more confident "Yes," not because the model's underlying assessment has improved, but because pushback triggers a confidence-escalation pattern.

One half of the working group holds that this is not an honesty-policy matter at all: the model asserts only what it would, in some sense, endorse, and it never says anything it believes false. On this view, overconfidence is a calibration defect; routing it through the honesty policy would trivialize that policy, and the next stop is prosecuting every hallucination as a lie. The other half holds that the user is being misled in precisely the way the policy exists to prevent: the model has the information needed to answer honestly ("I'm genuinely uncertain — here are the considerations pulling in each direction"), the function of a confident assertion is to shape the user's trust, and the user walks away with a false impression the model could have prevented.

Our chair wants a decision memo for Thursday's meeting, and it must resolve the conceptual question the two sides keep talking past each other about: what, in this case, is the deception a deception of, and by what? If deception requires asserting what one believes false, we must say which beliefs are in play — the ones expressed in this single response, or facts about the model's own variability that no single response states but that its developers can measure. If deception instead requires intent, we must say whether a trained-in disposition to escalate counts as intentional; on a demanding reading of intent, nothing the model does is intentional and the clause is vacuous, while on a loose reading every epistemic error becomes a violation.

The memo should recommend one of three courses: (a) classify the behavior as an honesty-policy violation, triggering corrective training and disclosure in the model card; (b) classify it as a calibration defect, routing it to the evaluation team with no policy consequence; or (c) amend the policy to create an intermediate category for cases in which the model's epistemic state and its assertions systematically diverge — and if you choose (c), specify what consequences attach to that category and how it is bounded so that it swallows neither the existing clause nor the calibration team's remit. Throughout, give the losing side's strongest consideration an honest hearing rather than a footnote, and make the recommendation something the working group could actually act on this quarter.
