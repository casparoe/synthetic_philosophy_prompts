"This is not a contradiction in the logical sense, since 'it's raining' and 'I don't believe it's raining' can both be true — the weather is one thing, my beliefs about the weather are another. Still, the sentence can never really be true. If it's raining, then I believe it's raining, at least if I'm standing out in it; the second half cancels out the first. So the sentence is self-defeating, which makes it the same basic thing as the liar paradox, where a sentence talks about itself and trips over its own feet. What makes it feel wrong to say out loud is the rule that you only assert what you believe: saying 'it's raining' commits me to believing it, and then the second half takes it back. This is basically Wittgenstein's puzzle, from his later writings. Also worth noting that the version with 'but I believe it's not raining' instead is exactly the same thing — the two forms are interchangeable, no point distinguishing them. And the same absurdity shows up in third person: 'it's raining but he doesn't believe it' is just as incoherent, because anyone standing in the rain is going to believe it."
