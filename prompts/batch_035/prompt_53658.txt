Working on a novel and I want the narrator to get something right that I can't. She's a bench scientist who gets roped into serving on the university's animal-care committee, and I've been reading up on how those reviews actually go: a protocol gets approved if the science is sound, the animal numbers are trimmed to the minimum needed for statistical power, pain gets managed with analgesia and humane endpoints, and the investigator checks a box saying they considered non-animal alternatives and found none suitable. What I keep snagging on is what the whole apparatus presupposes about the animals themselves. Nothing on the form ever asks whether the expected benefit is worth the harm to the mice -- a severe-pain study on rodents is approvable as long as the design is good, while the identical procedure on a human volunteer, or on somebody's pet dog, is off the table no matter how brilliant the science. And the paperwork itself isn't even consistent: the federal rules her committee enforces cover dogs and chimps with full inspections and record-keeping while lab-bred rats and mice basically fall outside them, so the same protocol means different oversight depending on the species. So, for my narrator: what theory of animal moral status is this practice actually running -- that animal suffering counts but only as a cost to minimize once the research is approved anyway? That animal interests can be traded against human benefit at some fixed (never stated) exchange rate? Or that the review is really about protecting the institution and the scientists' consciences rather than making a claim on behalf of the animals? And is there a coherent position that can defend the rat/mouse paperwork gap, or is that just something the practice sits on top of without being able to justify? I want the scene to end with her realizing the form never once asks the question she assumed it was asking, and I'd like that realization to be philosophically solid, not just dramatic.
