My labmate is really into integrated information theory and cornered me with an argument I can't see my way out of. It goes:

(1) By IIT's lights, how conscious a system is equals its integrated information, Φ — the causal power of the whole, in itself, to constrain its own past and future states, over and above what its parts can do separately. Pure feedforward chains score about zero, since no part of them is both a cause and an effect within the system.
(2) Now imagine a huge grid of very simple gates, each gate's next state the XOR of a few well-chosen neighbors, wired so activity crosses the whole grid in two or three steps, with return paths so nothing in it is feedforward.
(3) For such a grid, perturbing almost any gate changes the counterfactual future of a large fraction of the grid, and the same goes for its past, so the whole's cause-effect repertoire differs enormously from the union of its parts'. Φ scales up with grid size, and there's no ceiling besides resources.
(4) So a big enough grid of this kind would be far more conscious then you or me — even though all it computes is a parity-like function, with no memory, no senses, and no point to any of it.
(5) But a circuit like that is basically a giant error-correcting code; it seems like the paradigm of a mindless mechanism.
(6) So Φ is just something you can engineer into any well-connected circuit, and IIT ends up measuring not consciousness but roughly how hard it is to cut a circuit in half.

He says the theory's defenders will happily bite this bullet and accept that the grid is highly conscious, and that my discomfort is just intuition, not an argument. That can't be the whole story, can it? Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? And if the honest answer is to keep (4) and give up (5), I want to know exactly what I'm signing up for. Please answer in American English.
