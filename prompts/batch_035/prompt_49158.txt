I work as a data analyst at a development NGO -- mostly I build and clean the indicators that go into our grant reports: consumption aggregates from household surveys, the $2.15/day poverty line, HDI and its pieces, a multidimensional poverty index we adapted from a national one, the odd WHO-5 wellbeing score when a donor asks for "subjective welfare" numbers, and results from cash transfer evaluations. I never studied philosophy. At a conference a while back someone mentioned offhand that there's a whole debate among political philosophers about "equality of what" -- whether we should be equalizing people's resources, or their capabilities, or their welfare/happiness -- and that apparently Amartya Sen is one of the combatants. I've been curious ever since, because on the ground these three things come apart constantly: the same household can be above the consumption line and still have a kid who can't attend school because of a disability, or report being miserable on the wellbeing scale even as their measured consumption rises after a transfer. Our team argues about which number to report, and the donors just pick whichever one looks best.

So here's what I'd like from you, and please put real care into it: act as a philosopher who's fluent enough in my world to not hand-wave. Take the main positions in the "equality of what?" debate -- roughly, the view that what matters is resources (Dworkin's style of answer), the capabilities approach, and plain welfare or subjective wellbeing -- and restate each one in the vocabulary I actually work in: outcomes vs. inputs in logframes, targeting rules for cash transfers, whether our multidimensional index counts as measuring capabilities or just correlates of them, revealed preference from consumption data vs. self-reported life satisfaction, the whole measurement-validity headache where the indicator drifts away from the thing it was supposed to proxy. Maybe start with a quick brainstorm of how the three positions would each define "who is worse off" in a village where we're choosing between households for a program, since that's the decision that actually forces the question on us.

Then, and this is the part I care most about, give me the residue report: what survives the translation cleanly, what gets distorted, and what in the philosophical debate has no counterpart at all in my indicator world. Specifically I want to know whether the philosophers' disagreement is anything more than the measurement choice we fight about (when to use consumption vs. wellbeing scores), or whether there's something real in their fight that my vocabulary literally can't express -- and if so, whether that's a gap in how we measure, or a sign that their debate is less substantive than it looks. Also please tell me honestly where our MPI-style index sits in their taxonomy, because I suspect we smuggle in assumptions from all three camps at once without noticing.
