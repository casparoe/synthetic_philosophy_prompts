My friend and I have a running thing where he rules on whether stuff is rational or irrational on pure cold expected-value grounds, and he absolutely will not allow civic duty into it -- his line is that adding a 'because you should' term to the equation is just cheating to save the math from embarrassment. His track record so far:

- Voting for president from a safe state: irrational. Your odds of casting the decisive vote are like 1 in tens of millions.
- Voting in his condo board election (~40 voters, rules he actually cares about): rational.
- Recycling his own bins even though the city's program takes them anyway: irrational. His words were something like "my one bin is a rounding error on a rounding error."
- Wearing a seatbelt: rational.
- Donating $50 to a malaria fund: rational -- because, he says, the good isn't diluted across millions of people, it's concentrated on identifiable lives.

So here's my situation: I just moved to Wisconson, and there's an election coming that the polls have dead even, genuinely close to a coin flip. Turnout will be a few million. I want to know before I ask him: will he say voting is rational this time?

Honestly I care less about the yes/no than about the rule he's running. My guess is he only counts an effect if it's either big for him personally or concentrated on identifiable people, and something like "a 1-in-a-hundred-thousand chance of shifting outcomes by a few dollars' worth of welfare for each of millions of people" just doesn't register to him as real. But I assume that's not the only rule that fits his record. So: what are the other rules that fit it just as well, which of them split on my case, your best guess with reasons, and what further case should I pose him that would settle which rule he's actually using? Also, separately -- is he right that the diluted version doesn't count? Is there anything to the thought that millions of tiny slivers of expected good sum together the same way he accepts they sum when a charity does it in bulk?
