WHY HILBERT'S HOTEL ISN'T FULL

The famous story is that an infinite hotel with every room occupied can still take a new guest, because each guest moves from room n to room n+1 and room 1 opens up. Everyone treats this as showing that infinity is strange. I want to argue it shows something different: that we never defined "full" properly.

Step one. Call a hotel full when it can't take any more guests. The infinite hotel can always take more guests, no matter how many it has. Therefore the infinite hotel is never full. Not "full in a weird paradoxical way" — just never full, full stop. When we say every room is occupied, that's a statement about rooms and guests, not about capacity. So the "paradox" of a full hotel with vacancy is a verbal illusion: we proved one thing (all rooms taken) and pretended we proved another (no room for more).

Step two. This suggests infinite collections can have degrees of fullness even when they're the same size. Move every guest to an even-numbered room and now every odd room is free. Same number of guests, same number of rooms (you can pair guests with rooms one to one either way), but before the move there were zero empty rooms and after the move there are infinitely many. So two infinite collections can be exactly equal in size and still take up different amounts of space. This seems to me like a real property of infinite sets that the standard hotel story glosses over, because it keeps insisting only the one-to-one pairing matters.

Step three. Cantor's diagonal argument supposedly proves the reals are a bigger infinity than the naturals. But maybe it only proves what the hotel shows: that certain pairings don't exist. On the hotel, no pairing between guests and rooms that leaves nothing empty counts as settling anything, since the hotel looks "full" and "not full" depending on how you arrange people. Likewise maybe the reals and naturals are the same amount of stuff, and the diagonal trick just shows they can't be lined up, which is a fact about line-ups, not about amounts.

Conclusion. I suspect the real problem is treating an infinite guest list as a finished object. The hotel's guest list isn't a completed thing, it's a rule for making guests. If you keep thinking of it as a thing sitting there, you get the contradictions. If you think of it as a rule being carried out, the weirdness drains away, because a rule is never done and so never has a final state to contradict itself. Maybe all the infinity paradoxes are just this mistake wearing different hats.
