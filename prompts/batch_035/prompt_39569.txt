I write novels for a living, which means I spend my days building selves out of nothing: give a character enough consistent behavior and readers start positing a person behind it, the way you'd posit a center of gravity in a wheel. I gather that's roughly Dennett's actual view of real selves too — that the self is a narrative center of gravity, a useful posit that organizes the story rather than a pearl hiding somewhere in the brain. Anyway. I've been keeping a list of real-life cases where people edit the story of who they are, and I have gut verdicts on all of them, but I can't find the principle behind the verdicts. Maybe you can.

The guy who bullied me in school now says he was a scared kid lashing out because home was hell: fine, obviously, that's just what people do. A woman I know who wanted to be a painter retells her life as choosing her family over art — in fact no gallery would show her — fine, barely registers as a lie. My friend who divorced two years ago now insists she never loved her husband, though I watched her love him for a decade: not fine, and it bothers me way more than the painter one even though structurally its the same retcon. A cousin of mine came out of a cult with his whole biography rewritten — conversion story, his real life began at the baptism: not fine, but notice it was the group doing the writing, not him. And then there's the daughter of a woman with advancing dementia, who keeps her mother's self alive by telling her, daily, the story of who she was: totally fine, even necessary — even though that's also narration from outside.

So my tentative principle was: self-story edits are legit when you're the author, illegitimate when someone else holds the pen. But the friend case breaks it in one direction (she holds the pen and it's wrong) and the dementia case breaks it in the other (the daughter holds the pen and it's right). Is there some principle that gets all five, or close? And if there is, what does it say about the case I keep going back and forth on: a memoirist I know who compresses her chaotic twenties into a clean arc of becoming. She says the arc was always there and she just couldn't see it, and honestly her life since does run on that arc, but I remember the chaos and it was genuinly chaos. Is she discovering her self or inventing one, and does your principle even admit a difference?
