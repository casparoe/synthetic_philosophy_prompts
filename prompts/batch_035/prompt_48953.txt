A friend and I have just spent an entire evening arguing about whether David Lewis's account of convention, laid out in *Convention* (1969) and later refined in papers like "Languages and Language," could stretch to phenomena that emerged only after his death in 2001 — and we ended the argument no closer to agreement, so I am turning to you to adjudicate.

My position, roughly, was that Lewis's framework generalises remarkably well. Convention on his analysis is a regularity in behaviour that solves a coordination problem among a population, persists because almost everyone conforms, expects others to conform, and would prefer to conform conditional on others doing so, all sustained by common knowledge of these facts. My friend accepted that but insisted that most of what we argued about is only superficially convention-like: Wikipedia's editing norms, the unwritten rules of Reddit moderation, the "lurker-versus-contributor" equilibrium on social media, and above all large language models, which are trained on the textual output of a conforming population yet, she claimed, are not themselves parties to any convention at all, since they lack the preferences and reciprocal expectations Lewis's account requires. She also made a fuss about blockchain governance, arguing that a protocol whose rules are enforced by code rather than by mutual expectation is not a convention in anything like Lewis's sense, whereas I countered that the genuinely conventional part lies in the soft forks, the social coordination around which proposal to adopt, and the market's convergence on a canonical ledger.

So here is what I would like from you. First, reconstruct the strongest version of each of our positions as I have sketched them — steel-man both sides, and say where each of us is smuggling in assumptions Lewis himself would not have granted. Second, give your best-faith reconstruction of what Lewis would actually have concluded about each of the following, had he lived to see them: (a) emergent moderation norms in large online communities, where the "population" is fluid and pseudonymous; (b) the status of LLM-generated text as an apparent continuation of linguistic convention by an agent with no stake in the coordination problem; (c) cryptocurrency consensus mechanisms as hybrids of enforced rule and convention; and (d) the speed at which online speech norms now change, which strains the slow, precedent-based salience that Lewis relied upon when explaining how conventions arise and are maintained. Third, tell us which of these cases genuinely tests the limits of the Lewisian framework, which fit it comfortably, and where you think the account would need to be amended rather than merely extended. Finally, render a verdict on our dispute: was I too quick to claim the framework generalises, or was she too quick to deny it, and what specific point of disagreement between us most deserves to be settled by someone working on the evolution of norms today?

Please write your answer in British English, and please reason as a scholar would — take positions, don't just survey them.
