I'm an undergraduate in a philosophy of mind seminar on the unity of consciousness, and my term paper is turning on our professor's intuitions about when one body houses one subject of experience and when it houses two. She has a standing catalogue of verdicts she deploys whenever split-brain cases come up, and they're starting to strike me as less jointly decisive than she treats them as being. As best my notes capture it, her record is this: for the classic commissurotomy patient in the experimental confrontation — a different word flashed to each hemisphere, each instructing the hands to point at different objects, so the hands act at cross purposes — she says there are two experiencers, at least for those minutes; for the same patient at home a year later, cooking and chatting with nobody testing her, she says one, because cross-cueing and shared context keep the hemispheres functionally coupled; for a patient with congenital agenesis of the corpus callosum, even run through the same lateralized tests, she says one, always, citing compensatory subcortical pathways; for blindsight she says one experiencer with an informational gap, not a second silent one; for dissociative identity disorder she says one; for the octopus, despite most of its neurons living in the arms, she says one distributed experiencer; and for alien hand syndrome following a medial frontal lesion she says one, even mid-fight over the doorknob.

The case I want to put to her — the one my classmate and I disagree about, and the one my paper turns on — is this. A commissurotomy patient, a year out and well-compensated like the one above, is fitted for a single afternoon with an apparatus of the kind researchers have actually built for prolonged lateralized presentation: essentially a modified contact lens that restricts all visual input to one hemisphere for hours on end, while she goes about ordinary activities. There are no conflicting instructions, no test, no acting at cross purposes — just one silently isolated hemisphere receiving everything she sees while the other, the one that can speak, sees nothing all afternoon. Is there one experiencer that afternoon, or two?

I'd like a prediction of her verdict rather than your own view of the metaphysics. Specifically, I want the rules she could be running that are each consistent with her entire track record — I can see at least two candidates, one that makes two-ness depend on something momentary about how the hemispheres are being used at the time, and one that makes it depend on something structural about how the disconnection came about in the first place — what each rule predicts about the afternoon case, your best guess together with your reasons for preferring it, and the further case I could put to her that would most cleanly tell the rules apart. My classmate thinks the answer is obvious; I suspect her track record underdetermines it, and I'd like to know before I build a paper on her position.
