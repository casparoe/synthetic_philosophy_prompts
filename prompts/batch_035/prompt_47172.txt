I moderate a medium-sized internet forum, and over several years I have kept noticing something about the way slurs work that I cannot put my finger on. I have no training in philosophy or linguistics; I have simply been thinking about it on my own, and I would like to know whether there is an established concept for what I am seeing.

The pattern, in instances. First: a member quoted a slur inside a post whose whole point was to argue that the word should be banned from the forum — the slur was not aimed at anyone, merely displayed, in inverted commas — and the thread still blew up. A month later another member did much the same thing, quoting a court ruling, and nobody minded. The same word, equally mentioned rather than used, and opposite verdicts. Second: a long-standing member who belongs to the group the term targets uses it about herself and her friends all the time, and no one blinks; the identical sentence from a newer member got him banned within the hour. Third: when our book club reads older novels containing the word, whoever reads aloud substitutes a euphemism or skips it, and everyone understands perfectly well which word is meant and learns exactly as much as they would have from the original — yet the substitution feels compulsory, and the euphemism feels different in kind from the word itself, not merely politer. Fourth: I once tried to write forum rules distinguishing mentioning a slur from using one, and discovered that both reactions to mere mentions are stable and sincere — some readers are wounded by the bare appearance of the word on a screen, and others genuinely cannot see the problem as long as it is in quotation marks, and neither group is confused about what happened.

What I have come to believe, from my own reflections, is that the offence does not live in what the word means. You can convey the whole content of a slur with neutral vocabulary — you can state someone's ethnicity and add open contempt, and the result is vile, but it is somehow not the same act as slurring him. Indeed you can swap the slur for its polite counterpart and the sentence stays true or false in exactly the same situations, yet the two utterances are not interchangeable. Nor does the offence live purely in the letters, as case two shows. And it does not track the speaker's actual beliefs: sincere people produce the full effect, while bigots hiding behind polite euphemisms produce a different and, I want to say, lesser one. It is as though the word carries a deed rather than a description, and the deed is performed in some mouths and suspended in others, without the dictionary changing at all. For want of a better word I have privately been calling this the residue — the part of a slur that outlives quotation.

A friend with some linguistics behind her says this is simply the difference between what a sentence says and what uttering it does — the way saying 'I promise' does not describe a promise but performs one. But that cannot be the whole story, because quoting a promise is not promising, whereas quoting a slur still seems to leave the residue, at least for some people. So the speech-act idea fits my first and third cases badly.

What I would like to know, then, is whether there is an established notion of this thing — the deed a slur performs over and above stating its content, and why that deed survives quotation in some circumstances and not others. I am not after a bare label. If there are rival accounts of where the offence actually lives — in the word itself as a kind of taboo object, in the attitude it expresses, in something it assumes about whoever is willing to say it and to whom they are saying it, in the act of uttering it at all — I would like to see how each one fares against my four cases, especially the mention cases, where the speech-act explanation seems to me to fail. If the phenomenon straddles several concepts without being identical to any of them, I would like to be told where the edges are and what the residual difference is. Please answer in British English.
