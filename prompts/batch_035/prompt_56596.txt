I got pulled into the debate about mental causation and the "causal exclusion" argument, and after a few months of reading I've realised nobody in this literature seems to have moved in like thirty years, and I want a diagnosis of WHY, not another rehash of the arguments. Some background in case it's needed: as I understand it, Jaegwon Kim's exclusion argument goes roughly like this — (1) every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause (the physical world is causally closed), (2) my mental states do sometimes cause my physical actions, like when I decide to raise my arm and my arm goes up, but (3) if the physical cause is already sufficient, then the mental cause is either identical to the physical one, or it's redundant, or you get this weird systematic overdetermination where every action has two full causes, one mental and one neural. And Kim's punchline was that this squeezes non-reductive physicalism — the nice middle view where the mental is real but not identical to anything physical — out of existence. Everyone has a reply: some say the mental just IS the physical under a different description, some go emergentist, some say causation at the mental level is fine because of interventionist counterfactuals, some go full epiphenomenalist and say fine, mental states don't cause anything, deal with it. And it all just... sits there. Papers keep appearing and nobody concedes anything.

Here's my engineer's angle, and I want you to take it seriously but also tell me if it's naive. When we debug a production incident, the causal story we actually use is "the retry loop had a race condition and hammered the payment API" — nobody ever says "well actually the electrons in the CPU caused the outage." And if you asked whether the code-level explanation is "excluded" by the hardware-level one, we'd look at you funny, because we don't think of those as competing causes at all. They're the same system at different levels of description, and different levels earn their keep by different interests — debugging vs. chip design. So my working hypothesis is that this whole philosophy debate is a verbal dispute in disguise: the two camps mean different things by "cause" (something like production vs. something like explanatory relevance or difference-making), or they're secretly answering different questions — one about what makes things happen in the world, one about what explanations we're entitled to give — and the "exclusion" only looks like a problem when you cross-wire the two. But I'm aware philosophers have presumably thought of this, and the debate didn't end, so maybe my hypothesis is wrong or maybe it's right but it doesn't dissolve anything.
