Construct a thought experiment in which adopting a surrogate goal against an acausal threat pays off, together with a companion case — identical except for one feature — in which the same adoption backfires. Background, so we are on the same page: by a surrogate goal I mean a commitment made in advance of any threat to treat some small, otherwise-unimportant harm as a red line, reacting to threats against it exactly as fiercely as to threats against what one actually cares about; the idea is that a potential threatener, anticipating this policy, gains nothing from targeting the valuable thing and so has less reason to threaten at all. By an acausal threat I mean one in which the threatener cannot observe, communicate with, or causally interact with the victim at decision time: the threat must be prepared in advance on the basis of the threatener's model of the victim's policy, and that model may be no better than a shrewd analyst's — no perfect predictors, no simulations of the victim's decision.

Constraints:

1. Both cases must be set in an ordinary human setting — a negotiation, an insurance market, a hiring process, a public pledge — no AIs and no exotic physics.
2. In the first case, the surrogate commitment must genuinely lower the threatener's expected payoff from threatening; do not strawman the strategy by choosing a surrogate goal that any reasonable agent would decline to adopt.
3. The two cases must differ on exactly one feature, and it should be one of the following: (a) whether the commitment is observable to potential threateners before they decide to threaten, or (b) whether the surrogate harm can be inflicted cheaply by adversaries who want nothing from the agent at all. State explicitly which you chose.
4. Each scenario should be at most one paragraph, and the verdicts must be generated by the agents' incentives rather than stipulated — a case where the outcome is simply declared fails the brief.

After the scenarios: walk through the first case from the threatener's ex ante expected-loss calculation, and say what a causal decision theorist who never precommitted should do once the threat arrives, versus what an agent choosing its whole policy in advance — updatelessly — should do. Then show why the companion case flips the verdict, identify the single feature doing the work, and say what happens if you restore it. Finish with the strongest response a defender of surrogate goals can make to the backfiring case — in particular, whether they can insist that the failure was a badly chosen surrogate rather than a flaw in the strategy itself, and what consideration would settle the dispute between them and the critic.

The purpose of the pair is to isolate whether the strategy's advantage lies in deterrence through visible commitment or in the low cost of the surrogate harm. A case in which the threatener could simply be deterred by ordinary causal retaliation, or in which the surrogate commitment is costless and riskless by fiat, would be cheating.
