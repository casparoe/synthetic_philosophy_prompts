Quick note first: I considered the Chinese Room with a teaching-type prompt, but overdetermination in history with a procedure-type prompt seemed the best fit for your in-the-weeds-but-not-obscure constraint, so I went with that. The seeding (thesis committee, INUS conditions, preventer causes) is well within a mid-size LLM's knowledge, but the specific deliverable and the two worked cases are novel enough that it can't be answered from stock text.

I'm a philosophy professor (phil of science mostly, causation stuff on the side) and for the last few years I've been the token philosopher on the committee that grades our interdisciplinary senior theses, about half of which come from history students. Every spring the same fight happens. A student writes that the Cuban Missile Crisis "was caused by" the discovery of the missile sites, then spends the next paragraph listing five other things without which there'd have been no crisis at all, and the historians on the committee split over whether this is a coherent causal claim, a mushy one, or no causal claim whatsoever. One faction insists the event was overdetermined so no single-cause talk is even intelligible; another faction happily talks about "contributory factors" as if that meant something precise; and I sit there half-remembering Mackie's INUS conditions and Lewis's counterfactuals and failing to say anything usable in the three minutes I get before the next thesis comes up. I want a procedure the committee can actually run on a thesis chapter in about fifteen minutes, written for people with zero patience for philosophy: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure should stop and just say "this is not a causal claim, whatever else it is," and where it has to hand the decision back to us because the question is one of judgment, not analysis. We do about forty theses a year, the verdict has to come out as a letter grade plus a paragraph, and at least two committee members will roll their eyes at any vocabulary I can't explain in one sentence, so if INUS conditions make it in, they need a plain-English gloss or they're out.

Two things I specifically want the procedure to handle, because they come up every year and we handle them by vibes. First, genuine overdetermination cases, where you have two candidate causes each individually sufficient and neither preempts the other -- my pet example is a student thesis arguing the July Crisis would have produced a general European war even if the Sarajevo assassination had failed, since the alliance machinery and the war plans were all sitting there loaded. Does that mean the assassination wasn't a cause of WWI, or that it was one cause among several, or that "cause" is the wrong word entirely? Second, diffuse long-run causation, like "the printing press caused the Reformation," where there's no clean counterfactual because the causal story runs through thousands of small decisions that would presumably have gone differently in some other way eventually. I have a suspicion the right treatment of the second case is completely different from the first, but I'd want the procedure to show why rather than just assert it. Also, and this is the part I'm least proud of, I want it to be honest about where it will misfire -- I know prevention cases (causes that are absences, relief that prevented a famine from getting worse) are where counterfactual-style tests traditionally choke, and I'd rather the procedure flag those as its blind spot than pretend.

Then run it on three cases so I can see it work: the Franz Ferdinand thesis above, the printing press thesis, and one more -- a student who argued last year that the Allied strategic bombing campaign caused the German surrender, which half the committee loved for its rigor and half of us thought was historically wrong but couldn't say why in causal terms. Show me where the procedure lands on each, and if it gives an answer I won't like on any of them, so be it; I'd rather have a procedure that embarrasses me occasionally than another afternoon of vibes. Don't bother with reading lists or citations for me -- I want the machinery, not the bibliography.
