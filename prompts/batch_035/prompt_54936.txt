I've become convinced of what historians sometimes call the conciliatory reading of Galileo's conduct in the Scripture controversy: the reading on which Galileo's position was, at its core, the one Cardinal Bellarmine recommended to Paolo Foscarini in 1615 -- namely, treat Copernicanism as a mathematical device that predicts well, and wait for a demonstration before touching the literal sense of Scripture; and on which Galileo's real mistake in the Dialogue of 1632 was not the substance of his astronomy but a breach of discipline -- going beyond what his 1616 admonition allowed, and putting the Pope's own argument in the mouth of the simpleton of the book. On this view Galileo himself half-knew the rule: his Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina says the Bible teaches how one goes to heaven, not how heaven goes (a line he credits to Cardinal Baronio), and grants that Scripture never errs, only its interpreters do -- but the letter then quietly shifts from deferring on undemonstrated conclusions to demanding reinterpretation the moment he thinks a demonstration is in hand, which is exactly the point on which Bellarmine would have said no genuine demonstration then existed. Galileo's strongest card, the tides, was no demonstration at all, as it rests on a false theory; and neither the phases of Venus nor anything else he had shown excluded the Tychonic arrangement. So the conciliatory reading concludes that the Church's instruments of resolution -- the admonition, the requirement of demonstration, the eventual acceptance of heliocentrism once the evidence forced it -- were reasonable for the time, and Galileo was the impatient party who converted a manageable epistemic disagreement into a trial.

I hold this view, or at least I hold it more strongly than I used to. What would count against it? I want the exposure mapped honestly. In particular: which archival or interpretive facts, if true or if differently weighted, would sink it -- for instance, evidence about what the 1616 admonition actually said, whether the precept not to defend heliocentrism in any way was properly served on Galileo, or what Urban VIII understood himself to have licensed in the Dialogue? Which of these defects would the reading's committed defenders concede as counting against it, and which would they explain away? And how much of its resilience comes from a genuine interpretive insight, versus from insulation by qualifications -- the reading can always retreat from "the Church acted reasonably by its own epistemic standards" to "the Church acted prudently by any standards available in 1616" to something nearly unfalsifiable about context whenever an objection lands. If it has been insulated in that way, say so, and tell me whether that is a defect in a reading of this kind or just what historical interpretation is like. Please rank the defeaters from fatal through serious to merely awkward, and be explicit about which version of the conciliatory reading survives each one.
