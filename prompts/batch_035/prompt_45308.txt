I teach a second-year course in political sociology called Nations and Nationalism, and next week I'm running the first session of a two-week unit on the construction of national identity, before the students have read anything from Hobsbawm and Ranger's collection on the invention of tradition or from Anderson's book on imagined communities — I want the readings to land on experiences they've already had. The class has about 24 students, meets for 80 minutes, and I want them to feel, rather than be told, that plausible antiquity is something you can manufacture on demand, and that the older a tradition feels, the less its felt age tells you about its actual age. Please design a runnable activity for that session.

Here is the shape I have in mind, which you should refine, extend, or correct as you see fit. Split the class into five groups of four or five. Each group draws a card describing a fictional country and a concrete political need: e.g., a state that gained independence in 1961 from a larger neighbor and wants a founding ritual that differentiates it; a constitutional monarchy with a new, ambitious king who needs a coronation liturgy implying continuity with a dynasty that mostly wasn't; a post-conflict country trying to knit together two regional populations through a shared annual ceremony; a stateless diaspora building institutions abroad that must bind people who have never met each other. Each group gets 25 minutes to invent one national tradition — a ritual, costume, anthem convention, pilgrimage route, oath, monument-unveiling ceremony, whatever they choose — and to prepare a five-minute presentation. The presentation is delivered in the voice of a national museum docent or ministry official: it must assert or imply deep historical roots, and the group may invent whatever supporting evidence sounds plausible (folk etymologies, a rediscovered manuscript, a "medieval" fair, a claim that the tradition was suppressed under colonial rule and revived). Crucially, one of the five cards should instead describe a real tradition from a real country, presented in the same docent voice, without saying it is real — I will choose which one, so design the card so that I can substitute any real tradition into the same template. After all five presentations, students individually rate each tradition on how old it probably is, on a scale you should design, and we reveal the results before moving to the debrief.

For the debrief, I want it to do three things. First, map specific moments of the activity onto the analytical distinctions the readings will introduce — the difference between custom and tradition, the role of ritual repetition and invariance, the invented tradition's reference to a suitable (and suitably vague) historical past, and the way rituals scale the community so that strangers can imagine themselves as fellow members. Second, use the age-ratings as evidence: if the real tradition doesn't come out reliably older than the fabricated ones — and it usually won't — that is itself the lesson, and you should tell me how to run that reveal for maximum effect, including how to handle it if a student spots the real one. Third, be honest with me about what this activity cannot reproduce. My worry is that my students will experience fabrication from the inside and therefore never experience the sincerity side: nobody in the room will actually believe in their invented ritual, whereas the interesting thing about invented traditions in the world is that their participants believe in them completely. Tell me what the debrief must supply to bridge that gap, and give me a candid section on what will predictably go wrong on the first run — groups choosing costumes over rituals because they're more fun, presentations that go meta and jokey and thereby dodge the exercise, students who rate based on recognizability rather than asserted age — and how I should preempt or handle each.
