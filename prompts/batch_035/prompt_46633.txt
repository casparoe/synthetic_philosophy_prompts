My reading group (a philosophy of science thing, mostly retired people plus a couple of grad students) spent last Thursday on a classic puzzle our tutor says goes back to a paper of Bas van Fraassen's — it's usually called the Judy Benjamin problem, though he simplified the numbers for us. Judy is paradropped into a war-games territory divided evenly between the Blue army's region and the Red army's, so going by area alone her prior is 50/50 between the two. Each region is further split into a Headquarters Company area and a Second Company area, and her map shows the Red HQ area is only about a tenth of Red territory. She's dropped blind, so she spreads her credence uniformly over the whole territory. Then she recieves one radio message: if you're in Red territory, the odds are three to one that you're in the Red HQ area. The question we were meant to discuss was what her new probability should be that she's in Red territory at all.

Marco went first and said it has to stay exactly one half. The message is conditional on Red — it tells her how to distribute credence *within* Red (three quarters of the Red share to the HQ area, a quarter to Second Company, so 3/8 and 1/8 overall), and it says nothing whatsoever about Blue, and nothing about how the total Red share compares to Blue. Any updating rule that moves the Red total is smuggling in part of a message that was never sent. Yuki didn't buy that. She said the message is also evidence against the one scenario that would make what the radioman said come out false — being in Red territory but outside the HQ area — and the rational response is to knock that scenario down and renormalise, not merely to reshuffle within Red. Her case got stronger, she thought, in the extreme version: suppose the message had instead been that if you're in Red territory, you're *certainly* in the Red HQ area. Taken at face value that means she's learned she's in Blue or in Red HQ, and since Red HQ is a tenth of Red, her probability of being in Red collapses from 1/2 to 1/11 — so conditional messages plainly *can* touch the antecedent, and the only question is how much of a discount the weaker three-to-one message warrants. Marco replied that she's equivocating between a conditional and a disjunction: the radioman gave a conditional probability, not a probability for "Blue or Red HQ", and odds talk is inherently relative — it constrains the ratio inside Red and nothing more. Both methods satisfy the constraint, he said, so the constraint alone can't settle it, and between two methods that both work, his keeps her honest about how little she actually learned — minimal change on top of the prior. Yuki's last word was that "minimal change" is not the neutral option Marco pretends it is: holding the Red total fixed whilst cramming three quarters of it into a tenth of the territory is itself a substantive claim about how paradrops go, and once you admit any such claim you've conceded that the message, properly interpreted, bears on Red versus Blue after all. She also thought it mattered, and Marco never addressed this, that the radioman chose to tell her about Red rather than Blue in the first place.

They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't — at one point I thought each of them was winning, and by the end I wasn't sure they were even disagreeing about the same thing. Then tell me who's more right overall, and what, if anything, both of them are missing. Please check the arithmetic too, since I couldn't follow Yuki's 1/11 and don't trust my own checking.
