I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general readership, and somewhere between profiling a logician and reading Penrose's two big books (The Emperor's New Mind, and then Shadows of the Mind) I talked myself into the Lucas-Penrose position: that Gödel's incompleteness results show human mathematical understanding can't be matched by any formal system or algorithm, because for any consistent machine strong enough for arithmetic we can apparently see the truth of its Gödel sentence, which the machine itself can't prove. I've since written a column gesturing at this and I'm now doubling down, which is exactly when I should stress-test. So: what would count against the view? I want the arguments and observations that should move me, ranked from potentially fatal to merely awkward. Some replies I've already bumped into and don't find decisive: that we might just be an inconsistent machine (we make mistakes all the time, so why trust our "seeing" the truth of the Gödel sentence?), and the one where the mechanist says sure, you beat any particular machine I name, but you don't know which machine you are, so you can't construct *your* Gödel sentence -- that one feels like it might actually be doing real work and I'd like your honest read on whether it does.

Two more things I want you to take seriously. First, what would defenders of the view themselves concede as counting against it, as opposed to what critics claim? Lucas wrote his piece in the early sixties and has spent decades answering objections; I suspect he'd grant some points and not others, and I'd like to know which. Second, and this is the one that keeps me up at night: the pure logical argument has taken a beating from logicians, and Penrose himself seemed to acknowledge this by shifting the weight onto claims about new physics -- non-computable effects in quantum gravity, orchestrated stuff in microtubules, that whole program. But nothing there seems to have generated a testable prediction anyone outside the program took up, so the anti-mechanist conclusion now floats free of anything that could touch it. Has the position been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that even a problem for a position of this kind, or is it asking too much of a metaphysical claim about minds that it stick its neck out? Play it straight with me -- I'd rather find out I'm wrong now than in print. Write it as continuous prose please, no headings or bullet points or bold, just like a long email from a colleague who knows the literature.
