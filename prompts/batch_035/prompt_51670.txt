I am a novelist drafting a satirical piece for an anthology of travel writing about imaginary countries, and I have become stuck on one entry. The conceit: a Lonely Planet–style guide, roughly six hundred words, deadpan throughout, to a fictional republic that has achieved what its constitution calls perfect negative liberty. The state has not interfered with a citizen in decades — no regulations enforced, no taxes collected, no arrests made, no permits required — and the guide's cheerful tone should never acknowledge that the citizens seem oddly careful, quiet, and prone to glancing over their shoulders. I want the comedy to carry a real philosophical payload: the joke must be built on the actual distinction between the absence of interference and the absence of domination, the old observation that a slave with a wonderfully kind master enjoys non-interference in abundance while remaining unfree, and that the citizens' freedom seems to hang on something as flimsy as the government's continued good mood. The reader should come away having felt the republican critique of purely negative liberty without the guide ever stating it, and without the piece collapsing into a complaint about too little government rather than about unaccountable power.

Please include the usual guidebook apparatus and let it do the satirical work: a Getting Around section (the roads are immaculate and everyone drives very, very slowly), local customs (do not ask anyone why they whisper in the parliament square), a Tips for Travellers box, and a sidebar on the national holiday celebrating the anniversary of the last recorded act of state interference, which the citizens celebrate by staying home. Somewhere there should be a genial invented official — a minister, perhaps, whose entire job is to reassure visitors that the state could interfere whenever it liked and simply chooses not to — and his reassurances should be perfectly sincere and perfectly chilling. Resist the temptation to make the state sinister in any conventional sense; its power is rendered uncanny precisely by being unexercised, and the citizens are not oppressed but accommodationist, having organized their whole lives around the remote possibility of an interference that never comes.

Two constraints matter to me. First, please keep Berlin's contrast between negative and positive liberty in view but do not let the satire drift into attacking positive liberty instead; the target is the sufficiency of non-interference for freedom, not self-mastery or self-realization. Second, I would like one small structural joke repeated at least three times in different sections — some formula the guidebook itself uses, whose ironic weight shifts with each occurrence — so that the piece rewards a second reading. The ending should be a flat guidebook sign-off (visa requirements, best time to visit) that, in context, lands the whole argument in a single deadpan sentence.
