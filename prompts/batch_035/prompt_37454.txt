I'm three hundred pages into a novel about two siblings dividing what's left after their father's death: the sister has a progressive degenerative illness, the brother gambled away every share he was ever given and is now destitute, and their mother proposes to redistribute what remains to even them out. In the pivotal scene the brother, Maren, argues his way into half of everything, and I want the argument to be one a careful reader can't dismiss. It goes like this. (1) An inequality between people is unjust only if it reflects brute luck, meaning outcomes for which they are not responsible; that is the whole point of distinguishing the gamble you take from the disease you draw. (2) You are responsible for an outcome only if it flowed from your choices as the person you are, exercising your own values, judgment, and capacities. (3) Nobody chose the person they are: values, judgment, and capacities were settled before any choice was ever made, by genes and upbringing nobody selected. (4) So no outcome, however thickly wrapped in choices, flows from you in the sense (2) requires; the brother's bets are as unchosen, in the end, as the sister's illness. (5) Therefore luck egalitarianism, taken seriously, entitles the gambler to compensation on exactly the footing of the sick — which erases the distinction the view was invented to draw, and so the view defeats itself.

I'll confess the argument unsettles me more than I intended when I gave it to Maren. If it is sound, it seems to prove not just that the mother is right but that nothing anyone has is theirs in the way we ordinarily suppose — and, since I find I can't stop the thought there, that the sentence I've already drafted for my acknowledgments, about a self-made woman who raised me, is false in the same breath. Yet when I try to locate the trick, I can't: each premise looks like something its defender would say in good faith, and the failure, if there is one, is somewhere in the neighborhood of the slide from (3) to (4), where responsibility for choices quietly becomes responsibility for the self that makes them. My editor wants the rebuttal in the scene to land in a single speech, and I refuse to have the sister win by rhetoric.

So, as a piece of philosophy rather than plot: which premise should she reject, exactly what is wrong with it, and what does rejecting it cost her? I'd most like to keep (1) — the intuition that the sister's illness and the brother's roulette call out for different treatment is the one thing in the book I am sure of — and I'd like to know whether the standard escape routes (a compatibilist story about responsibility for choices without responsibility for character, Dworkin's attempt to split brute from option luck with hypothetical insurance, or giving up luck egalitarianism for something like relational or democratic equality) let me keep it, and what each of them makes Maren give up instead.
