The following question appeared on a philosophy of science exam:

Explain the no-miracles argument for scientific realism and the pessimistic meta-induction against it. Then present one realist response to the pessimistic meta-induction, and briefly assess whether it succeeds.

Here is one student's answer:

The no-miracles argument, proposed by Bas van Fraassen, says that the empirical success of our best scientific theories would be a miracle unless those theories were approximately true. It is an inference to the best explanation: among the candidate explanations of why our theories make accurate predictions, approximate truth is the best one, so we should accept it.

The pessimistic meta-induction, formulated by Thomas Kuhn, is the standard anti-realist reply. The history of science is a graveyard of theories that were empirically successful in their day and later judged false: phlogiston, caloric, the optical ether. From these past cases it follows deductively that our currently successful theories will also turn out to be false, which refutes scientific realism.

The best realist response is structural realism, developed by John Worrall. Consider the transition from Fresnel's wave theory of light to Maxwell's electromagnetic theory. What was retained across that theory change was not the ether, which Fresnel's theory posited and Maxwell's abandoned, but the mathematical structure: Fresnel's equations for diffraction phenomena survive intact in Maxwell's theory. The structural realist therefore claims not that the full content of mature theories is preserved, but only that their structure is. This concedes the pessimistic meta-induction's point about entities while preserving the no-miracles argument: Fresnel's theory was successful because its equations were right, and since the same equations hold in our current theory, the structure of our current theories is true. Hence the pessimistic meta-induction has no force against the sophisticated realist, and the no-miracles argument emerges completely unscathed.
