I've been lurking and commenting in the rationalist corners of the internet forever — old LessWrong threads, SSC comment sections, the occasional meetup — and at last month's meetup I somehow volunteered to give a 30-minute talk on the paradox of fiction. You know the thing: I genuinely grieved at the end of a novel last week, even though I knew full well the character never existed, and I'd feel embarrassed to be that upset about something I know isn't real. Emotions are supposed to track what we beleive, and here they apparently don't, and everyone treats this as normal. The audience will be sharp but almost entirely non-philosophers: programmers, a med student, one guy who has read a lot of Dennett and will absolutely try to route every question through "it's just different subsystems in the brain."

Don't write the talk for me — plan it. I want the dependency order: which pieces have to be in place before the others (for instance, does the audience need to get clear on what exactly an emotion is "aimed at" before the paradox can even be stated non-hand-wavily?), and what should come first. Pick the one example that should carry the whole talk and defend it — and tell me whether to avoid the worn-out crying-at-a-novel case, and whether fear at a horror movie is better or worse as the vehicle. Then the misconception map: I predict (a) "we secretly believe it's real on some level," which this crowd will love because it sounds like aliefs, and I can't decide whether to introduce alief myself early so I can control it or ban the word entirely so it doesn't become a hand-wave that "resolves" everything; (b) "suspension of disbelief," which most of them will toss off as if it were an explanation rather than a restatement of the puzzle; (c) the thought that the "solution" is that the emotions are somehow fake or watered-down — I want them to see that the phenomenology is full-strength and that's part of why it stings; and (d) the "so then it's rational to check your closet for the killer" reaction, which I suspect is actually a good test of whether they've understood any proposed answer rather than a dumb objection, but I'm not sure where in the sequence it belongs. For each: where in the arc do I defuse it, preemptively or when it comes up?

Also: what should I openly decline to cover in 30 minutes and say so — I'm thinking the separate "why do we enjoy horror at all" question, which always derails things, and maybe the fancy machinery around make-believe and pretending, at least under its technical name. And end with one question I can ask the room that would actually distinguish "got the puzzle and the shape of the main response types" from "nodded along," since with this crowd a decent fraction will claim to have understood it regardless.

Thanks in advance, I've read enough advice threads to know the answer will be better if I stop trying to plan the talk myself in the shower.
