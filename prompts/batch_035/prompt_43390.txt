I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar on the ethics of eating, and I also coordinate a small campus food-advocacy group: about a dozen student volunteers, a modest budget, and one semester left before we have to finalize next year's campaign messaging. A recent meeting left the group badly split, and I've realized we can't decide what our campaign should say until we decide which of the underlying questions actually matter.

The split went roughly like this. One member pressed the arithmetic that swapping beef for chicken is a disaster on almost any view of animal welfare: a single cow yields hundreds of pounds of meat while a chicken yields only a few, so per-meal chicken consumption means vastly more animals killed and reared, under conditions that are arguably worse per animal. On that view, our generic "eat less red meat" messaging could make things worse. A second member countered that crop cultivation itself kills enormous numbers of invertebrates through plowing, pesticides, and harvest, and that given our deep uncertainty about invertebrate sentience, we can't even be confident a fully plant-based diet reduces suffering relative to a diet built around grass-fed ruminants. A third thought all of this was scope-obsessed navel-gazing, and that we should just keep the message simple: eat less meat, full stop.

I'm not asking you to referee the meeting. Given what we are — students with a semester, a small budget, and influence over cafeteria purchasing and a public campaign, not a research lab — I want a prioritized account of which open questions we actually need answers to and which we can safely fence off. Some candidates: how sensitive is the beef-versus-chicken replacement argument to per-animal welfare estimates, and does it survive if one doubts that badness scales in any simple way with lifespan or time-to-slaughter? Does the invertebrate-death question hinge on settling invertebrate sentience, or only on better data about animals killed per calorie of each crop? Is the general problem of moral weights — comparing a cow's suffering to a chicken's to an insect's — upstream of everything we do, or can we get by with a few robust, widely shared assumptions? And are there questions I haven't listed that should outrank all of these, such as whether individual consumption changes even translate into changed production one-for-one?

Rank the questions by how much they should matter for our decision, defend the ordering explicitly — including your criteria and why you weight them as you do — and say what new evidence would move items up or down. Where a question can be set aside for our purposes even though it remains genuinely open, explain what lets us set it aside.
