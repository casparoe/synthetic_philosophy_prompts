I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy training beyond reading on planes, and a couple years ago I finally got through Dennett's Consciousness Explained (1991). The part that really stuck with me is his method, which he calls heterophenomenology. As I understand it: you record what subjects say about their experience like any other experimental data, but then you treat those reports as text to be interpreted — he compares the job to what a literary critic does with a novel, and says we should provisionally accept the world the subject reports on the way we accept a novel's world, as a fiction that may or may not match reality. All the actual explaining then happens in the third person: brain scans, reaction times, error rates. The subject's own take on their experience gets no special authority over anyone else's theory of them.

Then I went and read the pushback, and honestly the whole fight feels frozen in place since roughly the mid-90s. Searle more or less accused Dennett of changing the subject — explaining people's reports about consciousness instead of consciousness, which Searle thinks can't be studied from the outside at all. The Chalmers-style people say first-person data IS the data, so a method that demotes it to fiction has thrown out the very phenomenon. And there's a third crowd, the neurophenomenology people around Varela, who say the fix is to train subjects to give richer first-person reports and treat those as data alongside the brain recordings — Dennett's side seems to think thats fine but trivial, while his critics think it concedes everything.

Thirty-ish years on and everyone is still making the same moves. As far as I can tell nobody has been converted in either direction. So what I want is a diagnosis, not a scorecard of who's right. My hypothesis, take it or leave it: this isn't a disagreement about any fact, it's about what counts as data in the first place — Dennett's side treats the question of what counts as evidence as already settled by the rest of science, and the critics think that's exactly what's up for grabs when the topic is experience itself. Or maybe the two sides are answering different questions under one name: one is doing methodology for a science, the other is doing metaphysics about qualia, and the word "consciousness" glues them together. But I might be totally off, and I might also have botched the positions themselves — I'm working from pop-level reading here. So, two asks:

1. What kind of disagreement is this, really? Verbal, substantive-but-empirical, two-different-questions-under-one-heading, or bedrock intuitions nobody can argue for? Support the diagnosis with what the sides actually say, and tell me straight whether my hypothesis survives contact with it.
2. Given that diagnosis, what would count as progress? Some new conceptual distinction, a particular kind of experiment, a reframing of the question — or is the honest answer that this one just doesn't resolve, and I should stop waiting for it to?

And please push back on the question itself. If it rests on a confused premise — say, if I've turned Dennett into a strawman, or if the debate is actually way more alive than I'm making it sound — I'd rather hear that plainly than get a polite answer to a broken question. Definately tell me if that's the case.
