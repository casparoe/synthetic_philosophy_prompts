In my history of science seminar we've been discussing the cases where two people arrive at roughly the same idea independently — Newton and Leibniz with the calculus, Darwin and Wallace with natural selection, and, if the stories are right, Bell and Gray with the telephone. When I asked what these cases say about who deserves credit, a classmate (and, in milder form, our TA) leaned on a line I've heard all over popular science writing: ideas are like fruit on a tree. The tree is the accumulated tradition — prior results, instruments, shared problems, mathematical tools. When the season comes, the fruit ripens on its own schedule, regardless of who is standing underneath. Newton and Leibniz were just two people who happened to be under the same branch. From this my classmate drew conclusions: priority disputes are petty, since the winner is mostly a lottery; eponyms (Cartesian coordinates, Halley's comet) misdirect our attention; and if we care about producing discoveries, we should fund the orchard — education, infrastructure, open problems — rather than fuss over identifying and rewarding the pickers.

I pushed back in seminar and got nowhere, partly because the picture does feel right in some ways: the parallel work really was happening in both cases, and neither man conjured the prerequisites out of nothing. But something about the comparison seems to be doing more work than it's entitled to. So I'd like a proper audit rather than a vibe. First, lay out the correspondence table: what plays the role of the tree, the season, the ripening, the fruit, the picker, the orchard, and a windfall (a discovery made by accident). Second, say where the mapping holds and where it breaks — I can think of a few candidate breaks, and I'd like you to judge which are real and which are pedantic: ripening is a fixed process with a determinate end state, whereas "the same idea" may not even be the same idea when two people articulate it differently; picking a fruit doesn't alter it, but working out an idea changes what the idea is; and we seem to call a time "ripe" mostly in retrospect, after someone has picked, which makes the claim about the schedule hard to test (if something is discovered fifty years "late," was the fruit not ripe, or were the pickers doing more work than the analogy allows?). Third, separate the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion my classmate actually drew from the ones that don't.

Finally, tell me what survives. If the fruit picture collapses, does the conclusion go with it, or could my classmate defend it on independent grounds — for instance, by pointing to how many people were close on natural selection, or to cases where the "same" discovery really did require nothing but what the tradition already contained? Or does the analogy turn out to have been carrying the whole argument, in which case suggest what a better comparison would be — something that keeps what's true in the "time was ripe" talk (discoveries have enabling conditions that most would-be discoverers share) without importing inevitism and the irrelevance of the discoverer. I'd also like your honest take on the seminar question itself: in the Newton-Leibniz and Darwin-Wallace cases, was the credit essentially a lottery, or is there a principled way to say that one of the two did something the other didn't?
