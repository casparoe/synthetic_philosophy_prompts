I write about science policy, and for several years I have followed the hazard assessments of a national environmental-health advisory panel. The panel's procedure is to vote separately on the premises — quality of the evidence, exposure data, plausibility of a mechanism — and then to vote on whether the overall conclusion follows from them. My clips of how member votes became "the panel's finding" run as follows. On substance A, majorities accepted each premise and a majority of members also endorsed the conclusion; the report stated the conclusion as the panel's finding. On substance B, majorities accepted each premise, but a majority of the individual members rejected the conclusion as too strong; the report nonetheless presented the conclusion as the panel's collective finding, explicitly citing the premise votes, with the dissent relegated to an annex. On substance C, one premise failed to win a majority; the report described the evidence as suggestive but not established. The complication is that the panel's chair, an epidemiologist who has publicly favored precautionary readings of weak evidence in every one of these episodes, said at a conference last month that he finds the reasoning behind the panel's next assessment unconvincing. That assessment has exactly the structure of substance B: every premise wins a majority, yet a majority of the members is expected to reject the conclusion, and the chair is on record against it. I have a piece to file when the report drops. First, which rules could have generated this track record, and which are already excluded by the substance B episode? Second, predict whether the new report will state the conclusion as the panel's finding, give your reasons, and say honestly where the record underdetermines the prediction. Third, tell me what future case — presumably one in which the chair's views and the premise votes diverge in the other direction — would most cleanly separate the surviving rules, so I know what to watch for afterward.
