I am a junior in high school and one of three co-editors of our student newspaper, which comes out every two weeks. Increasingly we get letters and tips making factual claims we have no way to check the way a real paper would — no records requests, no staff reporter time, most claims second- or third-hand by the time they reach us. In my English elective we read excerpts from a nineteenth-century debate between the mathematician W. K. Clifford, who held that believing something on insufficient evidence is always wrong, everywhere, for everyone, and William James, who replied that when a live and forced choice can't be settled by the evidence in time, it's sometimes permissible to believe on faith. My teacher mentioned that most treatments of the debate assume a judge weighing one claim carefully, but that's not our situation: we face several small claims a week, with an hour of checking time between us, and a decision deadline that doesn't move.

I have a hunch — and this is the part I'd want you to take seriously and correct if it's muddled — that the real difference between Clifford and James isn't about how strict to be in general but about which claims are at what my teacher called "a James-style fork," by which I mean a case where the belief is urgent, can't wait for more evidence, can't just be suspended, and makes a difference to what you do, versus an ordinary claim where Clifford's demand for evidence is just... the right demand. So instead of asking which philosopher won, I want you to turn the two of them into a procedure our editors can actually run in fifteen minutes per claim. It should have an ordered set of questions, each with a plain test for when the answer is yes; it should include a step that sorts the claim into "fork" or "not fork" with a quick operational definition of a fork we can apply without reading James (something like: deadline before better evidence could arrive, suspension of belief itself changes what happens, and the belief is tied to an action we can't postpone — but refine or replace that if it's wrong); and it should mark honestly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as checkboxes, since the point of the assignment is for us to see where procedure runs out.

Then run the procedure on three claims, all roughly based on things we've actually received: (1) a letter asserting, on the basis of one anonymous student's account, that a vice principal said something offensive at an off-campus event two weeks ago — the paper is deciding whether to publish the allegation; (2) a tip that the varsity coach is resigning at the end of the semester, sourced to "someone who overheard athletics," which a sophomore is using to decide whether to bother trying out — she asked us to confirm it, and our confirming it publicly would itself spread the belief; and (3) my own belief, which I'll admit to, that a teacher graded my last essay unfairly because of a grudge, a belief I've mostly gathered from my own feeling of having worked hard and one friend saying the same thing happened to her. I want the procedure to give the honest answer on that last one even if I won't like it. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: where "sufficient evidence" is simply not something three teenagers with a biweekly deadline can determine, and what we should do at exactly those points.
