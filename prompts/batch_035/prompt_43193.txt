I'm a postdoc in environmental history, and I've been asked to write the opening chapter of an edited volume on the Anthropocene as a period concept. Every draft I've received so far takes a different starting point: one author anchors everything to the dip in atmospheric CO2 around 1610 that followed the collapse of indigenous American populations after European contact, another to the spread of James Watt's steam engine, a third to the layer of plutonium isotopes from nuclear weapons tests that shows up in sediments worldwide from around 1950, and one simply to the invention of agriculture. Each author picks the date that best fits the story they already wanted to tell, and the disagreement about the standard never becomes explicit. Before I write a chapter arbitrating any of this, I want the standard of arbitration written down.

Write me the desiderata: what any adequate proposal for the start of the Anthropocene has to deliver. I take it the requirements will come from at least three directions that don't obviously agree — stratigraphy (the boundary should be recognizable in physical deposits, ideally as a single globally correlated marker), Earth-system science (the boundary should track when human activity became a planetary force, which is a question about rates and magnitudes, not about layers), and history (the boundary should mark a real transformation in how humans live, and shouldn't require treating humanity as a single agent — the people who cleared the forests and the people who died of introduced disease in 1610 are not the same we). A stratigrapher friend of mine insists on one test I keep circling: that the boundary be identifiable by someone who has only the sediments and no archives — no history books, no datasets. That sounds clean until you notice how much of what makes 1950 matter to us lives in the archives rather than the mud, so tell me whether that test belongs on the list or is a camp preference. Also include at least one requirement about the decision procedure itself: who has standing to fix the date, and whether the geologists' rulebook for naming periods is even the right instrument for a concept that doubles as a cultural and political one. For each requirement, give the case behind it, and mark it non-negotiable or a preference of one camp; then tell me whether any subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, because if it is, the list itself becomes my chapter's thesis. You may close with a sentence or two on how the four candidate dates above fare against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria, not the scorecard.

End your response with a line of exactly the form \answer{...}, containing either the name of the requirement that does the most exclusionary work across the candidates, or, if you concluded some requirements cannot be jointly met, the phrase jointly unsatisfiable followed by the two requirements in tension.
