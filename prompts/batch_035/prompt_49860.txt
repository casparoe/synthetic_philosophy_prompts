I am a graduate teaching assistant leading a 90-minute discussion section, capped at eighteen students, attached to an introductory course on rational choice and practical reason. The session is on Kavka's toxin puzzle: a billionaire offers you a large sum at noon if, at noon, you genuinely intend to drink a toxin at one o'clock; the toxin will make you mildly ill for a day but cause no lasting harm. Once noon passes you have no further reason to drink, and the puzzle is that you seemingly cannot now form the intention you would need to form, because you already know that at one o'clock you will have no reason to follow through, and a genuine intention that you know will not survive cannot get started. Design a complete in-class activity in which students encounter this structure with real, if small, stakes, before the puzzle is named or lectured on.

The activity should proceed in two rounds. In the first, students face the classic structure directly: a modest cash reward (or equivalent course credit) paid at time T1 to those judged to genuinely intend to perform an unpleasant-but-safe task at T2, some minutes later — think a mouthful of bitter tonic, a brief cold-water hand immersion, or an equivalent you specify, with an explicit opt-out and a safety note. Include a workable mechanism for judging genuine intention, since that is the philosophically loaded part: say whether you would use written statements, structured interviews, peer prediction, or a designated judge, and be candid that the classroom version substitutes a fallible human judgment for the infallible introspective access the original stipulates, and what that substitution does to the game. In the second round, let students bid for the right to post a bond, forfeited if they fail to perform the task at T2, and observe how the availability of precommitment reshapes intention-formation. Provide the timeline, materials, rules, payoff scheme, a debrief that maps specific moments onto the puzzle's components, a frank account of what will predictably go wrong on a first run, and a note on which feature of the original case — the day-long illness, the large reward, the stipulated infallibility — the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied in discussion. I should say why this has become urgent for me: last month I signed my first commitment contract with a service that fines me if I skip the gym, and I have discovered that I cannot tell whether I intend to go or merely intend to intend, so I want my students to feel that uncertainty in their own bodies rather than read about it.

A note on tone: keep the activity philosophically clean — no mention of Kavka's name, the literature on resolute choice, or constrained maximization until the debrief, and in the debrief have students articulate, in their own words, the difference between engineering incentives and forming intentions, since that difference is what my contract with the fining service trades on.
