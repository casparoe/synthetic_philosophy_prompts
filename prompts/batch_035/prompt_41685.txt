My philosophy of language lecturer leans hard on Wittgenstein's language-game comparison, and I want to know how much weight it can actually bear. Her story is: words are like chess pieces, grammar is the rules of the game, understanding a word is knowing how to move it, and since chess has no hidden essence beyond the rules, there's nothing more to meaning than conforming to the practice -- so philosophers of language should just describe usage, never legislate. But it struck me that Wittgenstein's own point about games was that *games* have no common essence at all (chess vs solitaire vs ring-around-the-rosie), so the analogy can't mean language has a clean fixed rule structure the way chess does. And some of the disanalogies feel like they matter: you can cheat at chess and still be playing chess, whereas deliberately misusing a word seems like a different kind of act from conforming; a chess player can quit and go home, but a speaker can't step outside language to learn its rules the way you learn chess from a rulebook; and chess has a point -- checkmate -- while it's unclear what "winning" would even be for a language-game, even though the builders in the opening of the Investigations clearly have a purpose. Audit the analogy for me: give me the correspondence table, say which disanalogies actually bite on her descriptive-not-legislative conclusion versus which ones are just noise, and tell me whether Wittgenstein's other comparison -- words as tools in a toolbox -- would carry her conclusion better or worse.
