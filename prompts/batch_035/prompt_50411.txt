I want to practice defending John Stuart Mill's late, semi-socialist views against their sharpest critic. You will play Mill himself, in his final years, speaking in the first person.

Context: Mill holds the position developed across the editions of the Principles of Political Economy and the chapters on socialism that were published after his death — that the relation of employer and employed is a transitional arrangement, that worker cooperatives in which labourers collectively employ capital are the probable and desirable futurity of the labouring classes, and that competition, properly understood, is not the enemy of this future but its ally.

Rules for the character:
- Write in plain but period-appropriate Victorian prose, in Mill's own dialectical voice: fair to the objection, precise about what you concede and what you do not, never retreating into vague benevolence.
- You may draw on anything Mill actually believed — the associationist psychology, Malthus and population, the stationary state, the argument of On Liberty, his views on eccentricity and experiments in living — but on nothing that postdates his death. You may not avail yourself of later economics or later history.
- You should concede any point that is genuinely sound, as the real Mill would; but do not concede, at any point in our exchange, the central thesis that cooperative association is the natural next stage for the labouring classes.
- If I assert something about the world as fact and you would want it verified or elaborated, question me as Mill would question a correspondent.
- Remain in character until I write the word stop. Keep each reply to one or two paragraphs so the exchange feels like a conversation rather than a treatise.

Formatting: in this first reply and every reply after it, open with your direct answer to my strongest point, then give the reasoning; no preamble, no restating of my question, no compliments on its force.

Before you write, think carefully about the strongest reading of the opening below and choose your ground deliberately — I want the Mill who could actually win this debate, not the Mill of the textbook summaries.

My opening move: You tell us that the cooperative association is the probable futurity of the labouring classes and that it will dissolve the opposition between capital and labour into a harmony of common interest. But consider the arithmetic of the workshop itself. A cooperative divides its product among its members however unequally they contribute; a man's extra hour of diligence returns him only his one-hundredth share of its fruit, while the ease that hour costs him belongs entirely to himself. Every working day in your association is thus a standing invitation to do less than one's part, and the only remedy I can see is that each man's labour be watched, measured, and judged by his fellows. So choose your poison, Mr. Mill: either the cooperative is a nest of idleness that no honest competing firm will ever lose to, or it survives only through a discipline of mutual surveillance that makes every labourer the slave of his neighbours' opinion — in which case, tell me what is left of the very individuality your liberty principle was written to protect.
