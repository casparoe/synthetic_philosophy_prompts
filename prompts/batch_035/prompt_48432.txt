I write about science and ideas for a general audience, and I'm preparing a long essay on what mathematics is actually about. My trade has a default picture for this, and it appears in popular books, in interviews with working mathematicians, and in the explainers published whenever someone proves something big: the explorer and the continent. Theorems are not invented but discovered, as a mountain range exists before any survey team arrives; the Mandelbrot set is routinely described as having been there all along, waiting only for computers fast enough to draw it; and the clincher offered to lay readers is that any sufficiently advanced civilization, anywhere in the universe, would find the same theorems — so the theorems cannot be our creation. The picture is vivid, my editors like it, and I have noticed that it does real argumentative work: it is used to license the conclusion that mathematical objects exist independently of human minds, and that the nominalist alternative — on which mathematics is something we build out of symbols, definitions, and conventions — cannot explain the subject's objectivity.

I'd like a serious audit of this analogy, and I want a committed verdict rather than a menu of positions to choose among. Please answer in American English.

First, lay out the correspondence table: what plays the role of the terrain, the explorer, the map, the survey, the natives one might encounter, the impassable regions, and the feat of arriving somewhere no one has been.

Second, show me where the mapping breaks, and be particular about the breaks that matter. The explorer can walk to the mountain, photograph it, bring back a sample, and trip over it; a number or a set, if it exists at all, is non-spatial, outside time, and causally inert — nothing in mathematics ever answers back the way a landscape or a rival expedition does. The worry here is the one Benacerraf pressed: how could creatures embedded in the causal order know anything about objects outside it? Gödel, for one, tried to pay that debt in kind, writing in the revised version of his paper on the continuum problem: "But, despite their remoteness from sense experience, we do have something like a perception also of the objects of set theory, as is seen from the fact that the axioms force themselves upon us as being true." Whether mathematical intuition is more like a sixth sense or more like an intellectual conscience seems to be the hinge of the whole dispute, so tell me how much of the explorer picture survives if Gödel's sentence is granted, and how much survives if it is rejected as metaphor dressed as epistemology.

Third, sort the disanalogies into the fatal ones for the conclusion my colleagues keep drawing and the merely awkward ones — and keep the intended use in view. It is one thing to note that continents can be reached by several routes while proofs of a theorem feel unique; it is another thing to show that the gap breaks the inference from "discovered, not invented" to "exists independently of us." It is also fair to ask whether the nominalist has her own version of the picture — the explorer as a chess player who finds that certain lines of play are forced, without anyone supposing the lines live in a landscape — and whether that version carries objectivity as well as platonism's does, or worse.

Finally, take a stand. Does the inference to mind-independent existence survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or should I retire the explorer from my copy altogether? If the analogy is beyond repair, propose what should replace it for a lay audience; and if the honest answer is that no everyday picture can carry this weight, say so plainly — but then still tell me, definitively, which side of the platonism–nominalism dispute you would have my essay come down on, and why.
