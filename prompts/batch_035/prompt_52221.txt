I've been keeping a notebook of my own reactions to borderline cases — mostly around "bald," "heap," and "rich" — and I'd like help working out what view of vagueness they add up to, if any. Here are the verdicts as they stand. A man with roughly 700 hairs left: at the barbershop I'd call him bald without a second thought. The same man, same hair count, on a dermatology ward where everyone else has total hair loss — he is, by far, the hairiest person in the room — and "he is bald" starts to feel not just tactless but somehow wrong, as though the comparison class has quietly moved the line while the word stayed put. A sandpile of ten billion grains, with grains removed one at a time: I'm confident that at no particular removal does the pile stop being a heap — there is no step where that one grain made the difference. Yet when I run the sorites argument that ends with one grain being a heap, I'm equally confident some premise in the chain is flat-out false — which seems to require precisely the step I just denied exists. If someone forces a yes-or-no on "is 3,000 grains a heap," I lean no; I'd happily take a hundred-to-one bet against seven grains being a heap; but I refuse even odds anywhere near the boundary, and not out of risk aversion — I simply cannot pick a side, which feels different in kind from being uncertain how a coin will land. Finally, a legalistic verdict: if an insurance contract pays out on "baldness," I think a court must find either that he is bald or that he isn't — no "gap" finding is available — even though before the contract existed I'd have said the question had no determinate answer.

My tentative principle is that predicates like "bald" and "heap" are indexed to a comparison class that context shifts, so their extension is a moving target rather than a blurred line. But I can see this isn't the whole story: it doesn't explain why each sorites conditional still looks false at every step, or why the courtroom seems obliged to manufacture a fact of the matter where I'd swear there wasn't one, or why "he is bald or he is not bald" still strikes me as trivially true even for the hard cases.

So my request is this: is there a single account — one of the standard candidates like supervaluationism, epistemicism, contextualism, or degrees of truth, or something those don't map onto — that saves most of my verdicts? Please state it as precisely as you can, say which of my verdicts it has to overturn and why those are the right ones to lose, and then tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed: whether, for the 700-hair man on the dermatology ward, "he is not bald" comes out true, false, or neither — and, if your account says neither, whether "he is bald or he is not bald" still comes out true on it, since that pairing is the one I genuinely cannot reconcile.
