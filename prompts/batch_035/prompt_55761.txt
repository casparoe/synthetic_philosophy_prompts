My cousin Nina is a clinical ethics consultant and over the years I've collected her calls on which of a patient's preferences you're supposed to honor. Her record so far: the dialysis patient who begs for treatment whenever the pain spikes but calmly asks to stop once his meds are adjusted — she goes with the calm wish (he'd also told the staff that when he's in pain he'll scream for anything and they shouldn't listen). The woman with advanced dementia who left a note while still lucid saying any future begging to go home is the disease talking — she honors the note over the woman's current pleading. The guy refusing chemo during a depressive episode, saying he wants to die quietly — she overrides him and treats the depression first, betting he'll thank everyone later (thankfully he did). And the lifelong devout patient refusing a blood transfusion on a conviction shes certain is stable and central to who he is — she honors it. Next week she's got a new one and I'm curious what you'd predict: a man with a permanent brain injury who is now cheerful, coherent, fully informed, and consistent — he wants to blow his savings on travel and stop seeing his kids, which pre-injury-him would've called his own worst nightmare. No advance directive either way, and his family wants decisions made per the old him. What's Nina gonna say? I'm less interested in the verdict itself than in the rules that actually fit her track record — I can see at least two that cover all four old cases — what each one predicts here, which you'd bet on and why, and what further case of the kind hospitals actually see would really split those rules apart.
