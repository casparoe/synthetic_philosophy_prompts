I'm an undergraduate in a philosophy of psychiatry seminar, writing a term paper on Szasz, and I've run into a structural problem with my argument that I'm hoping you can help solve.

My paper attacks Szasz's criterion for what makes a condition a disease in the first place — his view, in outline, that genuine disease requires some identifiable bodily lesion or physicochemical abnormality, and that mere disturbances of conduct and communication are mislabeled when we call them illnesses. The trouble is that every objection I can find attacks the criterion from one direction only: we point to conditions without lesions — chronic pain, chronic fatigue, the anxiety disorders — that seem like real illnesses, and the Szaszian either bites the bullet or starts bargaining over what counts as a lesion. My seminar instructor suggested a more interesting line of attack: build a case where the criterion misfires in the opposite direction, certifying as disease something that no one, Szasz included, has any appetite for calling one.

So here is what I'd like you to construct for me.

A single-paragraph scenario, set in ordinary contemporary clinical life (a routine imaging scan ordered for something unrelated, an insurance physical, a sports-medicine clinic — your choice, but nothing exotic), in which a patient is found to have a well-documented physical abnormality of the brain, discovered incidentally, while being fully functional, symptom-free, and content with their life. The case must be engineered so that all four of the following hold:

1. Szasz's lesion criterion delivers the verdict that the patient has a disease.
2. A Wakefield-style harmful-dysfunction analysis delivers the verdict that the patient has no disorder, because the second conjunct fails in a specific, articulable way.
3. Our pre-theoretic judgment sides with the harmful-dysfunction analysis — it would strike a reasonable clinician as wrong, even ghoulish, to treat this person as a patient.
4. The divergence is generated by the criteria rather than stipulated. Please don't write the conclusion into the case; let each account compute its own verdict from the scenario's details, and make sure the lesion you posit is the kind of finding that actually shows up incidentally in real neuroradiology rather than an invented rarity.

After the scenario, please walk me through the demonstration. State exactly which feature of the case each criterion latches onto and why it yields the verdict it does. Then tell me:

(a) Which single feature of the case is doing the work — the one that, if you changed it, the two criteria would reconverge.

(b) How a committed Szaszian would most plausibly respond. I anticipate the move will be either distinguishing disease from illness (the patient has the disease but isn't ill, since illness requires the patient's own judgment of suffering or need for treatment) or demoting the lesion criterion from a sufficient condition to a necessary one. I'd like you to develop whichever response is strongest and then tell me honestly whether it is a principled repair or an ad hoc patch — in particular, whether it can be made without giving up something else Szasz needed for his argument that mental illness is a myth.

(c) What happens to the case when you remove the feature identified in (a). Does it collapse back into the familiar, stale dispute about biomarkers in schizophrenia, or does a residue of the objection survive?

A couple of constraints on scope. The scenario itself must be one paragraph of ordinary clinical narrative — no longer — though the analysis afterward can run as long as it needs. And please pitch everything for a reader who knows Szasz's myth argument in outline and knows Wakefield's analysis exists, but hasn't read the specialist literature: you can use terms like lesion criterion and harmful-dysfunction analysis without deriving them, but please don't rely on anything more exotic than that. Finally, wherever you report what Szasz held, keep it to positions he actually defended in print, and paraphrase rather than quote — I want to be able to cite the primary text myself later.
