I write about science and ideas for a mid-size paper, and a while back I did a column on the Lucas-Penrose idea that Godel's theorem sinks mechanism. A grad student wrote in with the argument laid out like this, and now I can't find my way out, except that it seems to prove way too much. (1) For any consistent formal system F strong enough for arithmetic, there's a sentence G(F) that F can't prove but that is in fact true. (2) I can see that G(F) is true, because I can follow the proof of the theorem itself. (3) If my mathematical thinking were exactly captured by some such F, there'd be a truth -- G(F) -- forever beyond me. (4) But I can see it, so no consistent F captures me, and the mind isn't a machine. So far I was on board. Then: (5) nothing in steps 1-4 used that F is a machine, only that it's consistent and strong enough. So let T be the total inventory of my mathematical beliefs, and I can see that G(T) is true -- and G(T) amounts, roughly, to the claim that I'm consistent. (6) But Godel's second theorem says no consistent system can establish its own consistency. So either I'm a special kind of thing that can do for itself what no system can do for itself, or something broke earlier. And if I really can just see my own consistency, then a machine that keeps adding each new Godel sentence as a fresh axiom can shadow me step for step, and the argument stops seperateing minds from machines at all. If I'm honest I'd like to keep (4) -- I liked the column. But I half-suspect the problem is (2), which quietly means "I can see that G(F) is true, granted that F is consistent", and when F is me, I'm not obviously in a position to grant that. Which step do I give up, why did it look fine, and what does giving it up cost?
