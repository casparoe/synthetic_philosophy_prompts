I make my living betting sports, mostly NFL and NBA, and I've spent a lot of time thinking about closing line value — the idea that the closing line is closer to the truth than any earlier version of the line, so if you beat the close consistently you're a winner even when you lose the bet. A friend who did a philosophy PhD keeps telling me this is "basically van Fraassen's Reflection principle" and I want to actually check whether he's right or just pattern-matching.

The version I've pieced together, probably badly: Reflection (from his paper "Belief and the Will," 1984) says something like — if you're certain today that tomorrow you'll assign probability x to some claim, then today you should already assign x to it. Not because tomorrow-you is smarter in general, but because conditional on the new belief being x, you should be at x now. Supposedly there's a Dutch book argument: if your current price and your conditional price disagree, someone can take both sides of you across time and arbitrage you. And supposedly there are counterexamples where people agree it fails — the stock ones involve your future beliefs being shifted by something that isn't learning, like being about to get drunk or brainwashed, where you can be certain what you'll believe and still not adopt it now.

Then, and this is the part I care most about, give me the residue report: which parts of Reflection map cleanly onto line movement and CLV, which parts you had to distort to make them fit, and whether anything in the philosophers' principle has no counterpart in how markets move — or whether the residue is my vocabulary missing something real. A friend of mine claims reverse line movement (the line moving away from the side most money is on) is exactly a Reflection counterexample in the wild, and I want your take on whether that's a fair parallel or a stretch.
