So I'm a PhD student in ML safety, interpretability-adjacent, and next week I'm presenting at our lab's joint seminar on a paper chapter about mechanistic anomaly detection — the ARC-style setup where you train a classifier to flag inputs that the model processes differently than usual, so you can catch "the model doing something new" without needing labeled examples of the bad behavior. My thesis: when such a system says the model is using a different mechanism, that's never a brute fact about the network. Which clusters of internal states count as "the same mechanism" gets fixed partly by which downstream behaviors we're trying to catch, so same-mechanism is interest-relative to a declared target class. And I want to argue this is fine, actually — the concept gets its grip from that relativity, and MAD systems should just declare the behavior-of-concern up front instead of pretending there's a mechanism boundary out there in weight-space waiting to be discovered.

The audience is roughly ten mech interp researchers who think in circuits and features and get impatient with definitional hand-wringing ("just measure it"), plus two philosophers of science who think mechanism individuation is the deepest question in the field and have been waiting years for someone from the ML side to walk into their seminar.

Give me the objections I'll actually get, in roughly the order they'll arrive, phrased the way each group would phrase them — the interp folks in their idiom (features, circuits, causal-scrubbing-style hypothesis testing), the philosophers in theirs (Craver-style decomposition, multiple realizability) — ordered by how much damage each one does. For each: a ~two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Include the question im hoping nobody asks.

Context that will help you target things: my running example is a model fine-tuned on a new input format, where whether the checker flags "new mechanism" flips depending on whether you cluster by raw activation patterns or by which pairs of queries the states support the same verdicts on. I claim the second clustering is the right one for safety and that this choice does all the work. But the obvious objection from the interp side is that the second clustering is just behavioral similarity in disguise, and if that lands the whole framework collapses into ordinary behavioral anomaly detection — which would, honestly, gut the research program. Be straight with me about whether that objection just wins, I'd rather find out now than in the seminar.
