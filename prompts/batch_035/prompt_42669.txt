I chair the hiring committee of a twenty-five-person social science department, and I've come to suspect we have an information-cascade problem in miniature. Our procedure for ranking the four finalists for our one tenure-track line is a roundtable discussion in a fixed speaking order — our most senior member, who is genuinely insightful, speaks first, and our most junior members speak last. Across three hiring cycles, the first two speakers' assessments predict the final ranking almost perfectly: not because they're always right, but because nobody after them volunteers the private reservations that made them lukewarm on the eventual top choice. Last spring a junior colleague told me privately that our top-ranked candidate's job talk was mostly the dissertation recycled with one new case study, but by the time her turn came the room had visibly converged and she said something diplomatic instead. When I raised this with a colleague over coffee, he said the economics literature has a name for this — information cascades — and that the unsettling part is that nobody needs to be spineless for it to happen: a sequence of individually rational agents can each correctly infer that the accumulated judgments of earlier speakers outweigh their own private signal, so each rationally ignores what only they know, and the group herds on the assessment of the first two speakers even when the aggregated private information in the room pointed elsewhere. He also said something I didn't fully follow about cascades being fragile — that a single public dissent can collapse one — which, if true, might matter for how drastic our fix needs to be.

The committee is split on what to do, and I need to referee it fairly. Roughly half want mandatory independent written assessments of each finalist, submitted before any discussion, followed by open discussion and then a vote. The other half think this is a cure for a fantasy ailment: the whole point of discussion, they say, is that junior members learn from senior members' reactions — seeing what questions our senior colleague asks of a talk is itself information — so flattening the room into one-person-one-signal throws away genuine expertise transfer, and expertise should carry more weight. Their clincher is that our last three hires have worked out fine, so where exactly is the damage? I confess I find that argument weakest of all: if cascades are real, a committee can herd its way into decent outcomes for years while systematically underperforming the information it collectively holds, which is precisely the kind of failure you can't detect from inside the room — the error doesn't show up as a bad hire, it shows up as the counterfactual better hire we never seriously considered.

Please draft the decision memo I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend one concrete procedure for our ranking meeting — specifics on what gets submitted before the meeting, by whom, and how the discussion and vote are then run (including whether the speaking order changes and what role, if any, the written assessments play once discussion begins) — and it should engage the strongest version of the losing side rather than caricature it, including the possibility that they're partly right that some deference to accumulated judgment is rational rather than pathological, and the question of whether the fragility point cuts for or against the full written procedure. Begin with the recommendation in your first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble, no restating my description of the problem, and nothing beyond the memo itself.
