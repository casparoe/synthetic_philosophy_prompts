For a long time I've held to a principle that sounded simple: inequalities are fine when they trace back to people's choices and unfair when they trace back to luck they never chose. Lately I've come to suspect that the principle, once you press on it, isn't one theory but a family with quite different members, and that some of the things we'd want from any of them can't all be had at once. So before I commit further, I want the specification rather than another defense of the slogan. Write the desiderata: what any theory that divides fate into what you chose and what you didn't has to deliver. Some candidate requirements I can already see, and I'd like them all assessed along with ones I've missed: a way of drawing the chosen/unchosen line that survives the case of the person whose upbringing made bad choices nearly inevitable (so the line's own inputs look like luck); a verdict on the gambler who knew the odds versus the one who couldn't have; a treatment of expensive tastes, costly religious observances, and the decision to have children; a stance on whether a floor exists below which the imprudent may not be allowed to fall even if they chose their way there, and whether admitting that floor quietly abandons the principle; and some account of how you even measure who is worse off across very different lives. For each requirement, give the case that motivates it and say whether it's non-negotiable or just what one camp happens to want. Most importantly, tell me if some subset of these is jointly unsatisfiable — that's what I actually need to know before going further. After that, briefly: how does a theory that ignores choice entirely, one built on imagining people insuring against their unchosen disadvantages, and a hybrid that keeps a guaranteed minimum, each fare against the list? Keep the scoring short; I care about the requirements.
