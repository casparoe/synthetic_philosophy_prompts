Draft the joint statement that would emerge from an adversarial collaboration between two parties who have spent a decade arguing about the common prior assumption in Bayesian modeling and formal epistemology, and who have finally sat down to produce a document both can sign.

The first party — call her the Harsanyian — holds that the common prior assumption is a methodological necessity and a regulative ideal. In games with incomplete information, or in any multi-agent analysis, the modeler must somehow represent the agents' beliefs, and on her view a difference in prior is always ultimately one of two things: a covert difference in information (in which case the state space was mis-specified and should be expanded) or a piece of irrationality to be exposed rather than accommodated. Agreement theorems and no-trade results strike her not as curiosities but as boundary markers of rational belief: they tell us what agents would converge to under full transparency, and approximating those conditions — through cheap talk, belief disclosure, or arbitration — is what well-run epistemic communities should actively do.

The second party — the pluralist — holds that prior differences are real, often reasonable, and not always traceable to anything usefully called information. Priors, he argues, encode structural model choices, inductive biases, and value-laden weightings of error costs that can legitimately differ between agents even under perfect transparency. Imposing a common prior, on his view, launders the analyst's own commitments into the model and erases the legitimate disagreement that a community should coordinate around rather than eliminate.

The joint statement should contain:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — about Bayesian updating, about what agreement theorems and no-trade results actually establish given their premises, about the distinction between descriptive and normative uses of the common prior assumption, and about what would count as progress here. Include enough substance that the section is not vacuous: both parties, for instance, can presumably agree on the conditional truths, but they may also be able to agree on stronger unconditional claims, and you should find which ones survive.

2. A disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is stated in wording its own side would accept — precise enough that a reader could tell what a resolution would look like, and not slanted toward either party. Test each row by asking whether the party it describes could read it aloud without objection.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party. Candidates the parties have floated, which you may refine, split, add to, or reject as pseudo-cruxes:

   - The expansion move. Any two Bayesians can be represented as sharing a common prior over an expanded state space in which facts like "which structural model this agent uses" are random variables. The Harsanyian reads this as showing that the common prior assumption is always available; the pluralist reads it as showing the assumption is trivially satisfiable and therefore does no work. Determine whether this particular dispute is substantive or merely verbal, and if it is verbal, say exactly which part and why.

   - The audit question. When two agents disagree after each has seen the other's full belief state, is the residue best described as "difference in prior" or as "difference in information the state space failed to capture"? The parties suspect their entire disagreement partly reduces to a bookkeeping choice about where the state space ends and the prior begins, but each has a different account of why the bookkeeping choice matters (or doesn't).

   - The normative force of the idealization. Even granting that common-prior conditions are never literally met, do the theorems describe an ideal that real agents and institutions should approximate, or an artifact of idealization on par with frictionless planes — useful for theory, misleading as a target for practice? Note that the two parties may agree on the frictionless-plane analogy while disagreeing about which side of the analogy common priors fall on.

   - The no-trade test. Persistent, hard-to-explain trade in liquid markets: does it count against common priors, against common knowledge of rationality, or against neither (e.g., liquidity provision and risk-sharing explain it)? The parties disagree about what this market evidence licenses.

4. For each crux, at least one concrete case, protocol, or argument both parties would accept as bearing on it, with each party's prediction recorded in advance. One such protocol both have already agreed to treat as evidence, and which you should state and use: the prior-merger experiment. Pairs of forecasters with documented, persistent disagreement are periodically required to adopt a randomly drawn convex combination of their two priors and thereafter update separately on their own information streams. Both parties accept that the pairs' subsequent calibration and resolution bear on the question of whether prior differences behave like noise (which merging should improve on) or like structural commitments (which averaging should degrade). Write down what each party predicts and why.

Constraints: the document should be something both parties could actually put their names to, so do not smuggle in a verdict on who is right. But do not paper over disagreement with hedges — the disagreements section should be the sharpest part of the document. End with a short joint note on what, if anything, both agree would actually move the debate, or a joint admission that they cannot yet identify anything.
