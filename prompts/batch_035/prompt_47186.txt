I've been writing fiction for about fifteen years, and there's a pattern in my working life that I have never managed to put a name to, so let me describe it and ask whether philosophy has one. When I draft a scene of dialogue, I make hundreds of micro-decisions — where to break a line, when to let a character lie, which sentence to cut — with a reliability I trust completely; the prose comes out right. But whenever anyone asks me to state the principle behind a given choice, what comes out of my mouth is either trivially empty ("it improves the rhythm") or demonstrably false as a general rule. I once taught a workshop where I wrote down ten of my working rules for dialogue on a whiteboard and my students used them to produce uniformly dead prose, while I could not write a single bad line under the same constraints if I tried. My ability to state my standards and my ability to meet them have almost no detectable relationship to each other, and the stated versions are worse than useless when transferred to anyone else.

There is a second half to the pattern that I suspect matters. I can state, precisely and with evidence, a fact about my own craft that I am entirely unable to act on: I know — in the ordinary, citable sense — that my endings rush, I can point at three published novels of mine where the final chapter compresses what should have taken fifty pages, and this propositional knowledge has never once helped me slow down. So within one person I find skill that cannot be articulated, articulation that does not transfer, and articulated knowledge of a failure that produces no improvement. A friend once mentioned offhand that philosophers have an old distinction between knowing-how and knowing-that, but my situation doesn't look like a clean instance of either side: it's the weird two-way dissociation, together with the fact that my post-hoc explanations of my own choices seem to be rationalizations rather than reports, that I'm trying to pin down. Is there an established concept, or family of concepts, that covers exactly this configuration — and if the standard ones only cover parts of it, I'd want to know which parts, what the nearest candidates fail to capture, and where the edges of each lie. A bare label won't help me; I want the carving.

I'd also ask that you be candid about the fit: say how confident you are in each identification you offer, and single out the point in your answer you're least sure of — I'd rather know where the concept I'm being handed is doing real work and where it's just the closest shelf I could reach.
