I'm a junior in high school and a member of our school's philosophy club. Last month we discussed Parfit's hitchhiker — the scenario where a driver rescues a stranded traveler only because she correctly predicts the traveler will pay her afterward, and where the traveler who reasons case-by-case about his own interest keeps his money only by never getting rescued, while the one who genuinely has the disposition to pay gets out of the desert. Our club advisor said this is supposed to show that being disposed to act against your own interests can serve your interests overall, and that people sometimes call this rational irrationality. I want to test how far that idea really goes, and I'd rather have a case built to order than another variation on the desert island.

Construct a thought experiment with the following specifications. First, the setting must be realistic — an ordinary workplace, lending, or family situation, with stakes a normal person could face — and there may be no perfect predictors: whoever predicts the agent's future behavior must be no better than a shrewd person who has known the agent for years. Second, the advantage of the irrational disposition must not come from merely seeming to have it; the case should be arranged so that faking is impossible or pointless, so that only an agent who genuinely has the disposition benefits. Third, the advantage must not depend on repeated dealings, reputation, or anyone finding out afterward — it should pay off even in a strictly one-shot situation.

After presenting the scenario, please do three things. Walk through the reasoning of three agents in the case: (a) an agent who decides, at each moment, whatever then best serves her interests; (b) an agent with the relevant irrational disposition; and (c) a fully rational agent who is allowed to publicly and irrevocably bind herself in advance if she chooses to. Then identify which single feature of the case is doing the work, and tell me what happens to the verdict if that feature is removed while everything else is held fixed. Finally, explain how a defender of case-by-case rationality would attack the case: whether she would deny that the disposition is really irrational, deny that the agent who has it does better, or insist that the rational agent in (c) can get the same payoff without giving anything up.

Please keep the entire answer under 650 words, and open with a single sentence stating which feature of your case you expect to do the work, so I can follow the argument better once the details arrive.
