I'm putting together a position statement on scientific method for a philosophy of physics reading group, and before I circulate it I'd like a consistency check, because I suspect something in my package is off and I can't tell what. Here is the package. First, I'm a scientific realist: the empirical success of our mature theories would be miraculous unless they were at least approximately true, so I think electrons and spacetime curvature are real. Second, I hold that confirmation is essentially predictive: a theory earns credit by successfully predicting observations, with the credit scaling with how surprising the prediction was beforehand, while merely accommodating already-known data earns almost nothing. Third, and partly as a consequence, I think string theory is currently confirmed to a negligible degree — after several decades it has produced no sharp, testable novel prediction, and the enormous landscape of possible vacua looks to me like a liability rather than an asset. Fourth, I think the recent fashion for "non-empirical confirmation" is confused: the no-alternatives argument (the absence of rival research programs raises the probability that the one we have is on the right track), the meta-inductive argument (this community's past programs, which had a similar profile, eventually succeeded), and the appeal to unexpected explanatory coherence among disparate constraints all strike me as sociology dressed up as confirmation theory. Fifth, and here is where I suspect the trouble, I think the anthropic multiverse explanation of fine-tuning is good science: the tiny observed value of the cosmological constant is well explained as a selection effect among a huge ensemble of vacua or universes in which the constant varies, and the 1998 observation of dark energy seems to me to have confirmed that explanatory framework to some real degree, since anthropic reasoning constrained the value in advance to within a few orders of magnitude. Sixth, I think inference to the best explanation is a legitimate route to belief — when a theory explains a range of phenomena that would otherwise be a string of coincidences, that counts in its favor even without novel predictions.

What I'd like from you is an audit, not a lecture. Which pairs of these commitments are genuinely in tension, which merely look like they are, and what is the cheapest repair in each case? I'm especially unsure how my second commitment — the predictive picture of confirmation — is supposed to interact with my realism and my enthusiasm for the anthropic explanation, since neither of those seems to be powered by novel predictions in the straightforward sense, and the ensemble that does the anthropic work in the fifth commitment is, as far as I can tell, largely the string-theoretic landscape whose epistemic credentials I denied in the third and fourth. But don't assume I've located the problem correctly. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, say for each what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the package, and tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I'd also like you to flag any tension that only appears if one of my commitments is stated in its strongest form and dissolves at a weaker formulation, so I can see where I have room to retreat.
