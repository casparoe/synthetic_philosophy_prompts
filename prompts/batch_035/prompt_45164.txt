Bit of a personal one. I've just come into some money from my nan's estate -- not life-changing, but enough that I feel I should be doing something genuinely useful with a chunk of it, and my partner and I have been going back and forth ever since. I want to give to a charity that does direct cash transfers to very poor households abroad. My dad thinks this is naive and keeps reaching for the old give-a-man-a-fish-teach-a-man-to-fish line -- meaning I should fund vocational training or tools or something that builds people's capacity rather than just handing over money. Meanwhile my oldest friend is a big Peter Singer fan and says the whole thing is like walking past a child drowning in a shallow pond: the fact that they're far away doesn't change what I owe them, and the pond case supposedly shows the only real question is whether I give at all, not how.

So I'm stuck between two analogies that both get used constantly in arguments about what rich people and rich countries owe poor ones, and they seem to point in different directions. The fish one says don't give the thing itself, give the ability to get the thing -- it treats poverty as basically a skills gap. The pond one says just give, now, and stop fussing about mechanism -- it treats poverty as an emergency where the donor's only excuse for not helping is that helping costs them something significant.

Can you audit both for me? I want a proper correspondence table for each: what plays the role of the fish, the fisherman, the pond, the bystander, the wet clothes, the whole works. Then where each mapping breaks -- I can already smell one problem with the fish one, which is that plenty of poor people already know how to fish and are poor anyway (no net, no pond, someone else takes the fish), and I want to know wether the proverb is doing any real work beyond sounding wise. Same for the pond: does it sneak in stuff about identifiable victims and proximity that a distant beneficiary doesn't have, and about how many other bystanders there are standing around the pond, and about whether the drowning is an emergency rather than a chronic condition? And then the part I actually need answered: do either of the conclusions survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and which one should a person in my position -- deciding between cash transfers and capacity-building programmes -- follow? Or are they not really rivals at all, but answers to two seperate questions? If so tell me what the better framing is, and what a better analogy would be if there is one. Please write the answer in British English (realise, programme, etc. -- not that I trust myself to spot the difference).
