Two engineers on my team have been arguing for over a year about whether the left/right distinction in politics picks out anything real, and I've been asked to moderate something like a structured write-up of where they actually stand. One of them — call him the axis theorist — thinks left/right is a genuine single dimension: whatever else changes, he says, the axis always comes down to how much inequality between people a political position is willing to tolerate or even value. The other — call her the coalition theorist — thinks that dimension is an illusion created by historical accident: left/right is just a label for two shifting bundles of groups that happened to be seated on opposite sides of a French assembly, and the bundles get reshuffled every few decades, so asking what "really" unites each side is like asking what really unites the customers of one supermarket versus another.

I've read that people who do adversarial collaborations write a joint document first: what both sides accept, what they disagree about stated in words each side would sign, the specific sub-questions that would actually move someone, and predictions each side commits to in advance. Please draft that document for the two of them. A few things I want it to cover: whether they can agree on some observable claims about how political positions actually cluster; whether the disagreement is partly just about what it takes for a distinction to be "real" (a natural kind versus a useful summary statistic versus a self-fulfilling social fact); and, as cruxes, at least (1) how each side predicts a new political issue with no prior left/right valence — say, regulation of AI, or geoengineering — will get absorbed into the axis or stay cross-cutting, and (2) whether someone's stance on economic equality reliably predicts their stances on matters that seem orthogonal to it, like criminal justice, immigration, or aesthetic judgments about hierarchy in workplaces. For each crux, record what each theorist would predict and what pattern of evidence each would concede is a problem for their own view. If you think part of their dispute is really a verbal one about the word "real," say which part and why. Format it as a numbered section of agreed claims, followed by a disagreements section, followed by the cruxes with predictions.
