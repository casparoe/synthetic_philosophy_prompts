I'm the chair of the design review board at a mid-size company that makes both consumer electronics and implantable medical devices -- same engineers, same test labs, very different consequences if something fails inside a person versus inside a kitchen. Our gate reviews are 30 minutes, weekly, and the recurring fight is always about the word "know." An engineer presents the test data and says we know the part meets spec; someone from risk management says no, we don't know, we have to re-run the fatigue tests on another batch and add an inspection step; and half the time the only difference between the two statements is what the part is going INTO -- the evidence is identical. Meanwhile we can't use "know" loosely in the submission documents to the regulator but can use it freely in the internal engineering report, and a junior engineer recently asked, seemingly naively, whether the word means something different in the two documents, which honestly stopped the meeting for a good five minutes.

I've been reading a bit about epistemology on the side -- something about how whether you count as knowing something can depend on what's at stake, and about how the meaning or standards of "knows" can shift with the conversational setting -- and it struck me that our board is implicitly operating on some theory here without ever writing it down, and probably not a consistent one. So I'd like you to act as a professional epistemologist who consults on practical decision procedures. First brainstorm a little: lay out what the philosophy actually says that's usable here -- does it license demanding more evidence when stakes go up even though evidence is unchanged, or does it only license being more careful about when you SAY "know," or does it conflate knowledge with being justified in acting? Then turn whatever survives into an actual procedure the board can run inside a review: an ordered checklist of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure terminates in a "yes, approved," where it forces more evidence, and where it must stop and hand the call to a human because the remaining question is one of values or risk appetite rather than facts -- mark those points explicitly, I don't want false precision.

Then run the procedure on three concrete cases so we can see it work and see it break: (1) a battery seal that passed all standard tests, going into a consumer speaker; (2) the exact same seal and same data, but going into an implantable pump where a leak means surgery to replace it; (3) a firmware fix that fixed a known crash bug in a home device, where demanding more evidence is cheap but adds weeks, and a marketing person insists the fix is "known safe" because nothing like it has ever failed before -- that third one I expect the procedure to answer in a way marketing won't like, and I want to see that. Also please be upfront about where the procedure will misfire: for instance where our stakes classifications will turn out to have been wrong in hindsight, or where someone could game the checklist to manufacture a "known." And tell me honestly whether the philosophy itself is actually settled enough that the procedure follows from it, or whether the procedure is just one reasonable operationalization among several -- if a colleague insists that knowledge can't literally depend on stakes and that we're just sneaking decision theory in the back door, I want to know if the procedure survives that objection or has to be rebuilt on different ground. Think carefully and don't just give me textbook definitions -- I need the thing my board can actually print out and use next Tuesday.
