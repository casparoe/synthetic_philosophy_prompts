A podcast I listen to on AI explained Searle's Chinese Room argument roughly as follows: Searle imagined a man locked in a room who follows an enormous rulebook for responding to slips of paper passed in to him written in Chinese, and who gets so good at applying the rules that his replies become indistinguishable from those of a native speaker. Since the man doesn't understand a word of Chinese, and since a computer program is nothing but such a rulebook, no program understands anything; programs are all syntax and no semantics. This, the host said, is why the Turing test was never a real test of understanding, and why Searle concluded that understanding is a biological capacity that no machine will ever have. When listeners wrote in with the systems reply — that the understanding belongs to the whole setup, man plus rulebook, rather than to the man alone — the host said that had been settled long ago, because Searle showed the man could simply memorize the entire rulebook and do all the work in his head, leaving nothing over in which the understanding could reside. The episode ended by drawing a present-day moral: this is why today's language models can never really mean anything by what they produce, and why their fluency should be regarded as a trick rather than talked up.
