I'd like a careful adjudication of a dispute about the two-envelope paradox. Please write your answer in American English.

The setup is the usual one: two envelopes, one containing twice the other, and you take one at random. My first friend argues as follows.

(1) Let A denote the amount in your envelope.
(2) The other envelope contains either 2A or A/2.
(3) Since the envelopes were assigned by a fair coin, each of these is equally likely.
(4) So the expected value of the other envelope is (1/2)(2A) + (1/2)(A/2) = (5/4)A, which exceeds the value of yours.
(5) The computation nowhere uses the value of A, so it is available before you open your envelope, after you open it, and equally to whoever holds the other envelope.
(6) So each holder should prefer to trade; if trades are free, each should swap, and swap back, and swap again, expected value rising by a quarter at every exchange of the same two envelopes.
(7) A decision rule that yields this is incoherent; therefore maximizing expected value is not a coherent requirement of rationality.

The usual diagnosis I've encountered puts the weight on (3): the conditional probabilities of doubling and halving cannot both be one-half for every amount you might see, so the indifference in (3) is not licensed. My second friend thinks this misses what is really wrong, and he offers a construction.

Suppose the envelopes are filled in pairs containing (2^n, 2^{n+1}) dollars for n = 1, 2, 3, ...; pair n is selected with probability (1/3)(2/3)^{n-1}; and the two envelopes are then dealt by fair coin. If you open your envelope and see 2^n with n at least 2, the other envelope contains 2^{n+1} with probability 2/5 and 2^{n-1} with probability 3/5, so its conditional expectation is (11/10) times what you see; and if you see 2, the other envelope certainly contains 4. (My own arithmetic confirms his.) He draws three morals. (i) In this setup, "switch, whatever you see" is correct at every observation, and not through any illicit one-half/one-half assignment — the probabilities are 2/5 and 3/5, and they favor switching — so the standard diagnosis, even where it locates a flaw in the first argument, does not touch the real phenomenon. (ii) Since switching is favored conditional on every possible observation, it is favored outright, and you should switch without opening. (iii) If, after switching, you open the new envelope, the very same computation favors switching back; each open-and-swap raises conditional expectation by ten percent, so the pump of step (6) is real and not an artifact of bad probabilities. He adds that in his setup the expected value of each envelope is infinite, takes this to show that expectation has simply broken down, and concludes that the first friend was right for the wrong reasons.

Please (a) say exactly where the first argument goes wrong, why the offending step looked unobjectionable, and what rejecting it costs; (b) verify the second friend's conditional computation, and explain whether and how switching can be favorable conditional on every observation even though the envelopes were dealt symmetrically — that is, what exactly fails, if anything does, when one passes from the conditional claims to an unconditional one; (c) evaluate the three morals he draws, especially the move from (i) to (ii) and the pump in (iii); and (d) adjudicate what, if anything, survives of the conclusion that expected-value maximization is incoherent. Where you invoke a theorem of probability, state it precisely rather than gesturing at it.
