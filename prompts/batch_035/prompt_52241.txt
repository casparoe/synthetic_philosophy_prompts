I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy, and our metaethics reading group is finishing the term on moral relativism. We've worked through the standard dialectic — descriptive cultural relativism as the empirical premise, the leap (or non-leap) to the metaethical thesis that the truth or justification of moral judgements is relative to a framework, the self-refutation objection, and the moral-progress objection — and we've ended somewhere uncomfortable: relativism looks either too weak to be interesting or too strong to be liveable with. For our last session I want to close with a poem rather than another paper, and I'd like you to write it.

Please take on the role of a poet who also holds a doctorate in metaethics — someone who knows the difference between relativism about truth and relativism about justification, who can tell Harman's version from Wong's, and who would never write something like "who's to say?" as though it were an argument. Think carefully before writing anything down: sketch to yourself what the poem's central confrontation should be, and how the form can carry the philosophy rather than merely decorate it, then compose.

The form I want is a pantoum: quatrains in which the second and fourth lines of each stanza return as the first and third lines of the next, with the poem closing by repeating the opening line (and, if you like, the third line of the first stanza as well) — so that the whole thing is woven almost entirely from recycled lines. I've chosen this form deliberately. A repeated line arriving in a new context can be read as saying something different, which is either the strongest image we have for moral relativism — the same utterance, embedded in a different world, carrying a different force — or a trick the reader plays on herself, the recontextualisation happening only in her head. Part of me wants the poem to leave that ambiguity standing, since it is really the question our whole term has been circling: when two people separated by a moral framework utter the same sentence and mean incompatible things by it, have we a genuine disagreement or merely a difference?

So the commission: write a pantoum depicting a confrontation between two speakers whose moral frameworks do not mesh — an anthropologist and a member of the community she studies would do, or any pairing that does the same work — in which at least one line is voiced by both parties at different points and means something different each time it returns. The poem should take seriously both the relativist's insight, that judgements get their point from a framework that need not be shared, and the standard worry that relativism leaves us with nothing to say to the other side at all; it should dramatise that tension rather than resolve it, and I'd rather it not smuggle in a smug universalist ending. Somewhere in it I want a trace of Bernard Williams' thought that some confrontations are merely notional — that you cannot genuinely confront a way of life you have no real option of living — but do this structurally or imagistically, without name-dropping.

Alongside the poem, please give me a short note, in continuous prose, explaining where the poem takes liberties with the ideas or leans on a contested reading — for instance, whether you've treated relativism as a thesis about truth or about justification, or assumed a reading of Williams that not everyone in the room will accept — and please single out the interpretive choice you are least confident about, so I can flag it for discussion. Please write the whole response without any headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables: the poem in plain lines, the note as flowing prose, the way one would write a letter to the reading group.
