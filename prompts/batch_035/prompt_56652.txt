I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background at all, and last week our team dinner somehow turned into a two-hour argument about whether "left vs right" even means anything anymore. Since then I've been trying to figure out what I actually think by writing down my gut reactions to whether certain bundles of views *genuinely belong together* or are just glued together by coalition politics, and now I want to know if there's a coherent principle underneath my gut reactions or if I'm just pattern-matching vibes. As an engineer I keep picturing it like running PCA on survey data: there's clearly a dominant first component, everyone answers correlated questions in correlated ways. But a principal component is just a direction of maximal variance — it doesn't have to correspond to anything real. It could be a natural kind, or it could be an artifact of two coalitions that happen to have sorted all the issues into two bins over a century of us-vs-them politics. Those feel like very different situations to me and I want to know which one we're in, or whether that's even a real distinction.

Here are my verdicts. Pro-redistribution and pro-choice travel together almost everywhere in the West, and I think that's a *real* connection, not coalition glue — they genuinely belong on the same side of something. Free-market economics and social traditionalism travel together in the US today, but that one feels contingent — go back 150 years and the classical liberal free-marketeers were often radical social reformers, so that pairing smells like an accident of how 20th century coalitions froze. Environmentalism and anti-nuclear sentiment both feel "left" to me, but the anti-nuclear part seems like a historical accident (the 70s left something in the water, pun intended) — being scared of nuclear power isn't intrinsically anything. Then the one that really messes with me: a pro-market, socially permissive libertarian is a real, common position, but to me it doesn't read as a *blend* of left and right or a midpoint — it reads as off-axis entirely, its own thing. Meanwhile a pro-market *social conservative* reads as genuinely, intensely right-wing, not as a blend either. That's weird, right? If left/right is one axis, both of those people should be intermediate between left and right, and they're clearly not intermediate in the same way. And a case I keep flip-flopping on: someone who wants heavy redistribution but is socially conservative (they exist in lots of countries) — are they left, right, both, or proof the axis is fake?

A friend who did poli sci undergrad told me the axis is "really" about attitudes toward hierarchy and inequality — the left is roughly "flatten it," the right is roughly "some hierarchy is natural and fine" — and that all my "contingent" verdicts come from issues that have nothing to do with hierarchy getting dragged along as coalition baggage. So that's my tentative principle, and I want you to stress-test it: does it actually deliver all my verdicts above, and what does it predict for cases I haven't listed — like a tech manager who loves hierarchy and stack rankings at work but votes left, or where nationalism fits (it's hierarchy-flavored between nations but anti-hierarchy toward global elites?). If the principle fails anywhere, tell me which of my verdicts has to go and whether some other principle does better, and please don't just tell me it's "complicated" — I want a real answer with the tradeoffs made explicit. Also, since I genuinely don't know this stuff: tell me what would change your mind, i.e. what argument or observation would push you toward "left/right is a fake category, just coalition sorting" or toward a different principle entirely, and flag where your own reasoning here is most likely to go wrong.
