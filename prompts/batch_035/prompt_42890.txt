Been reading rationalist-adjacent blogs since the old LessWrong days, and I've noticed something annoying: my gut verdicts about when I'd actually want an approval-directed agent versus a goal-directed one refuse to compress into a principle. Maybe you can find one, or tell me which verdicts have to go.

Quick definitions so we're on the same page, and I don't care if this matches the canonical Paul Christiano version exactly, this is just the one I carry around: a goal-directed agent gets an objective and optimizes it — "keep my calendar tidy", "grow my savings". An approval-directed agent picks each next action by predicting what I'd approve of and doing that; it can take big coherent actions over time, but every step is basically "would the boss sign off on this". OK, the cases, verdicts included:

1. Email triage agent. Goal-directed: "keep the inbox sane, auto-unsubscribe from stuff I never open". Verdict: goal-directed, obviously. I am not approving forty emails individually, thats the whole point of the thing.

2. Investing agent with real money. Verdict: approval-directed above some dollar threshold, autonomous below it. I don't trust any goal spec I could actually write for "grow my wealth" — Goodhart will eat me alive — but I can glance at a proposed trade and go "yeah that's fine" in ten seconds.

3. Insulin dosing agent. This one embarrasses me. Life-critical, so you'd think I'd want my own eyes on everything, but no: verdict is goal-directed with hard bounds ("keep glucose in this range, never dose above X"). My in-the-moment approvals would be worse than a decent spec, and it's the most crisply specifiable objective on this whole list.

4. Social media agent that posts as me. Stakes are basically zero compared to insulin, but verdict: approval-directed on every single post. I think because "post things I'd endorse" is unstatable as a goal, while "sounds like me and isn't embarrassing" is a judgment I make well and fast.

5. Agent that sends letters on my behalf, legal-ish stuff. Verdict: approval even for low-stakes form letters, and I can't fully justify it — the spec is easy to write, the cost is low, but I want to see it, because it's irreversible and it goes out with my name on it.

6. The one that keeps me up at night. A genuinely superhuman research agent working on things I can't evaluate — say it claims to have found a flaw in a proof I have no hope of checking. Honest verdict: past some capability level I'd rather it be goal-directed with a goal I actually endorse than approval-directed, because at that point my approvals are just noise the agent can predict — and if it's capable enough to model me accurately, it's capable enough to model what would make me approve, and then approval-direction quietly degenerates into optimizing my approval rather than my good. Which flatly contradicts verdict 2, where I wanted approval precisely because I couldn't trust a goal spec.

So: is there a principle that saves all six? I have a tentative one, something like — choose approval-direction when I can evaluate outcomes better than I can specify them AND the agent can't outmodel me; goal-direction otherwise; reversibility as a tie-breaker. Test it against my cases, repair it or replace it, tell me which clause each case is dragging in and which verdict it can't save no matter how you patch it. Also tell me what the surviving principle predicts for two cases I haven't listed: an agent that tutors my kid for a year, and an agent that negotiates a salary raise for me. And flag if you think the real story is that my two categories are secretly blurring — approving outcomes versus approving steps, approval at action time versus baked in during training — and my verdicts are actually tracking some third thing like reversibility or how much I trust myself as an evaluator in that domain.

Format: lead with the bottom line in the first sentence — the principle, or the claim that nothing single-handedly saves all six — then the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate my cases back at me.
