I work in software engineering, and something about my field's foundations has started to bother me philosophically. Nearly all deployed cryptography is justified by conditional proofs: a scheme is shown to be secure if some unproven mathematical statement holds — factoring is hard, certain lattice problems have no short solutions, a hash function behaves like a random one. Those conditions are open problems in mathematics; no experiment could settle them, and a proof either exists or it doesn't. Yet the whole industry, and the standards bodies, and the auditors, all treat these conditions as effectively settled — we run the world's commerce on them. The confidence behaves in every practical respect like ordinary empirical confidence, except that there is nothing empirical it could be confidence about. And the same community seems to hold the opposite norm elsewhere: a mathematician would refuse to attach a probability to an unproven conjecture on the grounds that its truth value is already fixed and simply unknown, and a journal would never let a proof cite a conjecture as a premise.

What epistemology does this practice presuppose? Does building global infrastructure on unproven mathematical assumptions only make sense if mathematical statements can rationally be held with intermediate confidence — some notion of probabilities over theorems that update over time, where a failed proof attempt or a decades-long absence of a crack in a primitive counts as weak evidence? Or is the practice compatible with the austere view that a mathematical truth is simply not the kind of thing one can be less than certain about until it is proven, with the industry merely making a decision-theoretic bet whose expected value happens to be dominated by the consequences of being wrong? I'd also like to know whether the practice is philosophically underdetermined — whether several quite different views of mathematical knowledge would license the same behaviour — and what would actually change in cryptographic practice if we made the implicit view explicit and took probabilities over open problems seriously, e.g. by letting them vary with evidence rather than treating each assumption as a fixed article of faith.
