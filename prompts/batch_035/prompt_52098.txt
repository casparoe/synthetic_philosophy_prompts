Elections in most democracies are governed by an informal but strongly felt norm of sincerity: civic-education materials, newspaper endorsements, and everyday moral talk treat tactical voting — ranking a compromise candidate above one's genuine favorite because the favorite "can't win" — as a small civic failing, a kind of dishonesty, and treat the sincere ballot as what an upstanding citizen owes the system. Yet the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem tells us that any voting rule over three or more alternatives that is non-dictatorial and onto is manipulable: the mechanism itself guarantees that some voter, in some preference profile, has an incentive to misreport. The same system, in the same polity, contains an opposite norm: a legislator who votes for a bill she dislikes as part of a whip-driven bargain is doing her job, and is praised for it as statecraft. And a third oddity: because ballots are secret, the sincerity norm cannot be verified or enforced — it is left entirely to conscience, which is not how we usually treat duties whose breach is invisible. What conception of voting, and of the voter, does this cluster of norms and design choices presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: does the sincerity norm treat the ballot as a report or testimony of preference — an input whose value depends on its truthfulness — rather than an action chosen for its consequences, and if so, is that presupposition coherent given that the rule provably cannot make truthful reporting in everyone's interest? Does the norm instead quietly rest on a background assumption, e.g., that manipulation stays rare because manipulators cannot coordinate, and would that assumption, rather than any duty of sincerity, actually be what the practice needs? Then address the asymmetry with the legislature: is the contrast defensible on principled grounds — some principal/agent theory of representation, or a difference in repetition and mutual knowledge — or does it reveal that our practices presuppose inconsistent things about what a vote is? Finally, tell me what would have to change if the presupposed expressive view of the ballot were replaced by a straightforwardly strategic, game-theoretic one: would we adopt rules that openly invite strategic behavior (such as approval voting, where "sincere" is not even well defined), abandon sincerity rhetoric while keeping the rules, or discover that the expressive view was doing indispensable work? Distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely fits well with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories of the ballot would license it as it stands.
