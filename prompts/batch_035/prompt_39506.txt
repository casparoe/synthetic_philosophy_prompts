I'm a philosopher (I teach in a philosophy department, mostly philosophy of language and early analytic stuff) giving a colloquium in a linguistics department in two weeks. Semantics there is done almost entirely within the dynamic tradition — the semantics group works with DRT, file-change semantics, update semantics, that whole framework — plus one senior person who's an old Strawsonian about presupposition, and a couple of syntax people who show up for the free food. There are basically no philosophers of language on faculty, just a visitor.

My thesis: the theory of descriptions is not a museum piece. Concretely I'm defending two claims. (1) For what I want to call "cold" contexts — legal texts, scientific writing, aphorisms, anything written to be assessed outside a cooperative face-to-face exchange — Russell's quantificational analysis is still the right one; the dynamic machinery (novelty/familiarity in the file, accommodation, etc.) is doing work about discourse management, not about the truth-conditions of those sentences. (2) Strawson and Donnellan correctly described facts about conversation, but none of it touches the quantificational paraphrase itself — it only shows the paraphrase is one disambiguation among several a speaker can be aiming at.

The sketch I'll actually give: I start from the observation that "the F is G" in a statute (say, a provision like "the surviving spouse shall inherit") can't have its truth depend on whether some spouse was previously salient in anyone's discourse file — the legislature isn't in a conversation with the reader. Then I argue that existence and uniqueness are part of what the law asserts, not presupposed background: if there's no surviving spouse, the provision misfires by being false-in-the-relevant-respect, not by lacking a truth value. Then I generalize: wherever a sentence is meant to be assessed without access to a particular utterance context, the Russellian reading is the default and the referential and dynamic readings are pragmatic overlays on top of it. I close by conceding the anaphora data up front — I'm not claiming "the donkey"-type sentences for Russell, at least not out loud.
