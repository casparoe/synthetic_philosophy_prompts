I'm a software engineer — about ten years in backend and ML infrastructure, currently at a company that trains large models — with no formal background in philosophy or policy. Our org is holding an offsite on safety strategy, and each of us is supposed to post a short position doc to the internal wiki beforehand so people can poke at each other's views. I've drafted my position and, before I post it, I want it stress-tested the way I'd stress-test a design doc: find the internal inconsistencies now, not in the meeting. Here is my position, six points:

1. I accept the competitiveness constraint more or less as commonly stated: any safety measure that noticeably costs capability or speed just moves the frontier to a less careful actor, so a single lab's unilateral restraint accomplishes little beyond handing the future to someone worse.

2. Given (1), the right way to pay the alignment tax is coordination: safety standards that are legally mandated and internationally harmonized, so every actor pays the same tax and nobody is competitively punished for being careful. In particular, things like responsible scaling policies should be legally binding rather than voluntary, because self-set thresholds get waived the moment enough money is on the line.

3. That said, if coordination lags, I'd rather the most careful lab win the race. A decisive lead buys breathing room: the leader can afford deployment gates, additional evals, and slower release cadences that actors in a neck-and-neck race cannot. On this view, racing ahead is itself a safety strategy rather than a betrayal of one.

4. I'm against compute caps or anything resembling a pause. Enforcement is a fantasy, and the moment one jurisdiction pauses, the advantage shifts to states with weaker oversight. You cannot pause your way to safety unilaterally.

5. I favor open-weight release for models below a stated capability threshold (say, autonomous self-replication or serious uplift for bioweapons), because weight diffusion spreads safety research and prevents a cartel of frontier labs. Above that threshold, weights should be restricted. I'm comfortable with tiering even though it complicates the picture.

6. I also think the alignment tax is converging toward zero anyway: post-training techniques like RLHF made models more useful and safer at the same time, which suggests safety and capability pull in the same direction, and so the coordination machinery in (2) is less urgent than the safety community tends to claim.

My honest assessment is that some of these clash and some only sound like they clash, but I can't reliably tell which is which, and I'd rather not find out from a hostile commenter.
