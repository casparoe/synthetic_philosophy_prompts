Two fellow doctoral students in my epistemology program got into a long argument after our reading group about whether practical stakes bear on knowledge, and I couldn't referee it. Ayesha defended pragmatic encroachment: she said the evidence needed to know can rise when being wrong would be costly, so someone who would rationally act on a belief in low stakes may fail to know the very same thing in high stakes, even with identical evidence. Ben, a contextualist about the verb "to know," replied that she had simply rediscovered his view: if standards shift, that is context-sensitivity, and calling the shifting factor "stakes" rather than "conversational standards" just adds a parameter to the same machinery. Ayesha said the difference is genuine and testable: on her view, a single unambiguous sentence can change truth value when the subject's situation changes while the conversation stays fixed, whereas Ben's view requires the word itself to express different relations across contexts of utterance. Ben countered that this is a distinction without a difference, since in practice stakes only get raised by someone raising them, which is a conversational move. Ayesha answered that the stakes can shift with no conversation at all: the bank closes in five minutes whether or not anyone mentions it. Ben's final move was that encroachment confuses knowledge with warranted assertion — what high stakes plausibly affect is what it is reasonable to assert or act on, not whether the belief itself constitutes knowledge.
