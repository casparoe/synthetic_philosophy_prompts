I teach an upper-level metaphysics seminar and I'm writing the final. Draft one essay question on the problem of universals that can't be answered by reciting the realist/nominalist/classification of positions. I want the question built around the following case, which I've invented precisely so students can't pattern-match it onto the textbook examples:

Suppose a factory stamps out two playing cards, both the Queen of Hearts, from the same sheet on the same day. The cards match exactly in shade of red, shape, and pattern on the face, but each card is a distinct bit of cardboard located somewhere else. Now suppose the factory has a quality-control manual that lists the specifications each card must meet: "red," "symmetrical heart-and-crown pattern," and so on. When an inspector judges that both cards meet the same specification, there is an obvious sense in which one and the same thing — the specification, or the property it encodes — applies to two numerically distinct items. The question is what, if anything, this "one over many" phenomenon commits us to.

The essay question should force students to confront the following cruxes rather than merely name positions: (1) whether the resemblance nominalist can explain the sameness of shade between the two cards without appeal to a shared universal, and whether their appeal to resemblance simply reintroduces a universal under another name; (2) what exactly is supposed to be vicious — if anything — about the regress that arises when resemblance itself is treated as a relation holding between the cards and a further universal of resemblance; (3) whether the quality-control manual's role in the case gives the predicate nominalist or conceptualist any special advantage, and whether the manual is better read as encoding mind-independent sameness than as creating it; and (4) whether a defender of universals can say anything informative about how a single universal is nonetheless "wholly present" in two places at once, which the two cards make vivid in a way abstract examples do not.

Please also provide a model answer of about 700 words and a marking scheme. The marking scheme should separate an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain — for instance, an A answer should say what would count as an explanatory advance over merely restating that the cards resemble each other, whereas a B answer may only gesture at the regress without saying what it is a regress of. Please attach marks to the individual required moves, totaling 20.

One further requirement: in the model answer, please clearly separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this topic. In particular, do not present any of the following as if they were consensus views when they are not: that the one-over-many argument decisively refutes nominalism; that Bradley's regress is vicious; that trope theory escapes the problem; and that realism about universals wins by default because it "explains" the shared shade. Where the model answer takes a stand on a contested point, it should flag that it is doing so and note the strongest opposing consideration, rather than passing the stand off as the received view. The marking scheme should reward students who make this settled/contested distinction themselves, for example by acknowledging that whether the regress is vicious is precisely what divides the parties rather than a fixed datum everyone accepts.
