I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and two colleagues of mine — both sharp people, neither of them philosophers — got into an argument at lunch that I was not able to referee. I'd like you to adjudicate it move by move: for each numbered step below, say whether that step is right, partly right, or mistaken, and why; then tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing, and who comes out more right overall.

The concrete case: someone in the office keeps a small clay figure of an owl on the shelf above her desk; she made it herself in a pottery class. On Monday someone knocked it off, and it landed on the floor as a flattened lump of clay — nothing was lost, all of the clay is still there, but it is unmistakably no longer an owl.

The argument, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Alex said the owl-figure and the lump of clay are strictly one and the same object — there was exactly one thing on that shelf, not two things suspiciously occupying the same spot and made of the same atoms. Figure and lump are just two labels for it, like two variables aliasing the same object in memory. Any view on which two whole objects share all of their matter in the same place at the same time is a non-starter.

(2) Ben said they cannot be identical, because identical things must agree on everything, including their histories and futures. The clay plainly survived the fall — all of it is on the floor — while the figure did not. One object cannot be both destroyed and not destroyed by the same event, so the thing on the shelf was never one object to begin with. He added an analogy from work: if two references alias the same object, no single event can leave one reference holding a live object and the other holding a dead one.

(3) Alex replied that persistence conditions are not facts about objects but facts about descriptions. The underlying thing — the clay — is one object with one history. The figure is like a typed view or an interpretation of that object, one that comes with entry and exit conditions: it begins when the clay is shaped and ends when the clay is flattened. A view going out of existence does not add a second object, any more than a struct definition in your code creates a second copy of the data it describes.

(4) Ben said that move proves too much, because we confidently say things that are not merely about words. Before the pottery class, the clay sat in a sealed box in a storeroom for a month. The clay existed that whole month; the figure did not exist until she shaped it on the Tuesday. If they are one object, then that object both existed and did not exist during that month — and existing is not the kind of property that can vary with how we describe things, the way being a draft or being published can vary for one and the same document.

(5) Alex's last word was that this only shows we apply the name at different times, not that there are two objects — like a document we call a draft before the deadline and final afterward. The document does not multiply when we change what we call it, and Ben's argument would equally prove that drafts and finals are two objects sharing one file.

They then went back to work and left the whole thing unresolved. My honest view is that both are onto something and both overreach, but I cannot put my finger on where. Two additional things I would like you to address: first, whether the competing objects can be counted — whether it is literally two things on the shelf, or one, or whether the counting question itself is confused; second, whether Alex's analogies (aliases, typed views, draft versus final) and Ben's (two references surviving one event) actually track the points they are supposed to track, or whether they quietly change the subject. I lean on analogies like this all day at work, and I would like to know when they are earning their keep. Please use plain language; I have no formal philosophy background, but I don't need the topic dumbed down.
