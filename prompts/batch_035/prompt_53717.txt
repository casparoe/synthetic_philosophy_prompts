I edit the reader-dilemma column of a small ethics blog where people write in about ordinary moral predicaments — whether to tell a sibling what their spouse said at dinner, whether to keep a promise that has become inconvenient, whether a white lie at a bedside counts as a lie at all. Commenters constantly take positions that sound like the particularism/generalism debate, but almost none of them know the labels, and when I use the labels they accuse each other of jargon. So I want to build a sorting quiz, titled "What Kind of Moral Reasoner Are You?", that places readers on the particularism–generalism spectrum by their verdicts on cases alone: no question may ask whether the reader believes in moral principles, whether morality has laws, or use any term of art. The quiz should have eight questions, each a short concrete case with two or three answer options phrased as plain verdicts ("that's fine here," "that would be wrong here, though it wouldn't be in other settings," and so on), plus a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto positions. The key must be able to output: exceptionless generalism of a roughly Kantian sort; W. D. Ross–style prima facie generalism, where principles are real but defeasible defaults; Jonathan Dancy–style particularism, where a feature's contribution can depend on the rest of the case; a pure judgment-without-principles position; and an "inconsistent — try again" output for readers whose answers don't cohere, with a sentence about what pattern puts someone there.

To keep the quiz diagnostic rather than a personality test, I have three requirements on the cases. First, at least two questions must be built so that the same feature flips what it contributes: something like a promise extracted under duress, or "it will hurt her feelings" counting against telling a hard truth to a friend but in favor of telling it to someone who needs to hear exactly that. These flip-cases are what separate Rossians, who keep a default, from Dancy-style particularists, who don't. Second, at least one question must separate absolutists from Rossians — a case where a principle is overridden and the reader has to say whether the override is real or just shows the principle was never exceptionless. Third, please don't use the murderer-at-the-door case; it's so canonical that readers answer from memory of the debate rather than from their own judgment, and it tips them off about what's being tested.

Finally, be honest about the instrument's limits. Flag any pair of positions the quiz cannot separate no matter how the cases are designed — I suspect Ross's prima facie view and Dancy's particularism agree on every concrete verdict whenever the default happens to be overridden, so the quiz may only be able to sort on the meta-question of whether the reader thinks the principle still exerts a pull in the case where it loses, and you should say explicitly that this is the only wedge available and whether it's a reliable one. Keep the whole thing, quiz and scoring key together, under 750 words.
