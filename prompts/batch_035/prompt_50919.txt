My mother retires this spring after forty years of teaching metaphysics, most of them spent on a single quarrel: whether the standard counterexamples to the conditional analysis of dispositions — finks, which would take the disposition away before it could manifest, and masks, which leave the disposition in place but block its manifestation — are genuinely different things or two faces of one phenomenon. Her career went into defending the second, unfashionable view: finks and masks, she insists, are the same thing seen at different stages, and the distinction between them is an artifact of how philosophers like to draw their conditionals. I'm giving the toast at her retirement dinner, where half the room will be metaphysicians who have argued with her about this since the 1990s and the other half will be relatives who assume "dispositions" means moods. Please write the toast so that the fink/mask issue does real structural work — ideally one extended analogy tying her position to her life, her teaching, or her retirement, so the family actually leaves understanding what she spent her life on. The analogy must be technically correct enough that her colleagues cannot spot a slip: she will be listening for one, and in forty years she has never let one pass. And it should land as affection rather than lecture. One specific request: she has forbidden ending with the standard toastmaster's move of declaring her "disposed to inspire" — after four decades spent failing to analyze dispositions, she refuses to be invoked as an unanalyzed one, so the toast needs a different close.
