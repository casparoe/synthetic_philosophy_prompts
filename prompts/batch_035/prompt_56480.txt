ok so I'm in a personal identity seminar this semester and we just got to Dennett's line that the self is basically a center of narrative gravity — a posited point around which the story of a person hangs together, not a little homunculus you find by looking inward. For my term paper I wanted to connect that to how the law actually handles these things, and now I'm stuck, because the law seems to quietly presuppose some pretty specific view of the self.

here's the practice. Advance directives are enforceable basically everywhere they exist: if I'm competent and sign a document saying that if I develop advanced dementia I'm not to be given a feeding tube even if I seem content, then years later, when I actually have advanced dementia and cheerfully ask for the feeding tube, my doctors and the courts treat the document, not my current asking, as what settles it. Wills work the same way: whether a will is valid turns on my mental state at the moment I signed it, not on anything true of me at death. So the pattern is that the earlier, competent self's wishes govern, and the later self's wishes can be legally irrelevant even when the later self is the only one still around to have wishes.

but the same legal systems refuse to let a past self bind a present self in almost every other context. You can't contract yourself into slavery, contracts require ongoing assent, you can drop out of a research study at any point even after signing a form promising to stay in, and most advance directive statutes say you can revoke the thing at any time, in any manner, without saying anything about who counts as "you" when you're no longer competent. So what is it about dementia specifically that lets the earlier self win?

What I want to know is what theory of the self the enforceability of advance directives actually presupposes. Is it the Dworkin-style view that there are two kinds of interests — judgments about how your life as a whole should go versus moment-to-moment comfort — and the competent self's global judgments trump the demented self's local ones? Or a psychological continuity view on which severe dementia severs identity, so enforcing the directive is basically like honoring the wishes of someone who no longer exists? Or does it fit Dennett's narrative gravity idea, where the author of the narrative keeps editing rights and the demented self has lost authorship? And is the practice even internally consistent, given the revocation rules — it seems hard to explain, on most of these theories, why a last-minute revocation by a competent patient counts but requests from a demented patient don't, unless there's a competence criterion doing all the work, in which case maybe identity isn't doing any work at all.

So reconstruct the implicit theory for me, tell me whether the practice strictly needs one particular view of the self or whether several rival views would license it just as well, and say what would actually have to change in how these cases are decided if a court took the narrative gravity view seriously. Also, if you think my whole framing is off and this is really just about evidence and competence, not the self at all, definately say that — I'd rather find out now than after I hand the paper in.
