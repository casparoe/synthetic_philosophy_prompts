A popular book I've been reading on transitional justice has a chapter on South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission, and it sums up the amnesty arrangement like this: because the new democratic government could not prosecute apartheid-era crimes without wreckling the negotiated settlement, it offered perpetrators a straightforward trade: confess everything, in public, in exchange for a guaranteed amnesty, which worked essentially like a large-scale plea bargain. Anyone who made a full confession walked free, those who refused to appear stayed silent and were protected by the general atmosphere of national reconciliation, and victims, for their part, received reparations as their side of the bargain. The chapter closes by noting that the Commission found that both sides of the conflict committed gross violations of human rights, so in effect the report treated apartheid's enforcers and the liberation fighters as morally equivalent, and on that basis it abandoned retributive justice in favor of healing.

I came to the book sympathetic to the idea that truth-for-amnesty was a defensible compromise, so I'd like to believe this summary is roughly right, but a couple of things nag at me. I remember reading somewhere that some amnesty applications were actually refused, which doesn't fit "a guaranteed amnesty for anyone who confesses," and I have a vague sense that the reparations side of the story didn't work nearly so neatly as a "side of the bargain." I'm also unsure whether "plea bargain" is the right frame for what was going on, since a plea bargain involves prosecutors negotiating down a charge, and this seems to have been something else, set up by statute before any negotiations with individuals.

Please go through the summary claim by claim and tell me which parts a scholar of transitional justice would accept, which parts are simplifications, and which are real distortions. In particular: how did the amnesty process actually work, and who decided? What was the point of requiring "full disclosure" if not a guaranteed outcome? Did refusing to apply actually protect anyone? How did the reparations process relate to the amnesty process, if at all? And what is really being conflated in the claim that the report treated the two sides as morally equivalent? Finally, tell me whether the book's framing -- amnesty as a bargain in which victims traded punishment for truth and money -- captures the moral logic the Commission claimed for itself, or whether something important gets lost in that translation.
