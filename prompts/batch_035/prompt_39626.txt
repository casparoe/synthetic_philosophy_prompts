A friend of mine who teaches introductory logic has an argument that she claims shows that mathematics has no rational authority, and I cannot locate the false step. (1) A deductive argument justifies belief in its conclusion only if accepting its premises compels acceptance of the conclusion. (2) In Lewis Carroll's dialogue, the tortoise demonstrates that writing down the inference principle as an additional premise never secures this: the tortoise grants Achilles every premise, including the conditional that expresses the rule, and still withholds the conclusion, and the regress restarts with each newly granted premise. (3) Therefore accepting premises, however many, never compels acceptance of a conclusion; something outside the set of accepted premises must do the compelling — a rule, a practice, a disposition to infer. (4) That something cannot itself be accepted on logical grounds, on pain of restarting the regress. (5) Therefore the ultimate authority of deductive inference is not logical: whatever authority it has derives from a rule or practice whose authority is not itself deductively established. (6) Whatever cannot be deductively established has, as an authority, only a conventional or psychological standing. (7) Therefore the authority of deductive inference is merely conventional or psychological. (8) Therefore no conclusion of any deductive argument is justified in a way that outruns convention or psychology, and mathematical proof in particular confers no distinctively rational authority. My friend insists that this is nothing more than taking the tortoise seriously. It strikes me as proving too much. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would prefer to keep (2), since the regress is what makes the problem worth thinking about, and I am also reluctant to deny (3). My suspicion is that somewhere between (3) and (8) the argument changes the subject, but each step looks unobjectionable on its own, and I cannot make that suspicion precise.
