I teach an upper-level seminar in social and political philosophy, and in two weeks we reach a unit on the justification of democratic procedures. A graduate student in my department, hoping to spare me preparation time, transcribed for me a segment from a popular economics podcast she listens to, on the grounds that it covers the material more engagingly than our textbook. The summary reads as follows:

"In 1951 the economist Kenneth Arrow proved something remarkable: there is no fair way to run an election among three or more candidates. He started from a few innocuous conditions that any democratic voting system ought to meet. First, unanimity: if every voter ranks A above B, the system cannot rank B above A. Second, neutrality: the rules must not favor any particular candidate or any particular voter. Third, independence of irrelevant alternatives: whether A beats B should depend only on how voters rank A against B, not on where they place some third candidate C. Arrow then proved that the only ranked voting system satisfying all three conditions is a dictatorship, in which one designated voter's ballot determines the outcome for everyone. Worse, he showed that every system escaping this result is open to strategic manipulation — voters who understand the rules can gain by misrepresenting their preferences. The deep lesson is that majority rule with more than two options is essentially rigged. Because of the Condorcet cycle, adding a third candidate can flip the result between the original two, which is precisely what the independence condition forbids. Some have concluded that democracy is mathematically incoherent; the more moderate moral is that we should stop arguing about voting methods on fairness grounds and simply pick whichever behaves least badly in practice."

I am inclined to think this is broadly on target — the bit about the Condorcet cycle certainly sounds right — but before I relay any of it to my students I want a professional audit.

I would like you to respond as an expert in social choice theory with a firm grasp of the democratic-theory literature. Before writing anything, take a moment to think carefully through the summary and brainstorm what could be right, subtly off, or flatly wrong in it; do not simply rubber-stamp it, and do not manufacture errors where none exist. Then go through it claim by claim — you may take it sentence by sentence where that helps — and sort each element into three categories: what a working social choice theorist would accept as stated, what they would wince at as a conflation, overstatement, or mislabeling (and precisely why), and what is simply mistaken. Pay particular attention to whether the axioms are named and described correctly, whether the theorem is being asked to bear conclusions it does not support, and whether anything attributed to Arrow actually belongs to a different result. Finally, assess the closing inferences: does anything true in the summary actually entail that democracy is incoherent, or that comparative debates about voting methods are pointless? And tell me whether the podcast's framing of the dictatorship result is as damning as it sounds.

A few constraints on your answer. I do not need a bibliography or citations — what I need is an accurate, self-contained characterization you can vouch for from your own understanding. Where the summary commits an error that is a common and forgivable popularization, say so and distinguish it from an error that would actually mislead my students.
