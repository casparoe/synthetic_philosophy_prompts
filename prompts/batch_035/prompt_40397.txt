I sit on the pre-deployment safety review board at a mid-size AI lab. Every launch proposal comes with a stack of risk claims — "the model won't give users actionable uplift on bioweapons", "the model won't reliably deceive users", that kind of thing — and we keep getting stuck on the same fight. One faction wants every claim argued under worst-case assumptions: maximally adversarial users, red teams with an unlimited budget, a model that is treated as a deceptively aligned actor that hides its capabilities until deployment. The other faction wants average-case reasoning: typical users, a realistic usage distribution, the kind of claims you'd make about any deployed system. The worst-case people say average-case just means "we hope"; the average-case people say worst-case makes nothing passable, so we'd end up deploying anyway and the guarantee becomes a ritual.

Write me a decision procedure the board can actually run on a single risk claim. Something like: questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; and a decision rule that outputs one of four verdicts — argue it worst-case, argue it average-case, argue it both ways and require the two arguments to agree, or escalate because it's really a values question the board shouldn't pretend is technical. Ground it in the real distinction between a worst-case guarantee (which is like a security guarantee against adversarial inputs) and an average-case guarantee (which is a claim about a distribution, only as good as the distribution). Be honest that "maximally adversarial" isn't well-defined — there's no biggest number, so "worst case" always caps somewhere, and where the cap goes is a choice, not a fact. The procedure should make that cap explicit instead of hiding it.

Constraints: we meet weekly and can spend about 20 minutes per risk claim, the board is half engineers and a couple of policy people with no math background, so it has to be runnable by people who wont read a 15-page doc. No deliverable can be "commission a 6-month study" — if the procedure can't be run in the room it's useless to us.

Then run it on three claims we actually get: (1) the model won't provide meaningful uplift for biological weapons attacks; (2) the model will refuse clearly harmful requests from typical users; (3) the model won't subtly manipulate users into unhealthy emotional dependence. Include at least one case where the procedure gives a verdict I won't like — for the record I'm on team worst-case — and at least one where it escalates instead of pretending to settle it. And say where the procedure will predictably misfire: the usage distribution shifts after launch, red teams aren't the real adversary, our average is over users we chose to imagine, etc. If the honest conclusion is that the worst-case/average-case split doesn't carve the space cleanly and some claims need a different axis entirely, say that too rather than forcing everything through the four verdicts.
