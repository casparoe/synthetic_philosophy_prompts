I co-run a small evening reading group in epistemology, and our final session of the year is on Kripke's dogmatism paradox — the one where, if you know some proposition, and you know that it entails a further proposition, you thereby know that further one, and so any evidence against it must be misleading: you are licensed to wave away whatever the thermometer says, because you already know it cannot be right. We ended our discussion in an uncomfortable stalemate. One faction held that the dogmatist, however strange it sounds, is being perfectly rational; another held that closure under known entailment has to break somewhere, though we could not agree where. I would like to close the session with a poem, and I would like it to be a sestina.

I want the form to carry the argument rather than decorate it. The six end-words should be: known, follows, misleading, closed, again, still. In a sestina the same six words return in every stanza but in a new order and a new context, and that is exactly the shape of the thought I want: the entailed proposition comes back unchanged in each new situation — behind the thermometer, behind the witness, behind the memory — while everything around it shifts, and each time it returns it means slightly more and slightly less. Please write it in the voice of the dogmatist, in the first person, so that the recurring words are her credentials, recited with increasing confidence. She should not be a straw man: the poem needs to show why her reasoning is genuinely tempting, why it feels like rigor rather than stubbornness, before the form lets us feel what it costs.

The envoi should be the turn. Somewhere in those three lines — which, as in the traditional sestina, should gather all six end-words, three at the line-ends and three inside the lines — she should catch the first flicker of the trouble: not that some particular evidence has refuted her, which by her own lights is impossible, but that nothing ever could have, and that she cannot tell whether this is the proof of her knowledge or its defeat. Please leave the paradox open; do not resolve it on her behalf. Someone who knows the closure literature should recognize the structure at work without being told, and someone who has never read a page of epistemology should still be able to read it as a poem about a mind closing.
