Is there a name for this? Whenever I try to actually run the utilitarian playbook on anything bigger than my lunch, I hit the same wall. Say I'm deciding between donating to a malaria charity this year vs saving the money, and I try to trace consequences: fewer sick kids -> more school attendance -> slightly different careers -> those people then have different numbers of kids who wouldn't otherwise exist -> ... and after three or four links the chain is pure noise, but the magnitudes keep growing, and if I tweak any single assumption the sign of the whole thing flips. Fine, I know people call that cluelessness. But here's the thing I actually want a name for: the move people (including my own brain) make right after hitting that wall, where they go "well, since the far effects are unknowable and there's no reason to think they're systematically good or bad, we'll treat them as canceling out and just go back to comparing the near-term effects we can actually see." I've watched serious EA types do this and I've watched my dad do it while deciding on a job offer. It's clearly doing real work -- it's what lets anyone act at all -- but it also seems to smuggle in a premise nobody argues for, namely that the invisible stuff is *symmetric* rather than merely unknown. Is there an established term for either the symmetry assumption itself or for the move of truncating the chain at the point where it gets inconvenient? And if there is, does anyone actually try to defend it, or is it one of those things everybody does without saying it out loud? Not after a bare label btw -- I want to know how it's supposed to relate to cluelessness proper, whether the label covers the assumption or the truncation or both, and how its different from just being risk averse or from waving off a Pascal's mugging style argument as too shaky to act on.
