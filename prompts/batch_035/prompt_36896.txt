I've recently been appointed to the equity review panel of my mid-sized city, and our first task is to decide which inequality measure the city should use when it evaluates competing budget proposals — whether to fund, say, a personal care attendant program for homebound residents, versus expanding subsidized transit, versus a general tax rebate. I took a political philosophy course years ago and remember the "equality of what?" question — the one Amartya Sen pressed in his 1979 Tanner Lecture — but I never expected to have to actually pick a currency of egalitarian concern rather than write an essay about the options. Before we choose between subjective welfare, resources, and capabilities as our metric, I want the specification a candidate metric would have to satisfy, written out in full.

Please write the desiderata list for an adequate "currency" for measuring and comparing how well off our residents are, for the purpose of ranking and choosing between municipal budget proposals. For each requirement, give the motivating case from our actual deliberations. The cases I most want handled: (1) a homebound resident with severe chronic fatigue who reports high life satisfaction, plausibly because her expectations have adapted downward over decades of illness, while her neighbor with the same condition reports misery — a welfare metric apparently calls the first one better off, and I want a requirement that speaks to whether that's a feature or a bug; (2) a sailing enthusiast who is healthy, employed, and childfree but claims his hobby makes him "effectively poor" next to families with children — a requirement about expensive tastes versus expensive needs; (3) a Deaf resident who firmly rejects the framing of deafness as a deficit and would resent any metric that scores him below his hearing neighbors — a requirement about not smuggling in a controversial conception of the good life; and (4) the plain fact that whatever we pick has to be computable from administrative data the city can actually collect about a quarter-million people. Beyond these, include whatever further requirements you think the literature implies — distinctions that must be respected (e.g., between how much advantage someone has and whether they have any claim on us at all), results from economics or psychology the metric cannot contradict, and so on.

Then tell me three things. First, mark each requirement as non-negotiable versus a preference of one camp, and, most importantly, flag any pair of requirements you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable — I suspect the anti-adaptive-preferences requirement and the no-controversial-conception-of-the-good requirement pull against each other, and I want to know whether the list itself, in the right form, is a result. Second, tell me which single requirement does the most work excluding the main contenders, as a brief remark rather than a full scorecard; I'll do the scoring in the panel meeting myself. And third, be candid about where the requirements you've listed are genuine demands of adequacy and where they are prejudices of one scholarly camp dressed up as necessary conditions. Please take the role of a political philosopher specializing in distributive justice who also has practical experience advising public bodies, reason carefully about each requirement before writing, and structure the deliverable as a numbered list of desiderata with the motivating case stated alongside each.
