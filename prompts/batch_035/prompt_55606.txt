I recently read a piece in a technology-policy magazine about the so-called "responsibility gap" said to arise with autonomous systems, and I'd like to know how much of its summary of the philosophical debate I can trust. The relevant passage ran roughly as follows: "Philosophers such as Andreas Matthias and Robert Sparrow have argued that when a machine learns its own behavior after deployment, neither the programmer, the operator, nor the commander can be held responsible for what it does, because control was lost at the moment of training. This leaves nobody responsible for the outcome at all — not even causally. Sparrow concluded that autonomous weapons must therefore be banned outright, while Matthias proposed that liability should always fall on the manufacturer. Critics reply that the argument proves too much: corporations and natural disasters also create situations in which no individual can reasonably be held responsible, so if such a gap made a technology illegitimate, we would have to give up most of modern life."

I found this persuasive, and I came away thinking the responsibility-gap worry is essentially an anti-technology argument dressed up as philosophy. Since then, I've heard that both Sparrow and Matthias are considerably more careful than the article suggests, but I can't locate the places where the article slides from their actual claims to something stronger or different.

The fifth point should give your overall judgment of whether the article is a fair representation and whether my takeaway survives the corrections.
