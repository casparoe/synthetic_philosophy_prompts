I teach a third-year seminar in moral psychology at a small college, and for the first time this term I am solely responsible for grading written work: my co-instructor, who normally handles the essay questions, is on medical leave until the spring. We had just finished a three-week unit on guilt and shame as moral emotions, culminating in the question of whether the balance between them is something a culture can deliberately shift. On the midterm I posed the question below. Most of the answers were middling, but one has me going back and forth — my impression of it shifts each time I reread it, and I would rather have a careful second opinion before committing to a grade than grade out of my own uncertainty.

The exam question the students saw:

Many societies have used publicity as a moral sanction — the stocks, letters of apology printed in local newspapers, and more recently various forms of online exposure. Suppose a government commission proposes phasing out such sanctions on the following grounds: they work through shame, and shame, unlike guilt, corrodes the wrongdoer's standing without offering any constructive path forward, whereas guilt, being tied to a specific act, is the "repairable" emotion. (a) Set out the standard functional contrast between guilt and shame as theorists draw it, and note where the commission's description fits that contrast and where it oversimplifies. (b) Can a culture choose its moral emotions in this way? Distinguish the descriptive question of whether cultures differ in their dominant moral emotion from the practical question of whether deliberate change is feasible, and say what such a change would have to work through for it to count as a genuine shift rather than a change in surface practices.

The student's answer, copied verbatim:

(a) Guilt and shame differ in their object and in what they move a person to do. Guilt is about a particular act: I did X and I feel bad about having done it, which is why it motivates confession, apology, and repair. Shame is about the self: I feel that I am bad, not that I did something bad. Because shame targets the whole self, it leaves nothing to fix — the guilty person can make amends for the act, but the ashamed person can only hide or try to disappear. This is also why shame is intrinsically social in a way guilt is not: guilt arises from conscience, and conscience is a private internal monitor, whereas shame requires an audience, someone whose eyes or imagined eyes the person is under. Benedict used this to distinguish shame cultures from guilt cultures: in shame cultures behavior is regulated by external sanction, in guilt cultures by an internal sense of wrongdoing. The commission's picture is basically the anthropologists' picture, and it is broadly right: shame sanctions like public exposure are worse than guilt-based responses because they attack the person rather than the deed. The one oversimplification is that shame also has some social uses, for example it protects reputation.

(b) Cultures plainly differ: Japan is a shame culture, America a guilt culture. The question is whether they can choose, and I think they can, because moral emotions are learned, and what is learned can be taught differently. If a society decided to shift from shame to guilt, it would need to change its sanctions from public to private ones, and to teach children to respond to wrongdoing with repair rather than hiding. The evidence that this works is that some societies have already made the shift: medieval Europe was far more shame-based — public penance, the pillory — and modern societies are not. So the shift is possible, though it would probably take generations. What would count as a real shift is that people no longer felt shame in response to wrongdoing, only guilt.
