I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas, mostly long-form pieces translating technical debates for general readers, and I'm currently at work on a column about Moore's paradox — the observation, due to G. E. Moore and much admired by Wittgenstein, that a sentence like "It's raining, but I don't believe it" strikes everyone as absurd even though it could perfectly well be true. In the column I've committed to a particular explanation of that absurdity, and before I file it I want someone to stress-test the commitment rather than just tidy it up. Please read this as a request for a philosopher of language working carefully through a live position, not as a request for a survey.

The explanation I've adopted is this: the absurdity is a fact about assertion, not about propositions. To assert that it is raining is to present myself as believing it — that is simply what the speech act of assertion involves. So the second clause of the Moore sentence cancels, in the same breath, what the first clause does: the speaker offers a guarantee with one hand and repudiates the warranty with the other. The proposition expressed is consistent, and the sentence can be true; what is impossible is performing the speech act sincerely. I find this satisfying, and I would like to keep it.

But three loose ends worry me, and I would like you to take each one up separately, on its own terms, before you draw any overall conclusion.

First, an evidential variant. Consider: "It's raining, but I have no reason whatsoever to think it is." No belief is being avowed or disavowed here, so the sincerity condition on assertion seems not to be engaged — yet many readers find this sentence almost as absurd as the original. Does this sink the assertion-based explanation, or is it a second, parallel phenomenon the view can absorb with a small principled extension? Second, embedding. "If it's raining and I don't believe it, the picnic is ruined." My explanation predicts that such sentences should be entirely unobjectionable, since nothing inside a conditional is being asserted; many readers agree, but some report a faint residue of absurdity. Which way of reading this fact helps the view, and which way hurts it? Third, tense. "It rained last Tuesday, but I don't believe it did" is typically judged less absurd than its present-tense cousin — a gradation my explanation seems to predict nothing about at all.

Working from those, give me a ranked map of what counts against the view I've adopted — from fatal, through serious but survivable, down to merely awkward — and for each item say whether a fair-minded defender would concede it as genuine counterevidence or explain it away, and whether that explaining-away is principled or ad hoc. Then step back and tell me honestly whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability, and whether, for an explanation of this kind, that would even be a defect.

And because I'll be staking a published column on this: close by saying what would change your verdict — which of the three loose ends, if argued differently from how you have just argued it, or which consideration I have failed to raise, would move you to a different conclusion — and flag the point in your own reasoning where you are least confident, so I know where my column is standing on thin ice.
