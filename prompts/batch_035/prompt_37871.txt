I teach a critical reasoning course at a small liberal arts college -- it's nominally for sophomores, no probability background beyond maybe stats 101, and honestly it's half a philosophy of practical reasoning course. Next week I've got a single 75-minute session on the two-envelope paradox and I want to actually plan it rather than just perform the trick. So don't write me the explanation, write me the plan: the order the pieces have to go in and what depends on what, the setup that should carry the whole session, the misconceptions the students will predictably form and exactly where in the sequence to head each one off, what I should openly decline to cover at this level, and one question I can ask at the end to check that it landed.

For context, my plan was to open with the setup -- you're handed one of two envelopes, one contains twice the money in the other, you don't know which -- and let them stew on whether to switch before I show them the argument that the expected gain from switching is (1/2)(x/2) + (1/2)(2x) = 5x/4, so you should switch, and then the same argument applies to switching back. But I'm worried about the sequencing: the moment they see the fallacious step, half the class will conclude the whole thing is just a typo in algebra, nothing philosophical, and the other half will conclude expected value is broken and start telling me about Vegas. So what has to be in place before I even show them the switching argument, so the eventual diagnosis lands as revealing rather than deflating? Relatedly, I do want to get to the genuinely disturbing part and not just patch the fallacy: the variant where the amounts are generated by some unbounded random process where switching really does raise expected value (the "always positive probability of a bigger amount" kind of setup), and the infinite-expectation version where you can get E(B) > E(A) and E(A) > E(B) depending on how you slice it. Is that reachable for this audience in one session, or should I be honest and cut it? If reachable, does it go before or after the diagnosis of the finite case -- does the deep version set up the puzzle, or does it reward students who've first seen the shallow one repaired?

On misconceptions: I can already predict a couple, like "the two envelopes just can't have equal expected values, that's impossible" and conflating conditioning on the actual amount in your envelope with treating that amount as a variable when it isn't one. What else should I expect, and where in the arc does each get absorbed? And what do I refuse to cover at this level -- the measure theory, whatever's going on with divergent expectations -- and how do I say so without making it feel like the course is hiding the good stuff? Finally, the distinction I most want them walking out with is between "the argument is fallacious" and "the conclusion is false," since those come apart in the exotic versions. What single question at the end would actually reveal whether they've got that, rather than whether they can just recite where the 5x/4 computation went wrong?
