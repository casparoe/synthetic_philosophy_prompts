My roommate and I have been arguing about sunk costs for like two weeks now and I want to come back at him with something better than the standard "the money's already spent, get over it" line. He's one of those people who invokes the sunk cost fallacy at everything — won't finish a bad novel, won't stay for the second half of a concert he paid for, that kind of thing. I actually think the blanket "never honor sunk costs" advice is too quick, and I've got half an idea for why but can't make it rigorous, so help me out.

The half-idea: some philosophers in the Parfit tradition say personal identity over time is shallower than it feels — that you're less one enduring agent and more a chain of temporally overlapping selves whose interests don't fully line up. If that's even roughly right, then maybe there's a case that honoring sunk costs is irrational at the level of the individual decision but rational at the level of the policy. My current self can't sign a binding contract with my future self, but it can act in ways that shape what my future self expects, and those expectations change what my later selves' options even look like. Finishing the degree I'm three years into isn't just money down the drain — the fact that my past selves stuck with things is part of how my later selves got coordinated in the first place. Argue for this position, and take the strongest version of it seriously rather than a strawman.

Also please deal with the obvious objection, which my roommate will definately raise: that a fully rational agent wouldn't need this kind of crutch, so at best I'm describing a patch for weakness of will — a second-best workaround, not genuine rationality. You can decide for yourself whether to concede that or bite the bullet, but either way explain why it doesn't sink the argument. Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence, then give the reasoning, no preamble and no restating my question back at me.
