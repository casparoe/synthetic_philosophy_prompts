In my Algebra II class we drifted into a strange debate: would the primes still be primes if no people had ever existed to think about them? A classmate shut the whole thing down by declaring that mathematics is discovered, not invented — as if that settled it. I have a feeling that one sentence could mean several very different things, and I would like to be able to say precisely which. Could you lay out the distinct claims the slogan might be expressing, ordered from the least to the most contentious — for instance, that mathematical truths do not depend on what any of us believes, that we do not choose what the theorems turn out to be, that they do not depend on there being any minds at all, and so on through whatever stronger readings there are, including the one about abstract objects existing in some nonphysical sense? For each reading, please say whether it seems trivially true, substantive but defensible, or just wrong, and, crucially, which of the readings would actually entitle my classmate to dismiss my question about the primes and which would leave it open. Present the readings as a numbered list I can bring back to class.
