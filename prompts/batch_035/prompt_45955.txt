I write novels, which mostly means I spend my days doing amateur inference to the best explanation on tiny scraps of evidence about why people did things, and I've noticed my verdicts don't line up in any way I can articulate, so I want someone to find the principle for me, if there is one. Case one: my sister went cold on me for six weeks right after I asked about Mom's will, and I'm confident she's furious with me rather than merely busy, even though she is genuinely often busy — the timing just lines up too well. Case two: my agent, lukewarm on my stalled manuscript for a year, suddenly started calling it "the one" the same week a rival agent showed interest at a conference, and I believe the rival-interest explanation, not that she reread it and had a sincere change of heart — but notice this is me rejecting the timing-based story, which is what did the work in the sister case, and I can't say why the structure flips. Case three: a reviewer trashed my last book while misdescribing the plot, and my friend swears the reviewer was actually reviewing a different book that came out that month — too neat, I don't buy it; the boring explanation is that they skimmed. Case four: my neighbor's blinds have been shut for weeks, papers piling up, and I saw his nephew crying in the stairwell carrying boxes out, and my boring-explanation instinct totally collapses here — I believe something bad happened, the dramatic one, because the mundane "long trip abroad" can't explain the crying nephew. Case five, the one I feel worst about: I've long believed editors just won't buy quiet, plotless novels, and every rejection letter saying it was lovely but not for us feels like confirmation to me — my husband points out the letters would look exactly the same if my theory were false, and he's right, but it still feels confirmed. Yet when a friend of mine insists agents only take on new clients in November, and she signed a client in November, I think that confirms nothing. So I have one standard for my own theories and another for hers, and I can't defend it. Is there a single principle behind all this — something about which explanations do real explanatory work versus which just fit, plus when a fit is evidence at all — or is the ugly truth that I just believe whatever makes the best story, novelists being exactly the people you shouldn't trust on that? And tell me what the principle says about the case I keep flip-flopping on: my editor's assistant has now "lost" my email twice in a row, incompetence or a very polite no, and I genuinely change my mind about it daily.
