I teach moral philosophy, and I have been seconded to a national bioethics commission that is drafting a licensing framework for mitochondrial replacement therapy — the technique sometimes described as three-parent IVF, in which nuclear DNA from a woman with mitochondrial disease is transferred into an enucleated donor egg so that she can have a genetically related child who will not inherit the condition. Our enabling legislation stipulates that any restriction we place on the technique must be justified by appeal to the welfare of the resulting children, and I have been assigned the philosophical section of the committee's report. The nonidentity problem is making this genuinely hard. If a prospective mother's only options are to conceive through the procedure (carrying some residual risk that the child will still develop mitochondrial disease) or not to conceive at all, then the child who exists because we licensed the procedure is not made worse off by our decision: the alternative for that particular child was never existence with a healthier genome, but no existence at all. Every policy lever we pull changes not only the conditions of future people but which future people there are.

I would like you to set out, for someone in my position, the most important implications of the nonidentity problem for this regulatory task. I am not looking for a survey of the standard positions; I need to know what follows for the actual drafting. In particular, please address the following, in whatever order serves your argument:

First, what becomes of a statutory welfare clause premised on making children better off, once we notice that no child our committee licenses into existence can be said to be worse off than they otherwise would have been? Does the clause collapse into an empty formality, or can a noncomparative or threshold-based account of harm keep it doing real work at the level of regulatory drafting — and if so, what would such an account have to say about a child born with a serious but non-fatal mitochondrial condition after a licensed procedure?

Second, how should the committee handle the apparent gap between harming and wronging? If we cannot identify a harmed child, is the remaining rationale for restriction something like an impersonal duty not to choose known-worse outcomes — and can that duty be stated in terms concrete enough for a licensing authority to apply, for instance in deciding which residual risk levels are acceptable?

Third, there is a live internal disagreement about consent: one faction wants the report to say that the resulting children cannot meaningfully be said to have consented to their creation, while another retorts that no one consents to their creation and that this proves nothing. What, if anything, does the nonidentity structure imply about whether consent-based reasoning is even the right register here, given that the putative consenting parties are brought into existence by the very decision at issue?

Finally, please say something candid about how much of this belongs in a public-facing regulatory report read by clinicians, lawyers, and patients rather than philosophers. Where the metaphysics is genuinely load-bearing for the wording of a rule, I need to see the load-bearing part stated plainly; where it is not, I need to be told so before I embarrass the commission.
