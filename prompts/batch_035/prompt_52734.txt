A national body that certifies sports coaches is redesigning its certification standards and has commissioned an adversarial collaboration between two philosophers to clarify, to the extent possible, what knowing how to do something consists in — because the answer will determine how much of coach certification can consist of classroom instruction, verbalizable principles, and written examination, and how much must consist of supervised practice and demonstrated performance. The two parties are a committed intellectualist in the tradition of the Stanley–Williamson analysis, on which knowing how to do something is propositional knowledge of a way of doing it, entertained under a practical mode of presentation, and a committed Rylean anti-intellectualist, on which knowing how is a capacity, disposition, or competence and not any species of knowing that.

Draft the joint statement the collaboration should produce. It should contain: (1) a numbered section of claims both parties can sign — about the phenomena to be explained (the difference between the novice who can recite the principles and the veteran who applies them, the expert who cannot say what she is doing, the learner whose articulable knowledge of technique outpaces her performance and the reverse), about the failures of simple formulations on both sides, and about the constraints any adequate account must meet; (2) a disagreements section in which each remaining disagreement is stated in wording its own side would accept — no strawmen, and no neutral-sounding fudges that covertly favor one position; (3) the cruxes, understood as sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party — I would expect candidates to include the regress argument (whether the intellectualist's appeal to a practical mode of presentation answers Ryle's regress or merely relabels it), the mismatch cases (the injured former champion who can no longer perform but can still coach, and the trainee who has memorized the manual but cannot execute), and the question of whether the dispute would survive if both parties stopped using the word "knowledge" at all; and (4) for each crux, a concrete case, thought experiment, or argument that both parties would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

Two constraints from the commissioners, which the statement must respect. First, wherever possible each agreed claim and each crux should be tied to their practical decision — for example, to whether written examinations could in principle certify knowledge-how, or to whether an apprentice's inability to articulate her technique is evidence of a ceiling on her expertise or merely an expected artifact of how automatic skill resists verbalization. Second, they have been advised that recent concessions on both sides may have changed the shape of the debate: some intellectualists now allow that the propositional knowledge constitutive of knowing how does not entail the ability to act, while some anti-intellectualists allow that possessing a skill can involve non-propositional knowledge of ways of doing things. The statement should record, with reasoning, whether these concessive and hybrid positions genuinely narrow the dispute or merely relocate it to the question of what a practical mode of presentation, or knowledge of a way, could be. If you conclude that part of what remains between the parties is a merely verbal dispute — a disagreement about how to use the word "knowledge" rather than about the underlying facts — say which part, defend the diagnosis, and state clearly what the substantive residue is.
