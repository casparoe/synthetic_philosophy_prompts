So, quick context: I'm a science and ideas journalist, and a couple years back I wrote a feature on people who try to put probabilities on civilizational collapse, which is how I fell down the doomsday argument rabbit hole and never really climbed out. The version I can't stop chewing on goes like this: something like a hundred billion humans have lived so far, and if I treat my own birth rank as a random sample from all humans who will ever live, then finding myself so early is weirdly informative. If humanity goes on to spawn trillions of descendants on a thousand star systems, I'd be in the first sliver of a percent — a pretty big coincidence. If we fizzle out in the next few centuries, my position is totally unremarkable. So, the Bayesian says, my early birth should count as evidence for an earlier end. I even tried the same move on dumb stuff to get a feel for it, like guessing how many more articles I'll write in my career based on how many I've written so far, and it half works and half feels like a parlor trick.

Here's the thing though. When I pitch editors on this, the pushback I get is all over the place and I genuinely don't know who's right. One friend says the whole thing collapses at the reference class — why count humans and not mammals, or observer-moments, or why not "members of technological civilizations" (which changes the arithmetic a lot)? Another says it's straight-up double counting, because if you've already updated on nuclear war and climate and engineered pathogens and so on, the doomsday argument is sneaking in extra pessimism through the back door — the future looks short partly *because* we already have reasons to think it's short. And a third guy, a physicist I interviewed, insists the argument can't be right because it would apply just as well to civilizations out there in the galaxy, and it feels crazy that I could learn about *their* longevity from my own birth rank here on Earth. Or does it? Maybe that's fine?

So write me an essay that actually lands somewhere. Does the doomsday argument justify a real update on near-term human extinction or not — is the self-sampling reasoning sound, fatally broken by the reference class problem, or double counting risks we've already priced in? Take a definite position and defend it against the strongest version of the other side, and since my editor loves numbers, give me a concrete figure for the size of the update you'd endorse — something like "it warrants moving your credence in doom-within-a-century from X% to Y%" rather than a vague 'it matters somewhat.' If you think the honest answer is that the argument is broken, say that plainly and say which objection kills it. I'm fine with you being confident, and honestly I'd rather have a committed take I can push back against than a tour of the options — I can get that from wikipedia.
