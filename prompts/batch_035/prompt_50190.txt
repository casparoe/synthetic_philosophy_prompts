I teach an upper-level seminar on language and thought — 12 students, one 90-minute session — and we're arriving at linguistic relativity. The students come in carrying the pop version they got from the internet: more words for snow, therefore thoughts unavailable to the rest of us. My job for the rest of the term depends on replacing that with the defensible version: not vocabulary as a limit on thinkable thoughts, but obligatory grammatical categories that train habits of attention, with effects showing up under time pressure and in memory rather than as barriers to concepts. Design an in-class activity where they feel this distinction instead of being told it, because the vocabulary-versus-grammar line never survives being put on a slide.

The conceptual point I want the rules themselves to carry: what a language forces you to encode every single time you open your mouth is different in kind from what it merely lets you name, since you can opt out of vocabulary but not out of grammar. So I imagine something like the following, though you should improve or replace it: one half of the class learns a twenty-five-word constructed language in which spatial relations must always be given in absolute terms anchored to landmarks in the room (no speaker-relative left and right) and every statement must carry a grammatical marker of evidence type — witnessed, inferred, or hearsay. The other half learns a variant with those same meanings available as optional words but never required. Both groups then run through a staged incident — a communication task under time pressure, followed by a surprise memory test of where things were and how each fact was known. Materials should be index cards and objects I can carry in a bag; no apps, nothing electrical.

The debrief should map whatever performance gap appears onto the obligatory-versus-optional distinction and onto the idea that the effects live in the moment of encoding for speech, in habitual attention, not in a whole worldview — and then should confront the strongest objection head-on: that a twenty-minute laboratory language proves nothing about a lifetime of speaking one. Be frank about which features of the real relativist claim the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply by talking, and predict what goes wrong the first time I run it — my own worry is that students will silently translate through English before speaking, which would blunt exactly the effect I'm after, so tell me whether the rules can be built to block that or whether I should just plan for it.
