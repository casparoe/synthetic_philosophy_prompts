I'm in a reading group on causation that's doing a session on omission, and my job is to write a dialogue that we'll discuss — the idea is that the two characters have to actually engage with each other, not just take turns reciting their position papers. One character is what you'd call a "real absences" person: she holds that the gardener's not watering the plants is itself a cause of their death, on par with, say, pouring weedkiller on them. The other is a deflationist: he thinks there are no negative events, so "the omission caused it" has to be compressed talk about something positive — the gardener's decision to stay inside, his walking past the tap, or just a counterfactual about what he'd have done — and that once you unpack it, the omission isn't doing any causal work of its own.

I want them to fight over three cases I made up rather than the standard examples, because everyone's memorized their lines on those. Case one: a cargo ship with a collision-avoidance system that has a last-resort evasive mode; a software bug means the mode never activates, and the ship drifts onto rocks. The deflationist wants to say the bug (a positive event) did all the causing, and "the system's failure to maneuver" is just the bug under a dramatic description — there's no second relatum there to be a cause. The real-absences person thinks this is exactly backwards: the impact of a rock caused the hull breach, but what made the impact happen was that no evasive action occurred, and the bug can't be the cause of the collision since with the same bug the ship could easily have been somewhere else. Case two is my attempt at the trivial-omission worry: I forgot to water my neighbour's plants while she was away and they died — she didn't ask me, I never agreed to anything, I didn't even know she had plants. The deflationist thinks if her theory counts my omission as a cause she's committed to the queen's not watering them being a cause too, since the counterfactuals look the same, and that's a reductio. She has some reply involving norms — my omission is causally relevant and the queen's isn't because I'm the neighbour and there's some expectation — and I want him to push back that this makes causation relative to who's asking, which he finds unacceptable for a relation that's supposed to be out in the world. Case three is the one I'm most pleased with: a lab freezer with a night watch of three people, protocol says any two of them who agree a temperature reading is anomalous must call the on-call supervisor. All three saw a weird reading, each privately decided the others would call if it were serious, nobody called, the freezer failed and the samples spoiled. Each individual's not-calling was, on its own, within the protocol; only the joint omission breaks it. She wants to say the watch's omission caused the spoilage as a single thing; he says there is no "the watch" as an agent, just three positive decisions, and if she posits a group omission she's invented a relatum that has no location, no time, and no owner.

Could you write the dialogue with real back-and-forth — each character conceding something when the other lands a point, and neither winning outright?
