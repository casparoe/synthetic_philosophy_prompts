Right now I'm wrestling with theories of causation and I keep going back and forth between the counterfactual view (X caused Y iff had X not happened, Y wouldn't have) and the interventionist view (X caused Y iff wiggling X, while holding the right other things fixed, would change Y). As a forecaster these were never separate for me: the barometer falls, the storm comes, and I never had to ask which was "doing" anything.

Here's my request. Build me a thought experiment, one paragraph, set somewhere ordinary (a farm, a lab, a kitchen, I don't care) where the two views genuinely split in a particular direction: the counterfactual "if X hadn't happened, Y wouldn't have" must come out TRUE, but an ideal intervention on X must leave Y completely alone. So the counterfactual test sees causation where the interventionist test sees none. Rules: no perfect correlations, no demons, no sci-fi, and -- this is the part I care about most -- the counterfactual has to be actually true in the scenario, not just true-sounding. Don't just tell me a story where it feels like Y depended on X; the dependence has to fall out of how the scenario is set up, presumably through some shared upstream thing that ties X and Y together.

After the scenario, walk through both analyses: show me the counterfactual verdict, show me the interventionist verdict, and tell me plainly which structural feature of the case is doing the work. Then give me the counterfactual theorist's best response -- I assume they'll say something like "in the nearest worlds where X doesn't happen, we should hold the upstream common cause fixed, and once you do, the counterfactual goes false" -- and I want you to actually evaluate that move, not just report it. Is holding the common cause fixed principled, or is it a patch glued on to save the theory from cases like yours? And one more thing: tell me what the case would look like if I flipped it, so the interventionist view is the one that looks bad -- I want to know whether your case is fair to the counterfactual side or just exploits a cheap trick, because if it's a trick then I haven't learned anything except that you can build one-sided examples.

Sorry if this is wordy, I've been reading too much philosophy and its contagious. Go easy on the formalism but don't dumb it down either -- I can handle the structural equations stuff, I did numerical weather prediction for a living.
