Around 1785 Jeremy Bentham wrote an essay—unpublished in his lifetime, apparently because he judged the public's prejudices made publishing useless or worse, and first printed only in 1931—arguing that consensual sexual acts between men were at most harms to the participants themselves and ought not to be criminalized. His grounds were the ones his penal theory would predict: punishment requires a real harm to others or a calculable social loss; the desire to punish these acts rested on antipathy, disgust, and religious prejudice rather than evidence of harm; and the standard social-loss arguments failed—in particular, since most men prefer women, the acts would not dent population or marriage, and if divine displeasure were the true sanction, one would expect nations that tolerated such acts to suffer visibly for it, which they do not. Roughly a century later, section 11 of the Criminal Law Amendment Act of 1885 (the Labouchere Amendment) criminalized gross indecency between men, and in 1895 Oscar Wilde was convicted under it and sentenced to two years' hard labour—though the machinery was set in motion not by the police but by Wilde's own libel suit against the Marquess of Queensberry, whose collapse exposed evidence that led to his prosecution.

Analyze this episode through the lens of Bentham's views on sexual morality. First, map it carefully: what exactly does his theory count as the harm in the case, who the victims are, and what the theory predicts about a law with no complaining victim and a basis in disgust—does his account of antipathy-driven punishment explain the shape of the Labouchere Amendment and the Wilde prosecution, or does it miss things (the expressive and stigmatizing function of the law, the role of class and public spectacle, the fact that nearly all the harm Wilde suffered was caused by the law and the trial rather than by the acts)? Second, run the analysis in the other direction: what does the episode show about Bentham's theory? Pay special attention to his own century of silence—he seems to have reasoned that an argument no one would hear could do no good, which suggests his utilitarianism had no account of how a majority's sentiments are ever changed. Distinguish clearly between where his framework genuinely illuminates the case, where it is merely silent, and where following it faithfully would get things wrong.
