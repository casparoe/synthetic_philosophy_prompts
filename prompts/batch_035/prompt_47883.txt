I run the website of a small philosophy-of-mind society, and I want to publish an interactive feature in the spirit of one of those online sorting quizzes, titled something like "Which solution to the combination problem would you actually accept?". The deliverable I need is a decision tree, built from the logical space rather than from a list of doctrines, that takes a reader who accepts panpsychism (at least tentatively) to a position on how micro-level experience relates to conscious subjects like us.

Each internal node should be a question the reader can answer from intuition alone, without having heard of any of the terminology — for instance: whether a swarm of bees might plausibly have a single experience over and above the individual bees' experiences; whether, if we can't make any sense of how small experiences compose into big ones, that is an objection to panpsychism or just a fact about composition generally; whether the experiential lives of micro-entities are supposed to be described by physics or to occupy what physics is silent about; whether a person's consciousness is literally nothing over and above the experiences of their micro-level constituents or something additional that arises from them; and whether a more attractive picture might run the other way, with a fundamental cosmic mind and our minds derivative.

The leaves must include at least: plain combinationism, on which both subjects and their experiences compose; the family of views on which experiential contents combine but subjects remain micro-level; the idea that composition is secured by a primitive experiential binding relation between the relevant micro-subjects; the deflationary line that the combination problem is no worse than composition generally and so not a special objection; and the cosmopsychist turn, according to which the fundamental mind is the big one and ours are derived from it. Label each leaf with the position's usual name where one exists, or note that it goes by several or none, and give one sentence on the commitment the reader should find most uncomfortable there — including, where applicable, the fact that the leaf relocates rather than dissolves the problem (for example, in the form of a decomposition problem).

Aim for roughly six to eight internal questions. Where two positions would answer every one of your questions identically, flag that explicitly instead of manufacturing a fake distinction, and say briefly whether you think the residual disagreement is verbal or substantive. The audience is educated non-specialists, so keep the node language plain, but do not oversimplify the theory at the leaves.
