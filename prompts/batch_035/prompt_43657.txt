Here's the student's answer:

"Friedman's point is that the question we should ask of a theory is not whether its assumptions are descriptively accurate but whether its predictions hold up, especially for cases the theory hasn't yet been applied to. He gives two examples. First, the leaves of a tree: we can hypothesize that each leaf is positioned as if it deliberately chose to maximize the sunlight it receives, given the positions of all the other leaves. The leaves obviously aren't doing any choosing, but the hypothesis yields correct predictions (denser foliage where sunlight is available, and so on), so it is a good hypothesis, and the fact that its assumption attributes deliberation to leaves is no objection at all. Second, an expert billiards player makes shots as if he had computed the optimal angles using the mathematical formulas that describe the paths of the balls. He cannot actually do the math, and if asked to explain his play he would give the wrong account, but the as-if hypothesis predicts his shots, so that is the theory we want.

This is what Friedman calls the F-twist: the more significant the theory, the more unrealistic its assumptions. The idea is that a significant theory explains a lot by a little, so it has to abstract away from most of reality, and that abstraction is just what unrealistic assumptions are. So on Friedman's view, unrealism in assumptions is a virtue — the more unrealistic your assumptions, the better your theory — and criticizing a model for having false assumptions is always a methodological error, no matter which assumptions are being criticized or how central they are to the model.

For the objection: Friedman's argument proves too much. If prediction is the only test, then anything that happens to predict well counts as good science. Worse, a theory with wildly false assumptions that happens to fit the data so far gives us no reason to trust it in new situations, since the false assumptions are exactly where the future failures would come from. So assumption-realism matters after all — not as an independent test alongside prediction, but as a guide to when we can expect the predictions to keep holding."
