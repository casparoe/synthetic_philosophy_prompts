I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar this term on contemporary epistemology, and we've just finished a unit on epistemic circularity and easy knowledge. My tutor keeps telling me to work out what principle, if any, lies behind my case-by-case verdicts rather than treating each case as its own special miracle. I've decided to take that seriously. Below are six verdicts I'm fairly settled on, plus one I can't settle, and I'd like you to do the reflective-equilibrium work on them.

The cases and my verdicts:

1. The fuel gauge. My housemate buys a second-hand car with a fuel gauge of unknown reliability. Over three months he notices that after every fill-up the gauge tracks how far he drives, and he never runs dry when the gauge claims a quarter tank remains. One evening the gauge reads near empty and he reasons: the gauge has an unbroken track record with me, so it's reliable, so the tank is nearly empty. Verdict: he does not come to know the tank is nearly empty this way. Each step looks individually impeccable, but the whole thing feels circular in a way I can't quite name.

2. The white table. I'm looking at a table in my aunt's house, in ordinary late-afternoon light, and it looks red. I'm happy to say I know the table is red. I'm not at all happy to say that, on the same basis, I know it isn't a white table bathed in red light — even though 'the table is red' entails exactly that. Verdict: the inference to 'not a white table lit red' is too easy in a way the plain perceptual judgement isn't, though both rule out the same possibility.

3. My own memory. This morning I remembered leaving my keys in my coat pocket, and there they were; this sort of thing has happened countless times over the years. Verdict: I plainly do know that my memory is at least decently reliable on such matters, and I can't see any way I could have got that knowledge except by using my memory — the record of its past successes is itself remembered. This feels entirely fine where the gauge felt bad, and I can't say why. If there's a rule that no source may vouch for itself, memory seems to break it constantly and unembarrassedly.

4. The customs dog. A sniffer dog at a ferry port; the handler's log records two hundred alerts, one hundred and ninety of which led to finds. Verdict: the handler can know the dog is usually right when it alerts, but cannot know how much it misses, because the dog is only ever taken as having been checked in places where it alerted. So this is knowledge of reliability, but of only one half of the record.

5. Here is a hand. Someone in the seminar claimed that perceiving my own hand lets me know there is an external world, by the same shape of reasoning as case 1. Verdict: I go back and forth. It looks structurally identical to the gauge case, which I judged harshly — yet I am not prepared to say I don't know I have hands, and 'I have hands' entails 'there is an external world' as tightly as anything entails anything.

6. My cousin's film recommendations. My cousin recommends films, and whenever I've watched one I've agreed it was good. Now I read her review of a new release and believe the film is good without seeing it. Verdict: fine — I know she's a reliable guide and would stake an evening on it. Yet I notice the past checks were made against my own judgement of the films, a standard I trust anyway, and I can't explain why this differs from the gauge.

What I'd like from you, in British English, in this order:

- Your principle, stated (roughly 200 words): give one principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely enough to generate verdicts. If you offer more than one, say what work each does.
- The principle applied to my six cases (roughly 600 words): say what it delivers for each, and flag anywhere it needs a clause that was motivated by only one case.
- The hard pair (roughly 300 words): focus on cases 1 and 3 (and case 5 if it helps). Say whether a principle can save both verdicts, or whether one must be overturned — and if so, which, and at what cost.
- Predictions (roughly 250 words): apply the principle to at least three cases I haven't listed — e.g. a barometer never checked against independent weather reports, one's own basic reasoning used to argue that one's reasoning is reliable, and the majority view of a community that one has only ever sampled through one's own senses.
- Verdicts I must give up (roughly 150 words): an honest statement of which of my verdicts your favoured principle cannot accommodate, and why you think the principle rather than the verdict should win.

Please don't pad the sections, and don't hedge the verdicts of the principle where it clearly delivers one.
