My friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about Steven Pinker's thesis that violence has declined, and we ended up somewhere more interesting than where we started. I've written up the idea that came out of it, below, and I'd like comments on whether it works. Please tell me whether the central analogy is illuminating or just gerrymandered, whether my "no fact of the matter" conclusion is defensible or a cop-out, and what the strongest objection is that my friend could still press. In your comments, please indicate how confident you are in each of your main verdicts, and explicitly single out the point you are least sure about.

Here is the draft:

The dispute about whether the world is becoming less violent is usually treated as empirical, but a large part of it is indexical. If a population of one billion has a homicide rate of 2 per 100,000 and then, a century later, a population of eight billion has a rate of 1 per 100,000, the per-capita answer is that violence has halved while the absolute answer is that it has quadrupled, and both answers are correct relative to their measures. Neither party to the debate is making an arithmetic error; they are answering different questions. The per-capita measure asks how safe a typical individual is, and the absolute measure asks how much violent badness the world contains. These come apart exactly when population changes, which is to say: across all of the history the debate is about.

The structural situation is familiar from population ethics. The average utilitarian and the total utilitarian confront the same divergence: adding people with lives somewhat below the current average lowers the average while raising the total, and neither reading is "wrong." The violence debate inherits this structure without acknowledging it. My friend, who insists on absolute numbers, is in the position of the total theorist: each violent death is a discrete loss, and the same number of grieving families exists whether they are drawn from a small population or diluted across a large one. I defend the per-capita side, which has the individualist's position: what matters, ultimately, is the risk faced by each person, and a world in which each person's chance of dying violently is halved is better for each of them, whatever the headcount does. We discovered that we could not find a consideration that favored one measure without first settling a prior question that is itself the whole dispute — just as arguments between average and total utilitarians typically beg the question at the level of the measure.

I conclude that "is the world becoming less violent" has no fact-of-the-matter answer in the absence of a chosen index, in something like the way "is this liquid more dense than that gas" has no answer until you say whether you mean per unit volume or per molecule. The empirical work is real, but it settles the trend of each index separately; the choice between indices is a normative commitment about what violence is for the purposes of the question — a fact about individuals or a fact about the world — and the debate would be cleaner if both sides said so instead of accusing each other of statistical illiteracy.
