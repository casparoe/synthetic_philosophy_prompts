For years now I have followed, and periodically participated in, the running argument between two people in my online discussion circle — a longtime rationalist-adjacent forum where we argue about everything from Newcomb's problem to deep learning interpretability. The argument is about the poverty of the stimulus in language acquisition. One party is a committed generativist nativist, raised on the Chomskyan picture: the primary linguistic data are far too thin and noisy to determine the grammar children converge on, so a richly structured initial state must be filling the gap. The other is a committed emergentist in the usage-based and distributional-learning tradition, for whom the supposed impoverishment of the input is an artifact of underestimating both the statistical richness of child-directed speech and the inferential power of general-purpose learning mechanisms — and who regards the case for innately specified grammatical content as a god-of-the-gaps that has been shrinking since the first good corpus studies and the first large language models. They have argued past each other for the better part of a decade. I finally talked them into a formal adversarial collaboration, and I have been tasked with drafting the joint statement — but I want to see a good version of it before I commit, so I am asking you to draft it first.

Write the document they should be able to sign. Specifically:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. These should be substantive enough to be worth signing — about what children actually achieve, about what the two camps' formal machinery does and does not establish, about what large language models' successes and failures do and do not settle, and about the empirical facts concerning child-directed speech that neither side any longer disputes. I am not interested in throat-clearing agreements about "language is complex."

2. A disagreements table. Each row should state one residual disagreement in wording that the party holding the view would accept as fair to itself — not a strawman of either the generativist's "special-purpose innate grammar" or the emergentist's "general-purpose statistical learning," but the position each would actually defend under their own name. If you find that the two camps use terms like "innate," "learned," and "acquired" in systematically different ways, flag which disagreements are partly verbal, and restate the verbal residue and the substantive residue separately.

3. The cruxes. My working hypothesis, which I want you to confirm, refine, or reject: the dispute decomposes into at least three separable theses that the two parties have been sliding between — (a) an insufficiency thesis about the actual input children receive, (b) an in-principle learnability thesis about whether any learner without built-in grammatical bias could converge on attested grammars from such data, and (c) an attribution thesis about what specifically, if anything, the initial state must contain. Test that decomposition. Then identify the cruxes proper — I suspect they include whether the classical learnability results, of the Gold style, constrain realistic learners with realistic data distributions or only worst-case learners over adversarially chosen hypothesis classes; whether the standard grammatical phenomena invoked as poverty-of-the-stimulus cases, such as structure dependence in auxiliary inversion, actually lack the relevant cues in real child-directed corpora; and what, if anything, current large language models demonstrate, given that they are trained on quantities of text vastly exceeding anything a child hears. Add any crux you think belongs on the list and say why.

4. For each crux, one concrete case, experiment, argument, or observational program that both parties would agree in advance bears on it — and, crucially, what each party would predict about the outcome if their own view were true, written down before the fact, pre-registration style. For example, if you invoke an artificial-language learning experiment with children, state what result each party would count as vindication; if you invoke training a model on a child-scale corpus of comparable size and register, state who predicts what. Where the two parties' predictions coincide, say so, and say whether that makes the crux a genuine crux or a pseudo-one.

Keep the tone of a signed document: neutral, precise, and mutually owned, with no adjudication of who is right. The value is in the drafting — in formulations neither vacuous nor slanted, and in making the residual disagreement exact enough that someone could design the study or the argument that would move one side or the other. Format: numbered agreed claims, then the disagreements table, then the cruxes with predictions.
