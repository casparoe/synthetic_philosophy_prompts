I'm assembling material for a graduate metaphysics seminar on material constitution, and I've noticed that nearly every classic case runs in the same direction: a lump of clay survives something its statue doesn't — the sculptor squashes the half-finished figure back into a ball, the lump is fine, the statue is gone. The harder direction is almost never engineered: a case where the statue survives a process that the clay does not. I want you to build me one continuous case that does both, in a realistic setting: a ceramics studio, no teleporters, no particle-swapping demons, no science-fictional machinery. Suggested ingredients (use these or better ones): a figure sculpted from a single batch of clay; an accidental squash before firing, which should generate the familiar verdict that the clay survives an event the statue does not; and a kiln firing, during which the clay is chemically transformed — fired clay becomes a different kind of material, no longer wet workable clay but ceramic. Stage two should pressure in the reverse direction: the statue survives firing (that is the point of firing it) while the original clay, arguably, does not. Crucially, you may not simply stipulate who survives in the case description: the pressure has to be generated by the materials science plus the metaphysics, not by the scenario asserting the verdicts. Keep each stage to a paragraph.

After the case, walk through what each of four views must say at each stage, and where it pays a theoretical cost: (1) the simple identity view, on which the statue just is the clay under a different description; (2) constitution-without-identity, on which statue and clay are distinct but coincident objects; (3) a sortalist or relative-identity response, on which survival is always relative to a sortal, so that "the clay survives" and "the statue survives" are not even rival claims about the same thing; and (4) eliminativism about statues and other artefacts. For each, flag what it must bite. Then identify which single feature of the case is doing the most work — my suspicion is that it is whether the firing counts as the clay's ceasing to exist or as a mere change of state in something that persists, and I want you to confirm or deny that. Say what happens to the case if that feature is removed, and give the strongest objection a defender of constitution-without-identity could raise against the case as you've built it.

Finally, and here I want you to commit rather than survey: tell me which of the four views handles the paired case best and why, and say plainly whether you think the reverse-direction stage is a genuinely new problem for the constitution view or a relabeled version of familiar worries about artefact restoration and matter-replacement. If you conclude that none of the views comes out clean, do not sit on the fence — rank them, pick the one with the cheapest failure, and defend the ranking.
