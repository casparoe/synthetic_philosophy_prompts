Suppose we treat George Berkeley's economic writings — his Irish queries of the 1730s — as a track record of verdicts on monetary questions, and I want to know how he would rule on cases that arose only after his death. His verdicts so far, as I understand them: (1) gold and silver coin has no intrinsic worth as money, its monetary value resting on opinion and common acceptance rather than the metal itself; (2) money works much like language, its value and meaning arising from shared use by a community, the way words do; (3) a national bank should issue paper notes, secured not on bullion but on the country's productive capacity — its manufactures, for instance — so as to keep the poor at their industry; (4) money that is hoarded or drained abroad is money taken out of circulation, and idle money is a loss to the nation, the real wealth of a people lying in their labor and skill rather than in their stock of precious metal; and (5) dependence on the credit of foreign merchants is a vulnerability a nation should rid itself of by being self-sufficient in its own currency.

Now three new cases. (A) Bitcoin: a money with no issuing state, no security, its supply fixed by an algorithm, its value sustained purely by the willingness of a dispersed community of users to treat it as money. (B) A central bank digital currency: state-issued fiat in electronic form, infinitely divisible, every transaction visible to the issuer, which could in principle be programmed to expire or be spent only on certain goods. (C) A small nation like Ireland surrendering its own currency to join a monetary union run from abroad, gaining stability in exchange for losing control over the issue of money.

For each case, tell me what Berkeley would say, but more importantly tell me what rule he is implicitly running. One candidate is a pure conventionality rule: whatever a community commonly accepts as its measure and medium is money, and the state's role is incidental — this rule seems to license Bitcoin. Another is an instrumental rule: money is a tool of national policy, justified entirely by whether it keeps a people industrious and the real wealth circulating — this rule seems to condemn Bitcoin, welcome the CBDC (perhaps enthusiastically, given point (4) about idle money), and frown on case (C). The two rules appear to diverge most sharply on case (B) if we imagine the CBDC being used to compel circulation; tell me whether they really diverge there or whether some further case — one you construct — would separate them more cleanly. Also say which of the five verdicts in the track record is actually load-bearing, since some of them may be doing no work at all. Please keep your answer under 400 words.
