My study group has been reading Nozick's tracking account of knowledge in *Philosophical Explanations*, and one of us produced an argument that seems to show the view cannot tolerate knowledge ever accumulating. (1) On the tracking account, S knows p only if S's belief would vary with the truth of p: if p were false, S would not believe p. (2) This is what lets the account answer radical skepticism without falling to it: if I were a handless brain in a vat, I would still believe I have hands, so I do not know that I am not a brain in a vat, even while I do know that I have hands — accepting, as Nozick does, that knowledge is not closed under known entailment, and accepting along with it the sort of conjunction critics have called abominable: that I know I have hands but do not know that I am not a bodiless envatted brain. (3) Now take two ordinary propositions I know: p, that there is a maple outside my window, and q, that I had coffee this morning, and consider p & q. In the nearest world where p & q is false — say the maple was cut down overnight and I have not looked again — I still believe p and still believe q, so I still believe p & q; my belief in the conjunction is insensitive. (4) So although I know p and know q, I do not know p & q: knowledge is not even closed under conjoining two known propositions. (5) Iterating, I never know the conjunction of everything I know; a detective who knows each item of evidence never knows them as a body; a lab that knows each result of a long experiment never knows the results as a whole. (6) But knowledge was supposed to be aggregative — that is what makes testimony, records, and collective inquiry possible — so a theory that delivers blanket skepticism about accumulated knowledge proves too much: it destroys the very advantage over skepticism it was built to purchase. I suspect the sleight of hand is somewhere in step (3) — something about which worlds count as nearest, or about the method by which I believe the conjunction — but I cannot make the objection precise, and I want to see what each rejection costs. Please tell me which step to reject, why it looked unobjectionable, and what the tracking theorist must pay to reject it (including, if you think the real cost lands on step (2), whether trading sensitivity for some other anti-skeptical condition — safety, say — would avoid the aggregation result while keeping the account's virtues). Please answer in American English, and end with a line of the form "Verdict: reject step N," followed by one sentence naming the flaw.
