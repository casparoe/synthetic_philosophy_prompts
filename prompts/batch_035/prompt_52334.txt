For the last few years I've been treasurer of a small volunteer fund that makes about fifteen grants a year to local community projects, mostly between $500 and $10,000. We recently decided to set aside a slice of the budget for long-shot bets — projects that will probably fail but would help a great many people if they worked. I have no math or philosophy background; I've just read on my own about expected value, and about the puzzles where it breaks down: the St. Petersburg lottery, where a fair coin is flipped until it lands heads and the payoff doubles with each flip, so the "expected value" comes out infinite; and something called the Pasadena game, where the payoffs alternate between winnings and losses in a way that makes the expected-value sum depend on the order you add the terms in, so there isn't really an expected value at all.

What I'd like is a procedure our committee can actually follow at a monthly meeting with pencil and paper, about fifteen minutes per proposal. It should tell us when a simple expected-value calculation is trustworthy enough to act on, when the resulting number is meaningless — undefined, infinite, or driven entirely by one astronomically unlikely outcome — and what to do instead in those cases. For each step it should say what counts as a good enough answer, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it should hand the decision back to the full committee because the question is about our values rather than arithmetic. Please be honest about which steps are really judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run it on three proposals we've actually faced or expect to face: (1) a $2,000 grant to a group offering free coding classes that will probably reach about twenty students; (2) a $5,000 grant to a project with a small chance of unlocking a much larger state grant, which several of our members are excited about; and (3) a donor's offer of a recurring publicity stunt that amounts to a coin-flip gamble with alternating winnings and losses that grow too fast for anyone involved to put a sensible number on. I'd like to see at least one case where the procedure gives the answer I don't want.

As you go, please mark clearly which parts of this are settled among people who study decision theory and which parts are genuinely contested — I don't want a contested view smuggled in as the consensus, since we'll be relying on whatever you tell us for years.
