In a number of countries, life and health insurers are barred by law, or bound by voluntary agreements with governments, from using predictive genetic test results when setting premiums, yet the same insurers continue to price on family history of disease, age, and sex — information that is itself genetic, only coarser and less accurate. A woman who has tested positive for a BRCA1 variant can therefore pay the same premium as a woman who has never been tested, even though the insurer knows such tests exist, knows some applicants have taken them, and could price accordingly. What view of objective chance does this pricing regime presuppose? Does it presuppose that the chance relevant to underwriting is the chance conditional on a legally sanctioned evidence base rather than on all the facts, so that the risk a policyholder presents is partly constituted by what insurers are permitted to know? Is that view coherent, and is it compatible with the Principal Principle, under which a rational agent who knows the objective chance of an outcome sets their credence equal to it — or does the regime instead require insurers to defer to a number they know to be the wrong one, treating chance-talk in pricing as a deliberate fiction? Also assess whether any chance-based distinction can explain why coarse genetic information is fair game while precise genetic information is banned, or whether that line only makes sense given presuppositions that are not about chance at all (say, about not charging people for news about themselves that they are powerless to change), and identify where the regime is philosophically underdetermined, such that several rival theories of chance would license it unchanged. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question. Write in American English.
