My roommate is a philosophy of art nerd and has an argument she says shows there's no fact of the matter about what counts as art, and I genuinely can't find where it goes wrong even though the conclusion feels nuts. It goes like this:

(1) Duchamp's Fountain — the urinal he signed and submitted to an exhibition in 1917 — is perceptually indistingushable from an ordinary urinal in a plumbing store. Same shape, same porcelain, same everything you could see or touch.
(2) The one is (now) a celebrated artwork, the other isn't art at all.
(3) If two things can be perceptually indistinguishable while differing in whether they're art, then whether something is art can't depend on its perceptual properties.
(4) So art status depends on something non-perceptual: the context the object sits in, the theories people bring to it, what the institutions of the artworld do with it.
(5) But there's no independent fact about which context is the "artworld context" — a urinal is in it only because the artworld treats it that way.
(6) And the artworld is just people who make, display, discuss, and respond to art.
(7) So whether someone counts as an artworld participant depends on whether the things they engage with are art.
(8) Put together: "x is art because the artworld treats x as art," and "the artworld treats x as art only because it's engaging with art." A circle. And a circular definition defines nothing.
(9) Therefore there's no fact of the matter about what's art — not that it's hard to define, but that "is it art?" has no answer at all.

And here's the part that makes me suspect it proves too much: she cheerfully bites the bullet and says the same argument shows there's no fact of the matter about money either (a banknote is money because the financial community treats it as money, and they're the financial community because they deal in money), about words meaning what they do, about what counts as a goal in soccer, about what counts as a law. All of it circles, so all of it is "no fact of the matter." That can't be right — or at least, if it's right about art it's right about all of social reality, which is a way bigger bullet than the one she advertised. But the circle itself looks real to me, and I can't just wish it away.

So tell me which step to give up and what giving it up costs. I'd rather keep (1)-(4), honestly — the indiscernibility stuff about Fountain and the Brillo Box thing always struck me as the one thing in philosophy of art that's just obviously true — so if the rot starts there I want to know exactly what I'm paying to keep it. Also, step (8) to (9) feels sneaky to me, the move from "circular definition" to "no fact of the matter," but I can't put my finger on why. Maybe definitions can be circular and still be carving something real? Is there a difference between a circle that's a cheat and one that's just how certain things get their status in the first place?

And please push back on me directly where it's warranted: if you think a premise of this argument is confused rather than merely false, or that my roommate and I misdescribed the move somewhere, just say that instead of politely working around it. Also fine to tell me she's actually right and the bullet just has to be bitten all the way down — I'd rather hear that than get a fudged step just because I'd like there to be facts about art.
