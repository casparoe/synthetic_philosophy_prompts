So I've been firmly in the dust specks camp for a few years now — the view that a sufficiently enormous number of trivial harms (dust specks landing in eyes, a moment of mild annoyance) can genuinely outweigh one instance of a horrendous harm, like 50 years of torture. The reasoning always seemed airtight to me: each speck is a real, nonzero bad for the person who gets it, nobody's speck matters more than anyone else's, so total badness has to aggregate somehow, and at some astronomically large number the total crosses over. The alternative — that there's some harm so bad that no number of specks, no matter how many, could ever sum to it — always struck me as positing a threshold nobody can locate or justify, and I've argued this position to my friends more times than I can count.

But I want to actually stress-test it, not just rehearse the standard exchange. Tell me what would count against it, ranked by how decisive it would be. A few things I'd specifically want covered. First, the sorites-style chain: go from torture to specks by shaving off one second of torture, then one more, replacing the last slivers with slightly worse specks, etc. — each individual step looks fine and yet the endpoints seem obviously different. Does that chain show anything, or is it just exploiting our bad intuition about tiny differences? Second, a variant my friends and I got stuck on recently: instead of each of the huge number of people getting a speck with certainty, each gets a one-in-a-billion chance of a moderate headache. The expected aggregate harm looks the same, but something feels different about dispersing it as tiny probabilities versus tiny certainties — does the aggregative view have anything to say about whether those two cases should come apart, or does it just say "same expected value, same badness"? And third, the thing that's been nagging me most: if a harm is below anyone's discrimination threshold — the person literally cannot tell, even in hindsight, the world with the speck from the world without it — in what sense was it bad *for them*? If the answer is "not bad for anyone, just bad in aggregate," doesn't that start to look like badness without a bearer?

Also, and be honest here: which of these would a committed aggregative consequentialist concede genuinely counts against the view, versus dismiss as intuition noise? Has the position been quietly qualified into unfalsifiability — every counterexample gets some story about why it doesn't count, so nothing could ever refute it — and if so, is that a fatal flaw for a view of this kind, or just what reflective equilibrium looks like in ethics? I'd rather have a real map of the exposure than a both-sides tour.
