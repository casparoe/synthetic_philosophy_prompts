I am a novelist at work on a book in which three women — an intended mother, a gestational surrogate, and an egg donor — hold genuinely incompatible views about whether women should be able to sell reproductive services and materials, and I want their disagreements to be real disagreements rather than caricatures of one another. The plot moves between a European country where surrogacy is prohibited outright and an American state where a compensated, contract-enforceable fertility industry operates openly, so I need the positions sorted with that contrast in view. What I need from you is a diagnostic instrument, not an essay.

Act as a political philosopher specializing in bioethics and the ethics of bodily markets. Think carefully before committing to a structure: brainstorm the logical space of positions first, then locate actual views inside it, rather than starting from a list of famous names and retrofitting questions to them.

The deliverable is a quiz consisting of exactly five numbered questions, each a concrete case described in a sentence or two, with two or three answer options apiece. Every question must be answerable by someone who has never encountered the terms commodification or exploitation — the quiz must sort people by what they say about cases, not by which label they find congenial. At minimum, the questions should turn on distinctions like the following: whether the European-style compromise under which altruistic surrogacy is permitted but any payment beyond expenses is banned tracks a real objection or is unstable; whether the objection to paying for gestation is the same as the objection to paying for eggs, given that the surrogate sells something more like labor while the donor irrevocably parts with a bodily material; whether an exploitation-based objection survives the case of a financially secure woman who chooses compensated surrogacy for reasons entirely her own; and whether compensating in forms other than cash — mortgage relief, housing, a guaranteed university fund for the surrogate's own children — changes anything or merely launders the transaction.

Follow the five questions with a scoring key mapping patterns of answers onto the leaves of a tree that must include at least these positions: the outright prohibitionist, for whom any third-party arrangement, paid or not, violates the dignity of reproduction; the compromise view that permits the practice only so long as payment is barred; the regulated-market liberal; the full-market view under which surrogacy and donation contracts are enforceable like any others; the autonomy-based feminist defense of the market; and the exploitation-based prohibitionist who would tolerate payment only under background conditions of economic equality that do not currently obtain. For any combination of answers that no named position occupies, note it and diagnose why nobody sits there — whether because the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Flag explicitly any pair of leaves the quiz cannot genuinely separate.

Close by telling me where the instrument is most likely to fail: which single question is most apt to mis-sort a respondent, and what argument or consideration would lead you to restructure the tree. Begin directly with the questions; no introductory or concluding paragraphs.
