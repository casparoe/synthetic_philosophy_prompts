I've spent most of my career as a journalist covering science and ideas, and a surprising share of that work is adjudicating claims of cover-up: some turned out to be true (the tobacco industry's decades of suppressed internal research; the Justice Department review finding that FBI hair analysts had overstated their findings at trial for years), most did not, and the two categories were not distinguishable at the outset by anything as simple as how the claim was phrased or who was making it. I've been commissioned to write a long-form essay on the epistemology of conspiracy theories, and before I draft another paragraph of it I want the specification, not the survey. Write the desiderata: what any adequate account of conspiracy-theory belief has to deliver, with the case behind each requirement. Three I already know belong on the list: it must handle what I think of as the Watergate problem — a criterion that classifies as conspiracy theorists the journalists who were, in fact, right about a cover-up has failed at the starting gate, which means the category can't be defined by falsity; it must explain the trust asymmetry, since the defining phenomenon is not blanket skepticism but selective credulity — the believer who dismisses a wire-service dispatch while accepting an anonymous Telegram post has a structured trust pattern, not an absence of one; and it must distinguish scale, because conspiracies in the ordinary legal sense happen constantly (price-fixing cartels, two colleagues quietly massaging a dataset), so a condition like "posits a conspiracy" carves nothing at all. Beyond those, I want it to cover the immunization phenomenon (the debunking that arrives as confirmation), the awkward empirical finding that believers tend to endorse mutually incompatible accounts of the same event, the requirement not to pathologize — some of the most thoughtful people I've interviewed about institutional failures hold a few such beliefs, and vice-counting is not an explanation — and, ideally, a practical desideratum: the account should tell a working journalist with two weeks and a phone which defeaters would justify moving away from an official account and toward a suspect one. Mark which requirements you take as non-negotiable and which are prejudices of one camp, flag any pair you suspect can't be jointly satisfied (my own suspicion is that demanding a sharp epistemic defect built into the category collides with the Watergate requirement), and say which single requirement excludes the most contenders. Then, briefly and only at the end, run the familiar approaches — the falsifiability critique inherited from Popper, the conspiratorial-reasoning diagnosis, the epistemic-vice treatment, and the deflationary view that a conspiracy theory is just a theory of a conspiracy the speaker dislikes — against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria; the scorecard I can do myself.
