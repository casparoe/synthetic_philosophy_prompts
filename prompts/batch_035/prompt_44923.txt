I'm on the content design review team at a mid-size company that makes a meditation/fitness app, and I've been tasked with building an internal checklist for deciding when a piece of persuasive copy or a UX pattern crosses from legit persuasion into manipulation. The thing is, "manipulation" gets thrown around constantly in our review meetings and nobody can say what it actually means, so different reviewers green-light or kill the same pattern for opposite reasons.

Here's the theory background I want you to work from (paraphrasing what I picked up, so correct me if I mangle it): the family of philosophical views I found says the difference isn't about outcomes (persuasion and manipulation can both get you a good result) and isn't just about deception. The core idea is roughly that manipulation works by bypassing or exploiting people's rational capacities -- the mechanisms by which they would normally weigh reasons -- while persuasion works through those capacities. Influencing someone's emotions isn't automatically manipulative (how else would a eulogy work?), but the views disagree about whether exploiting a bias or heuristic, like loss aversion or the identifiable victim effect, counts as bypassing reason or is just a normal way reasons reach people. Some versions add a condition about whether the influence would survive the person knowing about it, others say that's not enough because advertising openly admits it manipulates.

So: you're a professional philosopher who works on the ethics of influence, please think carefully about this and start with a bit of brainstorming about which distinctions actually matter for practitioners before you lock anything in. Then write me a decision procedure my team (mixed backgrounds, no philosophy training, ~15 min per review) can actually run on a proposed pattern: ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it terminates in a "reject" versus "flag for human judgment on values", and where it says the pattern is fine. It has to handle the hard middle cases honestly rather than pretending they're clear. Be explicit about the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run it on three patterns we actually have in the queue right now:

1. A "your streak is at risk" push notification our gamification folks love, sent at 9pm, which uses loss framing to get users to open the app. (Users on a 60-day streak open it way more than others, and retention says this works.)

2. A subscription upgrade screen where the annual plan is presented as the default with a big green button and the monthly plan is grey text in small font -- no false statements anywhere, everything is disclosed if you look.

3. A meditation content series where each session ends with a short narrative about a specific imaginary user (named, with a face) whose anxiety improved, used to encourage people to keep going with the program.

And here's the part where I need you to be honest rather than agreeable: at least one of these has to come out of the procedure in a way I won't like, and I suspect it's #3, since the same logic that condemns the streak notification could condemn the identifiable beneficiary story that our content team considers obviously good-faith. If that's where the procedure points, say so, and tell me whether the procedure has a principled way to distinguish them or whether it just exposes an inconsistency in what my company is comfortable with. Also tell me what the procedure can't do -- I don't want my team walking out of this thinking they have a manipulation-detecting machine.
