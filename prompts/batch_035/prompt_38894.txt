I've been reading up on Wikipedia's rules for history articles and can't figure out what theory of historical knowledge the whole setup is committed to.

Concretely, the rules go like this: no original research, everything has to be verifiable by citing published sources, and for historical topics the preferred sources are secondary scholarship — books and journal articles by professional historians, ideally recent ones. Primary sources (a charter, a chronicle, an inscription) can be cited, but basically only for what they plainly say, never for your own interpretation of them. Disputes between editors get settled by consensus among whoever shows up to argue, weighted in pratice by who knows the policy manual. And famously, the standard for inclusion isn't truth, it's verifiability — a claim can be well-supported and still excluded if no reliable publication makes it.

Here's what bugs me. On one hand this looks deeply Rankean: every claim about the past has to be traceble to a source, no freelancing, no vibes. On the other hand it's anti-Rankean in a funny way — the whole point of going back to the sources in the 19th century was to get past the received narrative, but Wikipedia forbids exactly that move. You're not allowed to read the chronicle yourself and revise the article accordingly, even if you read it carefully. An article on some medieval revolt can only ever be as good as the current state of academic publishing on that revolt, and an amateur with a genuine insight from the primary material is formally locked out of contributing it. Which makes the thing almost Herodotean instead — a compilation of what others say, except the informants are credentialed historians rather than priests and eyewitnesses. Meanwhile the preference for *recent* scholarship is its own commitment, treating the field as self-correcting over time, which not everyone in the historiography world would just accept.

So here's what I want from you. Reconstruct the theory of historical knowledge this practice presupposes: does it only make sense on a social or institutional view, where knowing about the past just *is* deferring to the right expert community at the right time? Or could a more individualist epistemology — every reader weighs the sources for herself, Thucydides-style autopsy — license the very same rules? Which parts of the policy are underdetermined, in the sense that several rival theories of historical knowledge would be happy with them, and which parts geniunely rule something out? And do the "verifiability not truth" standard and the preference for recent scholarship sit consistently together, or do they quietly rest on different views about whether historiography converges on the truth?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble and don't restate my question back at me. Also, keep straight what's actually settled among people who work on this (philosophy of history, social epistemology, that literature) versus what's genuinely contested — if the view you attribute to the practice is itself a contested take and not the consensus, flag it as such rather than just asserting it.
