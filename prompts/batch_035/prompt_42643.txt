I write as a retired analytical chemist who has spent the past two years reading philosophy in earnest; I ask in advance for patience if the dispute below trips over distinctions that philosophers regard as routine.

My wine-tasting circle met last week. Our organiser arranges each flight so that consecutive wines are all but indistinguishable, whilst the first and the last are evidently different, and members record a score for each wine as it is tasted. On this occasion, Marcus — a piano tuner by trade, and possessor of the most confident palate in the room — scored each of the seven wines slightly higher than the one before it, as he has done for months. Yet when the organiser asked him at the end to compare the first wine with the last directly, Marcus declared without hesitation that the first was clearly the better, and marked it so on his summary sheet.

Diana, another member, said his records were jointly inconsistent: if each of the six adjacent pairs is genuinely an improvement, then the seventh wine must be better than the first; if instead the first is better than the seventh, then at least one of the six steps was a deterioration; he cannot have it both ways, and ought to correct his scores.

Marcus replied that no measurement below the resolution of the instrument deserves any weight. Each adjacent pair differed by less than he could reliably discern, so each adjacent judgement carried almost no information; only the comparison across the whole span was a real measurement, and it favoured the first wine. There was no contradiction — merely six ties misrecorded as strict preferences, a bookkeeping artefact of the tasting order.

Diana objected that if they were ties, he should have written equal scores; writing strictly increasing scores asserts six strict improvements, and a man who writes what he does not believe is not suffering from an artefact but asserting a falsehood.

Marcus answered that the sheets permit no ties, so that forced tie-breaking within a ballot is convention rather than testimony. He added that even where a preference is just barely real, it need not compose: a difference too small to matter does not become a difference that matters merely by being repeated six times.

Diana's closing word was that this concedes everything. If the small differences are real enough to count as improvements, they are improvements, and improvements are transitive; if they are not, then "better than" on his usage is not betterness but passing fancy, and his summary sheet, which ranks by genuine betterness, contradicts the rest exactly as she said at the outset.

Neither would budge, and the room divided. I have read enough to suspect that the two of them have walked into a question with a long history, but not enough to referee it. Please go through their exchange move by move: say who is right at each step, assess whether Diana's closing claim — that betterness is transitive by its very nature — is correct, and identify what, if anything, both of them are missing.
