I'm a novelist, and for an essay I've been asked to state plainly what I believe about consciousness and where fiction stands relative to it, and then to have the position audited. Here is my working set of commitments:

(1) The Cartesian theater is a confusion: there is no inner screen where the contents of experience are assembled and shown to a spectator.
(2) Something like Dennett's multiple drafts model is right: processing is parallel and distributed, contents become conscious only relative to probes (reporting, deciding, attending), and before a probe there may be no fact of the matter about what exactly an experience contained.
(3) Stream-of-consciousness technique in Joyce and Woolf is phenomenologically accurate: it renders what experience is actually like from the inside, better than any report or science could.
(4) What fiction does that nothing else can is give readers direct acquaintance with another consciousness — not inference about a mind, but contact with it.
(5) The serial inner voice — the narrator in the head who tells one's experience to oneself, in order — is a genuine, load-bearing feature of the mind, not an artifact or afterthought.
(6) If the self is in some sense a constructed protagonist, then novelists build selves for a living, and fictional characters are, in kind, the same sort of thing natural persons are.

I honestly cannot tell which of these fit together, and I would rather find out now than in print. Please audit the set: which pairs or triples are in genuine tension, ranked by severity, each with what would have to give and what it would cost me; which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection; and which single revision would clean up the most. Structure your reply in four sections, in this order: "Verdict" (~100 words), "Real tensions" (~600 words, ordered by severity), "Apparent tensions" (~200 words), and "The cheapest repair" (~150 words). Do not soften anything: if some part of my position amounts to the Cartesian theater in literary costume, say so directly.
