At our reading group's last session on Wittgenstein's On Certainty, two members — Dana, who is finishing a dissertation on skepticism, and Emil, who works on social epistemology — spent over an hour in a disagreement I could not referee. I will reproduce the exchange as faithfully as I can, in the order it happened. Please adjudicate it move by move.

(1) Dana opened with the regress of justification. When I try to justify my belief that I have two hands, every ground I offer — looking at them, others' testimony, my memories — presupposes exactly the class of things the belief was supposed to be grounded in: that my eyes work, that people generally do not lie about hands, that the world has been roughly stable. So the certainties that stop the regress cannot themselves be earned by evidence; they are not known but function as preconditions of the game of giving grounds. Moore's proof fails not because it is false but because it tries to make a move in the game on behalf of something that is a condition of the game's being playable at all.

(2) Emil replied that this conflates two questions: what a justificatory regress shows about grounding, and what knowledge is. Perceptual knowledge is, on most accounts, non-inferential — I know there is a lamp on my desk without deriving it from premises — and no one disqualifies it on that ground; nor does the knower's inability to supply non-circular grounds for the reliability of her eyes retract the knowledge. If a hinge is a certainty that serves as a stopping point for justification, nothing in that role disqualifies it from being known, and Wittgenstein himself countenances a use of the first-person knowledge-claim that does not advertise grounds. Treating hinges as beyond knowledge over-intellectualizes knowledge, against the de-intellectualizing spirit of the very book Dana was invoking.

(3) Dana said the perceptual-knowledge analogy misses the distinction Wittgenstein actually draws, which concerns role rather than credentials. Empirical propositions are moves in the game — asserted, doubted, tested; hinges are like the riverbed that lets the river of empirical claims flow. The riverbed can shift, but that is rule-change, not the discovery of falsehood. And when a child says she knows she has hands, she is not reporting a belief that enjoys good epistemic standing; she is displaying that she has mastered something like a norm of the game. Calling hinges knowledge re-inserts them into the evidence game where they cannot survive — which is exactly the move that gives skepticism its grip.

(4) Emil pointed to the shift cases. Wittgenstein's own hinge examples — that he had never been on the moon, that the Earth existed long before his birth — are not all in the same tier as the claim that there is an external world at all; call the latter überhinges and the rest local hinges. Local hinges get defeated in ordinary life all the time: a forensic analyst shows my passport is forged, and the certainty that my name is what it says falls — defeated by evidence, discovered to be false. If something is defeasible, it is playing the truth-apt, belief-like game after all. The grammatical reading fits at most the top tier, and elsewhere it reclassifies ordinary epistemic misfortune as conceptual revision.

(5) Dana answered that every supposed defeat of a hinge rests on further hinges — that the analyst's instruments work, that her memory is generally reliable, that she is not being deceived about the whole course of her life. The defeater is always internal to the game; when a hinge falls, what has happened is not that a proposition was discovered false but that a rule was revised, the way a community might adopt a new standard of measurement. Emil's defeasibility criterion can never be applied from nowhere.

(6) Emil's rejoinder was that this proves too much. By parity of reasoning the skeptic is unbeatable too, since every anti-skeptical argument also presupposes something. But we do not concede that: we allow that perception is vindicated, in a benignly circular way, by perception's own track record, and epistemic circularity of that kind is not vicious. If benign circularity is good enough to keep perceptual knowledge, it is good enough to keep hinge knowledge; and if Dana refuses it there, she has no principled way to keep it anywhere.

At the end they turned to me and I had nothing. Please go through the six moves in order, rating each as clearly right, mostly right, partly right, or mistaken, with your reason for each rating. Then say what, if anything, both of them are missing in the exchange as a whole. End your reply with a final line of the form Verdict: — one sentence saying who is right about which specific points.
