I'm a third-year PhD student in epistemology, and my dissertation was supposed to be about self-knowledge, but I've spent months stuck on a debate that, as far as I can tell, has been essentially static since shortly after it began: the luminosity debate sparked by Timothy Williamson's Knowledge and Its Limits (2000).

The shape of it, as I understand it: a condition is luminous if, whenever it obtains, one is in a position to know that it obtains. Williamson argues that no non-trivial condition is luminous, using a case where you feel cold at dawn and then warm up by imperceptible degrees. The key premise is a margin-for-error (or reliability) constraint: in conditions like these, your belief that you feel cold counts as knowledge only if you'd still believe it in all sufficiently similar cases where it's true. Iterating that constraint along the chain of close cases, from genuinely cold to not-cold-but-indistinguishable, yields that at no point do you know you feel cold — even at the very cold start. Since luminosity looked like a close cousin of the KK principle (if you know that p, you're in a position to know that you know that p), the result threatens to unravel a good deal of internalist epistemology: privileged access to one's own mind, the idea that reasoning can take one's own attitudes at face value, and so on.

The responses have settled into three grooves, and nobody moves between them. Some deny the margin-for-error premise, either by rejecting the safety-style condition on knowledge outright or by arguing that introspection is somehow exempt from it. Others accept the argument but deny it touches anything anyone cared about, either because luminosity for total time-slices of the world is a different animal from first-person authority over one's own states, or because the conclusion is only about creatures with limited discrimination, which we already knew. And a third group simply accepts the conclusion and revises self-knowledge accordingly. Twenty-five years on, each group has its standard replies to the others' standard replies, and I can find no one who has changed their mind.

Here is my hypothesis, which I want you to take seriously but also to challenge: the stall is generated by an unflagged ambiguity in the word "condition." The critics hear "condition" as a phenomenally available state-type — something like feeling cold, the sort of thing you can avow without observation — whereas the argument needs "condition" individuated finely, at least as finely as a total state of the world at a time, for the margin-for-error machinery to get a grip. If you individuate coarsely, the reliability premise looks false or unmotivated, because close cases won't involve the same condition in the loose sense. If you individuate finely, the conclusion looks like a technicality about world-slices that no defender of first-person authority ever wanted to deny. If that's right, the debate is partly verbal — two questions under one heading — but the interesting residue is substantive: whether introspective self-knowledge is the kind of state that can satisfy a safety constraint at all, or whether it has a different structure (constitutive, rather than evidential) that makes the safety framework simply inapplicable.

So here's what I'm asking for: a diagnosis, not a survey. Using what each side actually says — the margin-of-error deniers, the "wrong target" respondents, and the revisionists — tell me whether the disagreement is verbal, substantive, or my hybrid. Is the real dispute about the individuation of conditions, about whether introspection is constitutive rather than evidential, about whether knowledge requires safety across close worlds, or about bedrock intuitions concerning a subject's authority over their own mind that neither side can argue for? Is a hidden formal question doing the work — for instance, whether the iteration of "sufficiently close" along the warming morning presupposes a fixed similarity metric, and whether some structure on the space of cases is tacitly assumed? And given your diagnosis, tell me honestly what, if anything, would count as progress: a sharper distinction, a formal model of one side's position, a new thought experiment, or an argument that the question is ill-posed and the debate should simply be dissolved. If you think no progress should be expected, say so and explain what that implies for someone choosing a dissertation topic in the neighborhood. I'd rather have a diagnosis I can test than a balanced run-through of the positions.
