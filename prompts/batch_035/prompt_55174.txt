hey, so im a phd student in moral psychology and I'm helping our lecturer put together the final for the upper-level seminar on moral responsibility. I've got most of the paper drafted but I keep failing on the Strawson essay question — every version I write can be answered by reciting the optimist/pessimist reconciliation and the participant vs objective attitude stuff, which they've all memorized by now. I want one that actually separates the students who understand the argument from the ones who memorized its labels.

Here's the angle I want the question to exploit — tell me if you think it works. Strawson's move, stripped to its skeleton, seems to be: the reactive attitudes are too deeply woven into human nature and our relationships for a wholesale switch to the objective stance to be a live option for us; and the adjustments we make — pleading mitigating circumstances, treating someone as deranged rather than malicious — are themselves moves within the practices, so there's no external standpoint from which to demand a justification of the whole edifice. But inevitability isn't justification. If deep embedding plus internal point-keeping were enough, jealousy or tribal prejudice would deserve the same vindication, and nobody wants to say that. So the question has to force students to decide whether Strawson has anything normative to say or is only explaining why we won't stop, and — this is the part I care most about — whether the reading on which the practices get their point from personal relationships (what people in the lit sometimes call the humanist reading) actually supplies a standard the practices can fail, or whether it just relabels the status quo as fine.

I also want them to work in the exemption/excuse distinction somewhere, but not as name-dropping — as the thing that only makes sense if the practices have a point, since on that reading exempting someone is suspending a relationship, not merely excusing an action done under strain. If they can't say why that difference matters to the argument, they don't get the marks.

Practical constraints for the deliverable: one essay question answerable in 90 minutes, aimed at students who've read the paper closely but where reciting the labels earns nothing. Then a model answer of around 600 words, and a marking scheme with five must-hit points, marks for each out of a 20-point total, plus a one-liner on each saying how students typically blow it. And please put the jealousy/tribal-prejudice analogy (or something like it) into the question text itself — I want to test analysis, not whether they can invent an analogy under exam pressure.

One more thing: don't let the model answer just conclude it's descriptive and not normative, done. The honest answer has to say what work each reading does and where each runs out, and a top answer should notice that Strawson can concede the reformist's point about particular practices while denying the wholesale one — and then say what that concession costs him. If an A answer and a B answer both "get it right," my marking scheme isn't doing its job, so make sure the scheme names the specific moves that separate them.
