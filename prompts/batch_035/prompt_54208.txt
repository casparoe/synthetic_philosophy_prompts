I'm a software engineer at a company that ships domain-specific conversational assistants — contract review, benefits-eligibility triage, incident summarization for on-call rotations. Every release, someone has to write one line in the model card, and today that line is chosen by vibes. The line is either "the system understands [domain] inputs well enough to route them without a human in the loop" or "outputs must be reviewed by a human before use." I want to replace the vibes with a written procedure, and I want the procedure to be philosophically defensible rather than just operationally convenient. Specifically, I want it grounded in the tradition that starts with Turing: the idea that the right to attribute understanding rests on behavioral evidence, and the long argument about what behavioral evidence can and cannot establish.

Some constraints, so you design something a practitioner can actually run. The evaluation happens once per release, per product. The evaluator is an engineer with a philosophy background of exactly zero, a budget of about three hours per system, and access to a handful of internal testers who can be briefed with a one-page script. The stakes are real: the "no human in the loop" line removes a mandatory review step that costs us significant per-ticket labor, so there is steady pressure to write it, and I want the procedure to be resistant to motivated reasoning — including my own. The product lead has to sign off on the procedure itself, so it should be legible to a non-philosopher in a page.

Now the part I care about most, and where I suspect the interesting conceptual work lives. I've absorbed enough secondhand philosophy of mind to worry about two familiar traps, and I want the procedure to navigate between them rather than pick one blindly. The first trap is treating any behavioral evidence as sufficient: the Chinese Room story, as I understand it, says a system can produce the right outputs for the wrong reasons, and I take the lesson to be that we should be asking what kind of behavioral evidence discriminates between genuine comprehension and sophisticated pattern-matching, not whether behavioral evidence is off the table. The second trap is the opposite one: demanding some inner criterion the evaluator can never check, which would make the procedure theater — the evaluator would just stamp whatever the behavioral results said anyway, and the philosophical reservation would do no work. I've also read (in popularizations, so possibly garbled) that there's a distinction somewhere in this literature between a test providing evidence for a mental attribute and the test's constituting that attribute — the passing of the test making it true, rather than indicating it's true. I don't fully grasp the distinction, but it feels like it matters for what my model card line claims: "understands well enough to route without a human" sounds like an evidential claim with a threshold built in, and I'd like the procedure to make that threshold explicit instead of leaving it inside an ambiguous verb.

Concretely, please give me:

1. A step-by-step procedure — the questions to ask, in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where a failed answer forces the "with human review" line, and where the procedure stops and hands the decision to a named human because the remaining question is one of judgment or values rather than something a protocol can settle. I'd expect it to include probes along these lines, but improve on them: questions whose correct answer requires using information the tester introduced earlier in the same conversation, only rephrased (to test whether the system binds to what was actually said rather than to surface lexical matches); questions where the honest answer is "I can't tell from what you've given me," since a system that never expresses uncertainty is suspicious in both directions; questions probing whether the system's answer changes when a fact stated earlier in the conversation is explicitly retracted or corrected mid-stream; and something to catch contamination — the possibility that the system passes because it has memorized this exact kind of exchange from training data rather than comprehended it, which for us is a live risk since our evals resemble publicly posted examples.

2. Two or three worked applications. One should be our contract-review assistant, which is flawless on standard agreements but degrades on unusual structures like an amendment that silently redefines a term used everywhere earlier — the procedure's verdict on this case should come out the way I don't want it to, and I'd like you to be honest about that rather than soften the protocol to spare me. Another should be an easier case that passes, so the procedure isn't a machine that only ever says no.

3. A statement of what the procedure cannot do. In particular, I want you to be explicit about which of its steps are genuine tests with a right answer, which are judgment calls dressed up as checkboxes, and whether the final model card sentence, on the evidential-versus-constitutive distinction above, is a claim the procedure could ever fully vindicate — or only ever support to a degree that a careful person should be willing to accept for this purpose, at this risk level, for this use case. If the honest answer is that the sentence should be rewritten to something more modest, say so and propose the wording.

Assume I know Turing's paper only through summaries and the Chinese Room argument only through a textbook paragraph, so define any term you use, and don't point me to a reading list — I need the procedure and the reasoning behind it, not a syllabus.
