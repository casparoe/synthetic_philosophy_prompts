I teach an upper-level seminar in applied ethics on consent — 16 students, one 90-minute session, in a room with movable chairs. The students have already read the standard tripartite account: consent is valid only when it is informed (not distorted by deception about what is being agreed to), uncoerced (not extracted by making refusal worse than a pre-existing baseline), and given by someone competent to understand the deal. They can recite this fluently. What I want is an activity in which they run into the hard parts themselves, before I name anything.

Design a staged-decision game in which students accept or decline small offers carrying real (but token-based) stakes — some version of a market or clinic of transactions, with rounds arranged so that each round manipulates exactly one of the three conditions while holding the other two fixed. By the end, the class should have generated its own pile of verdicts about which consents counted and which did not, which the debrief can then interrogate. At least one round should involve deception about a feature that most students would call inessential to the deal, and at least one should involve a penalty for refusal that is real but small, so the class can discover whether its own intuitions track the kind of pressure or merely its magnitude. Include a proxy round if you can manage it — a case in which one student consents on another's behalf — since none of the three conditions obviously applies there, and note in advance what you expect to happen.

Please give me: the setup and materials (nothing requiring real money or IT), the rules and payoff scheme in enough detail that I can run the game from your description alone, a round-by-round timeline fitting the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto specific parts of the tripartite account — in particular, which of the class's likely verdicts the account cannot explain, and how to use that gap to introduce the thought that validity might be scalar rather than binary, or that consent does its moral work by waiving a right rather than by expressing a will. Then tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and which feature of actual consent — above all, that the person consenting usually bears the consequences of a decision over time rather than within a single round — the activity cannot reproduce and I shall have to supply in discussion.
