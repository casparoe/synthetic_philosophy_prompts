I work in organ transplantation policy, and I've come to think the decades-long debate over the definition of death is a textbook case of an impasse: the same arguments have circulated since the Harvard committee's report in 1968 and the President's Commission's report in 1981, the sides are well known to everyone involved, and yet no one moves. To set the stage: whole-brain death is the legal standard in the United States, roughly equated with the irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem; circulatory-respiratory death is the alternative standard, now central to donation after circulatory death protocols, where the key move has been substituting permanence for irreversibility so that organs can be recovered minutes after the heart stops; and a persistent minority argues that the real criterion should be the loss of higher-brain functions, so that patients in permanent vegetative states would already count as dead. Meanwhile, courts keep issuing conflicting rulings, some states carve out religious or conscience objections to brain-death determinations, and the recent controversy over revising the Uniform Determination of Death Act suggests the official framework itself is unstable.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not primarily medical or scientific at all, but I can't decide between two readings of it. On the first reading, it is largely verbal: the word death is being asked to do two incompatible jobs — describe a biological event and serve as a normative gatekeeper for organ procurement, withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment, homicide law, and estate settlement — and the parties are picking which job without saying so. On the second reading, there is a substantive hidden question underneath: whether death is even a point event rather than a process, and whether the thing that dies is an organism, a person, or something else, such that the two camps are actually answering different questions under one label.

Which is it — or is it something else, like a disagreement about which cases count as the data, or a clash of bedrock intuitions that no evidence could settle? Please diagnose the structure of the dispute using what each side actually claims, not a neutral survey of the positions. In particular, I'd like you to say whether the permanent-versus-irreversible maneuver in circulatory-death protocols is a pragmatic fudge that papers over the real disagreement or a genuine conceptual advance. And given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a new distinction, a conceptual divorce (for example, decoupling organ-procurement rules from the definition of death entirely), an empirical finding, or nothing at all, and why. If you think the question is ill-posed as currently framed, say so and explain what the reframed question would be.
