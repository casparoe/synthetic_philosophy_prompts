I'm preparing a short position statement for an epistemology reading group that has spent the semester on the lottery and preface paradoxes, and before I circulate it I'd like somebody to audit it for consistency, because I have a suspicion that something in it doesn't hang together but no clear sense of what. Here is the set of commitments as I've written them down. First, I'm a fallibilist about knowledge: knowing that p doesn't require ruling out every possibility in which not-p, and in ordinary cases a proposition can be known even when its probability falls well short of one. Second, I accept that knowledge is closed under competent deduction from known premises. Third, I nonetheless think that no proposition of the form "this lottery ticket will lose" can ever be known, however long the odds: the belief is highly probable, but it is too much a matter of luck to constitute knowledge. Fourth, ordinary perceptual and testimonial knowledge — that I parked on Elm Street this morning, that my colleague will be at the meeting at six — survives reflection even though, once I honestly tally up the error possibilities, its probability doesn't seem to me obviously higher than the probability that a million-to-one ticket loses. Fifth, I think credences and full beliefs are genuinely different kinds of states: credences obey the probability calculus, while to fully believe that p is to treat p as settled for the purposes of reasoning and action, and a rational full belief can coexist with a small credence that not-p. Sixth, I hold that full rational belief is closed under conjunction — if you rationally fully believe p and rationally fully believe q, and you put the two together, you rationally fully believe their conjunction. Seventh, I think the careful author of a book with several hundred individually checked claims, who also knows from long experience that every book like hers contains at least one error, rationally believes each individual claim and rationally believes that at least one claim in the book is false.

The reading group tends to reach for Kyburg on the lottery case and Makinson on the preface case, but I've never been sure whether my verdicts treat the two as instances of a single underlying problem or as two problems that merely look alike. Sometimes I suspect the preface is just the lottery paradox wearing a suit, since both turn on tolerance for small error risks combined with some closure principle, and my intuitions come out differently in the two cases: I'm reluctant to grant knowledge in the lottery case but comfortable granting rational belief in the preface case, and that asymmetry feels like it should be either a principled difference or a bias I haven't earned. Other times I suspect the problem lies elsewhere entirely — perhaps with one of the two closure commitments, or with my insistence that full belief and credence are genuinely distinct states rather than one another's shadows.

What I'd like from you is an audit rather than a lecture. Go through the seven commitments and tell me which pairs or triples are in genuine tension, which tensions are merely apparent and dissolve on closer inspection, and for each genuine one how severe it is, what exactly would have to give, and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set. Then tell me which single revision, if I made it, would clean up the most. Please don't soften the verdict by flagging everything as equally problematic, and please don't turn this into a survey of the literature; write it in continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write a careful letter to a colleague.
