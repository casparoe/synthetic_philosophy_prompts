I got into an argument with my brother in law about China and it's stuck with me. His take is basically the Singapore-style argument: if a government keeps you safe, makes you richer every year, and the trains run on time, it's earned the right to run things without elections — call it a deal where you trade political say for results. I'd read somewhere that political scientists call this performance legitimacy, and I want to actually stress-test it on a specific episode instead of talking in generalities: the end of China's zero-covid policy in late 2022. I remember it well enough: the lockdowns worked great at first and the state milked that as proof the system is superior, then omicron made the whole thing unbearable, the Shanghai lockdown in spring 2022 started cracking the consensus, and then after the apartment fire in Urumqi there were those protests with people holding up blank sheets of paper in a bunch of cities. Within a couple weeks the government just... dropped the policy, almost overnight, in early December 2022, and never once admitted the protests had anything to do with it. Then infections exploded, and after that the official story became that the country had successfully beaten the pandemic.

So here's what I want: treat that whole sequence as a test case for the performance legitimacy idea, going in both directions. Like, if the theory is right, what does it predict at each stage — the initial boost from being the country that "handled it," the legitimacy hit when lockdowns stopped delivering and started just hurting, the sudden reversal once performance visibly failed? Does the theory explain why the reversal was so abrupt, or does it fumble there (like, the trigger was a fire and some blank paper, not GDP or any deliverable you could measure)? And where does the theory go silent or just misdescribe what happened? The part I keep chewing on is that the protests didn't really seem to be about performance at all — people weren't asking for better-run lockdowns, some were demanding the leader step down, and there was that guy who said he'd rather be the last generation than raise kids in that system, which sounds like a demand for a *voice*, not better service. Performance legitimacy seems to have no slot for that. And then the aftermath — the regime bounced back fast by just retelling the story, so maybe "performance" is partly manufactured by narrative control, not delivered by outcomes, which feels like it undermines the whole idea. Does the episode end up supporting my brother in law's deal (the system corrected itself, delivered the reopening, stability held) or is it actually evidence the deal is a mirage? Be honest about which parts the theory genuinely explains and where you're having to force it.
