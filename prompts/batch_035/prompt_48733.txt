I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a long piece about how experts reason about the future — superforecasting tournaments, failed technology predictions, the whole modesty-versus-merit debate. A recurring tension in my reporting is the clash between what Kahneman called the inside view and the outside view, and I've noticed that my own sympathies lie more with the insiders than the literature says they should. Before I commit to a thesis in print, I want to stress-test my position against a genuinely strong opponent, because my friends are tired of arguing about this with me over lunch.

Please play a committed defender of the outside view and epistemic modesty — think of someone steeped in the Kahneman and Tetlock tradition: reference classes are the only honest anchor, a fluent causal story about one's own special case is the root of the planning fallacy, and the feeling that this time is different is almost always self-flattery. Make this character sharp, not a strawman: they may concede small points (reference classes can be badly chosen, base rates go stale) but never the main one, which is that a sufficiently detailed inside view can, on its own, outweigh the base rate. Think carefully before you respond — weigh my actual argument rather than a weaker version of it — and stay in character until I say "stop." Keep each reply to a single paragraph of no more than 150 words, in American English.

Here is my opening move. Consider a project whose very existence changes the base rate, say the Manhattan Project: the prior success rate for secret wartime crash programs to build a novel weapon was effectively zero, because nothing of the kind had been attempted, so an outside-view forecaster in 1942, reasoning solely from precedent, would have predicted failure and would have been wrong for reasons visible from the inside — the physics was settled, the resources effectively unlimited. My claim is this: when a case is genuinely unprecedented, any reference class you can construct is defined only by superficial features, and applying the outside view just means averaging over a set of non-analogues. And here is the sharper part: deciding whether your case is unprecedented is itself a judgment you can only make by examining the details of your case from the inside. So the outside viewer, to apply their method at all, must already trust the very kind of inside reasoning they tell us to distrust. How do you escape that circle?
