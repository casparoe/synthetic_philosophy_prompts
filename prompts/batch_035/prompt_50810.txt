i'm a PhD student in HPS and I record a small podcast where philosophers have to defend their own actual views, not just survey the field for listeners. Next episode my guest is a philosopher who has spent her career on the selective realist reply to the pessimistic meta-induction -- the divide et impera move, where the claim is that the parts of past theories that did the real predictive work (the optical structure Fresnel got right, say) survived into the successor theory, while the idle machinery (the ether as a mechanical medium) got rightly dumped. Episode runs about 40 minutes, she's explicitly agreed to take hard questions, and my audience is mostly other grad students who know the textbook version already, so I want questions that hit the actual pressure points instead. I need at least one question each on:

- the hindsight problem: she marks off the working posits using the very fact of what survived, so how is her decomposition anything more than reading the answer off the back of the book? And I want the follow-up to be keyed to her likely reply, which is that there were criteria available at the time for telling working from idle parts, criteria that don't peek at the future.
- whether the view can survive a case where the abandoned part wasn't idle at all but did genuine novel predictive work -- phlogiston is the obvious candidate, but I'd let her choose her own case if she'd rather.
- the circularity charge: she says truth is the only explanation of predictive success, but that inference is itself an induction over past cases, which is exactly the kind of induction the pessimistic meta-induction poisons. What do I ask next if her answer is that the two inductions just cancel out and realism then wins on the no-miracles side?

Then one more adversarial question to close with: if the trimming job has to get this fine-grained, has she actually protected scientific realism, or has she quietly retreated to a position a structural realist could sign onto with no complaints -- and does she count that as a concession or a win? Bonus if you can work in a question where she has to stick her neck out about a current theory: which parts of today's physics does her criterion say will still be around in a hundred years, and what does her view cost her if history proves her wrong about that.

Keep the phrasing conversational, like something I'd actually say to a guest, not like reading a checklist at her. And dont ask her to define basic terms -- my listeners know what the PMA and the no-miracles argument are, they just havn't seen anyone made to defend the details.
