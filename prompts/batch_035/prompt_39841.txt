my philosophy reading group has been going in circles on self-deception for like three sessions and I'm starting to suspect the argument isn't really about anything. one guy is basically a deflationist — he says most cases people call self-deception are just motivated reasoning you don't notice happening, wishful thinking where you never form two beliefs at once, so there's no puzzle about how you can "deceive" yourself. the intentionalist in the group says that totally misses the interesting cases, like the husband who strongly suspects the affair, has the evidence sitting in a drawer, and carefully never opens the drawer while telling everyone things are great. that looks like something he is *doing*, strategically, with an end in view — managing his own information supply like he'd manage someone else's. the deflationist says fine, but that's just attention control plus ordinary bias, no inner con artist required. and around we go.

the standard paradox everyone starts from — that you supposedly can't believe p while believing not-p, and can't set out to fool someone who has access to everything you know, namely yourself — seems to push hard toward the deflationary side. but then the intentionalist counters that the deflationary story can't explain what's distinctive about the phenomenology: the sense that the self-deceived person is somehow complicit, the shame and the relief when they finally admit it to themselves. mere error doesn't come with shame.

my working hypothesis is that this is mostly a verbal fight about which cases deserve the *deception* label — that if we split the territory into "biased belief formation" and "strategic self-management of evidence" both sides turn out right about their own cases and the fight dissolves. but that feels too easy, and part of me thinks there's a genuine substantive question hiding in there, maybe about whether the person is responsible in a way they wouldn't be for an ordinary cognitive bias, or whether the self-deceived person literally knows what they're avoiding. so: what kind of disagreement is this actually — verbal, or two groups answering different questions under one heading (one about belief, one about agency), or a real fight about the facts of what goes on in someone's head? and given your diagnosis, is there anything that could actually settle it — a clearer definition, some psychological finding, a distinction nobody's drawn yet — or should we just agree on terms and move on to something else?
