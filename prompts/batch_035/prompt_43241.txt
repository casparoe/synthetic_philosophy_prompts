Last weekend my oldest friend and I spent a four-hour drive back from a wedding arguing about what corruption actually is, and we ended up in an odd place: I could list his verdicts on a series of cases, but I could not extract a stable principle from them, and now the argument has followed me home and will not leave. His verdicts, as best I can reconstruct them: a customs officer who waves through a truck of undeclared goods for a fee — corrupt, obviously. The senior surgeon at the hospital where he trained, who accepts an expensive bottle of whiskey from a grateful family after a successful operation, in a country where this is routine, technically against hospital policy, with no expectation on anyone's part that it changes anything — distasteful, he said, but not corrupt. A member of congress who steers a defense contract toward a major donor using only legal channels — contributions, lobbying, a perfectly regular procurement process — corrupt, even though nothing illegal happened. A municipal councillor in the small town his parents come from who gives a road-crew job to his unemployed cousin, who is as qualified as anyone else on the list and does the work properly, in a place where nobody, including the losing applicants, considers family preference wrong — not corrupt. A border guard who expects a small fee before a family's paperwork moves at all, in a system where every guard expects it and everyone knows it — corrupt, and here he was completely firm, though he could not say what separates this from the surgeon. A local patron who delivers real public works to his supporters while making sure a rival neighborhood's projects stall — corrupt, he insisted, even though the supporters genuinely benefit and he never pockets anything himself.

Two further cases came up just as we were pulling into the city, and we never resolved them. Case A: a clerk who accepts a modest payment to stamp documents the same day he receives them, when he would stamp them within a day or two anyway; he never refuses or delays anyone who does not pay, and no one has ever been worse off by any measurable amount. Case B: a licensing official, moved by neighborhood loyalty and taking nothing for himself, quietly shelves applications from the other side of town for months, and two businesses go under waiting. Predict his verdict on each, and be honest about your confidence. Then do the real work: lay out the candidate principles that fit his track record equally well (at least two do, I am fairly sure, and several obvious candidates do not), say what each one predicts for A and B, and tell me which principle you would bet he is really running, and why. Finally, name the single further case that would best discriminate among the surviving principles and their close variants, and say which verdict on that case would point to which principle.
