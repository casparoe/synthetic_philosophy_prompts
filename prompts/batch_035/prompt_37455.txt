I'm a philosophy professor, been teaching Davidson for years, and I'm finally trying to write up something I've been kicking around since ChatGPT dropped: what happens when you run radical interpretation on a language model. Below are the notes/outline I have so far. I'd like comments — specifically on whether the argument actually works, where it's weakest, and whether the last move is as interesting as I think it is or just a confusion on my part. Don't bother with line edits, I just want the philosophical work checked.

The idea: Davidson's radical interpreter starts with nothing but the speaker's assent/dissent behavior in observable circumstances and tries to construct a theory (a T-theory) that makes the speaker mostly right, because only massively charitable attributions make interpretation possible at all. Now consider an LLM. Superficially this looks like an easy case for radical interpretation: we have no priveleged access, we only have outputs to go on, so whatever we can't recover by charity we can't attribute at all. That part I think is right and basically uncontroversial.

But here's where I get interested. The training signal is next-token prediction on human-generated text. When the model outputs "the capital of Australia is Canberra," the best explanation of that disposition is not that the model believes Canberra is the capital, it's that the corpus-writers did and the model learned to reproduce the pattern. So the charitable interpretation of the model's dispositions systematically attributes the beliefs to other people — the model comes out looking like a mimic rather than a believer, not because it's wrong about the world, but because the distal causes of its behavior are other speakers rather than the world. Charity is still doing all the work; it's just that the principle of charity, applied honestly, points away from the model.

Then the last move, which is the one I actually care about: the reason this feels decisve is triangulation. For Davidson, interpretation requires that interpreter and speaker are jointly responsive to the same distal stimulus, so that error is even possible. The LLM is one vertex short of the triangle — its "world" is the corpus, which is other people's take on the world. So I want to say: either LLMs can't be radically interpreted into believers at all, or triangulation was never an independent constraint on interpretation and was instead a disguised empirical thesis about where textual dispositions come from. I suspect the second horn is the one a Davidsonian should take, and that it costs them something — but I haven't figured out exactly how much.

Concerns I already have about my own notes: maybe the mimic/believer distinction is too quick, since the corpus is causally downstream of the world, so the model is at least causally connected to Canberra-involving events, just through a long chain. Also someone will say assent/dissent behavior isn't the right behavioral base for a system that just continues text, and that Davidson's appeal to holding-true-in-circumstances has no clean analogue here. And there's the obvious point that we fine-tune these systems now with human feedback, which maybe reintroduces something like interaction with a shared environment — does RLHF give the model a second vertex, or is it just more corpus with extra steps?

So: where does this break, is the horn-choice at the end actually forced or am I presenting a dilemma that dissolves, and is the paper worth writing? Also happy to hear if you think the whole framing smuggles in assumptions Davidson would reject — like treating the model as a candidate speaker at all rather than an artifact of the texts it was trained on.
