so i'm teaching the philosophy of science chunk of our undergrad "human nature" course, and next week's session is on evolutionary psychology and the "just-so story" worry. i want a short quiz — five multiple choice questions, four options each — that all hang off the same little vignette, which i'll paste below. then i need an answer key that, for each distractor, names the actual misunderstanding it's designed to catch (we hammered this in TA training: distractors aren't random wrong answers, they're diagnoses).

here's the vignette for the questions. a researcher proposes that people's positive reaction to the smell of rain on dry ground (petrichor) is an evolved water-finding adaptation: ancestors on the savannah who found the smell rewarding did better at locating waterholes in the dry season. as evidence they note (i) the pleasant reaction shows up across alot of cultures, (ii) it seems to appear early in development, before anyone's had years of associating rain with relief from heat, and (iii) it's easy to tell an adaptive story for it — finding water mattered constantly. a critic replies that this is a textbook just-so story: you start from the trait, imagine a benefit, and declare victory.

things the questions should actually test:

- the difference between a hypothesis that hasn't been tested yet and one that couldn't be tested in principle. students conflate these constantly.
- the difference between showing a trait would have been fitness-enhancing and showing it's an adaptation for that function (the Panglossian slide, and Gould/Lewontin's point about alternatives like byproducts and exaptation having to be ruled out, not just out-narrated).
- whether cross-cultural presence plus early development gets the researcher anywhere — i.e., can a byproduct be species-typical and show up early too? (i want a distractor that assumes universality settles it, and one that assumes universality is somehow evidence against adaptation.)
- at least one question where the correct answer gives the researcher something — not "this is junk" but naming what kind of evidence would move the hypothesis out of just-so territory, e.g. comparative work on related species, a prediction that could genuinely have come out the other way, whatever you think is the strongest version of that move. i don't want a quiz where every right answer is "the critic wins."

constraints: nothing should be answerable just by knowing the phrase "just-so story" or who Gould is — the vignette has to do the work. a student who memorized "evolutionary psychology = unfalsifiable = bad" should do badly; a student who understands what adaptationist inference actually needs should do well. and don't make the right answers depend on knowing anything about petrichor specifically — it's just a case to reason about, i picked it because there's no famous literature students could pattern-match to.

at the end, tell me which question you'd expect to be hardest for a middling student and why, since i need to know where to spend the review time. please keep the whole thing on the shorter side — it's a 15 minute quiz, not an exam. and if you find yourself wanting to add a sixth question about something adjacent like heritability, dont, five is already more than the session can really cover. also one of the five should be a question where the answer options are all plausible-sounding methodological principles and only one of them states a real requirement rather than a slogan — i want to see if they can tell a methodological demand from a bumper sticker, and if you could make the correct option in that one slightly boring that would actually be a feature, since the seductive options are where the learning is.
