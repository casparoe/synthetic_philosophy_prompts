My roommate is one of those econ-podcast guys and he keeps reaching for the peacock's tail to explain luxury goods. His version goes like this: the peacock's tail is expensive to grow and expensive to carry around, and it's a *reliable* signal of fitness exactly because its expensive — a peacock in bad condition can't afford one, so the peahen can trust the display. Same deal with a guy dropping $40k on a watch: the point was never telling time, the point is that he can drop $40k on a watch and not flinch. Any cheaper signal would be fakeable, so the cost is what makes it honest.

He pulls two conclusions out of this and I want them checked seperately:

(1) Luxury spending isn't dumb or vain, it's doing signaling work that no cheaper signal could do — its rational in exactly the way the tail is.

(2) This one is the spicy one: he says you could tax luxury goods really hard and it would cost nobody anything real. If every watch gets 20% pricier, the signal survives — its like if every peacock's tail grew 10% longer, they'd all still be sorted by fitness. The money is being spent to be seen being spent, so taxing it just redirects it to the government instead of the watchmaker, and the buyer still gets his signal. Apparently this is why some economists like taxing positional goods.

Something about this feels off to me but I can't pin it down. For starters, the tail is a handicap in the strict sense — it makes survival harder, the peacock pays an ongoing fitness tax to wear it. A Rolex doesn't handicap its owner in any way I can see; if anything its a store of value you can resell. Also the peahen can't exactly request the male's bank statements, while a lot of the stuff the watch-guy wants to signal (that he's rich) is partly checkable by other means. And I have a nagging feeling the tax argument, even if his peacock reasoning for it is broken, might still be right for a different reason — or wrong for a reason he hasnt noticed, like that people can just switch to signaling through some other channel the tax doesn't touch.

So: give me the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the tail, the fitness/quality being signaled, the cost, the peahen's choice, the handicap. Then lay out where the mapping breaks. Then, and this is the part I actually care about, take the two conclusions one at a time and tell me which disanalogies are load-bearing for each — like, does the resalability point kill conclusion (2), or is it irrelevant because the signal only needs to be costly at the moment of purchase? And on the tax thing specifically: does it survive, and if so is it for his reason or a better one? If the peacock is the wrong source analogy somewhere, say what would work better.
