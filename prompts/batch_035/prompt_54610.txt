I am drafting a novel whose protagonist, a retired translator dying of a slow illness, claims to take her composure from Zhuangzi, and my manuscript keeps betraying me: in one chapter she treats death as one more turn of the world's endless reshuffling of things, in another she insists that nothing can be known about death and that fear is simply an unauthorized inference, and in a third she talks as though something in her will persist in a way that outruns the body. My editor has flagged this drift, and when I reread the source anecdotes myself — Zhuangzi drumming on a basin after his wife's death and then explaining his drumming, his conversation with a skull who reports that death compares favorably to life, the dying man with a fatal growth who refuses to be embittered on the grounds that the process may be trusted with anything, and Zhuangzi's instructions that he needs no burial because heaven and earth will do — I realized I cannot tell which of these attitudes each anecdote is actually meant to license. They are not obviously the same attitude, and my character has been cycling through them as if they were.

So I would like you to write me a diagnostic quiz, for my own use in building her character bible, that sorts a reader into the reading of Zhuangzi's attitude toward death they actually hold, as opposed to the label they prefer. Eight or nine questions, each built around a concrete case rather than a direct question about doctrine — no question may simply ask whether death is natural or bad or good. Beyond the anecdotes already mentioned, at least two questions should turn on the epistemic motifs: the butterfly dream, understood as a case about being unable to certify which state one is in, and the fact that Zhuangzi never, so far as I can find, flatly asserts that he knows what death is — he asserts things about our knowing and our grieving. Each question should have two or three answer options phrased in plain language that someone who has only read the anecdotes as summarized could choose between.

The scoring key should map answer patterns onto four positions: the process reading, on which death is a phase of the world's ceaseless transformation and grief is a misreading of one's place in that process; the skeptical reading, on which Zhuangzi suspends judgment about what death is and dissolves fear by showing it rests on a claim we cannot check; the survivalist reading, on which something of the person or their vantage is not annihilated; and the therapeutic reading, on which the metaphysics is scaffolding for equanimity and Zhuangzi would be indifferent among the three other theories so long as the composure holds. Two further requirements, please. First, include at least one question built so that it cannot separate two of the four positions, and have the scoring key say which pair it fails on and why — I suspect the process and survivalist readings blur together exactly at the wife-and-basin scene, and I would like the key to test my suspicion rather than assume it. Second, leave room for a respondent who chooses composure at every deathbed question while rejecting every metaphysical statement: if that pattern is a coherent position rather than a cheat, the key should say where it lives, and if it is a cheat, the key should say which answer exposes it.
