Quick heads up that this got long, but I'd genuinely appreciate comments on it. Last spring a cousin doing genealogy found out that our great-uncle Willi served in one of the German order police units in occupied Poland -- not the SS, one of those reserve police battalions like the one Browning wrote about (I checked, not his exact battalion, but the same kind of unit doing the same kind of work). Nobody in the family knew. He came back in '45, worked as a bookkeeper till 1980, everyone remembers him as quiet and kind to the grandkids, and now I have a folder of scanned documents about him and no idea what to do with it. So like a lot of people in this situation I fell down the rabbit hole of the whole Browning vs Goldhagen thing, and I've started writing something -- notes for an essay, not a real essay yet -- and I want comments before I try to make it into one. Specifically: (1) does the argument actually work or does it collapse somewhere, (2) am I being unfair to either Browning or Goldhagen, and (3) is the middle position I'm groping toward a real position or just a way of avoiding choosing.

Here are the notes.

The Browning/Goldhagen debate is usually framed as situation vs character: Browning says the men of the battalion were ordinary, pushed into killing by conformity pressure, deference to authority, war weariness, not wanting to look weak in front of comrades; Goldhagen says they killed because they wanted to, because a deeply eliminationist antisemitism was ordinary German culture, and their eagerness shows it. My first observation: I don't think this is actually one disagreement. It's at least two questions wearing the same clothes.

Question A: why were there shootings at all? Why did the machine of deportation and mass murder run on the labor of ordinary policemen rather than breaking down at that link? For A, the situational answer is almost sufficient on its own: the unit only needed enough men willing to pull triggers, plus a mechanism for quietly reassigning the ones who couldn't. Browning's own evidence supports this -- at Józefów the commander, reportedly in tears, offered anyone who couldn't do it the chance to step out, some did, and as far as we know nothing happened to them. The battalion absorbed the refusals. A unit is a filter, not a mirror: it doesn't tell you what a population is, it tells you what a population yields after selection. So Browning can be completely right about the throughput of the machine while telling you very little about the dispositions of any individual man inside it.

Question B: why did this man kill? This is the question I actually have, because of Willi. And here I want to say the situation/character dichotomy is doing something weird -- it assumes character is one global trait, either you're the kind who shoots or the kind who doesn't. But look at the texture of the records Browning works from: the same man who asked to be excused from shooting volunteered for the deportations; the ones who shot Jews in the morning drank together and complained about the brutality in the evening; men who took photos looted less, or more. These aren't two kinds of people. The dispositions are situation-indexed: shame in front of comrades operates on one lever, disgust at blood on another, ideological hatred on a third, and the battalion's organization consisted largely in pulling the levers in an order that got compliance out of men who would have refused under a different order.

Which brings me to the family papers. Two things stand out. Willi requested a transfer to office work in 1943, and it went through with a note from a superior about his being reliable and conscientious. So: he was there for at least some of it, he asked out at some point (conveniently, though, 1943, when it was getting clear how the war was going -- so is the request evidence of a conscience or of a weather vane?), and the bureaucracy that processed his request was the same one that processed everyone else's, without punishment. That last bit is what I keep turning over: the cheapness of refusal, on Browning's account, cuts both ways. Goldhagen's strongest point, it seems to me, is that they *could have refused and mostly didn't* -- Browning himself shows refusal was available. But Goldhagen runs that straight into deep ideological motivation, and I don't think it follows. Refusing costs you something social (your standing with the men, not your life), so non-refusal is a fact about the local price of a conscience, not a fact about what the man believed about Jews. The battalion set the price. Most men paid it rather than the other one.

So the position I'm circling toward, and the reason I doubt it: the Holocaust needed, at the level of the individual policeman, not monsters and not tabulae rasae, but men whose conformity was for sale cheap in the relevant situations -- and it needed, at the level of the organization, people who knew that and built the machine around the price. Explanation is then situational at the level of the rate and personal at the level of the man, and Browning and Goldhagen each half-own it. The worry I have is that this is just Browning with extra steps -- the conformity/peer pressure story already includes the observation that men respond to social price. Am I adding anything, or just reluctant to say what Goldhagen said and equally reluctant to let Browning's ordinary men off whatever hook I think they should be on? Also, on the family end: given the transfer request and nothing else, I have exactly one data point about one man's levers, and I'm aware I may be constructing a theory of the middle position in order to have somewhere comfortable to put my great-uncle.

Those are the notes. Tear them apart if they need it -- especially the "two questions" move, since everything hangs on it, and the cheapness-of-refusal point, since I think I'm using it against both authors at once and I can't tell if that's clever or muddled. Also tell me if the ending move, where I admit the whole thing might be motivated reasoning about Willi, makes the essay stronger or just makes it about me. I'm not trying to write a history paper -- it's more of a personal essay that has to be honest with the history -- so keep that in mind when judging how much the argument needs to be airtight.
