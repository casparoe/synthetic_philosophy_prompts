I'm teaching a module on philosophy of history and next term we're doing Whig history — Butterfield's 1931 complaint that historians keep writing the past as a steady march toward the enlightened present, with the historian's own era as the destination the whole story was secretly aiming at. My students mostly encounter the idea as a sin historians are warned off committing, a methodological footfault. To shake that up I want to screen Kubrick's 2001: A Space Odyssey and have them read it as whig history committed so openly and so lavishly that it stops being a mistake and starts being an argument. The famous match cut from the bone to the orbiting satellite compresses four million years into a single edit and asserts, rather than argues, that all of that time was one continuous story of tools and increasing mastery — read the past as prologue to us, then immediately pivot and read us as prologue to whatever the Star Child is meant to be. And the monoliths seem to literalise the whig historian's providence: some external intelligence nudging history along a line whose direction is given in advance.

But the film fights the reading too, and I want the students to see that rather than just file it under "progress myth, next". The first tool in the Dawn of Man sequence is a weapon, the thing the ape learns from the monolith is how to kill, and the film winks at the idea that the satellite in the match cut is itself a weapons platform. Then HAL — the purest product of the progress the film narrates — murders the crew, and the surviving human beats him to death with something very like a bone. So is the film a whig history of technology that knows it, a self-critical one, or does the Star Child ending just reabsorb the HAL episode into a bigger happy arc? I'd like a reading that takes Butterfield seriously — what exactly is the sin he's describing, and is the film committing it, refining it (say, into a claim about how beings like us must narrate deep time), or dramatising something his complaint can't see, like the possibility that in cosmic rather than human timescales some version of directional history might be true?

One honest note to end on: if you think this only works by stretching "whig history" way past what Butterfield meant — that the term is about partisan English constitutional history and parliamentary historian's anachronism, not about a myth of cosmic evolution, so I'm laundering a cool film essay through the wrong concept — please say so, and say which bit of the film or of the notion has to give to make the pairing survive. And tell me which scenes you'd actually point a seminar group to, since we only have two hours and I don't want to spend them on the boring middle bit.
