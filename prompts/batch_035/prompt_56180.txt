People keep reaching for the same analogy when process vs outcome supervision comes up: outcome-based reward is like grading a math test by only checking the final answer, while process-based reward is like grading each step of the derivation. The analogy then gets used to argue that process supervision will produce models that generalize better out of distribution and are harder to reward-hack, since the model can't luck into a right answer off garbage reasoning. How far does the picture actually carry? Give me the correspondence table -- what plays the role of the student, the exam, the grader, the partial-credit rubric -- then lay out where the mapping breaks. The one I keep wondering about: in the math test, the written steps actually cause the answer, but a model's chain-of-thought tokens may just be a post-hoc rationalization of a computation happening elsewhere, so is grading them grading "the process" at all? Then tell me which of the disanalogies matter for the intended conclusion and which are harmless, whether the anti-reward-hacking argument survives once they're on the table (maybe process supervision just teaches models to write plausible-looking steps, which is its own failure mode), and if the exam-room picture is the wrong analogy altogether, what would be a better one.
