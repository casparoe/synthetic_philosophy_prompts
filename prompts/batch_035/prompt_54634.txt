I want a decision problem that is a counterfactual mugging with all the sci-fi stripped out of it, and I want you to design it to spec rather than just retell the classic version.

The specs: the branch-determining event (the "coin") has to be genuinely independent of your decision and your dispositions -- I don't want the case quietly collapsing into XOR blackmail, where the offer is itself evidence about the disaster or about what kind of agent you are. The "simulation" can just be another person's judgment of you, made before the coin landed, no better than a shrewd coworker reading your character -- it can be wrong sometimes. Setting should be ordinary life: a business partner, a landlord, an insurance-ish arrangement, a family thing. No Omega, no demons, no perfect predictors, and stakes a normal person would actually face. The case should stay a mugging in the relevant sense: you're only ever in the branch where you're asked to sacrifice something, and whether things went well for you in the other branch depends on what you would have done here. Also, don't stipulate the verdict -- it should fall out of the payoff structure, not be announced in the setup.

After the one-paragraph scenario, check the specs for me. Walk through what causal decision theory says, what evidential decision theory says (they should split in the standard direction), and what an updateless or one-boxing policy says and why. Then tell me honestly which feature of the case is doing the work -- I suspect it's the independence of the coin, since real-world "coins" (who you happen to get as a counterparty, which market you ended up in) tend to correlate with people's dispositions, which is exactly what lets evidential reasoning sneak in the back door. Say what happens to the verdicts if that correlation is allowed back in. Also: does the case survive if the human predictor is only right, say, 60% of the time instead of almost always, or does the updateless policy's edge dissolve as accuracy drops, and at roughly what point? Finish with the strongest way a causal decision theorist would attack the case -- where's the weakest joint?
