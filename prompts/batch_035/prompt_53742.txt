I am writing a term paper for a philosophy of science seminar on Lakatos's methodology of research programmes, and I want to use a case the seminar has not touched: the competition between steady-state and Big Bang cosmology, roughly 1948 to 1970. The textbook treatment of this case is far too quick — the Big Bang made novel predictions, the steady-state camp degenerated, QED — and my supervisor will not accept that. Please write a term-paper-quality analysis that takes the complications seriously. The essay should run in both directions: the framework applied to the case, and the case as a test of the framework.

Since you cannot check sources, here is the factual basis to work from; treat it as fixed, and do not attempt to cite or verify it. Steady-state cosmology was put forward in 1948 by Bondi, Gold, and Hoyle: the universe expands, but matter is continuously created at a rate that keeps the density — and, on the perfect cosmological principle, the universe's overall appearance — forever unchanged. The rival evolving-universe line descends from Friedmann and Lemaître through Gamow's group in the late 1940s, with a hot, dense early phase; in 1948 Alpher and Herman calculated that such a phase would leave behind a relic radiation at a temperature of a few kelvin, a result essentially nobody pursued. The very label "Big Bang" was coined on BBC radio by Hoyle himself, reportedly in a joking spirit he later insisted was not derision. When Hubble's original, much-too-high expansion rate implied a universe younger than the Earth, steady state could shrug while the evolving-universe camp had to wait for the rate to be revised. Through the 1950s, radio source counts (largely from Ryle's group at Cambridge) increasingly indicated that the source population evolves, which steady state forbids; the statistical case was strong by about 1961. In 1964–65 Penzias and Wilson found the excess microwave noise that was identified as the relic radiation; steady-staters answered by rederiving it as ancient starlight thermalized by dust, but the radiation proved too isotropic and too exact a black body for that to work. Hoyle and collaborators never really converted, and a quasi-steady-state cosmology was still being elaborated decades later, in 1993, with Burbidge and Narlikar. Meanwhile the victorious programme kept absorbing large additions: dark matter in the 1970s, inflation in the early 1980s — introduced precisely to solve the horizon and flatness problems, which were genuine anomalies for the unadorned Big Bang — and dark energy in 1998. One further datum worth sitting with: Weinberg observed in the early 1970s that steady state was, in a way to its credit, the one cosmology definite enough to be refuted with the evidence then available.

Organize the analysis as follows.

First, reconstruct the competition in Lakatosian terms: for each side, the hard core, the positive heuristic, and the protective belt, mapping the decisive episodes — the age paradox, the source counts, the helium abundance, the microwave background, the steady-staters' successive adjustments — onto problem shifts, and saying when each shift was content-increasing or content-decreasing.

Second, defend a verdict, but make it sensitive to the choices that actually do the work in the textbook version. Specifically: (a) individuation — were Bondi and Gold's cosmological-principle version, Hoyle's field-theoretic version with a creation field, and the later quasi-steady state one degenerating programme with a growing belt, or three, and does the verdict change between readings? The same question applies to the winners: is there one Big Bang programme from Lemaître to the modern consensus, or several spliced together? (b) Novelty — the background-temperature calculation sat ignored from 1948 to 1965, and Penzias and Wilson were not testing anything; does the microwave background count as a novel fact, and does your answer depend on whether novelty means temporal priority or use? (c) The helium abundance, given that stellar nucleosynthesis — much of it Hoyle's own work — could also account for it, so what exactly was it evidence for? Give the verdict under each principled choice of these parameters, and say whether any defensible setting recovers the clean textbook conclusion.

Third, turn the analysis around and ask what the episode does to Lakatos. Does judging with hindsight reduce this to a rational reconstruction that quietly discards whatever actually moved the scientists? Were the holdouts after 1965 irrational by Lakatos's own lights, and does that reflect badly on the theory or on them? And confront the delayed-degeneration worry: after the victory, the winning programme absorbed dark matter, inflation, and dark energy. Was inflation, which solves two anomalies by positing an unobservable phase of expansion, a progressive problem shift or precisely the kind of content-decreasing auxiliary Lakatos warned against — and either way, what does that imply about how stable the 1965 verdict really is?

Finally, be candid about the analysis itself: identify where it is most likely to fail — which of the individuation or novelty choices, if made differently, would flip the conclusion — and what argument or historical finding would move you to a different verdict overall, including whether the factual narrative supplied above, being the version told by the winners, should itself be treated as part of the rational reconstruction under examination. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; the analysis is what matters. Aim for roughly two thousand words.
