A genetic counselor has just told my partner and me that any child we conceive will have a moderate but manageable condition: a life plausibly worth living, though one with noticeably more discomfort than most. As we weigh whether to try to conceive, I've stumbled into population ethics and onto what's called the procreation asymmetry — roughly, the view that while it can be wrong to create a life of suffering, there is no corresponding moral reason, let alone duty, to create happy lives. Please give me a concrete example of a prospective-parent decision in which this asymmetry and straightforward total utilitarianism deliver different verdicts — one where the total view is pushed toward a conclusion most people would find mistaken, while the asymmetry matches common sense. The example should also bring out the asymmetry's central difficulty: by counting a created person's suffering as a reason against their creation while refusing to count their happiness as a reason for it, the view seems driven toward forbidding the creation of any life containing nontrivial suffering, no matter how good that life is overall. Keep the example self-contained, so that a reader with no background in population ethics can follow it.
