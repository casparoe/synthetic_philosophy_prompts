Ever since my mom died a little over a month ago I haven't been able to stop circling the question of why there's anything at all instead of just nothing. I brought it up with my old roommate, who's a few months from finishing a philosophy PhD, expecting a shrug, and instead he said the question actually has an answer, just one nobody wants. He wrote it out for me and I've been staring at it alot since:

(1) Every contingent fact — every fact that could have been otherwise — needs a ground: something that accounts for it, that makes it so rather than not-so.
(2) Grounding doesn't loop (nothing grounds itself), and chains of it bottom out somewhere. He says even an infinite chain wouldn't help, because then the whole chain is one big contingent thing that still needs a ground.
(3) So every contingent fact is, at the bottom of its chain, grounded in facts that are not contingent — necessary facts, facts that hold no matter what.
(4) A ground is sufficient for what it grounds: if the ground is there, what it grounds can't fail to be there. So if a fact's ultimate grounds hold no matter what, the fact itself holds no matter what. Necessary grounds make a necessary grounded fact.
(5) But then take any contingent fact you like: by (3) its ultimate grounds are all necessary, so by (4) it's necessary — it couldn't have been otherwise. Contradiction.
(6) Therefore there are no contingent facts. Nothing could have been otherwise, ever. Including the fact that there's something rather than nothing — nothingness wasn't a possibility that lost out, it was never on the table, and the question I started with was never really a question.

He says this is basically Spinoza and that serious people have believed it. And here's my problem: I half despise this and half want it to be true. If (6) is right, then it's not true that my mom could have gone to the doctor a week earlier, which feels like it guts something important about her. But it also means nothing was ever at risk, nothing could ever have gone some other way, and there's a version of that which is almost bearable. So I'm exactly the wrong person to evaluate this, and I know it.

Everyone I've asked says the escape is denying (1) and letting some facts just be brute. Honestly, brute facts seem fine to me, but my roommate says that's not an escape, that's surrender — you'd be answering "why is there something rather than nothing" with "no reason, full stop," and he claims that's refusing the question, not answering it. So: which premise or step would you reject, and what does rejecting it actually cost? My instinct is that somewhere in the middle a single word is quietly doing two jobs, but I can't pin it down, and I'm not sure my instinct isn't just wishful thinking. And be straight with me about whether (6) is actually livable or whether it eats itself somewhere I'm not seeing.
