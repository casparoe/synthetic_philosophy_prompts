I am a novelist who has wandered into a metaphysical thicket by way of my own craft, and I would like a careful explanation of what I have stumbled into. Here is the situation. In my work I am constantly exploiting the fact that a sentence is not a list: "the horse bolted through the barley" is one thing said, a single unified thought, whereas "the horse; bolting; the barley; through" is a heap of fragments. I mentioned this to a friend who studied philosophy, and she said I had rediscovered the problem of the unity of the proposition — the old puzzle, associated with Frege and Russell, of what glues the components of a proposition or a fact into one item rather than many. She then told me about Bradley's regress, and there my understanding ends. As she presented it, if you say the horse is tied to its running by a relation, something must in turn tie the horse, the running, and the relation together, and so on without end. But I am not even sure whether this regress is supposed to be about sentences and propositions or about the world itself — about facts, or objects and their properties — and I don't understand what the regress is supposed to show. Is it meant to refute the idea that relations do any unifying work at all, or only the idea that they do it in a certain naive way?

What I would like, in American English and in reasonably formal terms, is an explanation that does three things. First, lay out the regress carefully, in both of its guises if they really are distinct — the unity of the proposition on the side of language and thought, and the unity of facts or states of affairs on the side of the world — and say how they are related and where, if anywhere, the argument trades on conflating them. Second, address the question of viciousness, which strikes me as the crux: I gather that not every regress is fatal, and I would like a principled account of when a regress is merely harmless — when, say, each successor relation is somehow of a lesser or different kind, or when the demand for a further unifier is just a confusion — and when it genuinely embarrasses the view that generated it. You might use my sentence about the horse as a running example if that helps, though I understand the deeper problem is not about grammar. Third, briefly sketch the main escape routes philosophers have taken: treating relations or instantiation as a special non-relational tie, positing states of affairs or structured facts whose unity is primitive, and whatever the other standard moves are, and for each say honestly whether it terminates the regress or merely relabels its first step. I care less about who held which view than about whether any of these responses actually answers Bradley, so if the honest answer is that the regress is widely regarded as unsolved, or as dissolved rather than solved, I would rather hear that than a tidy ending I don't deserve.
