I'm a software engineer on a team building conversational assistants, and a running argument with my manager has sent me back to my old philosophy of mind notes. He maintains that because our system tracks conversational context, corrects itself when users push back, and seems to discriminate reliably between, say, sincere requests and sarcasm, it must in some modest sense grasp the meanings of the words it processes. I keep reaching for Searle's old line that whatever a computer has is merely derived intentionality — meaning borrowed from its designers and users, the way a written word borrows its meaning from writers and readers — while genuinely understanding minds have original, intrinsic intentionality. But I've been rereading Dennett's reply to Searle, in particular his thought experiment about the two-bitser: an old American vending machine engineered to accept quarters, which is shipped off to a country where a physically indistinguishable coin circulates and, after some tinkering, comes to accept that coin. Dennett presses the point that even this humble machine's "beliefs" about what counts as a quarter have a real, if unromantic, historical etymology — in design, selection, and environmental setting — and he suggests that when you trace the story carefully, Searle's contrast between original and derived intentionality becomes very hard to state in a principled way.

I'd like you to compare the two positions for me. Specifically: does the two-bitser genuinely undermine Searle's distinction, or does it only show that intentionality can be misapplied or misdescribed, leaving the original/derived contrast intact? Searle would presumably say the machine never had any intentionality at all, and that Dennett is just indulging in instrumentalist talk — describing it "as if" it believed things for predictive convenience. Dennett, I take it, wants to say that if you refuse the two-bitser its modest intentional states, you will struggle to say anything non-mysterious about how our own intentional states earn their keep, since on his view ours are likewise products of design history (natural selection) rather than some intrinsic spark. One issue I find genuinely hard: my system, unlike a passive coin slot, is a learner — it was shaped by gradient descent on human-generated text, and its internal states weren't specified by any designer in the way a coin mechanism's are. Does that make its intentionality, if any, more like the machine's (derived from engineering) or more like ours (derived from a selection process that rewarded tracking the world)? Please set out both sides fairly, explain where exactly you think the two-bitser bites and where it misses, and then give your own verdict on whether the original/derived distinction survives — ideally with a clear account of what the distinction would have to amount to for it to do real work in arguments about machine understanding.
