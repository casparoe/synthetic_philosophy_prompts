I've recently been made head of analytics at a regional hospital network, and I keep landing in the same argument with our chief medical officer. The question is which number the executive committee should act on: the network-wide figure — readmission rates, mortality for a procedure, patient satisfaction — or the same data partitioned by ward, by case severity, by demographic. Twice now the aggregate and the breakdown have pointed in opposite directions, and each time the debate gets resolved by whoever argues loudest rather than by any principle. Please write me a decision procedure my team can actually run on a report before it goes upstairs: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in "report the aggregate," where in "report the breakdown," and where it must flag that the choice is not statistical at all — that whether the partitioning variable is a confounder, a mediator, or merely a descriptor of no causal relevance is doing all the work, and that someone with substantive clinical judgment has to make the call. Two constraints: it has to be runnable in under an hour by analysts whose causal-inference training amounts to a half-day briefing (so no formal causal graphs, though plain-language versions of the reasoning behind them are fine), and it must include a step for what to do when the partition was chosen after seeing the data, since an analyst who slices enough ways can reverse almost anything. Then run it on two real-feeling cases for me: one where the aggregate makes our flagship cardiac unit look better than the regional average but severity-stratified figures make it look worse, and one where a hand-hygiene initiative shows a clear effect network-wide but no effect within any single ward — and make at least one of the two come out in a direction I won't like. Finish by telling me where the procedure is most likely to misfire and give us the wrong number in front of the committee.
