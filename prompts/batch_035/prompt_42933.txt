So here's my situation. I write novels — literary-ish, mostly first person and close third, and I lean hard on narrators whose grip on themselves is, let's say, incomplete. And I keep running into this thing in the margins of my drafts, and my MFA program sure didn't give me tools for it, so I'm hoping you can.

The thing: some sentences in a narrator's mouth are perfectly grammatical, perfectly true within the story, and yet they read like a mistake. If my narrator says, in present tense, that it's raining but she doesn't believe it is, that comes off as a stroke, not as a fact about her. But if the older, retrospective narrator says she didn't believe it was raining back then, that's fine — normal, even. And "I believe my husband loves me" reads one way, "my husband loves me, though I believe he doesn't" reads another way entirely, and "I don't believe my husband loves me" is somehow not the same as "I believe he doesn't." My editor keeps marking these as continuity errors when I do them on purpose. I've since learned there's a whole philosophy literature on this — Moore's paradox, sentences that aren't contradictory but are insane to assert — plus some distinction about the difference between asserting something and reporting your belief in it, and apparently two versions of the paradox (one about belief, one about its negation). I am not going to read the literature. I have a deadline. But I want what the literature knows, distilled into something I can use.

So: I'd like you to take on the role of a professional philosopher of language/mind who has actually spent time around working novelists, not just other philosophers. Think carefully before you answer — I'd rather you spend a paragraph or two up front brainstorming the terrain (what the paradox and its variants actually show, what the assert vs. attribute-belief distinction buys us, why the tense and the narrative situation seem to matter) and then build me a procedure my future self can run while drafting.

The procedure itself: given any sentence where my narrator asserts something, reports a belief, or denies one — including free indirect discourse in the close-third stuff, which is where I live — it should tell me whether the sentence is (a) fine, (b) fine-but-costly, meaning it signals unreliability, self-deception, comedy, or a narrator losing it, and the cost should be named, or (c) just broken, a reader will parse it as an authorial slip. It has to be runnable by me, tired, in ten minutes, with no jargon surviving into the steps themselves. The questions should come in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and it should be honest about the points where it hands the decision back to me because the question is taste, not logic.

The wrinkles it MUST handle, because they're all over my book:

1. Retrospective first person — the older narrator knows things the narrated self didn't, and believed or disbelieved things the older self now knows were false. Which Moore-ish sentences does the time gap legitimize?
2. A narrator who is knowingly lying to the reader. If she's lying, what happens to all the belief-reporting — is she even asserting anymore?
3. Free indirect discourse in third person — the beliefs are being rendered, not asserted, and I don't fully understand what that does to the paradox, so the procedure shouldn't pretend the first-person analysis just transfers over unchanged.
4. Narrators whose self-knowledge is genuinely warped — the sentence that's Moore-paradoxical on the page might be the most honest thing that character can manage.

Then run it on three of mine, as worked examples: (1) present tense, "I love her, but I don't believe I do"; (2) retrospective, "My father never once lied to me, but I didn't believe a word he said"; (3) close third free indirect, something like: the rain kept on and Marcus believed it had stopped — though honestly feel free to sharpen my cases if mine are muddled.

And one more thing, and I want this dealt with straight: my favorite move in the whole draft is having the narrator flatly assert p early on, then two pages later report believing not-p, with no comment and no acknowledgment. My editor says it reads as an error. I say it's the whole point — the reader is supposed to catch it and realize the narrator can't. If your procedure comes back saying that move is broken, I need the procedure to include the strongest honest case that it's a feature, not a bug, and tell me what would have to be true in the surrounding pages for it to work as a feature. Don't just tell me to stop doing my favorite thing — tell me under what conditions it isn't broken, and be upfront if you think those conditions almost never obtain.

Finally, tell me what the procedure can't do — where it will misfire, where I'll have to decide by ear — so I know what I'm actually holding. Thanks, and seriously, brainstorm first, I can tell when an answer was assembled too fast.
