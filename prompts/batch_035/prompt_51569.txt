Been thinking about when exactly a slur derogates, going mostly by gut on cases. Two Black friends using the n-word with each other: fine. A white guy quoting a rap lyric who clearly means nothing by it: still not fine, somehow. A linguist writing a paper on slurs, mentioning the f-slur in the abstract ("the f-word functions as a slur against gay men"): fine. That same linguist using it in constructed example sentences throughout the paper: less fine, even though the author is gay and writing about reclamation. A homophobe saying "even [f-slur] people deserve housing" — the sentence content is formally pro-gay-rights but the word choice feels derogatory: not fine. A gay man calling another gay man the slur contemptuously mid-argument: not fine, and it feels like the word is doing the *same* derogatory work as in the homophobe's mouth. A straight guy calling his straight friend the f-slur as pure generic banter, not thinking of gay people at all: not fine, but I'm not sure it's the same badness as the homophobe case. And a translator keeping a slur that's genuinely in the old novel she's translating: fine, where inventing one that isn't in the original would not be.

Is there a single account — derogation built into the semantics vs. located in pragmatics/speaker intent vs. basically a matter of who's licensed to say it — that captures all or most of these? If one of my verdicts has to go, tell me which and why, and tell me what your preferred account predicts for a case I left out: someone who's an outsider by birth but has been fully absorbed into the in-group for decades using the word. Please answer in American English.
