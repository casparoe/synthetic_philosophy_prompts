I'm a science journalist working on a piece about consciousness, and I keep running into the desktop analogy. Interviewed a neuroscientist last week who said conscious experience is like the user interface on your laptop: the little trash can icon on the screen isn't a picture of anything inside the machine, it's a convenient fiction that lets you get stuff done, and likewise colors, sounds, the feeling of being a self -- none of it resembles the neural machinery underneath, it's just the control panel evolution handed us. He kept invoking Dennett's user-illusion idea and said this settles the qualia debate: nobody thinks the trash can icon is a mysteriously nonphysical object floating in the screen, so nobody should think the redness of red is either. We're all just confusing the display for the hardware.

The analogy is doing a LOT of work in that argument and I want to audit it before it goes in print. Can you lay out what maps onto what -- the icons, the desktop, the underlying circuits, the user, maybe the programmer -- and then where the mapping breaks? The thing I keep snagging on: in the computer case there's literally a person sitting in front of the screen using the interface, but if consciousness is supposed to be the interface, who's the user? The brain itself? That feels like it changes the whole structure of the comparison, and I can't tell if it's fatal or a nitpick. Also, does the analogy actually carry the conclusion he wants (qualia are harmless fictions, nothing deep is left unexplained), or does it quietly import stuff that isn't there -- like that the interface was designed by somebody for a purpose, or that an engineer can in principle open the case and see what's underneath, which may or may not be how access to our own brain works?

If the desktop picture fails somewhere important, is there a better analogy for what he's trying to say, or is the idea itself too tied to the analogy to survive without it? I'd love the correspondences laid out plainly (a table is fine, I might adapt it for the article), and please be honest about which disanalogies actually matter for his conclusion versus which ones are just philosopher-quibbles that don't touch the argument. Extra credit if you can tell me what a defender of his view would say the user is, and whether that answer keeps the analogy alive or quietly buries it.
