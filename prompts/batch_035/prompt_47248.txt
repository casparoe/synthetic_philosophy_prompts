For my epistemology seminar I've been tracking the regress problem — the one Agrippa's trilemma generates: any belief you justify with a reason needs its own reason, and then either the chain goes on forever, stops somewhere, or loops back on itself. The three standard responses to this seem to have been on the table essentially unchanged for a very long time. Foundationalism stops the chain at beliefs said to be self-warranting or justified non-inferentially, and the standard complaint is that this is either dogmatism or arbitrariness — why does the chain get to stop exactly where the foundationalist's favorite beliefs happen to sit? Coherentism embraces the loop and claims that mutual support among a system of beliefs can be genuinely epistemic rather than a fallacy of circular reasoning, and the standard complaint is that a perfectly coherent system of fictions could still be cut off from the world. Infinitism says infinite chains of reasons are at least possible, and gets told both that finite minds can't complete them and that even if they could, a reason transmitted through an endless chain arrives undiminished only if each link already has warrant to transmit — which reintroduces the original problem. My impression from the seminar literature is that each side has its replies to the complaints, that the replies are well known, and that nobody has been moved by any of it in decades. What I can't figure out is why the impasse is so stable.

I have a hypothesis I'd like you to diagnose rather than merely confirm or reject. It seems to me that the three positions may not be answering the same question. Foundationalism and infinitism both seem to be offering an account of the structure justification must have — a property of how warrants relate — while the objections to each are overwhelmingly about what finite human cognizers can actually do, i.e., about justification as a process someone could carry out. If that's right, then part of the standoff is a confusion between the structure of a justificatory relation and the genealogy of a belief in some particular mind, and the debate might partly dissolve under a distinction rather than be resolved by an argument. But I suspect this can't be the whole story, because there also seems to be a genuine normative disagreement hiding in the middle: whether a belief that stands at the head of a chain needs any positive epistemic property at all, or merely the absence of defeaters. That question doesn't look verbal, and the sides really do seem to disagree about it.

So: is the regress debate substantive, verbal, or a case of several distinct questions — structural, psychological, normative — being run under one heading? In your diagnosis, please make clear what work each side's characteristic moves are actually doing, and distinguish the parts of the impasse that are just people talking past each other from the parts that reflect a real and perhaps unresolvable difference about what justification is. And tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a sharper distinction, a formal model of warrant transmission, findings about how finite agents actually manage the problem, or nothing short of giving up the question of whether the regress even poses a threat to be solved. I'd also like to know whether you think the fact that ordinary epistemic practice never seems to need this question answered tells us anything about it.
