Write a villanelle on Zeno's dichotomy paradox in which the form itself carries the philosophical work. The two refrains should be the paradox's twin poles: one refrain states the halving rule — before you reach the end, you must first cover half of what remains — while the other states the plain fact the paradox cannot digest, namely that every time, you arrive. The tercets should enact the geometric series: each stanza should cover a smaller fraction of the journey than the one before it, so that the poem's own motion compresses toward its destination as it goes. The turn should fall where a villanelle's turn must fall, in the closing quatrain, where the two refrains — which have been alternating and passing each other by for the whole poem — finally stand adjacent as the last couplet. That adjacency should do the work of convergence: the destination is not any term of the series but the limit the terms approach, the point at which halving and arriving turn out to describe the same stretch of road.

The poem should be mathematically literate without being a lecture. It should be compatible with the standard resolution — an infinite collection of intervals can sum to a finite whole, and infinitely many sub-tasks do not make a task infinitely long — while still honoring the residue the resolution leaves behind: no interval of the run is the final one, so no moment within the run is the moment the last task gets done, and yet the run has a last moment. Do not wave this away with a gesture at wonder; hold it precisely, and let the meeting of the refrains in the final couplet be as close to a resolution as the form permits. A reader who knows the paradox should find the poem right about it, and a reader who doesn't should find it a good poem anyway. Avoid naming Zeno or citing the mathematics explicitly; carry the halving in the imagery and rhythm (one stanza may let the diction quietly register the sequence — half, then a quarter, then an eighth), and use the word infinity at most once.
