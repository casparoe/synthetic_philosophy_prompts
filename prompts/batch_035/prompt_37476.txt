I'm a novelist — literary realism, mostly — with a new book underway whose central character does full-service sex work in a mid-sized European city, and I've spent the past year reading the policy arguments around her trade so that her world is rendered accurately rather than atmospherically. The debate I keep circling is the one between the Nordic model (criminalize the purchase, decriminalize the sale), the German and Dutch licensing regimes, and New Zealand-style full decriminalization. What unsettles me is not the empirical back-and-forth but the sense that the two camps are disagreeing about something other than what they claim to disagree about — a phenomenon I know intimately from craft: when two characters argue about the dishes, they are arguing about the marriage.

So here is my request. Restate the Nordic-model-versus-decriminalization debate in the vocabulary of narrative craft, as faithfully as you can — the terms I actually use daily: point of view and who is granted interiority, focalization, free indirect discourse, the distinction between what a character does and what is done to her, backstory and how it presses on a scene without ever appearing on the page, unreliable narration, the difference between a character who is written and one who writes herself. Tell me which positions in the debate are, at bottom, disagreements about who the narrating character is — whose interiority the reader is licensed to enter, whose voice carries the free indirect discourse. Tell me which are disagreements about backstory — whether what preceded the first scene explains it or merely colors it, whether a woman's entry into the trade is the inciting incident or the world of the novel. And where the debate makes claims that are empirical rather than narrative — about trafficking flows, exit services, what happened in Germany versus what happened in New Zealand — flag those plainly as stage directions: things my vocabulary can register as fact or error but cannot adjudicate, and that I should not let myself settle with a metaphor.

Then give me the residue report, which is what I actually care about. Which claims translate cleanly into craft terms, which translate only with distortion — I suspect agency and choice distort badly, since in craft we would say a character is capable of surprise or she is not, which is a different axis entirely — and which have no expression in my vocabulary at all. Don't smuggle in the assumptions of craft and call them translation; I will notice. And tell me which way the residue cuts: is narrative vocabulary missing something the policy debate genuinely sees, or is the debate less clear than it looks — two camps fighting about focalization while claiming to fight about law? I ask because I have to decide, in the next year, whose story this novel is, and I would like to know whether the philosophy of the thing will help me or merely flatter me.
