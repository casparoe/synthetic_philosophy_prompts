I teach the philosophy of language, and next term I'm assigning Austin's How to Do Things with Words alongside Oscar Wilde's The Importance of Being Earnest on a hunch I want you to test and develop: the play is a laboratory in which each of Austin's felicity conditions gets deliberately violated, and the performatives go through anyway. My students will have read Austin but not the play, so your reading should treat the scenes in enough detail that they'll recognize them afterward, and it should be written as something I can adapt into a seminar handout — roughly 1500–2000 words, no citations or reading lists.

The reading must work through the following scenes, which I take to be the load-bearing ones:

1. The premise. Gwendolen will only marry a man named Ernest, and Jack, whose name is not Ernest, resolves to be rechristened. Is Wilde dramatizing the Austinian point that a name is conferred by ceremony rather than discovered — baptism being a paradigm performative — or mocking it? And how should we read the fact that the rechristening never occurs because Jack turns out to have been named Ernest all along: does the ending repair the performative or reveal it was never needed, and does it matter that the revelation arrives via the handbag and Miss Prism's testimony, that is, through evidence and testimony rather than through any act of naming?

2. Cecily's diary. Before she has ever met Algernon, Cecily has written out their entire courtship and engagement, down to the proposal and the ring. When Algernon does arrive, the engagement is already on record. This seems to be the philosophically strangest scene in the play for Austin: a performative that manufactures the past it purports to record. Is it a counterexample to anything Austin actually claims, or does it belong to a different category — fiction, inscription, institutional record? I want your honest verdict here, not a clever one.

3. The sincerity condition. Austin classifies a promise made without the intention to keep it as an abuse — an infelicity, though not a misfire. In Wilde's world nobody is sincere: Algernon's Bunburying, Jack's invented brother, both women's somewhat flexible diaries and vows, even the title virtue of earnestness itself. Yet the engagements, confessions, and the eventual marriages apparently succeed. Two readings present themselves and I can't decide between them: either the play shows that sincerity is not a genuine felicity condition at all, or it shows only that insincerity is undetectable in a society where nobody checks. Which is better supported by the text, and what would Austin have to say about each?

4. Lady Bracknell's interview of Jack. Her interrogation — the lost parents, the handbag found at Victoria Station, the lines and sidings — looks like a formal gatekeeping of participant suitability, which is among Austin's conditions on who may successfully perform a given act. Are her verdicts themselves performative, and is she best read as the play's one genuine expert on felicity conditions?

I'd also like a closing section on where the reading strains. Wilde precedes Austin by decades, and the play is arguably about Victorian earnestness and the hypocrisy of sincerity-as-social-virtue rather than about speech acts at all. Say where the Austinian lens genuinely illuminates the play, where it is mere anachronism, and whether there is anything in the play that the speech-act literature itself lacks the resources to capture — or that it captured only later, in the objections to Austin.

One requirement on how you write it: please keep separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this material. The broad outlines of Austin's taxonomy, the locutionary/illocutionary/perlocutionary distinction, and the misfire/abuse distinction are settled enough that you can state them directly; but whether sincerity is a felicity condition in the same sense as the others, what Austin's ultimate view of performatives amounted to after his own revisions, and essentially every interpretive question about Wilde are matters of live disagreement. Where your reading depends on a contested interpretation — of either Austin or the play — flag that dependence explicitly rather than presenting it as the consensus view.
