I'm a PhD student in international relations, and I moonlight as a research assistant at a policy institute where our main product is short rapid-response memos for parliament staffers -- the kind of thing where someone reads a hawkish op-ed at breakfast and wants a one-page take by lunch. Half of these op-eds run on historical analogies: some columnist says a diplomatic concession is "another Munich," a defense think tank says US-China is trapped in the "Thucydides trap" the way Sparta and Athens were, someone warns about dominoes falling, someone else invokes Vietnam to argue every intervention is a quagmire. My boss wants a reusable procedure we can actually run in fifteen minutes per memo that decides whether such an analogy is doing real work or just decorating a conclusion the author already held. Please think of yourself as a decision theorist / philosopher of history type who knows the relevant stuff -- inside vs outside view, reference classes, base rates, what Tetlock found about expert political judgment -- and really think through the conceptual issues before writing the procedure, like what it even means for an analogy to be "valid" when no two historical situations are genuinely identical.

Concretely I need: an ordered checklist of questions (what makes an answer to each one sufficient, where the procedure can terminate with "reject the analogy," and where it has to punt because the disagreement is about values, not facts), and then run it on three cases: (1) a claim that US-China relations fit the Thucydides trap and war is therefore likely, (2) a claim that some negotiated arms-control concession is another Munich, and (3) a claim that America's relative decline will go smoothly because Britain's handoff to the US as hegemon went smoothly -- that last one is an analogy I personally find pretty convincing, and I want you to be honest if the procedure cuts against it. Also tell me plainly where the procedure will misfire or get gamed by someone who wants a particular verdict.

At the very end, give me a single machine-readable line, \verdict{...}, with your accept/qualify/reject call for each of the three cases in order (e.g. \verdict{1: qualify; 2: reject; 3: accept}) so I can log the outcomes and check later whether the procedure was right.
