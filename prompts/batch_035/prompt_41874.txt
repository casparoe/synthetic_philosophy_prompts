I'm on the board of a mid-sized nonprofit that funds early childhood programs in one metro area — preschool literacy stuff, home-visiting programs, summer reading camps, that kind of thing. At last month's meeting one of our longest-serving board members (who is, awkwardly, also one of our biggest donors) showed up with a printout of a big twin study — Scandinavian registry, thousands of twin pairs — showing reading ability in school-age kids is something like 70% heritable, and he says this means the ceiling on anything environmental is the leftover 30%, so our literacy grants can't do much, and we should redirect money toward identifying genetically at-risk kids early instead. A couple other board members seemed half-persuaded. To his credit he's not being a jerk — he thinks he's the only one taking the science seriously and everyone else is just being squeamish about genes.

The executive committee asked me to circulate a short written memo before next month's meeting recommending what we do — keep funding the literacy programs, shift money, run his screening idea as a pilot, whatever. Can you draft it for me? A few things I want it to do:

- Actually engage his argument on the merits. I don't want the standard "it's complicated" hedge. I want it to say clearly what heritability does and doesn't measure — like, what population the 70% is computed within, what happens to the number when environments get more or less equal (the PKU example and the height-and-nutrition analogy seem relevant but I'm not sure I could explain them right), gene-environment correlation, that stuff — and which parts of his inference survive that and which parts don't.
- Be honest about the strongest point on his side, not strawman him. E.g. if it's true that heritability tends to be high in populations with uniform environments, does that cut his way, ours, or neither? And is there a version of "target kids at risk early" that's actually defensible, even if the genetic framing is off?
- One wrinkle: the programs we fund all operate in one low-income school district, and the twin study he cited was done in a country with basically universal, uniform schooling. If heritability is a within-population statistic, does it even transfer to the population we serve? I think this matters but I'm not confident I'm stating it right.
- End with a concrete recommendation the board can vote on, and a paragraph we could send the donor that won't feel like a brush-off.

I'd like it readable by smart laypeople — our board has a retired nurse, two lawyers, and a guy who owns car dealerships. And keep it to something like 2 pages, since nobody reads past that. Thanks!!
