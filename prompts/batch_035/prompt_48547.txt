i'm teaching a session on sor Juana Inés de la Cruz for an early modern philosophy course and i want to open it with a poem instead of a lecture. we're reading the Respuesta a Sor Filotea, where she defends her studying against the bishop of Puebla (who wrote to her under the pen name Sor Filotea), alongside "Hombres necios" — the redondilla poem where she turns on men for condemning women for faults the men themselves cause. i want you to write the sister poem she never wrote: same form, octosyllabic redondillas with abba rhyme (that's what "Hombres necios" is), maybe 6-8 stanzas, but aimed at the men who bar women from universties and libraries rather than the ones slut-shaming. structure it the way she does: each stanza a fresh turn of the screw on the same hypocrisy, and end on a rhetorical question in the same mold as her closer — ¿O cuál es más de culpar, / aunque cualquiera mal haga: / la que peca por la paga, / o el que paga por pecar? — but transposed to education, something along the lines of who's more to blame, the woman who steals her learning in secret or the man who locks the library and then calls her ignorant. pick your own version, but it has to be a real Sor Juana-style reversal where the two halves mirror and indict each other, not a modern gotcha.

it should also work in her kitchen empiricism — the bit from the Respuesta where she catalogs what she's learned watching eggs fry and syrup thicken and says that if Aristotle had cooked, he would have written much more — the point being that the domestic work men use to keep women out of the schools is itself observation of nature. and give it a subtitle doing the same job hers does, the one announcing that the poem argues from the inconsistency of men who blame in women what they themselves cause.

one thing i feel strongly about: don't leave her humility ambiguous. write the speaker as using self-abasement as a weapon — the poor-ignorant-me voice that makes the addressee look worse with every bow — not as genuinely apologetic. commit to the irony, dont stradle it; same with the closing question, make a definite choice and make it land.

english or spanish or a mix, your call, but if english keep something of the octosyllabic snap and the abba rhyme intact. and after the poem add a short note on where you took liberties — with her actual views, the history, or the form — so i know what to flag for students.
