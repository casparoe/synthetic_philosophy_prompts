I'm putting together a short piece arguing that "collapse" is not a fact about societies but a fact about the vantage point from which we describe them, and I'd appreciate critical comments on the draft notes below. Please tell me where the argument is weakest, whether any of the historical claims are misleading, and whether the concluding move succeeds.

Draft notes:

1. The cases usually lumped together as "collapse" — the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, the Classic Maya lowlands, Rapa Nui — differ enormously in what actually happened. In the Bronze Age, palace economies and international exchange networks disintegrated within a few generations, and literacy disappeared in some places; in the Maya lowlands, dynastic kingship and monument-building stopped and many urban centers were depopulated, while millions of people and much of the culture persisted; on Rapa Nui, the population crashed, but mostly after sustained European contact rather than through self-inflicted ecocide alone. A single word stretches over biological catastrophe, political reorganization, and a change in which activities a society treats as central.

2. One natural response is that the cases share a structure: a rapid, involuntary loss of complexity. Call this the Tainter-style view — societies invest in complexity to solve problems, returns diminish, and eventually the investment cannot be sustained, so the society simplifies. This gives "collapse" a purely descriptive core.

3. But I doubt the descriptive core does the work. A society that stops building stelae, or stops keeping ledgers, has lost complexity in one sense; but peasants who never engaged with palatial administration experience the same event as the disappearance of extraction and tribute. Whether the simplification is a failure depends entirely on whose capacities we count. The metric is never neutral: population, monument output, literary continuity, carbon output, inequality, life expectancy — each delivers a different verdict on the same sequence of events.

4. So my thesis: "collapse" is a evaluative overlay on top of heterogeneous processes. It presupposes an identification of the society with certain institutions — usually the ones that produce the archives through which we know it. Archaeological visibility is doing covert normative work: we call a period a collapse partly because the kinds of activity that leave records stopped. The "Dark Age" is dark mainly to us.

5. Counterargument I want to take seriously: the per-capitarian objection. Whatever vantage point you choose, being killed, enslaved, or starved is bad for the person it happens to. If the Bronze Age collapse involved widespread violence, famine, and mortality spikes, then calling it a failure is not perspectivalism run amok — it's just true.

6. Tentative conclusion: distinguish collapse-as-event (a describable, measurable process of simplification and demographic decline) from collapse-as-verdict (the judgment that a way of life ended that mattered). Historians should keep the first and drop the second; the second belongs to the participants and mourners, not to social science.

Questions for you: Is the move in note 3 from "metrics disagree" to "the concept is evaluative" too fast, given that metrics disagree for plainly descriptive concepts too? Is my characterization of any of the three historical cases off in a way that matters? And does note 5, once conceded, undermine the whole thesis, or can the draft absorb it by treating violence and famine as objective harms that are nonetheless distinct from societal failure?
