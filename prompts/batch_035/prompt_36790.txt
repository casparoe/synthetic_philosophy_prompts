I teach an intro ethics course and for the next unit I'm pairing Hardin's lifeboat essay with Lord of the Flies, which most of them already read in high school. Read the novel through Hardin's frame and tell me honestly whether it fits. His whole setup needs a boat with a carrying capacity and swimmers in the water, but the boys' island has plenty of fruit and no perimeter problem, noone is trying to get in, and the violence is over legitimacy (the conch) and the one piece of capital (Piggy's glasses), not over rations. So the question I want answered: is the island a lifeboat, a tragedy-of-the-commons, or neither, and when the frame fails to fit, does that say something about Hardin's argument or only about Golding's? I want the reading to take both seriously -- which scenes actually trade on scarcity and which on authority, what to do with the ending where the naval officer plucks them out of one collapsing arrangement into a bigger one (a warship mid-war), and where the novel plainly refuses to be about allocation at all. And flag if any single scene does the lifeboat math better than Hardin's own examples do -- Simon's death, maybe, or the decision to keep the signal fire going -- since that would make a great essay question.
