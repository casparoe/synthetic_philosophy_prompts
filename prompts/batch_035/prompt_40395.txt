I'm a retired chemist who fell into philosophy a few years after leaving the lab, and my little reading group has been doing game theory and the epistemic side of it. Last month someone brought an argument that I can't shake, because if it works it seems to prove way too much.

(1) For two people to have common knowledge of some fact, an infinite stack has to hold: I know it, you know it, I know that you know it, you know that I know that you know it, and so on all the way up.
(2) We're finite creatures — nothing going on inside a human head explicitly represents infinitely many nested facts.
(3) So no two people ever have common knowledge of anything, not of the rules of a game and not of each other's rationality.
(4) Backward induction in the centipede game, and iterated deletion of dominated strategies in things like Basu's traveler's dilemma, are both supposed to require common knowledge of rationality.
(5) So those arguments never describe real players, and it's perfectly sensible in the traveler's dilemma to claim near the top instead of grinding all the way down to the miserable $2 outcome.

Here's the part that makes me suspicious: if (3) is true, it sinks a lot more than centipede. Lewis's whole account of conventions — everyone driving on the right being sustained by everyone knowing that everyone knows, etc. — would be a story about creatures that don't exist. And Aumann's result that two rational agents with the same priors can't agree to disagree could never apply to a single actual pair of humans. Yet I've driven on the right for fifty years without dying, so something in the argument must be off.

The friend who brought it says the fix is easy: we only ever have approximate common knowledge and the formal infinite stack is a mathematician's fiction. That answer feels like a shrug to me. Honestly I can't tell whether the problem is (2) itself, or the jump from (2) to (3), or something even earlier. Which step would you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? And a side question: does Rubinstein's email game, where a tiny probability of a dropped message wrecks the whole chain, show my friend is partly right after all, or is that a different point entirely?
