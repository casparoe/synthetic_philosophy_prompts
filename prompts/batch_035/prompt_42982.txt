I am the executive director of a small private foundation with two principal donors — call them A and B — who jointly control a discretionary fund of roughly two million dollars per quarter and hold systematically different ethical views. A is a hedonistic utilitarian who cares almost exclusively about the reduction of suffering, including that of farmed animals, and assigns no moral weight to the existence of additional happy lives as such. B is a pluralist who assigns enormous weight to the long-term future, suspects that ensuring the existence of many future generations matters more than anything else the fund could do, and also holds some rights-based constraints — for instance, against funding anything that involves deceiving research subjects, whatever the payoff. Their disagreements are stable, well-articulated, and not up for revision; my job is not to reconcile them but to help them cooperate despite them.

There is an idea I want to build the foundation's allocation process on: moral trade. As I understand it, the thought is that agents with different values can sometimes each get more of what they want, by their own lights, by making deals with each other, precisely because their disagreements make their evaluations of some margins point in opposite directions or differ sharply in magnitude. The disagreement itself then becomes a surplus to be divided. What I need is a procedure my staff and I can actually run each cycle, given that the donors will each give us about a day per quarter, my staff have no philosophy training, and the board has to sign off on whatever deal we broker.

Write that procedure. It should have, in order: (1) an intake step — a structured interview, not a seminar — for extracting from each donor, in their own terms, the moral weights, priorities, and constraints that matter for the coming cycle's candidate projects, with guidance on how to record lexical priorities and side constraints so they aren't silently flattened into ordinary weights; (2) a test for identifying tradeable margins — places where the two donors' marginal evaluations of the same budget line differ enough, or point in opposite directions, that a deal could make both better off by their own lights — together with criteria for distinguishing a genuine mutual improvement from one that merely looks mutual because we averaged the donors' views; averaging is exactly what the procedure must never do, since the foundation is committed to no one's ethics but each donor is committed to their own; (3) the deal-making rules — how to price one margin against another, what to do when one donor's side constraints rule a trade out entirely, and how to split the surplus fairly when the deal leaves both strictly better off but one much more so; and (4) an explicit hand-off: which questions are factual or arithmetical and belong to the procedure, and which are questions of value, trust, or bargaining power that the procedure must route back to the donors rather than answer itself.

Be honest about where the procedure will misfire. The one I already worry about is incentive: moral trade gives each donor a reason to misreport their moral weights to get a better price, and unlike a market for goods, we have no way to verify introspective reports. Note any others you see.

Then run the procedure on two cases from our last cycle. First: A wants to fund corporate campaigns that speed the phase-out of battery cages, which B regards as acceptable but marginal; B wants the money for research on preventing engineered pandemics, which A regards as overvalued but not bad. Second: B wants to fund advocacy for human settlement beyond Earth, which B sees as securing the existence of an astronomical number of future worthwhile lives and A sees as an act of large-scale moral wrongdoing — the creation of additional sentient beings, most of whose lives would contain, on A's view, more suffering than happiness. I want to see at least one point where the procedure returns an answer I will dislike, and I will tell you now that I expect it to: my own guess is that it will recommend each donor fund the other's priority in exchange for reciprocal funding, doubling both sides' throughput relative to a fifty-fifty split, and that each donor will accept. If that is what the procedure says, say so plainly, and say where in the procedure my discomfort is supposed to be recorded — or whether it is simply nobody's business, since the procedure serves the donors' values and not mine.

Keep the whole thing under 1,400 words. No citations, no reading lists; define anything technical inline. A numbered procedure with prose commentary is fine; skip tables and diagrams.
