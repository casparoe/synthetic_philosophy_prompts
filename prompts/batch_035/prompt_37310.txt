I'm a third-year PhD student in archaeology, working on a regional survey project in a valley that has produced an enormous quantity of undecorated coarse ware, stone wall foundations, terrace systems, and scattered rural sites — but essentially no texts, no inscriptions, and no elaborate prestige goods. The spectacular sites in the neighboring region get the headlines, but my material is the kind most of the world's past actually consists of. My recurring frustration is that every interesting claim about my valley — whether it was organized as a chiefdom or something more distributed, whether there was a temple economy extracting surplus, whether the terrace walls reflect corporate labor institutions or individual household effort, whether a cluster of unusual finds at one hilltop site marks a sanctuary or just a good midden — seems to hang on a long chain of inferences from mute objects, and nobody can tell me how strong any link in that chain is.

I know the classic landmarks of this problem well enough: Hawkes' ladder, with its claim that technology is easiest to infer from material remains and religious ideas nearly impossible; Binford's middle-range theory and its promise that ethnographic and experimental analogies could discipline institution-level inference; Schiffer's work on formation processes and the worry that much of what we attribute to past behavior is actually post-depositional noise; the direct historical approach of working backward from historically documented groups; and the post-processual counterattack insisting that meaning is always context-dependent. What I can't find is anyone sorting out which of the genuinely open problems in this space deserve the effort. The one that worries me most is what I'd call an equifinality problem: the same material signature — say, standardized ceramic assemblages plus nucleated settlement — seems compatible with several different institutional arrangements, and I've never seen a principled account of when, if ever, we can discriminate between them.

Here is what I'm asking for. Suppose I have one three-year postdoc, a statistician and an archaeobotanist as collaborators, and a commitment to working with regional surface data rather than only exceptional, well-preserved sites. Rank the main open questions in inferring beliefs and institutions from material culture — the ones you think actually matter, not just the fashionable ones — and defend the ordering explicitly: which are upstream of the others, which are tractable with my kind of data and collaborators, which are neglected relative to their importance, and which you'd tell me to drop because the answer wouldn't change what I could responsibly claim about my valley. Please also address the prior question of whether "belief" and "institution" even belong in the same inference problem, or whether lumping them — as Hawkes' ladder implicitly does — is itself a mistake worth undoing. And tell me what would have to happen, methodologically, for your ranking to change: what evidence or new technique would move a neglected question up the list? I want a defended research agenda, not a survey of positions.
