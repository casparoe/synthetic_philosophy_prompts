The draft sidebar, as submitted:

"Everyone learns that Cantor proved the real numbers uncountable: no list of the reals can be complete, because diagonalization always produces a real missing from the list. It therefore comes as a shock to learn that, by a theorem of the Norwegian logician Thoralf Skolem in the 1920s, if ZFC — the standard first-order axiom system for set theory — is consistent, then it has a model containing only countably many sets. A model of ZFC must contain a set it calls the real numbers, and ZFC proves that the reals are uncountable. How can a countable structure faithfully represent a theory that proves uncountable sets exist?

The resolution is that countability is relative to the model. Let M be a countable model of ZFC. Inside M, Cantor's theorem still holds: M satisfies the sentence 'there is no bijection between the natural numbers and the reals.' But from the outside, we can see that all of M's sets, including its reals, can be paired off with the natural numbers. There is no contradiction, because the pairing we see from the outside is not a set that exists inside M. M's version of Cantor's theorem rules out only bijections belonging to M, and ours does not. Even notions that seem absolute — 'countable,' 'uncountable,' indeed 'finite' itself — can change their extension when we move between models.

But where exactly is M's blind spot? The natural numbers of M need not be the genuine article: M can contain nonstandard 'natural numbers' that are infinite from our external point of view, and the bijections M accepts as genuine are matched up with these. What M calls a function need not be a function in the real world. This, in the end, is what dissolves the paradox: M's counting devices are not ours, so of course M cannot see our bijection.

Skolem drew a philosophical moral from his theorem. Since any first-order axiomatization of set theory will admit these countable models, the notion of uncountability can never be pinned down by first-order axioms; it is always relative to a model. He concluded that set theory as a foundational enterprise was thereby undermined, and even Gödel — whose incompleteness theorems pointed in a similar direction — later agreed that Skolem had shown set-theoretic notions to be meaningful only relative to a chosen model. The lesson extends to the continuum hypothesis. Because ZFC has models of every infinite cardinality, the question of the true size of the continuum simply has no answer: the continuum hypothesis is neither true nor false, and Skolem had demonstrated this decades before Cohen's celebrated independence results made it official."
