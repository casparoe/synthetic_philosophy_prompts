Write a four-stanza pantoum — sixteen lines, with lines 2 and 4 of each stanza returning as lines 1 and 3 of the next — on conceptual slippage in the letter-string microdomain of Hofstadter's Copycat project. The domain consists of analogy puzzles like this one: if the string abc changes to abd, how should the string ijk change? The rival answers turn on fluid concepts. Read the change as "replace the rightmost letter with its immediate successor" and ijk becomes ijl; read it as "replace the rightmost letter with d itself" and ijk becomes ijd; move to a string like mrrjjj, where the rightmost object is not a letter but a run of identical letters, and the natural answer requires letting the concept "rightmost letter" slip into "rightmost group," so that mrrjjj becomes mrrkkk.

The pantoum is the natural form for this, since it is built entirely out of lines that come back; your task is to make them come back wrong in exactly the right way. Each time a line returns in a later stanza, alter it — swap one letter, substitute one word, shift a punctuation mark — so that every slip is a real slippage rather than decoration: the returning line should be recognizably the same line, but with one of its concepts re-bent, the way "rightmost letter" re-bends into "rightmost group." The first stanza should pose the analogy, with the question mark standing over ijk; the middle stanzas should let the rival answers contend; and the final stanza should render the resolution. The last two lines of a pantoum are, by convention, the opening stanza's first and third lines returned at last — and here the poem faces its real decision: you may bring them back verbatim, the only lines in the poem to return unchanged, or you may slip them one final time like all the rest. Choose, and let the choice carry the argument — about whether a concept is a fixed essence that survives its instances or a role that is remade each time the string it belongs to is remade. Someone who knows the Copycat literature should be able to tell which slip is doing which work; someone who doesn't should still find a good poem about how nothing comes back the same.
