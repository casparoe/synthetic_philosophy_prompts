I run a discussion group on rational choice that mixes philosophy graduate students with a few decision-theory-curious economists, and I want a diagnostic decision tree for Kavka's toxin puzzle — not a survey of who has written what, but an instrument that sorts a person into a position by their reactions to cases. To fix the setup: an eccentric billionaire will deposit one million dollars in your account tomorrow morning if at midnight tonight you intend to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow afternoon; the toxin will make you ill for a day but threatens nothing lasting, and drinking it produces no benefit of any kind; the money is yours regardless of what you do after midnight, and you are free to change your mind.

Build the tree in two passes: first construct the logical space from the diagnostic questions themselves, then locate the positions within it — do not start from a list of names. The tree should begin from a reader who accepts the setup, including that the billionaire can distinguish genuine intention from pretense and that by drinking time nothing further can be gained or lost. Every branch node must be a concrete case or claim that a reader with no knowledge of the position-labels could answer from their own reactions alone. The following questions must appear somewhere in the tree, though you may rephrase, split, or supplement them:

(a) A variant in which the toxin causes one mildly queasy hour rather than a day of illness: does anything change, and if so at which point — the rationality of intending tonight, or of drinking tomorrow?
(b) A variant in which, one minute before drinking time, you are told you may redecide with no penalty: could the intention formed at midnight have been genuine if this offer can be made to you at all?
(c) A variant in which, before midnight, you may sign an enforceable contract imposing a two-million-dollar penalty for failing to drink: does signing alter the analysis, and does what it alters tell us the original puzzle is specifically about internal commitment?
(d) A variant in which the billionaire pays not for an observed, occurrent intention but for the standing disposition you actually have, whether or not anyone ever observes it: does your answer here come apart from your answer to the original, and at which node?
(e) A variant in which the million goes to a stranger you have no concern for, and you are asked only to intend — and find you can: is the intention's rationality tied to your own payoff, or to payoff as such?
(f) A question probing whether tonight's intention-formation is an action performed for reasons or something that merely happens to you when your reasons line up; if the latter, the tree needs a branch for the reader who thinks the question of whether it is rational to intend is ill-posed from the start.
(g) A question probing the time-slice issue: whether rationality evaluates the midnight self and the drinking-time self separately, or the person as one deliberator extended through time.

The leaves must include, at minimum: Kavka's own conclusion; the simple instrumentalist view that one simply cannot rationally intend to drink; the Gauthier-style view on which rationality assesses dispositions and constrained plans over a whole life rather than single acts, so the rational person both intends and drinks; a resolute-choice view on which the agent commits to a plan at midnight and refuses to reopen it; a planning-theory view on which intention has its own rationality, resistant to but defeasibly open to reconsideration; a time-slice view on which the morning self rationally abandons whatever the evening self formed; and a deflationary view on which intending is not under the will's direct control, so the puzzle rests on a false picture of how intentions are formed. Any further leaf you discover in the logical space should be given a name or marked as unoccupied, with a sentence diagnosing why nobody sits there — because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Two further requirements. First, identify any two leaves between which your questions cannot draw a sharp boundary, and say what case would be needed to drive them apart, if such a case can be constructed at all. Second, attach to each leaf one sentence stating the commitment that leaf finds most difficult to defend — for instance, what the Gauthier-style leaf must say about the agent who signs no contract yet still drinks, or what the deflationary leaf must say about people who plainly do form intentions at will in ordinary life. The finished tree should contain between ten and fourteen branch nodes, and no question may trade on a reader's preference for a label over their judgment about a case.
