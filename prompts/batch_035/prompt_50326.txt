Next month I'm leading a session of the philosophy discussion group I run at my local library — about a dozen adults, no formal background, but several sessions in: we've done personal identity and free will, and the phrase "hard problem of consciousness" has been tossed around without ever being properly taken apart. I want next month's ninety minutes to be about the meta-problem of consciousness, as Chalmers has framed it: not why there is something it is like to be us, but why we go around saying there is — why we make claims like "no description of brain processes captures what the pain is like" — and what it would take to explain those claims in the same terms we'd use to explain any other human behavior. Don't explain the meta-problem to me; I need a plan for explaining it to them. Specifically: what has to come first — I assume some version of the hard problem has to be on the table before the meta-problem is even a question, but I don't know how much of it, or whether dwelling on it first will just make the session into another hard-problem session. Which single example should carry the whole thing, and what must that example have and lack? I'd guess the zombie twin is the natural candidate, but I worry it drags the metaphysics of possible worlds in before the explanatory point is in place — tell me whether that worry is right and what should carry the load if it is. The misconceptions I expect, roughly in arrival order: the engineer in the group will hear it as an argument that consciousness is an illusion and be delighted; the two dualists will hear a physicalist ambush and close ranks; and at some point someone will say that if you've explained why we make the claims, you've ipso facto explained the feeling itself, so the hard problem dissolves. Tell me exactly where in the sequence each of these should be headed off — before the idea lands, or as a live objection afterwards — and how. Also tell me what I should openly refuse to settle at this level (whether consciousness is physical seems like one) and how to say so without it sounding like a dodge. Finally, give me one question I can put to the group at the end that would show they've genuinely distinguished the meta-problem from the hard problem rather than just heard a new piece of jargon.
