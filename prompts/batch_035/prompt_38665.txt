My friend and I have just come in from a very long argument about which interpretation of quantum mechanics Leibniz would have embraced had he lived to see twentieth-century physics, and I would like a considered second opinion.

My friend's position is that Everett's many-worlds interpretation would have been irresistible to him, on the grounds that Leibniz already believed in an infinity of fully articulated possible worlds, of which only the best is actualised. My rejoinder was that Leibniz's possible worlds differ crucially from Everett's branches: possible worlds exclude one another and only one is created, whereas the Everettian multiverse grows by unitary evolution and splits without any principle of choice at all; I suspect Leibniz would have found a blooming, unpruned totality of worlds a rebuke to his views on harmony and selection. I also pressed a positive case of my own for Bohm's pilot-wave theory: the idea of a wave that guides particles without ever pushing them recalls Leibniz's pre-established harmony, in which monads agree without causal traffic between them, and his well-known hostility to Newtonian gravitation as an unintelligible occult quality suggests he would insist on a theory whose dynamics is transparently intelligible. My friend replied that this gets Leibniz backwards, since the pilot wave acts on particles while the particles never act back, which is hardly a Leibnizian harmony between equals; and he pointed out that the principle of sufficient reason would anyway cause trouble with any version of quantum mechanics that tolerates brute outcomes.

Setting our quarrel aside, could you give a reasoned assessment of where Leibniz's metaphysics would most plausibly land him among the Copenhagen, Everettian and Bohmian options? Please weigh the considerations above, bring in any further aspects of his philosophy that you think bear on the question (for instance his analysis of matter into monads, his relational conception of space, or the role of divine choice in his theodicy), and tell me frankly whether my friend's possible-worlds argument is as strong as he believes it to be. I would be grateful if you could write your answer in British English.
