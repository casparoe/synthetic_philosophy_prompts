I am a second-year PhD student in machine learning, with a substantial undergraduate background in philosophy, and I have roughly three years and one sympathetic advisor to spend on the conceptual side of the shutdown problem in AI alignment — the question of how to build agents that permit themselves to be corrected or switched off, given that a goal-directed agent seems to have instrumental reasons to prevent both. My program expects empirical work, but I want to write a dissertation that is mostly conceptual and formal, and I must commit to a small number of questions before my next committee meeting.

From my reading so far, the open questions appear to include at least the following, though I may be missing some, and part of what I want from you is a better map:

1. Whether corrigibility should be analyzed as an incentive problem (making shutdown and modification not instrumentally counterproductive for the agent) or as a preference-modification problem (building the agent so that it wants to be corrected before it knows what the corrections will be) — and whether these are competing analyses or two levels of a single account.
2. Whether proposals based on deliberately incomplete preferences — constructions in which the agent is made indifferent between an outcome and that same outcome reached by way of the operators shutting it down and steering — can be made stable under the agent's own deliberation and self-modification, or whether such indifference is always a knife-edge that realistic learning or planning will erode.
3. How corrigibility relates to value learning: are they complements, with a corrigible agent serving as a safe platform for learning values, or substitutes, in which a sufficiently good value learner no longer needs to be corrigible and layering both creates conflicting incentives?
4. Whether the toy formal results generalize. The one I have in mind analyzes a simple helper robot that models whether its human will press the off-switch, and finds that under some assumptions about the human the robot is incentivized to prevent the switch from being pressed, while under others it is indifferent or obedient. Does this survive temporally extended plans, hidden information, and multiple principals, or is it an artifact of a one-shot, single-human framing?
5. What, if anything, corrigibility demands of the agent's attitudes toward its own future states — that is, whether shutdown is best formalized as a behavior, as a disposition, or as something concerning the agent's treatment of its successors.

Which of these (or of others you think I have missed) should I prioritize, and in what order? I care far more about the reasoning than about the list. For each item, I would like to know: whether it is upstream of the others, in the sense that its answer changes what the remaining questions even mean; whether it is tractable for someone with my skills — comfortable with formal epistemology, decision theory, and toy Markov decision processes, not with large-scale experiments; how neglected it is relative to its importance; and what evidence or developments over the next two or three years would move it up or down the list. I would also like your honest view on whether the whole framing is confused in some way I have not noticed — for instance, whether corrigibility names a single property at all, or a family of properties that merely look similar from a distance — and, if so, what a better decomposition would be and how it should reorder my priorities.

Please do not give me a reading list or citations; my advisor will handle the literature. What I need is a defended agenda for three years.
