I run a workshop for early-career ML engineers at a small research org — 14 people, one 90-minute session, no lecturing allowed as a format except the last 15 minutes. I want a game that makes them *run into* specification gaming and Goodhart's law with their own hands, before anyone hears either name. Here's roughly the shape I imagine, but you have latitude on the details.

Participants are split into two kinds of roles: "models" and "evaluators." Models produce some artifact over several rounds (you pick — maybe headlines, or short pitches, or drawings, whatever works in a room with whiteboards). Evaluators score the artifacts each round. The catch: the evaluators only have access to a public rubric, a proxy, while I (the facilitator) privately hold the true objective the rubric is supposed to be measuring. Models get the scores and revise toward higher scores. Stakes should exist but no real money — points, candy, bragging rights, something that actually moves people. Rounds should be short enough that the divergence has time to compound.

What I specifically want the debrief to do: map what happened onto the distinction between optimizing the proxy and serving the goal, and ideally onto the standard taxonomy of Goodhart-style failure modes (regressional, extremal, causal, adversarial — I think those are the usual four, correct me if my memory is off). It would be extra nice if different groups diverge in *qualitatively different* ways so the debrief isn't one story but several, but I don't know how to engineer that reliablly — if you have ideas, include them; if not, say so rather than pretending.

Also include: a materials list, a timeline that fits 90 minutes with the debrief, what predictably goes wrong the first time someone runs this and how to recover mid-session, and an honest note on which of the failure modes the game structurally *cannot* produce no matter how it's run (I suspect pure regressional Goodhart is basically noise-selection and hard to stage deliberately, but I'd like your view). The participants are sharp and cynical about evals already — some will spot the trap in round one, so the design should ideally have something for them too, some way the obvious "it's a proxy, obviously we game it" take turns out to be incomplete.

One more thing: when you hand me this design, tell me how confident you are it actually produces the effect as described, and single out the specific part you'd least bet on working. Don't give me a sales pitch — I want to know where this one is fragile.
