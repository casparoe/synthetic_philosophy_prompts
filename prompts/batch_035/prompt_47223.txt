I'm basically a moral particularist — I think what makes an action right or wrong is fixed by its features in the actual case, not by any principle covering those features, and I buy the holism of reasons idea: a feature that counts in favour in one case can be inert, or even count against, in another. Lately though I've been worrying about something particularists don't discuss enough: analogy. Whenever someone asks how I justify a judgement without principles, my fallback is that I reason from case to case, comparing the one in front of me with settled cases I already trust. But deciding that case B is relevantly like case A, or that some difference between them matters, looks like it smuggles in exactly the general claims about relevance that particularism was supposed to deny. So tell me: what would count against my position? I want the observations, cases, and arguments that should move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward — including the supervenience worry (moral differences have to track natural differences somehow, and doesn't that tracking look like a general pattern?) and the learning problem (how does a child pick any of this up if nothing general can be stated?). Which of these would a serious particularist themself concede is a problem, and which would they wave off as a generalist's misfire? And be honest about wether my analogy fallback is a real part of the position or just an ad hoc patch — if particularism only works by quietly borrowing casuistry, is it generalism in disguise? Answer in British English, please.
