At a recent dinner, two friends of mine — call them Marcus and Elena — got into a sustained argument about what makes an exercise of will "free," and I'd like a careful adjudication of it. The case they fought over: a third friend, a concert pianist, has for years run her life through a scheduling system she designed herself. It now dictates her practice blocks, meals, and sleep, it reschedules anything she tries to displace, and it sends escalating notifications when she deviates. She reports that she dreads it most days, that she has tried several times to loosen it, and that each time she reinstated it within weeks — usually telling herself the loosened version "felt wrong." Neither Marcus nor Elena doubts her basic story; they disagree about what it shows.

The exchange went roughly like this:

1. Marcus opened by saying she has plainly engineered away her free will: her day is decided by a system, not by her; a will worth having would leave room for her to choose Tuesday's Tuesday on Tuesday, and hers leaves almost none.
2. Elena answered that this assumes free will worth wanting is spontaneity, and it isn't: it's acting from arrangements one has authored. On her telling, binding yourself in advance is a paradigmatic free act, not a loss of one — the pianist built the system precisely so it would outvote her weaker daily self, and that is her stronger, considered self winning, which is what freedom is for.
3. Marcus granted that self-binding counts when it remains revocable in practice, but argued this arrangement fails that test: her attempts to revise it keep collapsing, the system effectively argues back against its own revision, and a cage you built yourself and cannot now leave is still a cage. Endorsement that cannot be acted on is not endorsement, he said, just absence of exit.
4. Elena replied that the exit exists — deleting the system takes minutes — and that what she keeps doing is choosing not to use it. A stable pattern of not-revoking over years is better evidence of what she actually wants than her momentary dread, which all self-discipline involves; if we let momentary aversion veto long-standing commitments, no one who trains for anything has free will either.
5. Marcus's closing move was that this makes the test unfalsifiable: Elena counts the reinstatements as authentic endorsement, but he counts them as adaptive preference — sunk-cost protection and identity-preservation dressed up as choice. If both readings fit the same behavioral record, then "what she really wants" has no observable consequences, and Elena's criterion quietly collapses into "she has free will worth wanting whenever she never actually escapes." A criterion that can't be tested, he said, isn't doing any work.

They turned to me to referee, and I found I genuinely couldn't; I don't know how to weigh a yearning for spontaneity against a decade of self-imposed structure, or what would even count as evidence between them. So I'm bringing it to you.
