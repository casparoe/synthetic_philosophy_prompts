My colleague Maya and I have spent several lunch breaks arguing about whether a larger population is better, and I have begun to suspect that her answers, which sound case-by-case, are actually generated by some consistent but unstated rule. She rates the quality of a life on a scale from −100 to +100, where 0 is a life exactly on the border of being worth living, and she agrees that the value of a world depends only on how many lives it contains and at what quality. Her verdicts so far on which of two worlds is better:

1. Ten billion lives at 90 versus twenty billion lives at 90: the second, and not by a whisker.
2. Ten billion lives at 90 versus a trillion trillion lives at 1 (lives only barely worth living): the first, and she calls it not even close.
3. Ten billion lives at 90 versus those same ten billion at 90 plus a trillion trillion additional lives at 45 (comfortable, decent lives, but well short of flourishing): the two worlds are exactly on a par — not better, not worse — and she insists this holds no matter how many of the 45-lives are added.
4. Adding any number of lives at −15 to the first world makes it worse.
5. Ten billion lives at 90 versus twenty billion lives at 85: the second.

I have not yet managed to pin her down on additions whose quality falls between roughly 46 and 84 on her scale.

Next week she has promised a verdict on the case I have designed for her: ten billion lives at 90 versus those ten billion at 90 plus a trillion trillion lives at 40 — lives that are clearly worth living but somewhat below the level that she treats as making no difference.

I would like you to predict her verdict on this case, but I am more interested in the reasoning behind the prediction. What rules are jointly consistent with her five verdicts? I can see at least two shapes of rule that fit — one on which lives below some quality mark subtract from a world's value, and one on which such lives merely contribute nothing — and I suspect there are others. Which rule best explains her track record, and what weighs for and against each candidate, including how parsimoniously each handles her flat refusal, in verdict 3, to let numbers matter at 45 and her equally firm treatment of genuinely bad lives in verdict 4? Given the best candidate, what should she say about my new case, and how confident should I be in the prediction given what her verdicts leave underdetermined? Finally, please design the case (or cases) that would most sharply discriminate between the leading candidates — including, if useful, ones probing the 46-to-84 range she has so far dodged.

There is a practical urgency to this. Maya is about to publish an essay on whether governments should respond to falling birth rates with subsidies for having children. Assuming the additional lives such subsidies would induce have expected quality around 40 on her scale, what will her rule force her to conclude about them, and would her conclusion be different if the expected quality were around 60? Does the underdetermination in her verdicts to date matter for the policy question, or do all the surviving candidate rules agree there?
