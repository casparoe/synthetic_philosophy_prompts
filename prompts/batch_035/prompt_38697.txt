I am trying to understand why the debate over global workspace theory and the access/phenomenal distinction has been stationary for so long, and I would like a diagnosis of the stall rather than another tour of the arguments.

The impasse, as I see it: Ned Block, in a 1995 target article in Behavioral and Brain Sciences, separated phenomenal consciousness (what it is like) from access consciousness (availability for report, reasoning, and control), and objected that workspace-style theories — Baars's and, later, Dehaene's — are theories of access that simply do not address phenomenality. He then tried to establish the empirical independence of the two notions through overflow: Sperling's partial-report experiments and subsequent work on a fragile, high-capacity visual store suggest that subjects briefly experience more than they can report, so phenomenology seems to outrun access. The workspace camp answers that these results show only a rich but unconscious buffer, and that since a report is the only channel through which we ever learn about anyone's experience, the inference from a subject's feeling that she saw the whole array to a claim about unreported experience is theory-laden; Dehaene goes further and ties consciousness constitutively to globally broadcast, reportable information, so that consciousness without access comes out as a contradiction rather than a discovery. Cohen and Dennett, in a 2011 paper, pressed the methodological version of this complaint: a neurobiological theory built on an experience/function division cannot be confirmed or falsified, because any measurement of experience routes through function. Since roughly 2010, nobody on either side has given ground: each overflow result gets reinterpreted as an unconscious buffer, and each reinterpretation is dismissed as question-begging.

My working hypothesis is that this is not an empirical disagreement at all but a disagreement about what counts as data: one side treats first-person impressions of richness as evidence about experience, the other accepts only what a subject can report or a signal can register, and so the two sides never contradict each other about any shared fact. But I am not confident. It might instead be a genuine empirical dispute that is merely very hard; a verbal dispute trading on two senses of the word conscious; or a case of two camps answering different questions under one heading — one group theorizing the machinery of broadcasting, the other theorizing what awareness is like — with the appearance of conflict generated by their shared vocabulary.

Please diagnose the disagreement. Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually commits itself to, not in a survey of names. Tell me whether the dispute is verbal, methodological, or substantive; identify the specific commitments that generate the stall; and say what, if anything, would count as progress — an experimental design that circumvents the report criterion, a conceptual distinction, a reformulated question, or nothing at all. If your diagnosis implies that nothing would move it, say so plainly and explain why that follows from the diagnosis rather than from fatigue with the topic.
