I TA a decision-analysis course — the one where sophomores learn decision trees and expected monetary value and think that's the whole of rationality. Next week I get fifty minutes on the result people call Good's theorem. For my purposes, stated plainly: if you have to pick an act now, you take the one with the highest expected value; but if instead you'll get to observe something relevant for free before acting, and you re-pick once you've seen it, the expected value of that setup is at least as high as picking blind. The gap between the two is what the information is worth. Headline version: a rational agent never turns down free relevant information — where "free" covers both getting it and using it, and the agent actually has to re-optimize after seeing it.

Don't explain the theorem to me. Plan the fifty minutes.

1. Order of pieces. Do they need to grind through one "how much would you pay for this test?" calculation by hand before seeing the general statement, or does the general statement come first and create the appetite for the calculation? Tell me which and why — and keep in mind they've seen Bayes' rule but are honestly wobbly on it, which might matter for what has to come first.

2. The carrying example. Every textbook reaches for the oil wildcatter deciding whether to pay for a seismic survey before drilling. Should I use that, and at what level of concreteness, or is there something better? Whatever you pick, spell out what the example must have (a prior you can write on the board, an observation that shifts it by a factor you can do in your head, two acts whose payoffs depend on the state) and what it must not have (side issues like risk aversion — I don't want the example carrying freight it doesn't need).

3. Misconceptions, in the order they'll actually show up. I can already see two: "so more data is always good, period" (forgetting the free-and-costless clause and the re-optimizing clause), and, as a seperate thing, hearing "never lowers the expected value" as "can't leave you worse off than you'd have been" — it can, in particular realizations; it's the expectation that's protected. Where in the sequence should each get headed off? What else should I brace for? Related: I gather there are genuine exceptions out there when the information doesn't stay private — something like, if everyone learns the same news, the gains from insuring each other evaporate, so the same fact that helps you decide also destroys the deals you could've struck in ignorance — plus cases where you'd want to precommit to ignore what you're about to learn. I'm honestly not sure wether any of that belongs in a sophomore section; you decide how much to gesture at, and tell me why.

4. The check. One question to end on where the tempting answer is the wrong answer, so I can tell the people who got it apart from the people nodding along.

5. What to refuse to explain at this level, and say so out loud, so it doesn't fester.

And if something has to get cut to fit the time, tell me what goes first.
