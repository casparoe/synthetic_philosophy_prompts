I'm taking an epistemology course and we just finished Clifford's The Ethics of Belief and James's reply to it. I wrote up an argument for my paper showing that Clifford's rule eats itself, and my TA said it "proves too much" — she wouldn't tell me where it goes wrong, just that she thinks I equivocate on some word somewhere. The paper's due Friday and I honestly can't see the flaw.

Here's the argument. (1) Clifford's rule: it's always and everywhere wrong for anyone to believe anything upon insufficient evidence — the shipowner's sin wasn't what he believed but that, in Clifford's words, "he had no right to believe on such evidence as was before him". (2) Evidence for a belief counts as sufficient only if it is itself believed on sufficient evidence — otherwise "sufficient" does no work and you've just pushed the insufficient-evidence problem back a step. (3) My only evidence for the reliability of memory is remembered, my only evidence for induction is inductive, my only evidence for testimony is testimonial; when I try to check these sources, they are themselves the checks. (4) So by (2), the reliability of memory, induction, and testimony are things I believe on insufficient evidence. (5) Every empirical belief I hold rests on at least one of those three. (6) So by Clifford's rule, every empirical belief I hold is held wrongly — including belief in Clifford's rule itself, since I got that from my professor (testimony) and from being able to recall and generalize from the reading. (7) A rule that makes every empirical belief impermissible isn't a rule for regulating belief, it just forbids believing outright. (8) So Clifford's rule is self-undermining and false.

Where does this break, and what does rejecting the bad step cost? Here's what worries me about the obvious fix: if I loosen (2) so that circular support from memory/induction/testimony can count as sufficient, I've basically handed James the game — his whole point was that we're sometimes entitled to believe ahead of the evidence when the option is live, forced, momentous, and undecidable on intellectual grounds, and I'd be conceding exactly that entitlement, just for boring sources instead of God and free will. Doesn't fixing the argument save James rather than Clifford? If I have to choose, I'd rather keep Clifford's rule intact than keep (2), but I want to know what I'm trading away either way. Keep your answer under 400 words, and please identify the exact step you reject, not just a general vibe.
