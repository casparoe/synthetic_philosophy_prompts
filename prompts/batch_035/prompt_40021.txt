As part of my coursework in behavioral genetics this term, I keep running into the same gap between what heritability actually is — a ratio of variances within a particular population, in a particular range of environments — and how the number gets used in public writing. This has become a practical problem: I'm on the editorial board of our university's student-run science magazine, and a fair share of what we publish or reject turns on heritability claims. A piece last term reported an h² of about 0.8 for a cognitive trait from a twin study and concluded that differences in school funding barely matter; another treated a GWAS-derived percentage as if it applied to named individuals; a third quietly carried a figure measured in one national cohort across to a claim about a very different population. My co-editors and I adjudicate these by feel, and I'd like to replace that with something we can actually run.

Please write a decision procedure for us. The working conditions: a typical claim arrives as a sentence or paragraph inside a draft, we have about twenty minutes per piece, we have the article and at most the study's abstract in hand, and an editor-in-chief signs off on anything we flag as a borderline call. The procedure should pose its questions in a fixed order, state what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and specify the stopping points — where it clears the claim for publication, where it requires a specific revision to the wording, and where it escalates to the editor because the residual problem is one of framing or emphasis rather than fact. It should be honest about which steps are real checks and which are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and about what the procedure cannot do at all — for instance, it cannot audit the primary study's statistics, and I suspect it also cannot settle how much hedging a readable sentence can bear.

The conceptual machinery I'd want it to draw on, in whatever distilled form survives twenty minutes: the distinction between what a heritability estimate licenses and what it is constantly taken to license — nothing about individuals, nothing directly about malleability, nothing about causes of differences between populations that differ in environment; high heritability coexisting with decisive environmental intervention (the classical PKU case); heritability shifting when the environmental range shifts, so the same trait can yield different h² in populations where some environmental input is nearly constant versus widely varying; the difference between broad and narrow heritability, and which kinds of claims (about plasticity versus about response to selection) each one bears on; and, where the claim rests on MZ/DZ correlations, the rough checks a reader can run from an abstract alone — the Falconer-style computation of h² from the two correlations, and the signal of shared environment when the DZ correlation comes in well above half the MZ correlation.

Then run the procedure on three test claims. First, the classic: a columnist writes that since twin studies put the heritability of the trait near 0.8, compensatory programs are a poor investment — I want to see exactly where in the sequence that sentence dies, and which of its two claims (the number, the inference) survives if any. Second, a subtler one I expect we'll actually face: a well-written piece cites a heritability of 0.7 for a trait measured in a single affluent, relatively homogeneous cohort, and generalizes it to justify a claim about a policy affecting a much broader and less homogeneous population — I want to see how the procedure separates a defensible number from an indefensible scope. Third, include a case where the procedure returns a verdict my board won't like: say, a rhetorically convenient claim we're inclined to wave through that the procedure nonetheless flags. Close by telling us what will predictably go wrong the first month we use this — the shortcuts we'll be tempted into, the claim-types it will misfire on — and whether there is any class of heritability claim the procedure should simply refuse to adjudicate.
