Social epistemology PhD student here (dissertation on testimony, so naturally I see my topic everywhere), and I've kind've gotten obsessed with the implicit epistemology built into Wikipedia's sourcing rules. Sketch of the practice as I read it, and tell me if I'm off:

The Verifiability policy states that "Wikipedia's content is determined by published information rather than editors' beliefs, experiences, or previously unpublished ideas or information," and the No original research policy flatly says that "If no reliable independent sources can be found on a topic, Wikipedia should not have an article about it." So an editor who personally witnessed an event, or proved a theorem, or ran an experiment in their own lab, can't use any of that as the basis for an article claim — first-hand knowledge is banned as a foundation, only testimony transmitted through vetted channels counts. Meanwhile there's a whole apparatus for ranking the channels: academic and peer-reviewed publications are usually the most reliable, then books from respected publishing houses, reputable newspapers, and so on, while outlets without editorial control or fact-checking structures get downgraded or excluded. And separately, when sources disagree, editors are told to present the disagreement while "giving each side its due weight" — majority views dominate the article, fringe views are described as fringe.

Here's what's bugging me. On its face the whole edifice looks aggressively anti-reductionist: the encyclopedia treats certain channels of testimony as a basic source of justification it can rely on wholesale, without any individual editor having earned trust in those outlets through inductive track-record checking (nobody at Wikipedia has verified a journal's whole publication history, and the policy doesn't ask them to). But other parts look reductionist in spirit: ranking sources by type and demoting outlets with bad track records is exactly the induction-on-particulars that a reductionist says all testimonial trust must bottom out in.

So, my question: what theory of testimony does this practice actually presuppose, if any? Is there a coherent implicit view here — maybe anti-reductionism relocated from the individual to the institution, where the entitlement lives at the level of the encyclopedia as a body rather than in any particular editor's justification? Or is the practice philosophically underdetermined, such that several positions in the reductionism/anti-reductionism debate would license it equally well? Please separate what the rules strictly require from what they merely sit comfortably with. And here's the stress test I keep coming back to: suppose the community decided to rewrite sourcing policy on strict reductionist grounds, requiring that every act of relying on a source be earned by track-record evidence available to the relying editor herself. Could the encyclopedia still function at all, or does the no-original-research rule make that literally incoherent — given that even checking "this journal published this paper" is something the checking editor knows only through further testimony?

Also flag any internal tension I've missed. The one I half-see: verifiability is supposed to sever justification from truth (the community long repeated that the bar for inclusion is verifiability, not truth), but due weight seems to encode a prior on which views are actually right — you give the mainstream view most of the article, not just any view held by one prolific outlet. Does the practice need a notion of "where the sources stand" that's genuinely independent of "where the truth is," and does either the reductionist or the anti-reductionist have an easier time making sense of that separation?
