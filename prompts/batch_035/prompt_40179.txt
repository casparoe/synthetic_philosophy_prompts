I am a novelist — four published books, all of them the polished, ambivalently ironic literary fiction of the educated class — and I have been invited to keynote a joint session of a graduate creative writing program and a university philosophy department. My thesis is Tolstoyan in spirit though not in all its conclusions: that the criterion he defends in What Is Art? — that a work's merit lies in the sincerity of the feeling it transmits, and that this transmission, the infection of the reader, is what separates art from counterfeit — is substantially right, provided we discard his religious universalism and his demand that genuine art be comprehensible to the peasantry. My own addition is a testability claim: on Tolstoy's account, infectiousness should be observable in what survives translation — sincere feeling crosses languages, while effects that depend on class-marked registers and on workshop craft flatten out — and by this test the contemporary workshop story, precisely the kind my audience teaches and writes, is mostly counterfeit. I expect the room to be stacked against me: MFA faculty who are professionally committed to the autonomy of form and craft, and two analytic aestheticians sympathetic to institutional and historical definitions of art, who will doubt that sincerity is even visible in a work rather than a fact about its maker. I will concede that Tolstoy turned the criterion against his own greatest novels and that this is a cost, but I intend to hold the core claim. Prepare my question-period notes: exactly five numbered items, no introduction and no conclusion, each item no longer than a few sentences — the objection phrased in the idiom of the person most likely to raise it, then my best reply, with an honest note on where that reply is soft. Let one of the five be the question I am hoping nobody asks, and let it be the one about my own books.
