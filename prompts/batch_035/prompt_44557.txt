I have no background in philosophy — I work in logistics — but I've spent the better part of a year circling what I gather is called the doomsday argument, and I've reached the embarrassing position of being unable to tell whether it's a genuine discovery or an elaborate trick. I'd like your honest credence, with the reasoning laid out.

The version I find hardest to dismiss goes like this. Somewhere on the order of a hundred billion human beings have ever lived, and I have no special reason to think my position in that sequence is anything other than typical. If I treat my birth rank as a random sample from all the humans who will ever exist, then it's very unlikely I'm in the first tiny sliver of humanity. But the kind of futures people paint for a technological civilization — space settlement, trillions upon trillions of eventual descendants — would put me in the first hundredth of one percent. The argument concludes I should heavily discount those long futures and take seriously the possibility that the total number of humans is not enormously larger than the count so far. Please give me your credence in a concrete version: that the total number of human beings who will ever be born is less than one trillion. I want a number, and I want the decomposition that produces it.

By decomposition I mean something like the following, though I'm sure you can do it better than my sketch: the probability that reasoning about one's own birth rank is legitimate at all, versus something like a confusion about probability; the probability that the self-sampling assumption is the right way to handle this, versus the self-indication assumption, under which finding yourself to exist at all favors hypotheses with more observers and the whole argument cancels out; the probability that there is any principled answer to who counts in the reference class — every Homo sapiens, every observer smart enough to run the argument, every conscious being in the universe; and then, on top of the anthropic machinery, your ordinary empirical prior about how long technological civilizations survive, since even without the argument I understand the future is uncertain for ordinary reasons. Assign a rough probability to each component, say one line about why, and show how they combine into your final figure. Then tell me which component the final number is most sensitive to, and what argument or evidence would move it the most.

Two complications of my own I'd like you to address directly. First, an objection that occurred to me and that I've never seen answered to my satisfaction: it seems the argument would have run just as well for a person living ten thousand years ago, and would have told them the end was near, and they would have been wrong. Does that show the reasoning is broken, or is it exactly what you'd expect from a rule that is right ninety-five percent of the time and wrong five percent — in which case the objection proves nothing? Second, a thought I can't fully work out: I only ever find myself asking this question because I happen to be the kind of creature, at the moment in history, where the question occurs to me. Perhaps early humans shouldn't have been able to run the argument at all, because they lacked the concept, and perhaps that asymmetry means the sample isn't as random as it looks. If that's a real consideration, I'd like to know whether it strengthens the argument or dissolves it.

For reference, if it helps you locate my confusion: my own credence swings between about 0.35 and 0.8 depending on the day, which tells you I don't actually have one. I'm not asking you to pick a side in a style dispute — I'm asking what a careful reasoner should actually believe, and where in the decomposition the reasonable disagreement is really happening. And if you think the whole framework is subtly incoherent in a way that makes the question ill-posed, say that too, but give me your credence anyway and let the decomposition show where the incoherence enters.
