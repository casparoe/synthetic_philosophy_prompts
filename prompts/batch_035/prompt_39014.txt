You are an expert in the philosophy of economics and decision theory, with a long track record of teaching technical material to mixed audiences without either condescension or hand-waving. Please think through this carefully — brainstorm the candidate orderings, examples, and framings before committing to any of them — and then give me a definite plan, not a menu of options.

I run a monthly reading group of about twelve people: three economics graduate students, two psychologists, a historian, and the rest educated non-specialists. Next session is ninety minutes plus open discussion, and my job is to teach revealed preference and the meaning of utility. The group has already read the standard textbook chapters, so they will arrive believing two things: that utility is a quantity of happiness that choices track, and that revealed preference is trivially true, because preferences cause choices and choices therefore reveal them. My own view — argue with it if you think it wrong, but take a position — is that both beliefs are confusions, and that the interesting content of the doctrine lies exactly where the two confusions meet: it only looks trivial if you quietly assume the very thing it was supposed to replace.

What I want from you is the plan for the session, not the session itself.

1. The order of ideas. What has to be established before what, and why. In particular, I suspect the ordinal–cardinal distinction cannot simply be stated to this audience but has to be earned by some route, and I want your route, plus a sentence on why the obvious alternatives are worse. Choose one sequence and defend it.

2. The carrying example. One concrete case that has to do the work of the whole session. It must be rich enough that the same behavior can be read as revealing a preference, as failing to reveal one, and as revealing the absence of any stable preference at all — depending on what else we hold fixed. It must not be a shopper choosing apples over oranges; the group has seen that a hundred times and will switch off. Specify what the example must contain, what it must be kept free of, and why the natural alternatives fail.

3. The misconception map. I expect, roughly in this order: the mental-substance reading of utility; the circularity complaint ("he chose it because he preferred it, and we know he preferred it because he chose it"); the thought that once preferences are behavioral, utility talk can simply be deleted; and — from the psychologists — sour-grapes and adaptive-preference cases, which they will raise as though these were objections to the whole framework rather than boundary conditions on it. For each misconception: the exact point in the sequence where it should be headed off, and whether it should be headed off by an argument, a further example, or an explicit concession.

4. Where the session must end. My candidates for the takeaway: (a) utility as a mere bookkeeping device for consistent choice, with no hedonic content at all; (b) utility as real but measurable only at the level of populations and experiments, not the individual chooser; (c) the revealed-preference framework as a useful idealization that quietly assumes the rational agent it was meant to define. Pick one as the group's takeaway and tell me why, in a sentence I could put on the last slide.

5. The check. One genuine question — not a quiz question — I can ask in the last ten minutes whose answers would show me whether they have grasped the distinction between a preference revealed and a preference assumed, rather than merely acquired new vocabulary for it. Tell me what a good answer looks like and what the predictable near-miss sounds like.

6. What to leave out. Whatever else you judge beyond this audience in ninety minutes — consistency-restoration results, the neuroeconomics literature, anything of that kind — name it so that I can say so out loud and it does not hang over the room as a mystery.

Throughout, commit. If you think my picture of the group's arriving beliefs is wrong, say so and plan for what they will actually think instead. If two orderings are genuinely close, still choose, and tell me what made it close.
