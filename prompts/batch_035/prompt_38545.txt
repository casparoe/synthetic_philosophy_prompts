Longtime reader, first-time poster here. This past weekend's LessWrong meetup left me with an argument I can't get out of my head, and I need help finding where it breaks. One of our regulars — stubborn local-hidden-variables guy, the type who's read every paper ever cited in a Bell test discussion section — walked me through this and I *think* it proves too much, but every time I try to point at the bad step my finger comes up empty.

As best I can reconstruct it: (1) Bell's theorem only refutes local hidden variables when you grant extra assumptions beyond locality itself — that the detected pairs are a fair sample of all emitted pairs, that detection events aren't correlated with the hidden variables in a sneaky way, and so on. (2) These assumptions are about the detectors, not about the theory, so they're independent premises that a local theorist is free to deny. (3) Therefore, for any Bell experiment with imperfect detectors, there exists a fully local model in which the source emits pairs according to some local pattern, and the detectors just happen to only register the subpopulation that reproduces the quantum correlations. (4) Since such a model always exists, no experiment with nonideal detectors refutes local hidden variables — it refutes locality *plus* detector assumptions, and he never signed up for the detector assumptions. (5) He even conceded the loophole-free tests (Delft and the others, 2015) closed the detection and locality loopholes simultaneously, but says the move just recurs one level up: the escape is now that the source and the detector settings trace back to correlated initial conditions in the common past, so the settings were never statistically independent of the hidden variables in the first place. (6) And in general — his own generalization, not mine — for any experimental anomaly and any theory, there is always some consistent way to pin the anomaly on conspiratorial behavior in the unobserved parts of the setup rather than on the theory itself. So local hidden variables stands unrefuted, and, he cheerfully adds, so does everything else. He actually said "sure, and the luminiferous aether was never refuted either, it just became silly to believe in," and THAT is where I feel the whole thing comes apart, but I can't articulate why the refuted/became-silly distinction is doing real work there rather than being a vibes distinction.

My instinct is that step (6) is true under some reading of "refute" (deductively rule out?) and irrelevant under the reading science actually uses, but when I say that he just beams and says "great, then Bell's theorem doesn't refute local realism either, welcome to my side." Which feels like cheating, but I can't locate the asymmetry between the aether case and the Bell case. Is it that the detector assumptions were independently testable, got tested and closed, and each closing forced his escape hatch to get more conspiratorial, until the one that's left (the shared-initial-conditions one) is the kind of hypothesis that would equally excuse any experiment from ever refuting any theory — at which point it's not an argument for local realism anymore but an argument that he can't lose? Or is there a cleaner diagnosis I'm missing? Tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs him. He claims he's happy to pay at step (6), and I want to know whether he's actually getting local realism for free there or whether the price tag is just hidden somewhere he's not looking. sorry for the wall of text, this has been bugging me all week.
