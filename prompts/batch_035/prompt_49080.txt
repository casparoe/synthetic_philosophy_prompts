I have been trying to work out, from my own reactions to particular claims, when a generalisation about a generational cohort is legitimate and when it is a stereotype dressed up as sociology, and I would like your help extracting the principle. My verdicts: saying that Millennials were economically scarred by entering the labour market around the 2008 financial crisis seems fine. Saying that Gen Z reports higher anxiety than earlier cohorts did at the same age also seems fine, though more fragile. Saying that members of the Silent Generation tend towards frugality because their childhoods coincided with Depression-era scarcity and wartime rationing seems fine. Saying that Gen X are disaffected, cynical latchkey kids seems not fine — yet it wears exactly the same clothes as the Silent Generation claim, inferring a character trait from formative circumstances. Saying that Boomers ruined the housing market seems not fine either, though it is at least an economic claim of the kind I accepted for Millennials. And I am uneasy about boundaries: the person born in 1981 and the person born in 1983 are meant to belong to different generations, yet almost nothing in the formative-event story separates them, while the person born in 1983 is meant to share a generation with someone born in 1996, whom the usual stories barely fit. So: is there one principle — perhaps about shared causal exposure at a formative age, or about how sharply the cohort is carved off, or about whether membership supports prediction rather than mere description — that captures all these verdicts, or nearly? If not, which of my verdicts should I surrender, and what does the principle that survives imply for cases I have not listed, such as a claim that a cohort's political leanings were fixed by the wars fought when its members were young adults? I would also like to know whether the resulting principle leaves generations as real kinds or demotes them to something like editorial conveniences. Please answer in American English.
