I write a science-and-ideas column for a mid-sized outlet, and roughly twice a month I end up in the same jam: some anomaly or surprising result makes news, there are three or four candidate explanations circulating, and my editor wants a paragraph that says which one readers should actually believe. I keep gesturing at "inference to the best explanation" — the philosophers' idea that you don't pick the hypothesis that's likeliest-sounding, you pick the one that would, if true, make the evidence most expectable (one philosopher's word for this was that we pick the "loveliest" explanation, which I've stolen for column purposes). But gesturing isn't a method, and my readers can smell it.

What I want is an actual procedure I can run in an afternoon before deadline. Something like: questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops and says "write it up as genuinely uncertain." It should translate the standard explanatory virtues — scope, precision, unification, parsimony, having a mechanism — into usable form, and crucially tell me what to do when they conflict, since parsimony and scope seem to pull in opposite directions half the time. It also has to handle two traps I keep falling into:

1. The explanation that "explains everything" can be garbage — fitted to the data after the fact, like a wiggly curve drawn through every point versus a straight line that misses some. How do I distinguish an explanation with real unifying power from one that's just been gerrymandered?
2. Sometimes the best explanation of the data is different from the best explanation of why I'm *hearing about* the data. A single striking study reaching my inbox is itself a data point (it was maybe selected for being striking), and I don't know how to fold that in.

And please be honest about which steps are real procedure and which are me making a judgment call and dressing it up as a checklist — I know some of it will be, I just want it flagged so I can own it in print rather than launder it.

Then run the procedure on three stories so I can see it work:

- A physics collaboration announces that some particles seem to arrive sixty nanoseconds early. Candidate explanations: new physics, a loose fiber-optic cable, a clock synchronization screwup. (This one's close to a real case, so the cable being the answer won't surprise you — use it to show the machinery turning over.)
- A famous social-psychology finding, replicated dozens of times over a decade, fails badly in a big multi-lab effort. Candidates: the original was fraud, the effect is real but fragile and context-dependent, or it was never really there and the earlier replications were selective. I'd love the procedure to vindicate "fragile but real" because that's the story I've told readers before — so tell me if it does that, and if it doesn't, don't soften it.
- A small town has a cancer rate noticeably above the national average, and families are convinced it's the old chemical plant upstream. The boring candidate is that it's a statistical fluctuation plus the fact that someone went looking. I suspect the procedure lands on the boring one, which is going to read as awful in print — show me how to apply it there anyway, and whether the procedure has anything honest to say about when "correct but unprintable" should make me write the story differently.

Finish with what the procedure can't do — the cases where I'm going to have to tell readers "nobody outside the room can know," and why that's a feature rather than a dodge. Lay the whole thing out so I can keep it pinned above my desk. And go easy on the jargon, my readers are smart but they're not philosphy grad students.
