i'm putting together a little zine of poems about decision theory paradoxes for my reading group (long story), and the one i most want to get right is Death in Damascus. you probably know the case: a man in Damascus runs into Death, Death looks briefly surprised — which frightens him — and tells him he'll be coming for him tomorrow, in Damascus or in Aleppo. so the man figures the obvious move is to flee to Aleppo that very night. the catch, as everyone in my group likes to put it, is that the reasoning that tells him to flee is exactly what Death predicted: whatever way he deliberates, he walks into the city Death is already waiting in.

i want a villanelle spoken in the man's voice, that night, while he packs. the two refrains should be the two voices arguing in his head. one is the dominance voice — wherever Death is, my going there or not going there doesn't move him, so i might as well place myself where he isn't. the other is the prediction voice — he already knows what i'll decide, so whichever city i choose is the city he's in. the form should do real work here, not just carry the mood: villanelle refrains come back whether you want them or not, which is kind of the whole point of the case, so each time a refrain returns it should land differently — the night wearing on, the man realizing he can't think his way out of a prediction that is about his thinking. and then in the final tercet, where the two refrains finally get to stand next to each other, that's where i want the coin to appear: the only move he has left is to take the choice out of his own hands entirely and let a flip decide between the cities.

but — and this is the part our group argued about for an hour — the poem shouldn't pretend the coin is a clean escape. if Death can predict coin tosses too, or at least predict whether the man will actually follow where the coin tells him, then the flip is just one more thing that was foreseen, and the man is back where he started, only now he's tired and has a coin in his hand. so i want the very last line to leave it open: coin mid-air, man not yet sure he'd even obey it if it came up against him. someone who knows the decision theory here should be able to tell which refrain is which voice and see why the repetition of the form is doing the work of the case, and someone who doesn't should just get a good, slightly creepy poem about trying to out-think something that already thought of you. no jargon inside the poem itself — no talk of boxes or decision theories — it should all be in the man's plain words.
