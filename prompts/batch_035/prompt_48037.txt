I've been reading Whitehead's The Aims of Education (the 1929 essay collection) for a course on philosophy of education, and I keep wondering how he would react to the stuff my younger brother does every day, since none of it existed when he died in 1947. My brother is learning Japanese almost entirely through a gamified app with a streak counter, and he does his history revision on an app that schedules flashcards for the exact moment you're about to forget them, which apparently some spacing algorithm computes. There are also these AI tutors now that walk a student through a problem step by step, intervening whenever they stumble so they basically never fail or go down a dead end, and big "personalised learning" platforms that diagnose exactly which subskill you're weak on and drill it until you pass, then move you on. My lecturer says Whitehead had this cyclical picture -- romance, then precision, then generalisation -- and was obsessed with warning against filling students up with what he called inert ideas, plus he seemed to think education went badly wrong whenever you tried to skip a stage or rush the early one.

So my question: what would Whitehead actually make of all this? I want to be careful not to just assume he'd be a fuddy-duddy about it. The spaced repetition thing in particular confuses me, because his whole rhythm-of-education idea is about cycles over time, and here's a technology literally engineering the timing of encounters with material -- maybe that's an ally of his view, or maybe it optimises something totally different because it's timing memory, not timing understanding. And the AI tutor that never lets you fail seems like it could be in tension with the romance stage, since wandering and being confused and solving things badly at first seems to be part of the point of that stage -- but maybe he'd think a patient tutor is exactly what enables productive wandering. I'm honestly unsure even where he'd come down on gamification (he did complain about making knowledge heavy and dead, but the app version of that argument would be about the streak counter, not about rigour as such).

Please could you reconstruct, as an argued prediction rather than a loose "he'd probably dislike screens" vibe, what positions he'd take on each of the three -- the gamified app, the spacing-algorithm flashcards, and the never-let-them-fail AI tutor -- grounding each in the actual claims of his education essays (especially the rhythm of education and the anti-inert-ideas material, and whatever he says about the dangers of premature specialisation)? Where his principles pull in different directions, say so instead of forcing a verdict. And flag honestly where you think we just can't extrapolate, because the technology is too far from anything he contemplated. Answer in British English, and keep the typos of mine out of it.
