I should say at the outset that my training is in physics, not philosophy: I spent my career in condensed matter physics, and I have only recently begun reading philosophy of time seriously. I have been working through a popular book on time written by a physicist for a general audience, and I have begun to suspect that its physics is more secure than its philosophy. I would like an assessment of whether its treatment of one particular debate is fair.

The book presents the debate over how objects persist through time as follows: "Einstein showed that simultaneity is relative to a frame of reference: two observers moving past one another disagree about which spatially separated events are simultaneous, and neither is wrong, since the slicing of the world into moments is perspectival in the way 'here' is. It follows that there is no objective division of the world into past, present, and future, and the universe is best understood as a timeless four-dimensional block in which 'now' marks nothing in reality. Now, if objects are real at all, they must be parts of this block; and since the block extends in time in just the way it extends in space, objects must extend in time in just the way they extend in space. A persisting thing is a four-dimensional 'worm' whose temporal parts at successively later moments succeed one another as the successive stretches of a road succeed one another as you drive along it. Philosophers call this perdurantism; its rival, endurantism, holds instead that a persisting thing is wholly present at each moment it exists, rather in the way an actor is wholly present at each point of his walk across a stage. But endurantism is refuted by a simple puzzle about change: a banana is straight when new and bent when old, and if the very same whole thing is present in both states, it is both straight and not straight at once, which is a contradiction. So objects persist by having temporal parts, just as the block universe requires — a conclusion now accepted by nearly everyone who has thought seriously about relativity. And once this is seen, the ordinary notion of personal survival dissolves into tenseless fact: your 'future self' is not you moving on through time but a later part of you, standing to the present part of you roughly as your hand stands to your arm."

I am inclined to accept a good deal of this, partly because the relativistic premises are ones I spent a career using, and partly because the author writes with the confidence of someone reporting results rather than taking sides. But I have read enough philosophy by now to know that confidence is cheap, and a few things about this passage strike me as suspicious: the slide from "the world is a block" to "objects extend in time as they do in space" seemed to happen without argument, and I have gathered from other reading that some philosophers accept the block while rejecting the worms.

Please go through the passage and tell me what a philosopher working on persistence would accept, what they would wince at, and what they would regard as simply wrong. I would like you to distinguish, in particular, the claims that follow from special relativity itself from the further metaphysical steps the author takes to be forced by it; to say whether the "refutation" by the changing banana is a refutation or a live argument with standard endurantist replies; and to say whether the concluding claim about personal identity — that my future self is related to me as my hand is to my arm — follows from any part of the passage that survives correction. I should also like to know whether the claim that the worm view is "accepted by nearly everyone" reflects the actual state of the debate. If parts of the summary are accurate but are doing work they should not be doing — for instance, if a claim about the geometry of spacetime is quietly standing in for a claim about how things persist within it — I would be grateful if you would identify exactly where the substitution occurs.
