My epistemology reading group just finished a session on Moore's "Proof of an External World," and afterward two members, Kira and Dev, got into a dispute about whether the proof actually works that neither of them (nor, honestly, I) could settle. The exchange went roughly like this. (1) Kira opened by conceding the proof's formal credentials — the conclusion that there is an external world follows validly from a premise Moore plainly knew, namely that he had a hand, raised it right there — and argued that this is all a proof ever requires. The skeptic's demand for a proof whose premises no doubter could possibly doubt, she said, is a demand nothing in mathematics or science could meet either, so the demand itself is confused, not Moore's argument. (2) Dev replied that a "proof" that no one who needs the conclusion will accept, and no one who accepts the premises needs, is a proof in name only: Moore's assertive "here's my hand" is exactly what the skeptic denies, so the proof presupposes its conclusion in everything but logical form. (3) Kira answered that question-begging is relative to an audience and its grounds — an argument only begs the question against someone whose warrant for the premises runs through the conclusion — and that Moore wasn't trying to convert the doubter but to point out that anyone with ordinary perceptual knowledge already has warrant for the premise that doesn't go by way of any argument at all; if perceptual experience provides immediate warrant for "here is a hand," the proof transmits that warrant to the conclusion. (4) Dev brought up the classic zebra case — you know "that's a zebra" by looking, yet the argument "that's a zebra, so it isn't a cleverly painted mule" seems not to transmit your warrant, because your visual ground doesn't rule out the mule scenario any better than it rules out the vat scenario — and claimed Moore's proof fails in the same way: the visual experience warranting "here is a hand" is exactly what a deceived brain would have too. (5) Kira's comeback was that if Dev were right, perceptual knowledge would be impossible across the board, since every perceptual belief is compatible with skeptical alternatives, so the transmission-failure diagnosis is just skepticism dressed in technical vocabulary; and she noted that the relevant-alternatives machinery Dev is leaning on is itself contested — if merely possible alternatives can't defeat warrant, the zebra argument transmits fine, and Moore's along with it. (6) Dev closed by saying that maybe so, but then Kira has quietly upgraded a modest little lecture into a full-blown epistemology of perception, so at the very least the proof is not the self-standing, common-sense deliverable it's advertised to be.
