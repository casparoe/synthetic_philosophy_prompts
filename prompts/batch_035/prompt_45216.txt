I'm taking a seminar on classical Indian epistemology, and the unit on Nyāya has me arguing with a friend from an analytic epistemology course. The Nyāya tradition counts śabda — reliable testimony — as a distinct pramāṇa alongside perception and inference: the word of a competent, sincere speaker (an āpta) can itself generate knowledge, rather than merely supplying a premise that perception and inference then have to warrant. My friend is a convinced reductionist in the Humean style: he thinks apparent testimonial knowledge is always inference in disguise, drawn from the speaker's observed track record plus background beliefs, so śabda is at best a convenient heading and not a basic source. I've given him my own number: my credence is roughly 0.85 that the anti-reductionist claim is true, construed as follows — in at least some cases, a hearer with no independent evidence of this particular speaker's reliability acquires knowledge from a sincere assertion, and the epistemic warrant for that belief is not exhausted by what perception and inference, operating on the hearer's other evidence, could generate. His credence is about 0.2.

Please give me your own credence in that claim, but what I really want is the decomposition. Some terms I think belong: (a) whether there are any genuine cases of testimonial warrant that cannot be reconstructed as inference from independently available evidence — including the case of a young child absorbing information from a caretaker before it has anything like a track record, which strikes me as the hardest case for my friend; (b) whether the Nyāya conception of a pramāṇa — an instrument that reliably generates true cognition — maps cleanly onto the contemporary question about basic warrant, or whether translating the dispute across traditions smuggles in a disagreement that wasn't there; (c) whether my phrasing "not exhausted by" makes the claim trivially satisfiable, since a defender of my friend could grant it while insisting testimony is only a defeasible, derivative source, which would collapse our dispute into a verbal one; and (d) whatever further terms you think a careful evaluator needs — for instance, whether the answer generalizes to other putatively basic sources like memory, which the Nyāya tradition itself handled differently from testimony. Assign each term a rough probability with a one-line justification, show how they combine into your final number, and tell me which term you are least confident in and what kind of argument or case would move it most.

Finally, locate the disagreement between my friend and me. Given that he sits at 0.2 and I at 0.85 on the same proposition, which term are we most plausibly divided over? And is our disagreement more likely substantive, turning on what actual cases of learning from speech are like, or verbal, turning on how much reduction a source must undergo before it stops counting as basic? If you suspect our two numbers aren't even tracking the same question, say so plainly — I'd rather learn that now than after the term paper.
