You are a philosopher of science who has spent years working on formal accounts of scientific progress, and you currently sit on the committee that writes the comprehensive exam for our PhD program. Your task is to draft one exam question on the problem of verisimilitude, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. Think carefully about the design of the question before writing: brainstorm two or three candidate framings, discard the ones a well-prepared student could answer by reciting the standard history (Popper's qualitative account, its collapse in the 1970s, the similarity-theoretic repairs of Tichý, Oddie, and Niiniluoto), and only then commit to the framing you will use.

The question you write must be answerable in ninety minutes and must be built around the following scenario, which you should present to the students in full:

Two rival theories of a damped oscillating system are on the table. Theory A postulates the correct functional form -- a sinusoid multiplied by an exponential envelope -- but gets the damping constant wrong, so its predictions drift away from the true trajectory in the long run. Theory B postulates a purely exponential decay, the wrong functional form, but is calibrated so that it matches the true trajectory exactly at every data point anyone has so far measured. A third theory, C, is a curve-fitting polynomial that fits all measured points to arbitrary precision but has no principled structure at all.

Against this scenario, the exam question should require the candidate to do all of the following: (1) explain what considerations pull in opposite directions when we ask which of A, B, or C is closest to the truth -- closeness of fit, truth of the underlying structure, and strength or content of the claims made -- and argue for a principled way of weighing them; (2) address whether the verdict is stable under re-description of the vocabulary in which the theories are stated, engaging the worry that translation between descriptively equivalent languages can reverse similarity comparisons; and (3) say what the scenario shows about whether truthlikeness is a property that attaches to theories intrinsically or only relative to a chosen space of relevant parameters, and defend a stance on that last question rather than leaving it open.

Beyond the scenario, impose one further requirement on the question: it must not let a candidate earn a pass by name-dropping. A student who writes only "Popper's account failed, Tichý and Oddie fixed it with similarity measures, Niiniluoto added degrees of truth" should score poorly even if every clause is accurate, because the question must demand that they construct and defend a comparison themselves, with the costs of their choice made explicit.

Now write the three deliverables:

First, the exam question as the students would see it, including the scenario above stated in your own words and any further specification needed to make the question determinate. Second, a model answer of roughly 700 words that shows what a genuinely excellent candidate would produce -- it should take a definite position on the A/B/C comparison, show the trade-off reasoning behind it, handle the language-dependence worry honestly (including conceding whatever needs conceding), and argue for a stance on the intrinsic-versus-relative question. Third, a marking scheme of five must-hit points worth five marks each (25 total, with the remaining marks reserved for overall quality of argument and writing). For each must-hit point, state precisely what earns the marks, what a near-miss version looks like that earns only partial credit, and add a one-line note on the error most candidates actually make at that point.

Aim the whole package at the level of a PhD candidate who has taken one graduate course in philosophy of science but is not a specialist in formal truthlikeness theory. Any technical apparatus you invoke should be explained in the model answer, not presupposed, and you should not cite or quote specific published works -- refer to positions and argument-types generically, since the exam is closed-book and the point is to test reasoning, not recall of bibliography. Write the exam question, model answer, and marking scheme in a formal, polished register suitable for direct inclusion in the comprehensive exam packet.
