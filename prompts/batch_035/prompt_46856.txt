Next week I have to run a fifty-minute section of an introductory ethics course on the question of whether lying is morally worse than merely misleading. The audience is first-year students with no philosophy background; the previous week covered Kant on lying, including the murderer-at-the-door exchange, so a few of them will arrive ready to insist that all false communication is equally forbidden, while most will arrive with the opposite folk conviction — that a technically true statement is just a clever lie with a lawyer's polish, and the whole distinction is verbal bookkeeping. My own worry cuts against both: that once they see the mechanism of misleading (saying something true, arranged so that the hearer infers something false on top of it), they will swing to the view that the technical truth is automatically the morally safer route, as though the truth of the sentence absolves the inference it was built to trigger.

Don't give me the explanation itself; give me a plan for delivering it. Start with the dependency structure: my working assumption is that they cannot evaluate the moral question until they can mechanically sort acts into lies and misleading statements, so I intend to open with the mechanics — a lie asserts what you believe false, while a misleading statement asserts something true and engineers a false inference from it — but I don't know how much of the Gricean apparatus to invoke by name, or whether to keep everything at the level of what you say versus what you get the hearer to conclude. Then the carrying example: I've been toying with a lukewarm reference letter that praises an employee's punctuality and typing while implying, by silence, a verdict on everything else, but I suspect it lacks the right shape. Spell out what a good carrier case for this session must have and lack — I think it needs the same harmful belief to be conveyable by a flat lie and by a true statement, so that the difference between the two acts is a difference in mode rather than in content or consequence, and stakes serious enough that students actually care — and then either rescue my reference-letter case or replace it, telling me why.

Finally, give me the misconception map. Predict the errors in the order students will actually produce them, and tell me where in the sequence each defusal belongs, rather than leaving them for a cleanup at the end. The ones I anticipate: that the moral difference tracks how much harm is done (it can't, since the two acts can be matched for harm); that a lie just means a false sentence, which the true-but-misleading cases are designed to break; and the post-lesson overcorrection I described above. I also want your judgment on one genuinely hard case: the bald-faced lie, told openly in front of people who know it is false — the student who denies cheating to a teacher who watched the whole thing — where the speaker asserts what he believes false but intends no one to believe it. Does that case belong in this session, and if so at what point in the sequence, or is it a distraction at this level? Then tell me what I should openly decline to cover and say so — the formal machinery of implicature, the casuist tradition on mental reservation, the legal line between perjury and mere evasion — and close with one question I could pose in the last five minutes that would show whether a student can apply the distinction to a fresh case, rather than recite it back to me.
