I spent thirty-six years in condensed-matter physics before retiring, and I have devoted the winters since to catching up on metaphysics, which I now find harder than band theory ever was. I have constructed an argument that I can neither refute nor accept, and I would be grateful for a careful diagnosis: which step I should reject, what the rejection will cost me, and whether some step trades on an ambiguity — and if so, how the two readings should be stated and which one the argument actually needs.

The argument is, I gather, a version of what is called Bradley's regress, but with a premise I supplied myself, out of professional habit.

1. Every truth about the world is made true by something in the world. For "this electron is spin-up" to be true, the world must contain the electron's being spin-up — a state of affairs, and not merely an electron on the one hand and spin-up on the other.
2. A state of affairs is a composite of object and property, and a composite is not the mere list of its parts. An inventory reading "one electron; spin-up" could be accurate even if the electron were spin-down; the inventory lacks unity.
3. Nothing composite is unified by its parts alone. In my working life this was close to a methodological axiom: a proton and an electron do not compose hydrogen merely by both existing; there must be a binding interaction, and "there is a proton and there is an electron" is not a report that there is a hydrogen atom. I see no reason unity should come free in ontology when it never came free in any physics I practised.
4. So a state of affairs requires a tie: something that binds the object to the property.
5. A tie that binds two things is thereby related to each of them; otherwise we have not progressed from the list, but merely lengthened it to three entries.
6. Whatever relates the tie to the object is itself related to both, and so on without end.
7. An endless chain of ties never delivers the unity the original list lacked, since at every stage we have only another list awaiting a tie.
8. Therefore no state of affairs is ever completed: nothing makes any proposition true, and it is not the case that this electron is spin-up — even though the experiment says it is, with error bars I trust more than any argument I have ever constructed.

The conclusion is absurd, and I know Bradley drew radical conclusions from neighboring reasoning, concluding that relations are in some sense unreal; and I gather others have responded by denying that there is any tie left to explain. From where I sit, both responses look like refusing to pay a bill one has already run up. My own instinct is to keep premise (3), which strikes me as a conservation law for ontology — no binding without a binder — but I notice that keeping it appears to cost me the truth of every measurement I ever took, so my instinct is suspect. Tell me which premise to surrender and what the surrender costs; and if some step is ambiguous, name both readings and say which one the argument requires.
