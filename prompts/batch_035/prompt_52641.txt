I'm drafting an essay in the philosophy of art on why originals matter, and I'd be grateful for your comments on the argument as it stands. Please write your response in British English.

The thesis I'm developing is that the value of an original over an indiscernible duplicate is best understood epistemically rather than aesthetically. Imagine a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a Rembrandt: identical brushwork, identical craquelure, identical ageing of the pigments. The two objects differ not in their intrinsic properties but in what they are evidence of. The original is a surviving record of the artist's decision process: it licenses inferences about how it came to be — how the artist resolved problems, what constraints he worked under, what he was trying to achieve — that the duplicate does not, because the duplicate's causal history is different even where its surface is not. On this view, the wrong of forgery is not that it misrepresents how an object looks (it doesn't) but that it severs an evidential relation: a forged painting hanging in a gallery systematically misleads the community about the history of art-making, making the wrong closer to fabricated data in a scientific paper than to ordinary deception about an object's appearance.

The complication is that the epistemic account seems to cover too little and too much. It covers too little to the extent that our attachment to originals outstrips their evidential value — and I suspect it does: much of the market's and the museum-going public's attachment resembles the veneration of relics, a value constituted by a social practice of tracing a causal chain back to a revered origin, which would persist even if the chain carried no information anyone actually used. Distinguish, then, the epistemic original from the ritual original. My claim is that most canonical objects are a blend of the two, that forgery-horror tracks the epistemic component while price tracks the ritual one, and that this explains the otherwise odd fact that forgeries are condemned most harshly precisely when they are hardest to detect — when the epistemic damage is greatest — while heavy restoration, which arguably damages the evidential record more, is tolerated. I set aside Nelson Goodman's autographic/allographic distinction from Languages of Art at the outset, since it classifies arts by whether forgery is possible rather than explaining why the forgery devalues.

Questions I'd like your comments on:

1. Does the argument survive the workshop case, where the master designs the composition but assistants execute most of it? The evidential record then attaches to the design decisions rather than to the hand; is that a refutation of the account or a friendly amendment?
2. Is the epistemic/ritual distinction doing genuine explanatory work here, or is it a relabelling of the familiar instrumental/non-instrumental divide in aesthetics?
3. I'm uneasy about one step: if the duplicate would defeat any physical test, on what non-question-begging grounds does it differ evidentially from the original? Can the argument be repaired, or does this hole sink it?
4. Does the closing claim about restoration hold up, or does it overreach?

I'd welcome comments on the argument's soundness and on whether the overall line is worth pursuing; please don't focus on style, and I don't need references or further reading.
