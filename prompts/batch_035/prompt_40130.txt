I teach a 300-level epistemology seminar (16 students, one 80-minute session) and next week we hit Fitch's paradox — the one where you assume every truth is knowable in principle and apparently end up deriving that every truth is already known. On paper my students nod along, but I don't think they feel how the trick works: the way a Moorean claim (p is true but nobody knows p) is precisely the kind of thing that can't survive becoming known. I want a game where they generate that collision themselves before I name any of it.

Half-formed idea, rework as needed: round one, students earn points by writing down claims that are (a) true and (b) not known by anyone in the room — maybe a sealed jar of coins, index cards, whatever materials make the unknown part checkable later. Round two, the class adopts a verificationist rule, something like: any true claim that gets asserted must be capable of becoming known by everyone. Then they try to cash in their unknown truths under that rule and watch the Moorean ones self-destruct the moment anyone seriously tries to know them. Payoffs in candy or particpation points, no real money. The rules need to be tight enough that gaming it — writing something trivially unknowable, or a claim that stops being true when you check it — doesn't win.

Then the debrief: which specific moments in the game map onto which steps of the actual derivation (factivity of knowledge, knowledge distributing over conjunction, the knowability principle itself), so I can point back at things they actually did rather than lecture. Tell me what will predictibly go wrong the first time I run it — my guess is someone writes down a claim about the future or about their own current mental states, and I need to know how to handle that without derailing everything.

Also be honest about the thing the game can't reproduce: my worry is that any staged version with real people and real checking naturally becomes temporal (nobody knew it *then*, before we counted the coins), and the temporal reading is one of the standard escape routes from the paradox rather than the paradox itself. So tell me where the facilitator has to step in and hold the idealized single-agent, single-moment reading in place, and where the activity is most likely to quietly teach them the wrong lesson. Along the same lines — where do you think your own design is most likely to fail, and what would I see in the room that should convince me to redesign from scratch rather than patch it? I'd rather hear that now than discover it mid-debrief.

One more thing: my co-instructor thinks the whole lesson is just a logic trick with no real payoff for the anti-realist who motivated the knowability principle in the first place. If the activity can hand me one concrete reply to her, great, but don't force it if it isn't there.
