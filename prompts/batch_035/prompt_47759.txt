So I'm taking this course on climate ethics and we've spent two weeks on discounting, and I've gotten obsessed with one specific corner of it. The way my prof set it up, the social discount rate economists use can be decomposed into roughly three pieces: (1) a growth term, where future benefits count for less because future people will likely be richer, so a dollar or a utility unit does less good there; (2) a risk/uncertainty term, since policies might fail or humanity might not even be around; and then (3) this residual piece called "pure time preference," which discounts a benefit purely in virtue of when it occurs, regardless of who receives it or how well off they are. It's that third piece I want an essay about: can it be defended, or is it just arbitrary favoritism toward people who happen to be closer to us on the timeline, no better than discounting the interests of people born in March?

Here's the twist that got me writing this. In section, a friend of mine argued something like this: everyone already accepts pure time preference at the personal level. You care less about a headache next year than a headache today, and nobody calls you irrational for that. And given that your future self is, on psychological-continuity views, arguably not the same person as your present self in any deep sense — connectedness fades over time, future-you is basically a stranger who happens to share your memories — discounting your future self is already discounting somebody else's welfare. So the friend claims the moralist's big objection is a fraud: there was never any such thing as discounting your own welfare, all discounting is discounting of others, and it's all fine. If intra-personal discounting is legitimate even though the discounted party is (on some view) a different person, why exactly is inter-personal discounting forbidden? I sort of suspect this proves too much — if your future self is a stranger, then prudence looks like just a narrow special case of egoism, and it becomes weird to say rational people must be prudent but must not discount their countrymen or whatever — but I can't fully articulate why, and honestly I waffle back and forth between "pure time preference is fine within a life but indefensible in public policy" and "the personal/social distinction does no work at all."

So: write an essay weighing whether pure time preference can be defended. Cover the standard objection (that discounting by mere temporal position is like discounting by birth month or spatial distance), but take my friend's parity argument seriously too — does the intrapersonal analogy actually support pure time preference across persons, or does it instead drag intrapersonal discounting into disrepute? Think about whether the verdict should come out differently for individual decisions versus government policy (e.g., it's one thing for me to discount my own future, another for a ministry to discount the welfare of people who will exist in 2150 who have no say in the matter), and whether the growth and risk terms can quietly smuggle in everything a pure time preferencer wants without the arbitrariness. Please actually reach and defend a conclusion though — I don't want a wishy-washy "there are considerations on both sides" ending. If you think pure time preference is legitimate everywhere, or nowhere, or legitimate only in one of the two contexts, say so and argue for it.
