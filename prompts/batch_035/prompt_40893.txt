quick context: i'm a phd student in development econ and for reasons i still dont fully understand i'm on the allocation committee of a small student-run giving fund. we hand out maybe $80k a year, mostly donations people pledged at graduation. we've accumulated a track record of decisions and next week there's an application i genuinely can't call, so help me figure out what we'd have to say to stay consistent with ourselves.

our precedents, roughly:

1. approved, full ask (~$40k): bednet distribution. the bog standard one, deaths averted per dollar, the whole thing.
2. approved, half ask: an unconditional cash transfer program, after a long argument about whether recipients would "waste" it. the committee didn't care, the RCT evidence was good.
3. rejected: a grant to a local tenant union doing organizing around housing policy. the writeup said outcomes were unattributable and speculative, which honestly could describe half of waht we've funded.
4. approved with conditions: a fellowship that places grad students into civil service jobs (regulatory agencies, ministries, that kind of thing). the defense at the meeting was leverage — one good person in the right ministry is multiplicative. nobody pretended we could measure it.
5. rejected: an application to fund career coaching that pushes people into earning-to-give finance jobs. the reasoning in the minutes was replaceability — the counterfactual hire donates nothing, so the donations aren't really attributable to the person, so why fund the pipeline.
6. rejected: an intervention that was extremely measurable (genuine RCT, effect sizes, the works) but delivered through a partnership with a company that has awful labor practices in its supply chain. we turned it down even though on pure cost-effectiveness it beat the bednets.

the new application: a mid-career guy wants a two-year personal stipend (~$35k/yr, it's salary-gap funding) to leave his job at an agricultural research nonprofit and go work at a facial recognition company that sells to governments, at roughly 4x his salary, with a signed pledge to donate 50% of post-tax income to our top charities. the personal fit is real, he'd genuinely get hired, and the company's product is — let's say — not obviously good for the world. nothing cartoonishly evil, but the kind of thing people would protest if our university partnered with it.

my questions:

- if we want to be consistent with our own track record, what should we decide? give me a best guess and the reasons for preferring it.
- what are the candidate rules that fit the precedents about equally well, and what does each one predict on this case? i can see at least a measurability rule (which anyway doesn't cleanly survive precedent 6), the replaceability rule from #5, the leverage/multiplier rule from #4, and whatever anti-institutional principle explains #6, and i'm pretty sure they don't all agree here.
- which precedent is the closest analog, and is it genuinely analogous or just superficially similar?
- if the precedents underdetermine the decision, what principle are we adopting with each choice? concretely: if we approve, what are we committed to the next time someone wants to earn to give somewhere gross, and does the replaceability objection from #5 just evaporate? if we reject, does the leverage rule from #4 survive at all, or do we have to admit the fellowship only squeaked through because ministries are ideologically comfortable?
- and what case, if it landed next month, would best tell the competing rules apart? i want to design the discriminating case on purpose, not stumble into it at 11pm before a meeting again.
