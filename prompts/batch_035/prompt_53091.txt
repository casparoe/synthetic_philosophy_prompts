Government appraisal practice discounts the future: cost-benefit analysis converts costs and benefits arriving in later years into smaller present values by applying an annual discount rate, so a benefit delivered a century out counts for only a small fraction of the same benefit delivered today, and regulations whose payoffs are distant — climate mitigation, nuclear waste storage, biodiversity programs — live or die on the rate chosen. Several governments have lately complicated the picture by using lower or explicitly declining rates for horizons beyond thirty or fifty years, on the ground that uncertainty about the far future warrants gentler discounting than the near future. Meanwhile the same states make commitments that appear to presuppose the opposite: they subscribe to sustainability principles under which future generations' interests carry no less weight than ours, and their private law compensates delayed wrongs — a pension wrongly withheld for decades, contamination discovered long after it occurred — at full real value, with no reduction for the time elapsed. What theory of the value of time does systematic discounting of this kind presuppose? Is it pure time preference, on which future well-being simply counts for less; an opportunity-cost rationale, on which a present benefit competes with what the same resources could earn if invested; epistemic discounting, on which the shrinkage tracks uncertainty about whether there will be anyone there to enjoy the benefit; or something rooted in the non-identity problem and the thought that people who do not yet exist cannot be wronged now? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably, and say which rival views of temporal value it quietly rules out. Then address the apparent inconsistency: can the discounting of policy appraisal and the non-discounting of compensation law be reconciled under some single coherent view, or must one of them give? Finally, suppose a government candidly adopted pure time preference as its official rationale, abandoning the opportunity-cost defense. What in its appraisal practice would have to change — for instance, the ranking of long-delayed welfare benefits against financially productive investments, the rationale for declining rates at long horizons, or the treatment of intergenerational projects that transfer costs rather than benefits forward — and what would remain untouched?
