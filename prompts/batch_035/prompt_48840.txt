I'm taking a philosophy of language course, and we've just finished the unit on Kripke's Naming and Necessity. I understand the official line: "water is H2O" is supposed to be an example of the necessary a posteriori — necessarily true, but knowable only through empirical investigation. But the more I think about it, the more it seems like the necessity must have come into existence at some point in history. Before the 1780s, when chemistry hadn't yet settled the composition of water, surely it was an open question what water turned out to be — a contingent matter, like any other empirical question. Once Lavoisier and Cavendish worked out the composition, the proposition became necessary. So my first question is: at what point does a truth change from contingent to necessary? Is it when the relevant community fixes the reference of "water," when the science is settled, or something else entirely? It seems odd to say the modal status of a proposition shifted over time, but I don't see what else the Kripkean picture could say.

There's a related thing I'm struggling with, which is why the necessity doesn't make the chemistry somehow self-verifying. If "water is H2O" is true in all possible worlds, then no possible experiment could have come out any other way — there's no possible world where chemists analyze water and find XYZ instead. But chemistry is an empirical science: the whole point is that its findings could have surprised us. Did we just get lucky that the necessary truth happened to coincide with what the labs found, or was the outcome of the investigation somehow guaranteed in advance? My professor hinted that I'm running together two different senses of "necessity" here, but I don't quite see which two. Could you explain where exactly my reasoning goes wrong, and what the Kripkean view actually commits us to about what chemists in the 1780s were doing — were they discovering a necessary truth, or making one true?
