I'm a novelist, and I've been asked to run a single ninety-minute session for an MFA workshop on writing other cultures. The students arrive certain that national character is a polite synonym for stereotype — so certain that the session's real question, whether the notion is coherent at all and whether it is ever acceptable to generalize about a people, never gets argued, only dismissed; and I earn my living generalizing about people, one at a time, so I have a stake in the answer. Plan the session for me rather than giving it: what must be established first, before that question can be debated instead of waved away; the one carrying example, what it must have and what it must lack — I suspect it cannot be a bare statistical fact like a birth rate, since a population-level number seems to leave character doing no work, and it cannot be so exotic that students cannot test it against anything they know; the misconceptions they will bring or form, and exactly where in the sequence to head each off — I would guess at least these: that national character is just climate or geography determinism, that whatever is statistically true of a population is thereby a characterization of it, and that a generalization about a people's circumstances is a generalization about the people; how the sequence should separate what a novelist is entitled to do, namely write a character as formed by shared institutions, histories, and constraints, from what would be a claim about essence; a closing exercise that would show me which students can actually draw the line between a generalization a novelist may write and one they may not; and what I should openly refuse to settle in ninety minutes, and say so.
