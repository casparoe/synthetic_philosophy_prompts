Quick context: I'm in an upper-level ethics seminar and this week was on demandingness objections to consequentialism. After section, two classmates of mine, Priya and Dan, got into it for like forty minutes over coffee and eventually asked me to referee, and I honestly couldn't, so I'm hoping you can.

It started because Priya said the demandingness objection is basically fatal to consequentialism. Her opening move was the standard one: if you take the maximizing view seriously, you have to keep giving until the next dollar would do more good in your hands than elsewhere, which for anyone in a rich country means giving away nearly everything, never taking the career or relationships or hobbies you actually want, and treating your whole life as a resource. No theory that says that about ordinary decent people can be true, she said, so consequentialism is refuted.

Dan's first response was that this isn't an argument at all, it's just reporting an intuition, and the intuition is exactly what you'd expect to be broken. Our sense of what we're "allowed" to keep was built for small communities where the needy were visible and finite, and it never had to cope with a world where the pool of preventable suffering is effectively bottomless. So the feeling that the theory demands too much is itself evidence of nothing — it's like complaining that arithmetic gives inconvenient answers.

Priya said fine, forget the gut feeling, here's a real point: on this view nothing you care about has any independent standing, because any project of yours can always be outweighed by enough benefit to strangers. That's the integrity thing — a theory that leaves no room for you to be the author of your own life treats you as a mere pass-through for value, and "a theory humans could actually live by" is a constraint on moral theories, not a bonus.

Dan had two replies to that. First, saying a theory is "too" demanding presupposes a threshold somewhere — some level of sacrifice that flips from required to too much — and any principled place you draw that line looks arbitrary, so maybe demandingness is just a scalar fact about theories that can't refute anything. Second, he said the objection proves too much: commonsense morality, once you take the drowning child case seriously, is also wildly demanding — you'd have to rescue the stranger at the cost of your expensive shoes — so the only difference is that consequentialism does the arithmetic out loud.

Priya then brought up fair shares: her thought was that your duty is capped at what you'd owe in a world where everyone did their part, so once you've given your share you're off the hook even though others slack. Otherwise your obligations balloon because of strangers' noncompliance, and whether you may keep anything depends on how badly other people behave, which is crazy. Dan said that caps-morality is itself smuggling in a nonconsequentialist idea — that the point of your giving is to play a role in a cooperative scheme rather than to actually reduce suffering — so if that's what saves you from total demandingness, you've quietly changed theories.

Can you adjudicate this move by move — rate each step of the exchange from clearly wrong to clearly right, with brief reasons, and then say who is more right overall? Also, if there's something both of them are missing that would reframe the whole thing, I'd want to know that too (they never once talked about whether it matters that the costs of moral error fall on other people when you demand too little, but on you when you demand too much — I have no idea if that's a real consideration or just something I thought of in the shower). Please answer in American English.
