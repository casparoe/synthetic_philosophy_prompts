You know how companies have run internal prediction markets where employees bet on ship dates, and election betting markets are now quoted by pundits as the best available probability estimates? The justification is Bayesian: traders show up with different private information, and assuming a shared prior they'll bet until the price reflects everything anyone knows, so the price can be read straight off as a probability. But the same formal setup also yields a no-trade theorem (Milgrom and Stokey's, sitting right next to Aumann's agree-to-disagree result): if everyone genuinely shared a prior and traded only on information differences, a rational trader would refuse both sides of a purely speculative bet, and the market would never get any volume at all. So the institution seems to need the common prior assumption to make prices *mean* anything, and needs it to fail for anyone to trade in the first place. That's the puzzle I want untangled.

You're a formal epistemologist who has also thought alot about real institutions. Think carefully, and maybe brainstorm a few candidate views before committing: what epistemology does the prediction-market practice actually presuppose? Is it "priors are roughly common locally," or "trading is driven by liquidity needs, risk attitudes and overconfidence rather than information," or "the price is just the wealth-weighted average of people's competing priors"? Say which parts of the practice strictly require which assumption and which are compatible with several. And if you took seriously the idea that participants hold genuinely different priors — what would prices still be good for? Would a prediction-market price still be a probability that an outside observer should adopt?
