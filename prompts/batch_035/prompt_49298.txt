I'm a journalist on the science-and-ideas beat at a mid-sized outlet -- one week it's epigenetics, the next it's whatever Polymarket did wrong. I've written a fair amount about forecasting tournaments, superforecasters, election markets, all that. Readers keep asking me about futarchy, the Robin Hanson idea where you vote on values but bet on beliefs, and I keep giving them a shrug dressed up as a paragraph. So I've been trying to figure out what I actually think, going by gut reactions to cases the way I do when a story is too technical for me to fake expertise. I want you to find the principle behind my verdicts, if there is one.

My verdicts, for what they're worth:

1. Public real-money markets on elections, Fed decisions, whether a bill passes: good. I cite them in copy, they beat pundits, no hesitation.
2. A newsroom running an internal market on whether its own big investigations will hold up or get retracted: fine, actually seems obviously smart.
3. Futarchy proper at the city level -- define some metric like median income or life expectancy, run conditional markets on policy bundles, implement whichever side the market favors: gut says no. But I can't articulate why an accuracy-tested market is worse than a city council that can't predict anything.
4. A market on whether a specific politician dies in office (the Polymarket assassination-price thing): clearly not fine, but I notice it feels different from the election markets in (1), and both are just markets on events that other people can influence. That bugs me.
5. A billionaire openly spends millions to push up the price on a market about whether a tariff will help employment, purely to get the tariff implemented, planning to eat the losses: not fine. That's manipulation, full stop.
6. Except then I read the counterargument that profit-seeking manipulators end up subsidizing information, because informed traders take their money, and markets get MORE accurate not less, and I find myself half-convinced. So (5) flips on me depending on which paper I read last.
7. Markets on 30-year-out policy outcomes, climate stuff, pension reform: thin, dumb, dominated by hobbyists. But then again so is the electorate on those timescales. My verdict is that the market would still be worse somehow, even though I'd trust markets over politics for the 6-month stuff, and I can't defend that asymmetry.
8. A government that just publishes market prices as advice but keeps human discretion: totally fine, obviously fine. But then it's not really governance-by-market at all, so what did we gain.

The tensions I already see: (5) vs (6), obviously, and (3) vs (8) -- market-as-advisor fine, market-as-decider not fine, even though the decider version is the entire futarchy pitch. There may be more tensions I'm not seeing.

Now the actual ask. You are a political theorist with a serious mechanism-design habit -- think someone who has read all the Hanson papers AND the critics, and who has no stake in futarchy being right. Please think carefully about this rather than pattern-matching to "prediction markets good" or "prediction markets libertarian silicon valley thing." Start with some brainstorming: lay out two or three candidate principles that could generate my verdicts (a few different ones -- maybe one about incentives to cause the outcome, one about who bears the costs of error, whatever else fits), then pick the best one and state it precisely. Tell me which of my verdicts it cannot acommodate and would force me to overturn -- I'd rather lose a verdict than keep all of them with a mushy principle. Then apply it to a case I haven't listed: a political party that binds itself in advance to nominate whichever candidate leads a designated prediction market six months before the convention. What does the principle say, and does that answer feel right? Be honest about where my gut verdicts are just vibes, and please dont flatter me.
