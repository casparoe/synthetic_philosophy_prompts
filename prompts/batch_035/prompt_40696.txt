My cousin, who took a semester of ethics in college, gave me this explanation of why I shouldn't waste my time on hedonism, and I want to know how much of it to believe:

"Aristotle spotted the fatal flaw before anyone had even formulated the theory. The things we call pleasures — the pleasure of a hot shower, of solving a proof, of getting even with someone — have nothing in common except the word itself, the way a white swan and white snow have nothing in common. So 'pleasure' is a homonym, like 'bank,' and that's the heterogeneity problem: hedonism says well-being is the balance of one stuff, pleasure over pain, which requires pleasure to be a single thing that can come in larger and smaller amounts, and Aristotle showed it isn't. Sidgwick conceded the point, and utilitarianism survived only by swapping in preference satisfaction instead. The only people who still call themselves hedonists are basically nostalgic Benthamites — Bentham being the one who thought all pleasures differ only in intensity and duration, like so many pounds of a single commodity."

I'm inclined to think hedonism is roughly right about well-being, so I'd like to know where this is unfair. Go through the summary claim by claim and tell me which parts are accurate, which are simplifications a defender would tolerate, and which are genuine misrepresentations. In particular, I want to know whether the homonym claim really does trace back to Aristotle or is a later gloss on him, whether Sidgwick's treatment of the heterogeneity of the agreeable counts as a concession, and whether the move to preference satisfaction should be described as rescuing hedonism or abandoning it. Then give me your actual verdict: does the heterogeneity problem, stated properly, refute hedonism outright, refute only crude or sensational versions of it, or leave it standing with refinements? Take a definite side rather than laying out the options for me — and say what a hedonist who agrees with you would have to concede along the way.
