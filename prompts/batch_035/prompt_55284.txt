A few of us in my reading group spent some weeks on David Lewis's Convention (the 1969 book), and afterwards two members got into it about whether the pickup-after-your-dog custom at our local dog park counts as a convention in Lewis's sense. Background on the park, since the details ended up mattering: there's a bag dispenser by the gate that the city doesn't refill -- the regulars restock it themselves -- and everyone picks up after their own dog. Nobody ever proposed any of this, it just settled in over the years. The morning regulars all know each other, and there's an unspoken thing where if you're a known scooper people will hold the gate, chat, save you a spot at the picnic table, and if you've been seen leaving piles, people get cold.

Maya's opening move: this is a textbook Lewis convention. It's a regularity in a recurring situation, people conform, expect others to conform, and conform partly because others conform; and it's arbitrary in Lewis's sense -- the group could just as well have settled on a buddy system where whoever's closest scoops regardless of whose dog it is, or on chipping in for a paid attendant, and once any of those was generally expected it would have done just as well. That arbitrariness is exactly what Lewis asks for.

Ben's counter: no, it fails at the heart of the defintion. For Lewis a convention is a coordination equilibrium -- given that the others conform, your conforming has to be your best move. But scooping is a public-good contribution: if everyone else cleans up, one more bag from me makes no visible difference to the park and costs me a gross chore, so conditional on the others conforming, my best reply is to skip it. The regularity is being held up by guilt and dirty looks, not by the payoff structure, and Lewis's account is built for situations where deviation doesn't pay, period.

Maya's reply: Ben is computing best replies with only the physical costs in the payoff column. The actual payoffs include the social response -- the park works because being seen to skip it has a price. Given that the others conform (and watch, and remember, and freeze you out of the coffee circle), conforming is the best reply after all. Lewis is quite clear that part of what a convention does is generate expectations that make deviation unattractive, so this isn't a bug in her story; it's the mechanism.

Ben's next move: fine, but then watch what happens to the analysis. If "given that others conform" is allowed to silently include "given that others will punish me," the conditional-preference clause gets satisfied by any enforced regularity whatsoever. A protection racket where shopkeepers pay up because, given that the muscle shows up weekly, paying is the best reply, would come out as a "convention" too. He thinks Maya has saved the dog park only by making the defintion vacuous, and that Lewis wanted to distinguish convention from rule-following-under-threat, which this move erases.

Maya's last word: the distinction survives, but it lives in the arbitrariness clause, not the conditional-preference clause. The racket isn't a convention because there's no alternative regularity that would have served nearly as well -- the muscle prefers this arrangement to every alternative, and the shopkeepers prefer none of them. The dog park is different: the buddy system and the attendant were genuinely availible equilibria, the group's expectations could have locked onto any of them, and nobody had a structural interest in which one won. That asymmetry, she says, is exactly what Lewis's arbitrariness condition is for.

Then give me an overall verdict on whether the dog-park custom is a Lewis convention, and tell me whether Ben is onto something real about where Lewis's framework starts to bend once norms carry sanctions.
