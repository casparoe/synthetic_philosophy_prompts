I am a retired neuroethologist — most of my career went to arthropod sensory physiology — and since retiring I have been working through the philosophy of action and the free will literature. There I met the digger wasp that gave the debate its mascot: the female Sphex drags her paralyzed cricket to the burrow mouth, leaves it, goes inside to inspect, and if the cricket is moved a few inches away while she is inside, she re-emerges, drags it back to the threshold, and inspects again — looping as many times as we care to perturb her. Dennett coined the term sphexishness for the anxiety this provokes. I want to state the position I have come to hold and have you tell me, rigorously and adversarially, what would count against it.

The position, which I call strong continuity, has three commitments. First, the wasp illustrates nothing peculiar: all adaptive behavior is counter-insensitive outside its evolved operating envelope; the wasp's envelope is merely small, and the cricket trick is just where we can see its edge. Second, human flexibility is the same phenomenon writ large — more routines, wider envelopes, and meta-routines that swap routines — so the apparent difference in kind between us and her is an artifact of scale. Third, since anything worth calling libertarian free will requires a difference in kind, there is none, and the only freedom worth wanting is the compatibilist's.

I have also tried to sharpen the thesis in a way I have not found in the literature, and I want the answer to engage it rather than fall back on the standard generalities. Define an organism's counter-sensitivity as the number of distinct perturbations to an ongoing routine it absorbs before it either exits the routine or revises it. Strong continuity predicts a smooth phylogenetic and developmental continuum on this measure, with no threshold separating the wasp from a human infant. I further suspect that two capacities the literature runs together — the ability to exit a stalled loop and the ability to respond to genuine novelty — actually dissociate across species, and I am unsure whether that dissociation, if real, helps my thesis or quietly destroys it. Please address this.

So: what would count against strong continuity? I want the defeaters ranked, each labeled as fatal, serious, or merely awkward, and each annotated with whether a committed defender of the view would concede it as evidence against them or absorb it. Consider at least: comparative-cognition and developmental findings that would reveal a discontinuity my continuum excludes; formal or computational arguments that no architecture of routine-stacking and routine-swapping can produce some capacity humans demonstrably have; the phenomenological objection from the first-person experience of breaking a routine that has stalled; and the worry that "wider envelope" lets my second commitment absorb any counterexample whatsoever, in which case I need to know whether that insulation is fatal for a thesis of this kind or an acceptable feature of a claim about differences of degree. Be candid about which parts of the position risk nothing and are, for that reason, not doing any work.

Close with an honest verdict on how exposed the position actually is. The very last line of your response must begin "Verdict:" followed by, in at most fifteen words, the single consideration you judge most dangerous to strong continuity.
