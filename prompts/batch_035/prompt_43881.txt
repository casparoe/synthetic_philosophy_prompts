I'm a policy analyst doing comparative work on how different countries regulate who gets into prized positions, and I've ended up with three schemes on my desk that everyone files under the same heading but that, looked at closely, seem to take quietly different things for granted about fairness. I can describe the schemes; what I can't do on my own is work out what each one implicitly assumes, and that's what I'd like help with. Please don't just restate the official justifications — I'm after what the design of each rule itself commits you to, whether the designers realized it or not.

The three schemes, briefly:

1. Norway requires, on pain of serious consequences, that each gender hold at least 40% of the seats on the board of every public limited company; failure to comply means the board is not validly elected, the register refuses to record it, and ultimately the courts can compulsorily liquidate the company. The rule applies symmetrically to both genders, covers only the board and not the CEO or the executive team, has no sunset clause, and was not preceded by any official finding that women had been discriminated against in board selection. (It has recently been extended to larger private companies, but that detail doesn't matter for my question.)

2. In the United States, universities were permitted, until the Supreme Court ended the practice in 2023, to consider an applicant's race as one factor among many within an individualized, holistic review of the whole file, while setting aside a fixed number of seats for a racial group was forbidden, as it had been since the late 1970s. The justification offered was the educational benefit of a diverse student body, not redress for past exclusion.

3. India reserves a fixed proportion of seats in public employment and publicly funded higher education for members of designated castes and tribes and other historically disadvantaged classes. Membership is fixed at birth and hereditary; the categories are defined by centuries-old social hierarchy; the courts have capped total reservations at roughly half of all seats; and the scheme has been running for over seventy years with no end date.

Here is what puzzles me. The Norwegian rule doesn't wait for anyone to prove that selection was biased, doesn't expire, and treats the under- and over-represented gender identically — which looks like it assumes that the distribution of powerful positions between the sexes is itself a matter of fairness, rather than a remedy for wrongdoing, though I'm not sure. The American scheme forbids exactly what the other two do — assigning seats by group — which looks like it assumes that treating a person as a member of a group is the very thing fairness prohibits, while still allowing race to be weighed. The Indian scheme assumes the categories track a real, inherited disadvantage that warrants compensation, yet its 50% cap seems to assume that most places must stay open to unrestricted competition — and I can't tell whether those two assumptions fit together or fight each other. And across all three: the American rule aims at the composition of an incoming class, the Indian rule at access to jobs and education, and the Norwegian rule only at boards, a handful of positions per company — yet each is said to serve equality.

So, please tell me, for each of the three schemes: what the design of the rule strictly presupposes about what the relevant inequality is (bias at the point of selection, inherited disadvantage, the distribution of power itself, something else), which rival views of fairness each rule quietly rules out and which it is merely compatible with, and where each scheme is internally tense or underdetermined — for instance, whether Norway's symmetry is really inconsistent with a compensatory reading, or whether the Indian cap on reservations could be defended on more than one theory. Distinguish carefully between what the scheme presupposes and what its defenders happen to say: I want to know what the rule shape itself commits you to.

Please answer with a table with the following columns: Scheme (one row each for Norway, the United States, and India); Stated rationale, in one sentence; What the design strictly presupposes; Views it quietly rules out; Views it is merely compatible with; Main internal tension, if any. After the table, add a paragraph of at most 150 words on which presuppositions, if any, all three schemes share, or a plain statement that they share none.
