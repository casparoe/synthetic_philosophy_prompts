I'm a mid-career analyst at a transport ministry, and a philosophical puzzle about our standard methods has been nagging at me for a while, so I'd like you to take on the role of a professional philosopher who also knows some welfare economics and to reason through it carefully — please brainstorm the possibilities before settling on a reconstruction rather than just naming one theory and defending it.

The practice is this. Our appraisal manual instructs us to monetize all effects of a proposed project on everyone affected — time savings, noise, emissions, accident risk — and add them up across persons into a single net figure. A rail line that gives each of a million commuters a small benefit but imposes serious noise and blight on the few hundred households beside the track is recommended whenever the sum comes out positive. There is a standard escape hatch for the awkward cases: if the gainers could in principle have compensated the losers and still come out ahead, the project counts as an improvement even though no compensation is ever actually paid — the line is built and the trackside residents simply absorb the loss. We also attach a single uniform value of a statistical life, the same number for every person, so an intersection upgrade expected to prevent a handful of deaths is credited with that number times the count, regardless of who the would-be victims are. In short, value flows between persons like liquid between containers, and the identities of the containers don't matter to the bottom line.

What view of persons does this practice presuppose? Philosophers sometimes object that utilitarianism, by summing welfare across people, fails to take seriously the separateness of persons — that one person's loss can't straightforwardly be made good by another's gain — and I can't tell whether our manual presupposes exactly that aggregative view, whether the hypothetical-compensation test smuggles in something importantly different (a fiction that everyone was somehow party to a trade that never occurred), or whether the whole thing is just an institutional convenience that several rival theories of the person could equally well live with. What convinces me there is a real implicit theory somewhere is that the same government flatly refuses aggregation in other spheres: it would never let a hospital take organs from one unwilling traveler to save five patients, however astronomical the net benefit, and it bans paying people for kidneys even where a willing market would leave both sides better off by our own accounting methods. So the state aggregates welfare freely when it plans infrastructure and refuses to aggregate it when bodies are involved, and I'd like to understand whether that boundary reflects a coherent principle about the separateness of persons or is just an unexamined historical accident. Please reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely coexists with comfortably, and tell me concretely what would have to change in the manual — distributional weighting, mandatory actual compensation, caps on how concentrated the burdens may be — if we took the separateness of persons more seriously than the manual currently does. Finally, since I'd rather not rebuild my professional worldview on a shaky reconstruction, tell me what argument or consideration would most change your verdict about what the practice presupposes, and identify the point in your reasoning where you think the reconstruction is most likely to be wrong.
