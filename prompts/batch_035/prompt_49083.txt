I run a small UX research consultancy (8 people, mostly junior analysts) and we have a recurring fight about how our insight reports get written. Nearly every finding gets phrased as a claim about what's going on inside users' heads: "users believe the checkout is secure", "participants wanted more control", "customers trust our brand because of the reviews section". A philosopher friend of mine got me reading about the position that folk psychology -- the whole belief/desire vocabulary we lean on -- might just be a bad theory about the brain that future neuroscience will do away with entirely, the way "possessed by spirits" went away as an explanation of mental illness. I don't need to be convinced of that. But it's made me notice that a lot of what my analysts write is mind-reading dressed up as research, and when a client pushes back ("how do you KNOW they believe that?"), the analysts have nothing to say beyond re-describing the behaviour again with the same mental words.

What I want is a procedure my analysts can actually run on each claim in a draft report before it goes to me for sign-off. Say 10-15 minutes per claim, no neuroscience knowledge assumed. The procedure should say: questions to ask, in order, what counts as a sufficient answer, and crucially what to DO with the claim depending on the answers -- keep it as-is, rewrite it in behavioural or dispositional terms (what they clicked, what they'd likely do next time, what made them hesitate), or dump the claim entirely because it's doing no work a video clip wouldn't do better.

A few things it has to handle, because they come up constantly:

1. Claims where the mental vocabulary is genuinely earning its keep -- e.g. a finding that's really about expectation violation or surprise -- versus claims where "believed" just means "clicked without complaining". Your procedure needs to tell them apart.
2. The claims clients LOVE, like "users find the brand trustworthy", which are unfalsifiable mush but keep everyone happy. I suspect the procedure should strike these out, and honestly part of why I'm asking is that I want cover for doing that. But be upfront if you think the procedure is being too harsh there.
3. Cases where the analyst's interpretation of a mental state was doing real interpretive work -- the analyst actually inferred something from hesitation, tone, what participants laughed at -- and flattening it to behaviour would lose it. Where does the procedure hand it back to a human because the question is judgement, not fact?

Then run the procedure on three claims from an actual recent draft: (a) "Users believe the checkout is secure because they completed the purchase without hesitation", (b) "Participants wanted more control over notification settings" (they did -- 9 of 12 said so unprompted in interviews), and (c) "Customers trust our brand" (based on... vibes, honestly, and one participant who said "yeah I guess").

Also tell me where this procedure will misfire or get gamed -- analysts will learn to write claims that survive whatever checklist you give them, so what does it distort? And say plainly what the procedure can't do: I'm aware that a 15-minute checklist won't settle whether beliefs exist. I just want to know if that matters for the practical question. I don't want citations or a reading list, just the procedure and the worked examples.

One more thing: it's got to be one page, because if it's longer nobody will use it. If you have to cut philosophy to fit, tell me what you cut and why it was safe to cut it.
