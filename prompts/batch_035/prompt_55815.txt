I've been reading a popular science book about perception, and its chapter on "top-down effects" says roughly the following:

"Perception is not a camera. The brain constantly uses what it already knows and wants to fill in and sharpen what you see, and this phenomenon is called cognitive penetration. It has been proven experimentally again and again: tired hikers literally see hills as steeper, hungry people literally see food as closer, and people holding a heavy clipboard literally see the world through a weightier lens. Since beliefs and desires literally change what things look like, perception is never neutral. Everything you see is filtered through your expectations, so two people with different beliefs literally see different worlds, and there is no such thing as an unbiased observation — which is why scientists from rival theoretical camps can examine the same data and genuinely disagree about what they saw."

I found this compelling but a bit unsettling, and when I brought it up with a friend who studies philosophy of mind, she muttered something about the book "conflating penetration with judgment effects and with attention" and dismissed the whole thing as standard pop-science overreach. She didn't have time to explain, though, and the book itself anticipates this objection and calls it nitpicking — it says that insisting on a distinction between perception proper and later judgment is just defenders of the old "passive eye" picture refusing to face the evidence.

I don't know enough to referee this. In particular, I'd like to know whether the distinction my friend gestured at is a real distinction that matters, or whether the book is right that it's a technicality. Finally, evaluate the last two sentences — the move from "what you see is filtered through your expectations" to "there is no such thing as an unbiased observation." Does that conclusion follow from the parts of the summary that survive scrutiny, or does it outrun even the most generous reading of the evidence?
