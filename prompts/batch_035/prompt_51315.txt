I'm an undergraduate taking a course in the philosophy of technology (we just finished a unit on virtual environments and simulated experience), and I also serve as the student representative on my university's XR Governance Committee. The committee oversees our campus's virtual extension, a persistent VR campus where some seminars are held and where students maintain avatars, decorate private virtual study rooms, and trade cosmetic and functional items on a marketplace run by the university. Items there are deliberately scarce: the platform caps certain objects at fixed numbers, so a "Founder's Lantern," of which only twenty exist, has become a genuine status object, and students have traded considerable time and even real money (via a token system tied to dining credits) to acquire them.

A case has come to the committee that has split us three ways, and I've been asked to circulate a written opinion before our next meeting. The facts: a student exploited a physics glitch to pass through a locked virtual door, entered another student's study room, and took her Founder's Lantern, which he then sold on the marketplace before the platform noticed. The university can of course roll back the server state, restoring the lantern and reversing the sale. The offending student argues that nothing wrong occurred at all: nothing physical was taken, the university can undo the "theft" with a keystroke, and the item is nothing more than a database entry whose scarcity is artificially engineered by the platform in the first place. The victim says the rollback doesn't undo what she experienced — a stranger entered her private space and took something she'd worked for — and that if the university shrugs at this, the whole campus's sense of ownership and privacy is hollow. One faction of the committee wants this treated as theft and home invasion analogues under the student conduct code, with real disciplinary consequences; a second faction wants it treated purely as a technical bug report (fix the door, roll back the state, done); and a third wants a middle position where violations of virtual persons and spaces count but virtual property disputes don't, on the theory that property in a world where scarcity is a deliberate design choice is too arbitrary to ground discipline.

Please draft the written opinion I'll circulate. It needs to recommend one concrete course of action for this case, and also set out a policy the committee could apply to future incidents. Beyond the practicalities, I'd like it to genuinely engage the underlying questions rather than assume them away: are virtual objects the kind of things that can be stolen, and does the fact that their scarcity is engineered by the platform undermine that? Does the possibility of a rollback erase the harm, or only the loss? And if we sanction the virtual intrusion and taking, are we committed to the further claim that everything wrong in-world is wrong in a conduct-code sense — or can we draw a principled line, and where? The opinion should also give an honest treatment of the strongest version of the offending student's position, since half the committee currently finds it persuasive, and they won't be moved by hand-waving.
