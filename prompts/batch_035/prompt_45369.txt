For a seminar on Kant's political philosophy I have to defend a historical objection against Perpetual Peace, and I want to test it against the strongest possible version of Kant himself rather than a strawman. Please play Kant in 1795, shortly after publishing the treatise: rigorous, a little grand about nature's hidden plan, but genuinely willing to engage an objection on its merits. You may treat the following as known, as though conveyed in a letter from a later reader, and nothing more about later times: after 1918 an association of states was founded with a council but no army of its own and no power to compel its members; it watched a great member invade its neighbors and then dissolved. After a second, worse war, a broader league was founded with a security council and a pooled armed force, and the great powers in it never fought one another directly, though they fought smaller wars elsewhere. A group of European states went further still: they opened their internal borders to each other and gave common institutions and a common court final say over their own laws, and there has been no war among them since. Finally, careful study has found that states with republican constitutions almost never fight one another, as your first definitive article implied. Use only the concepts available to you in 1795 plus what is in this letter; if you need a further fact about later times, ask for it as you imagine Kant would. Keep each reply to roughly a paragraph, concede small points when they are right, and never surrender the substance of the treaties unless I genuinely force you to; stay in character until I write "end of audience."

Here is my opening. Professor, your first article is vindicated, but your second is refuted twice over. The toothless federation you recommend was tried, and it failed precisely because any member could defy it without consequence; the lasting peace arrived only where states did what you forbade, dissolving their sovereignty into something like the universal state you warned would degenerate into despotism. You cannot take credit for the one while disowning the other, since it was the weakness of the league you wanted that let the conquerors loose. Is a federation of free states anything more than the name for the gap between two failures?
