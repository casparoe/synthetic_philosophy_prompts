I write a detective series — eleven novels over twenty-six years — and between the first and the latest I have rewritten everything about my detective: her backstory, her voice, her reasons for taking cases. Not a sentence survives from the first book, yet my editor, my readers and I all speak of her as the same character. My editor says she is a river, not a statue: the water passing through London is new every moment, but the Thames is still the Thames because the channel persists — what matters is the form and the continuity of flow, not the stuff. She uses this to argue that identity is conventional all the way down: the character is whatever we go on calling the same, and by extension a person is whatever their community keeps calling the same — so my nagging sense of having killed the woman in the first draft is confused, since there was never any substance there to kill. This comforts me, but I suspect the analogy is doing all the work. Please audit it: lay out the correspondence — what plays the role of the banks, the watershed, the mouth, the name on the map — then show where the mapping breaks. I can already see one place it may not stretch: the channel of a river is fixed by something outside the river, and I am not sure anything outside my character plays that role; and a river cannot be drained, plank by plank, and reassembled by a scavenger into a rival Thames, the way Hobbes's old ship can. Does the comforting conclusion about convention survive once the breaks are on the table, or was the river carrying it the whole way? Please answer in British English.
