I am grading a midterm examination for an upper-level undergraduate course in social epistemology, and I would like help assessing one student's answer. The exam question I set was the following:

"Reductionists and anti-reductionists disagree about what grounds a hearer's justification for believing what she is told. (a) State each view clearly, naming its principal historical and contemporary proponents. (b) Explain the circularity objection to global reductionism. (c) Explain how a reductionist might respond by distinguishing local from global reductionism, and assess whether that response succeeds."

The student submitted the following answer:

"Reductionism about testimony, whose classic exponent is Hume, holds that a hearer's justification for believing testimony must ultimately rest on non-testimonial grounds: testimony transmits justification earned elsewhere rather than generating new justification. Anti-reductionism, associated with Thomas Reid and defended more recently by Coady and Burge, holds instead that testimony is a basic source of justification, on a par with perception and memory: in the absence of defeaters, a hearer is entitled to take what she is told at face value. Indeed, on the anti-reductionist view testimonial justification is so basic that it cannot be defeated even by evidence that the speaker is unreliable, since a basic source of justification is by definition one that generates justification come what may.

The central objection to reductionism is circularity. To justify trust in testimony, the reductionist must gather evidence of its reliability, but nearly all such evidence — historical records, the reports of other observers, statistical surveys — is itself testimonial. The reductionist therefore presupposes the very thing she set out to establish. Hume himself pressed this point in his discussion of miracles, arguing that the reductionist's evidential standards cannot be met without appealing to the testimony whose credentials are in question.

In response, the reductionist can distinguish global from local reductionism. Local reductionism holds that the general reliability of testimony must be established through observation; global reductionism holds only that a particular testimonial report needs checking when there is some antecedent reason to doubt it. Since the local reductionist leaves everyday, unproblematic testimony untouched, and the circularity objection threatens only the global version, the objection can be sidestepped."
