My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether rational caution in the face of risk is ever anything more than diminishing marginal utility of money. Her view is that every apparently risk-averse choice can be absorbed by a suitably concave utility function over wealth, and that a theory which locates caution elsewhere, in the way probabilities are weighted, as risk-weighted expected utility theory does in the tradition of Lara Buchak, is at best a redescription. My view is that the two explanations come apart, and that there must be observable patterns of choice that one can accommodate and the other cannot, with any parameter settings. We failed to construct such a pattern on the spot, so I would like a thought experiment built to do it properly.

Construct the case to the following specifications.

Setting: one person, ordinary domestic circumstances, and stakes of the kind a household actually faces — insurance deductibles, repair plans, deposits, refunds. No casinos, no lives, no exotic devices, and nothing that asks the reader to grant an empirical premise beyond the story.

Act one must end in a genuine tie. The character makes one or more cautious choices, and both candidate explanations must be able to rationalize them: expected utility theory with some concave utility function over money, and risk-weighted expected utility theory with utility linear in money and all of the caution located in the weighting of probabilities. The tie should not be stipulated; each theory should have a natural parameter setting that fits what the character does.

Act two must contain the wedge. It must introduce something that separates the two explanations by appeal to their own structural commitments rather than by decree. The shape I have in mind is a pair of choices, each individually mundane and unremarkable in isolation, that together form a pattern expected utility theory cannot permit with any utility function at all, while risk-weighted expected utility theory can permit it with one fixed, sensible weighting function. I leave the details to you.

The stakes should stay small relative to the character's wealth, so that the concave-utility reading of the caution in act one is already under some strain — the familiar calibration point that the curvature needed to explain refusing modest favorable gambles would entail outlandish caution at large stakes. Please note explicitly whether your case leans on this or stays neutral about it; my friend and I could not agree on whether it should matter.

After the scenario, show your work. Walk through each act, stating the verdict each theory gives and the parameter settings that produce it; identify which feature of the case is doing the work; tell me what happens if that feature is deleted; and give the strongest available reply on behalf of the expected utility theorist. I anticipate at least two escapes open to her — for instance, positing a direct utility for certainty or peace of mind, or relocating utility from money to something broader — but address whichever one is strongest, not all of them.

A word about what the case is for, since that fixes what counts as cheating: it exists to adjudicate a theoretical dispute, so it must generate its verdicts from the theories rather than build them in. If the character's behavior in act two could only be rationalized because you stipulated an inconsistency into her, the case fails, and I would rather you say so yourself than paper over it.

Please structure your response as follows, in this order: (1) The case — both acts together, no more than 350 words; (2) The tie in act one — about 150 words; (3) The wedge in act two — about 200 words; (4) Which feature does the work — about 150 words; (5) The expected utility theorist's strongest reply — about 250 words; (6) Where this leaves my argument with my friend — about 100 words.
