I'm designing the opening unit of a graduate seminar on the naturalization of intentionality, and I want to begin before we read any proponents: the students should first have to confront the adequacy conditions themselves. Write the desiderata for a theory of mental content — what any account of how a physical state acquires content must deliver, with the motivating case or consideration behind each requirement.

The purpose to fix in advance: the theory should be good enough to underwrite the distinction between a system that misrepresents its environment and a system that merely malfunctions — that distinction is what my seminar will keep returning to. So the desiderata should be requirements for a theory that can do that work, not merely for one that is vaguely naturalistic.

Among the cases the list should confront, I want at least these, though you should add others you think belong: a state whose actual tokening causes are broader than its content (so that the theory must explain how error is possible rather than letting content collapse into whatever triggers the state); the swamp-creature duplicate with no evolutionary history, whose states seem contentful though the theory's history has nothing to say about it; novel or uninstantiated contents; the choice between distal and proximal causes as the bearers of content; states selected for avoidance rather than detection, where the state's function and what it seems to be about come apart; and the worry that teleology buys normativity too cheaply, by quietly trading on selected-effect functions that are themselves contested.

Then address three things I most want to see. First, mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are prejudices of particular camps — for instance, whether a demand for atomistic content or for content-bearing subpersonal states is a genuine adequacy condition or just loyalty to one research program. Second, identify any pairs of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — say, determinacy of content together with a strictly historical selection-based grounding — and say what gives if they conflict. Third, name the single requirement that does the most exclusion work among existing approaches, and explain why it, rather than the naturalism requirement, is where theories actually die.

Skip the scorecard against specific proponents; the students will construct that themselves once we read them. The deliverable is the specification.
