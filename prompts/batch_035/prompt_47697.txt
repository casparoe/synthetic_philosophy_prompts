I am a retired seismologist who spent most of my career on probabilistic hazard assessment, and I have lately been reading decision theory as a retirement hobby. Something keeps nagging at me, and I would like to know whether philosophers have a settled concept for it.

In my working life we routinely attached numbers to very rare events — annual probabilities of large earthquakes on the order of one in ten thousand — and everyone understood these were model-based extrapolations with no direct empirical check, since the record is too short. Recently I have been reading arguments, in philosophy and in policy discussions about catastrophic and future risks, that follow a different pattern. A scenario is proposed with an astronomically small probability and an astronomically large harm or benefit, and the product of the two is claimed to dominate every other consideration. The probability is not small the way a rare earthquake is small; no amount of data could confirm or disconfirm an estimate like one in ten to the fortieth, and reasonable people assign estimates differing by many orders of magnitude. Meanwhile the stakes are scaled up until the product matters, so the conclusion is largely invariant to the estimate itself.

The configuration I want a name for is this: a decision rule that is perfectly sensible when probabilities are data-informable becomes hostage to scenarios whose probabilities are uninformable in principle, because the stakes can always be scaled to compensate. I notice people respond in two ways that both look to me like refusals to do the arithmetic: either they discount the tiny probability to something effectively negligible, without saying at what threshold and why, or they cap the stakes, without saying where and why. I have also seen the observation made — I believe in connection with a Petersburg-type lottery — that someone who maximizes expected value over unbounded payoffs can be led through a sequence of pairwise choices, each locally optimal, that leaves them worse off with certainty. That resembles an ordinary money pump, but the usual remedies for money pumps, such as enforcing a transitivity requirement, do not obviously touch it, since the trouble seems to come from the unbounded payoff rather than from inconsistent preferences.

So, my questions. Does this general configuration — the sensitivity of expected-value reasoning to uninformable probabilities, together with the two escape routes of probability discounting and utility capping — have an established name or names? How exactly does it relate to the St. Petersburg paradox, the nearest thing I know: is it the same phenomenon or a different one, and if different, what distinguishes them? Is the distinction I am trying to draw between data-informable and uninformable probabilities the same as the risk-versus-uncertainty distinction I have seen attributed to Knight, or does his line fall somewhere else? And where does the genuine philosophical disagreement sit once the formal facts are granted? I take it there are theorems to the effect that expected-value maximization over unbounded payoffs entails the vulnerability I am describing, so I would ask you to keep clearly separate what is settled mathematics from what is actually contested among philosophers, and not to present any one of the escape routes as though it were the consensus position.
