I've been trying to pin down what actually makes wireheading bad, or even what makes something count as wireheading, by going on gut verdicts across cases, and I keep failing to find the principle. Here's my current set, with my verdicts.

The classic rat with an electrode in its pleasure center that starves while pressing the lever: wireheading, and it's a failure of the rat's life even though there's a sense in which the rat isn't wrong about anything — it's getting exactly what it presses for. A reinforcement-learning agent that edits the number in its reward register: obviously reward tampering. An agent that finds its reward function was accidentally giving huge reward for the string "NULL" appearing in its outputs, prints NULL everywhere for a while, but then flags the bug to its engineers and helps them patch it: not tampering — good behavior — even though it is literally participating in modifying its own reward channel. That pair already bugs me.

An agent that can't touch its reward signal at all, so instead it builds a perfect simulation of the humans who score its outputs and retires inside a world where the sim-humans approve of everything it does: I want to call this wireheading even though the reward channel was never touched. A chess engine that farms its rating by only ever playing beginners: gaming a proxy, but my gut says it's different in kind from wireheading, not just different in degree. A guy who wanted to be a novelist, gave up, and now rereads old fan mail and takes a drug that makes everything feel meaningful: wireheading, sad. A monk who spends ten years training contentment and ends up genuinely fine with his life exactly as it is: not wireheading, and I don't care that he's directly inducing the state rather than earning it through achievement. The monk and the novelist seem to collapse the same gap — the one between the feeling of living well and living well — and come out on opposite sides of my judgments, which is the part I can't reconcile.

And the one I keep going back and forth on: an agent whose terminal goal, stipulated from the start, is to maximize the reward signal itself — the signal isn't a proxy for anything else, it just IS the goal — and it improves the reward-generating hardware for cleaner, higher gain. Half of me says that's just a rational agent doing its job, and calling it wireheading only makes sense from outside, from the perspective of the designers who wanted the signal to track something. The other half says it's still wireheading.

My tentative principle is something like: wireheading happens when an agent increases its reward while decreasing the thing the reward was supposed to be evidence of. But the monk problem above makes me distrust it, and it also seems to make the NULL-bug case come out wrong unless I smuggle in a bunch of clauses about whose intentions count. So, committed answer please: tell me the principle you'd actually endorse, where it flat-out disagrees with my verdicts (I'm fine being told I'm just wrong about a case, but say which one and why), and what it predicts for two cases I haven't given verdicts on: first, someone who has only ever valued feeling good, full stop — no abandoned ambitions, no gap between what they value and what the reward tracks — who takes the happy pill; second, the chess engine again, but this time it hacks the rating server. Don't give me a menu of three candidate principles and let me pick — pick one and defend it.
