I am a retired physicist who now teaches an evening course on the philosophy of science, and I should be grateful for help with grading. I set the essay question below, and a student has submitted the answer that follows. Please give a mark out of 25 and the specific comments that justify it, in point-by-point form, so that nothing of substance in the answer goes unmarked.

The question was: "A climate modeller complains: 'My ensemble model issues probabilistic forecasts — it gives a 95% band for warming by 2050 — and no single observation could conflict with a probability distribution. Popper's criterion says a theory is scientific only if it forbids something observable, so probabilistic forecasting is not science; and if the forecast fails, we simply re-tune the parameters and issue a new one, which Popper would call an ad hoc rescue.' Set out Popper's criterion of falsifiability, explain how Popper himself proposed to handle the status of probabilistic statements, and assess whether the modeller's conclusion follows. Distinguish Popper's own position from later developments in falsificationism where relevant."

The student's answer, in full:

"Popper's criterion of falsifiability was a criterion of meaning: a statement is scientific only if it is empirically meaningful, and it is empirically meaningful only if some possible observation would contradict it. Because universal laws ('all swans are white') forbid their counter-instances, they pass the criterion; a single black swan refutes the law. Since no observation can conflict with a probability ascription, the modeller is right that, on a strict reading, his forecast is unfalsifiable. But Popper saw this problem and answered it with a methodological rule: an event to which the hypothesis assigns sufficiently small probability is to be treated as ruled out, so that its occurrence falsifies the distribution. Hence probabilistic theories are falsifiable in exactly the same strict sense as deterministic ones, and the modeller's model is unproblematically scientific. As for re-tuning, Popper's Duhem point is that a test never confronts a hypothesis alone, so blame must be assigned somewhere; his student Lakatos developed this into the distinction between progressive and degenerating research programmes. On the parameter question, Popper held that a theory is refuted only by a better rival theory, so re-tuning that anticipates new phenomena is legitimate science, and the charge of ad hocness fails. In sum, the modeller is wrong on both counts: the criterion does not exclude probabilistic forecasting, and parameter adjustment is not automatically ad hoc."
