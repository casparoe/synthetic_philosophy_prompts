I'm a third-year undergraduate currently taking a seminar on the philosophy of well-being, where we've spent the last few weeks on hedonist theories. In the final session, our instructor sketched a view she called tranquilism: the only thing that has final prudential value is the absence of craving and suffering; positively toned mental states have no value of their own beyond being the felt absence of a lack. On this view, satisfaction isn't a good thing that happens to you when a desire is met — it's merely the ending of a bad thing, the extinguishing of a want. Rather than argue about the view in the abstract, I've been keeping a list of my own gut verdicts on cases, and I'd like help figuring out what principle, if any, they add up to — and in particular whether any recognizable version of tranquilism can capture them.

Here are my verdicts:

1. If I could either end a migraine I've had all day or receive an equally intense and equally long stretch of pleasure from listening to a favorite album, but not both, I'd end the migraine, and I don't feel remotely torn about it. The relief seems more urgent than the pleasure.
2. The same priority seems to hold at low intensities and for strangers: preventing a stranger's mild headache always beats providing a stranger a pleasure of equal intensity and duration. But if everyone is already free of suffering, giving someone a pleasant evening seems worth doing — a real but small contribution, not merely neutral.
3. I think the engaged life of a mountaineer or a researcher — full of frustration, anxiety, and obstacles — goes better for the person living it than a life of chemically induced contented quiescence with the same or even less suffering in it. The projects seem to add value that you can't reduce to the balance of mental states.
4. Looking forward to a vacation seems to me to add to how well my life is going during the weeks of anticipation, even though anticipation is literally a form of craving — I'm restless until the trip arrives. Tranquilism seems committed to counting that restlessness as bad, or at best as neutral, and I can't make myself feel that.
5. Verdict 1 and verdict 3 pull in opposite directions and I can see it: in the migraine case I'd never accept suffering in exchange for pleasure, but in the mountaineer case I'd accept frustration and anxiety as the price of projects I care about, and I don't think that's simple innumeracy on my part.

The case I keep going back and forth on is a population version of the question: creating a person guaranteed to be permanently tranquil but blank — no suffering, no craving, no projects, no pleasures — versus not creating anyone. Some days I think that's exactly as good as creating no one, which feels tranquilist; other days it feels worse than creating no one, which feels like something stronger than tranquilism; and occasionally I suspect it's slightly better, which contradicts my verdict 3.

What I'd like: state the principle or principles, whether tranquilist, asymmetric-hedonist, or something else entirely, that best captures verdicts 1 through 4, clause by clause, with each clause tied to the verdict that motivates it. Then tell me directly whether verdict 3 (or 4) has to go, or whether verdict 1 has to be weakened from "always" to something like a defeasible presumption, and what it costs to go either way. Then tell me what the resulting principle predicts for cases I haven't listed, including my population case, and at least one genuinely novel case of your own construction — something like a blissful gourmand whose bliss depends on constant, escalating craving, or a craving-free person who insists her life is going wonderfully while her loved ones disagree.
