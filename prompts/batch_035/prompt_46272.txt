Next month I'm giving a talk in an interdisciplinary cognitive science program defending a semi-serious version of the Aunt Hillary stuff from the "Prelude... Ant Fugue" dialogue in Gödel, Escher, Bach -- the idea that a mind could be realized at the level of an ant colony, with individual ants playing roughly the role neurons play in us. I want to be careful about what I'm claiming: not that any actual ant colony is minded, but that there's no principled cutoff between the kind of distributed organization that makes minds and the kind real colonies have, and that Hofstadter's thought experiment is more than a cute analogy. My own twist, which I suspect is where I'll get in trouble: I argue that if such a mind existed, its thoughts would be spread out over days and weeks rather than milliseconds -- a colony-level "moment" of experience would take so long that the colony's mentality would be invisible to us for roughly the reason our neurons can't see OUR minds -- and that our intuitions about what counts as one cognitive system are basically calibrated to human timescales, which begs the question.

The room is a mix, and they'll come at me from different angles. Most of the faculty are embodied/enactivist types who think cognition is grounded in a single organism's sensorimotor loop with its environment. There are two old-school functionalists who are officially sympathetic to multiple realizability but love pressing on implementation details. And there's a behavioral ecologist who studies actual ant colonies and visibly bristles at superorganism talk -- she thinks colony-level descriptions are just convenient statistics for us observers, not real causal levels.

Things I expect to come up and want covered: whether the colony has anything like integrated, persistent colony-level states or just overlapping local interactions; whether there's a unified sensorimotor loop at the colony level or only lots of ant-level ones; whether anything at the colony level does causal work that isn't just the ants doing their thing (the "levels of description vs levels of nature" problem); and whether my timescale argument is doing any real work or is just a way of making the claim unfalsifiable. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks -- I suspect it's the one about whether my claim, drained of human timescale assumptions, has any content left at all.

E.g. what's empirically known about colony-level integration in real ants is an empirical matter and I don't want to overstate it, the claim that cognition requires embodiment is a live philosophical dispute not a fact, and the functionalist multiple realizability story that my whole talk leans on has serious critics who know the literature. Flag for each objection whether my best reply is resting on a consensus position or on my own contested move, so I don't accidentally present a contested view as settled in front of people who will notice.
