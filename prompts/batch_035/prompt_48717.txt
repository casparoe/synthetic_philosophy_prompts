Next month I present to our economics-and-philosophy workshop a paper defending the following thesis: the classical backward-induction argument in games like Rosenthal's centipede is not merely unrealistic as a description of human players, but defective as a piece of reasoning, because it instructs a player who reaches an allegedly unreachable node to infer that her opponent must be irrational — an inference whose premises the situation it is triggered by undermines. My positive proposal is that backward induction should be reconceived as defeasible: at the first node its conclusion is a default, but each observed pass by the opponent is evidence that progressively weakens it, so a rational player in a long centipede should continue for a number of nodes fixed by her prior over the opponent's type, and only then take. On this account the experimental finding — subjects pass for a while and then take, with the take-point drifting later as stakes rise — is predicted rather than explained away as noise.

The audience is about fifteen people. Four or five mathematical economists who take Aumann's 1995 result, that common knowledge of rationality entails the backward-induction outcome, as having settled the matter, and who will want to know whether I claim the theorem is false, misinterpreted, or simply beside the point. A decision theorist working on epistemic game theory who will want to know where my type space and the prior over opponent types come from, and will likely press on whether my defeasible reasoning is just forward induction in disguise — and if so, why I present it as a repair of backward induction. Two philosophers of economics who are already sympathetic to the self-undermining complaint and will push me on whether anything survives: if the inference only works as a defeasible default, in what sense is it still induction from the last node backward? A couple of experimentalists who will ask whether my account predicts the observed take-points with fixed priors or only accommodates whatever data arrives. And one visitor known for complaining that these debates run together three notions — rationality as payoff maximization, rationality as consistency of beliefs, and rationality of the iterated beliefs themselves — and who will ask which of the three my defeasibility claim attaches to.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased the way that specific person would phrase it, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft and how much I should concede up front. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks — I suspect it is the one about whether my account, once the prior over types is filled in, is just Bayesian updating on an enriched game and so leaves the classical equilibrium analysis untouched for players with degenerate priors — but give me your own judgment about whether that or the interaction with Aumann's theorem is the more dangerous one.
