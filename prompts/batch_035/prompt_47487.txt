As a journalist covering science and ideas, I've spent the last few years drifting into a position I now defend in print more often than I probably should: that rational disagreement between well-informed people is always explicable, and ultimately removable. My touchstone is Aumann's agreement theorem — the 1976 result that two agents with common priors whose posteriors for some event are common knowledge cannot have different posteriors at all. From there I've generalized enthusiastically: I've written columns claiming that when two smart, honest, well-informed people disagree, the disagreement must trace to private information, a hidden difference in priors, or a lapse of rationality somewhere — never to a stable, irresolvable split in judgment between equally good reasoners. I even teased a piece diagnosing couples who argue about household finances on identical evidence as suffering from a failure to make their opinions common knowledge, in the spirit of the results showing that agents who simply announce their current posteriors back and forth must eventually converge. My editor liked it. I'm less sure I should.

What I'd like from you is a map of this position's exposure. Please separate the narrow formal theorem from my broader journalistic extrapolation, since I suspect they have very different resilience: the theorem follows from the common-prior assumption and the common-knowledge setup, so I want to know which observations or arguments would genuinely count against each layer, and which escape routes — dropping common priors, weakening common knowledge to something less ideal, treating announced opinions as cheap talk — are live rather than merely logical possibilities. Then rank the defeaters for the extrapolated view from fatal through serious to merely awkward: what would a committed defender concede as counting against it, and what would they wave away? For instance, should persistent, refined disagreement among genuine epistemic peers who have exchanged their full reasoning worry me, or does the view have a principled account of it?

Finally, an honest verdict on the structure of the view: it looks worryingly easy to insulate, since any observed disagreement can be absorbed by positing hidden information, divergent priors, or human noise, and I can always retreat to saying the theorem only ever concerned idealized agents. Is that retreat legitimate — the theorem being a mathematical fact that no observation could touch — or have I built something unfalsifiable by qualification, and if so, is that a problem for a view of this kind, or just the ordinary situation of a formal result being over-applied to messy human believers? I'd particularly like to know what the view predicts that its rivals don't, because if it predicts nothing testable about actual disagreement, I may owe my readers a correction.
