I write about science and ideas for a mid-sized paper, and my editor just greenlit a column on why people care so much about originals vs forgeries. The problem is I've been keeping notes on my own gut reactions for a while and they don't really line up, and I'd rather not publish 900 words of vibes. So here are my verdicts, and I want to know if there's a principle underneath them or whether I have to give some up.

Standing in front of a genuine Vermeer, I feel something I know I wouldn't feel in front of an honestly labeled, visually identical replica in the next room. The replica leaves me cold and I think that's the correct reaction, not a prejudice. But I happily read novels in translation and paperback — mechanically copied, mediated by a translator — and never feel like I'm missing the real thing, and I can love a cover song as much as, even more than, the original recording. That seems like it should be the same issue but doesn't feel like it.

Then there's the museum case: if the Rijksmuseum announced tomorrow that its beloved Rembrandt was mostly by a student, I'd want the label changed but I wouldnt want it taken down, and I suspect most visitors' experience would barely change even after the news. Yet with the van Meegeren fakes — once hailed as lost masterpieces, now nobody crosses the street for them — I think the collapse in their value is correct, and I can't explain why, since the canvases themselves didn't change, only what we believe about them.

Two more that I suspect are doing damage. On wine fraud: collectors drank fake old vintages at tastings and raved about them, and when the fraud came out they said the knowledge retroactively ruined it. Part of me thinks that's silly — the pleasure was real when it happened — but I also know I'd be mortified serving a fake to guests, even a delicious one. And on restoration: a painting that's seventy percent repainted by a conservator after damage still counts to me as the original, while a painting that's seventy percent by the master's own workshop doesn't, and I can't see why the conservator's hand is more forgivable than the apprentice's.

The case I keep going back and forth on: my late father's watch, which I wear, versus an identical model that turns up new-old-stock in a dealer's drawer. Same object from the same factory, but the new one means nothing to me. That one feels obviously right and also seems to have nothing to do with art, which makes me think whatever principle handles the museum cases should handle this too, or admit it can't.

So: is there one principle that gets all of these, or close to it? What does it say about the watch, and about the wine — does the retrospective-ruining feeling have to go? If some verdicts have to be dropped, tell me which and why. Please write it as continuous prose, no headings or bullet points or bold — like a long email from a friend who happens to know this stuff. Don't hold back on making me give something up, since the column is better if its honest.
