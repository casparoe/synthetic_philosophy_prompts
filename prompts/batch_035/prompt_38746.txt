In my graduate seminar on the export of logical results into philosophy of mind and philosophy of science, I want to examine two extra-mathematical deployments of Gödel's incompleteness theorems that run in opposite directions, and I would like a careful comparative analysis of them rather than the standard survey of abuses.

The first deployment is the one descending from J. R. Lucas's 1959 paper "Minds, Machines and Gödel" and developed in Roger Penrose's The Emperor's New Mind and Shadows of the Mind. Its direction is inflationary: since any consistent, effectively axiomatized formal system containing enough arithmetic has a true sentence it cannot prove, whereas the human mathematician can (supposedly) see the truth of that very sentence, our mathematical understanding outruns any algorithm, and the mind is not a machine. The second deployment is Stephen Hawking's suggestion, in his Dirac Centennial lecture titled "Gödel and the End of Physics," that the incompleteness theorems cast doubt on the project of a complete theory of physics: if the ultimate physical theory is a finite formal system, it cannot, by its own resources, establish its own consistency, and so the dream of a final, self-certifying theory of everything is in trouble. Its direction is deflationary: the formal system falls short of its own ambitions rather than the mind transcending the formal system.

Please compare the two transfers along the following axes, and be evenhanded rather than treating either party as a naive tourist.

First, precondition mapping. The theorems carry strict conditions — effective axiomatizability, enough arithmetic to encode syntax, and consistency (or the weaker conditions needed for the second theorem in particular). For each deployment, identify what in the target domain is supposed to play the role of each precondition. In particular: what exactly is the "formal system" for Hawking — is a physical theory, understood as a set of equations plus interpretive apparatus, the kind of thing that is effectively axiomatized in the relevant sense? And what is it for Lucas and Penrose — is it the hypothesized Turing machine that simulates the idealized mathematician, and does that hypothesis come with a determinate axiom set whose consistency we could even ask about?

Second, entitlement to the consistency premise. Both arguments have to get a consistency assumption into place before the theorem bites: Lucas and Penrose need to know that the machine simulating the mathematician is consistent, and Hawking needs to presuppose that physics is. Assess who is in better standing here. Is the assumption that the physical world is consistent more or less defensible than the assumption that a given machine-model of an idealized mathematician is? Note the asymmetry that the Lucas–Penrose argument needs not merely the truth of the consistency claim but our knowing it, while Hawking needs only its truth — and say whether that asymmetry tracks anything deep or is an artifact of the two conclusions being of different logical types.

Third, conclusion strength. Suppose, for the sake of argument, that each transfer went through cleanly. What exactly would each have established? For Hawking, it is worth asking whether incompleteness really tells against a theory of everything in his own stated sense — a set of rules that enables prediction to arbitrary accuracy — since such a theory need not be complete with respect to arithmetic, nor prove its own consistency, in order to do its predictive job; the gap between "no system certifies itself" and "no complete theory exists" must be examined honestly. For Penrose, the corresponding question is whether the theorem yields the modest conclusion that we are not any particular machine, or the strong conclusion that we are not any machine at all, and what additional premises do the work in widening the scope.

Fourth, direction. One transfer uses the theorem to inflate the powers of a knower beyond any system; the other uses it to deflate the ambitions of a system below its own hopes. Is one of these directions structurally better supported than the other, or is the direction irrelevant compared with whether the structural preconditions actually hold in the target domain?

Finally, situate both against Gödel's own cautious disjunctive view — that his results show either that the human mind surpasses all machines or that there exist mathematical questions undecidable by any human means, with no commitment as to which disjunct holds. Argue for a verdict: which of the two deployments is the more defensible use of incompleteness outside mathematics, which single feature of the theorems is most responsible for the weaker argument's failure (for instance, the slide between truth and provability, or the assumption that the target domain wears its axioms on its sleeve), and what a defender of the losing side would most plausibly say in reply.

Aim for rigor in stating the theorems' conditions, and do not treat either party as a caricature; the interesting question is not who invoked Gödel illegitimately, but precisely which joint of the analogy gives way, and whether it is the same joint in both cases.
