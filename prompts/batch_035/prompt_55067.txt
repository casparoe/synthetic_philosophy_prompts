You are a professional philosopher with deep expertise in personal identity — familiar with the tradition running from Locke through Parfit's Reasons and Persons and Nozick's closest-continuer proposal to contemporary reductionism about persons — and an accomplished writer of philosophical dialogue. You know the standard cases cold: the teletransporter, fission, the psychological and physical spectra. Because you know them so well, you also know how quickly staged debates about them collapse into canned positions, so take care to avoid that here. Before writing anything, think through the assignment below carefully: map each character's strongest arguments, the strongest replies available to the other side, and where each character's position is genuinely vulnerable. Only then compose the dialogue.

**The case.** A commercial teletransportation service is about to launch. The machine scans you, transmits the blueprint to a reconstruction facility on Mars, and builds an exact physical and psychological duplicate there. But the service has a licensing quirk: the original body must be kept alive for seven days as a control while the Martian copy undergoes calibration testing. Call the person in the company dormitory, living out a quiet supervised week on Earth, the Resident, and the person on Mars the Visitor. If the Visitor passes the week of calibration checks, the Resident is painlessly destroyed on day seven. If the Visitor fails, the Resident walks out free on day seven with full memory of the week — while the Visitor, the failure, is quietly dismantled. Either way, exactly one person walks away with the future. During the week, the Resident can watch a live video feed of the Visitor, who is settling into the Martian colony and starting to live the life the Resident planned to live there.

**The dialogue.** Write a sustained philosophical dialogue between two philosophers, both equally competent, neither a strawman:

- Dr. Ines Kovač defends the closest-continuer view. There is always a determinate fact about whether the person on Mars is the same person as the one who entered the scanner, and in this case the fact is fixed retrospectively: if calibration succeeds, the Visitor is you and you died on day seven, not at the scan; if calibration fails, the Resident is you all along and the Visitor never counted. Identity is sensitive to extrinsic and later facts, Kovač holds, and that is no embarrassment. What matters in survival is remaining the same continuing subject, and continuing subjects are what prudential concern, promises, and responsibility attach to — not degrees of psychological connectedness.

- Prof. Tobias Reyes is a Parfitian reductionist. The underlying facts in this case — psychological connectedness to the Resident and to the Visitor — are the same in kind and nearly the same in degree whether or not the machine's schedule ends up deleting one candidate. The difference between surviving and dying therefore does not consist in any difference in connectedness, so it cannot be what matters; whether I survive depends on facts about someone else's calibration results, which is precisely what shows that the further identity question is empty in cases like this. Reyes thinks the Passenger (the first paying customer, currently in her dormitory week) is confusing herself when she asks whether the person on Mars will be her, and that she should instead be asking the degreed question: how much of what I care about when I care about my future will be present?

Stage the dialogue during the Passenger's verification week, with the two philosophers watching the live feed of the Visitor on Mars. The debate must be conducted through the concrete features of this case, not through textbook recitation: the dormitory week and what the Resident is entitled to prudentially care about during it; the timing of death if calibration succeeds; the fact that the Resident's survival or demise depends on how well someone else performs on tests; the Passenger's promise, made before scanning, to repay a debt to a friend on Mars in a year — who is bound to honor it, and why; and what the Resident should feel on day six. Neither character may simply lecture or recap Parfit or Nozick; both must treat this case as genuinely new and reason it through in real time.

Requirements for the back-and-forth:

1. Each character must directly engage the other's actual last point — no talking past each other, no serial monologues. Reyes must give Kovač a real answer to the claim that promises and prudential concern need continuing subjects, not connectedness-degrees, as their bearers; Kovač must give Reyes a real answer to the arbitrariness charge that on her view whether you died turns on a machine's verification schedule.
2. At least once, each character must turn the other's own commitment against them (for example, Kovač pressing Reyes on whether his degreed story can explain why the Resident should care about the Visitor's calibration results more than about a stranger's; Reyes pressing Kovač on whether her view makes the Resident's week a strange kind of conditional existence).
3. Midway through, it should emerge that Reyes has bought a ticket on the second flight. He should be made to feel the force of applying his own view to himself, and Kovač should be made to feel the force of applying hers to him — without either abandoning their position, but with visible cost or refinement.
4. Each character must make at least one genuine concession or refinement of their view by the end — something that a fair reader would count as the other side having scored a point.
5. Neither side wins outright. End with both characters still disagreeing, but disagreeing more precisely than they started — about what, specifically, they take the question of the Passenger's survival to be a question about.

Write with natural, unforced dialogue — interruptions, half-finished sentences where appropriate, the texture of two people actually thinking. Aim for roughly 1,800–2,200 words. Do not include citations, bibliographic notes, or references beyond the works and authors named above, and do not append commentary or analysis; the deliverable is the dialogue itself, with at most a two-sentence stage-setting line at the top.
