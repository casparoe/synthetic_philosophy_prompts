A friend of mine who took a course on postwar German philosophy tried to summarize Karl Jaspers's *The Question of German Guilt* (1946) for me, and I suspect she got some of it wrong, but I can't tell where. Here is roughly what she said:

Jaspers divided Germany's guilt into four kinds. Criminal guilt covers the crimes themselves, and since those crimes were committed by the German state, every German citizen shares in this criminal guilt simply by belonging to the state — that is what collective guilt means, and Jaspers accepted it. Moral guilt, by contrast, is the kind that can be tried at a court like Nuremberg, so it applies to the actual perpetrators and only to them. Metaphysical guilt is just moral guilt under a religious name — guilt before God for what one's conscience failed to prevent. So in the end Jaspers endorses the Allies' doctrine: every German is guilty of the crimes, and the nation should be treated accordingly.

Something about this seems off — I've heard the book described as refusing both wholesale condemnation and wholesale exoneration, which doesn't fit her version. Please go through the summary piece by piece: what would a careful reader of Jaspers accept, where are the misrepresentations, and does her conclusion that Jaspers endorses national guilt as she understands it follow from anything he actually claims? I'd especially like to know whether the four types of guilt do the work of showing that "the nation is guilty" can be true in one sense and false in another.
