In my Comparative Government elective we have been debating immigration and duties to compatriots, and one classmate keeps leaning on the same comparison: the nation is a family scaled up. We speak of the "motherland," of "brothers and sisters" in the national community, of ties of "blood" to ancestors, and since families rightly favor their own — you feed your children before strangers — it seems natural and legitimate for citizens to give priority to compatriots over foreigners, and to treat the national bond as deep, inherited, and basically unchosen. Our teacher complicated this by describing the view of some historians, like Eric Hobsbawm and Benedict Anderson, that most nations are surprisingly recent constructions: many traditions we treat as immemorial (particular ceremonies, dress, flag rituals) were deliberately manufactured within the last two or three centuries, and national solidarity depends on people imagining kinship with millions they will never meet. My classmate was unimpressed; he replied that families are also partly constructed — think of adoption or blended families — and still generate real special obligations, so the analogy survives the historians.

I would like a proper audit of the family analogy rather than the trading of intuitions we get in class. Please lay out what maps onto what: what plays the role of the parents, the genes, the shared home, the childhood, and the fact that you cannot choose your family? Then show where the mapping breaks — for instance, whether national membership is really unchosen given naturalization, emigration, and internal dissent, and whether obligations among millions of strangers could plausibly work like obligations among people who raised you and whom you see daily. I also want to know which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion he is using the analogy to support (special priority for compatriots) versus which ones are interesting but irrelevant to it, and whether that conclusion survives once the relevant breaks are on the table. Finally, evaluate his rebuttal that families are constructed too, and tell me whether some alternative comparison — my teacher floated something like a club, or a community of people who happen to read the same newspaper — would carry the same conclusion or quietly change what we owe to fellow members.
