I'm a backend engineer at a mid-size company (no philosophy background at all, so go easy on jargon or define it) and something about how we handle privacy has been bugging me for months. In practice we treat a dataset as "privacy-safe" the moment direct identifiers get stripped -- names, emails, account IDs -- even though the behavioral data we keep (purchase history, device model, coarse location, timestamps) could very plausibly be linked back to specific people if someone put in the work. Yet the same team, same people, will turn around and say browser fingerprintting is unacceptable because users don't expect to be tracked that way, and that our server logs are totally fine to keep forever because "nobody reads those." It feels wierd that all three attitudes coexist. I was ranting about this to a friend who did a philosophy degree and she just said "so what do you all think privacy IS?" and honestly I couldn't answer.

That's basically my question for you. What does this bundle of practices actually presuppose about what privacy is? Is it privacy as anonymity (once we can't tell who you are, nothing is violated), privacy as control or consent (fine as long as the user clicked agree at some point), something about reasonable expectations, or what? Do the three practices presuppose consistent views or are they just incompatible with each other? Please reconstruct the implicit theory (or theories), and be clear about which parts of our practice strictly require a given view versus which parts could be licensed by several different views -- I want to know where the practice is genuinely committed and where it's just sloppy.

And then the part I actually care about: what would concretely have to change in our engineering -- logging defaults, retention policies, what even counts as identifying data -- if the company committed to one of the rival views instead of the others? Like, if privacy is really about expectations and context rather than anonymity, does anonymization even matter, or is it a fig leaf? Is there some single coherent view under which all three of our practices make sense, or is the inconsistency real and one of them has to go?
