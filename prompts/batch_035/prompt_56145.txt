I'm an observant Jew with yeshiva-level learning. I'm fluent in the halakhic vocabulary — din and rachamim, pikuach nefesh, aseh docheh lo ta'aseh, yeihareg ve'al ya'avor, the principle that the commandments are to be lived by rather than died for, and the whole apparatus by which halakhah codifies when and how one norm overrides another. I grew up on the akeidah, the binding of Isaac, as the paradigm of tested obedience. I have never read Kierkegaard.

Restate his Fear and Trembling for me in that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. The parts I'd want mapped: his claim that Abraham's act suspends the ethical, where the ethical is the universal — that in which the individual is at home and intelligible to everyone — and faith is an absolute relation to the absolute; his contrast between Abraham and the tragic hero, who gives up something particular for the sake of the universal, can therefore be understood and wept over by the community, while Abraham can neither explain himself nor be understood by anyone; his insistence that the exception of faith cannot be made intelligible to a third party, so no community and no judge can certify it; and the silence — that Abraham can pray to God and comfort Isaac, but has nothing to say to anyone else in the language of the ethical, which is why Kierkegaard's pseudonymous author says he can understand the tragic hero but not Abraham.

Then give me the residue report, item by item:

1. What translates cleanly? My instinct is that a divine command overriding a norm is not news in my vocabulary; it is the daily content of halakhah. Every case in which pikuach nefesh pushes aside Shabbat looks like a teleological suspension of the ethical in the literal sense, and every hierarchy of obligations is a codification of which suspensions are permitted when.

2. What translates only with distortion? In halakhah the exception becomes a rule: it is stated, taught, applied to new cases, and certified by a court. For Kierkegaard the whole point seems to be that the exception cannot be stated as a rule, because stating it as a rule returns the individual to the universal. If you translate the knight of faith as a person who correctly applies a hierarchy of commandments, you may have translated away precisely the thing he says Abraham was.

3. What has no expression at all? Is there anything in my vocabulary for the individual as such, over against the community and its norms, in a relation that no rabbi or court can check — or is that exactly what my vocabulary exists to make unsayable?

Then give me your verdict on the residue: does it point to something the halakhic framework cannot say and perhaps needs to, or does it point to something Kierkegaard only gets to say because the ethical he suspends is a morality addressed to all rational agents as such, a picture halakhah never claimed to fit — so that from inside halakhah his suspension looks like a category error, the refutation of a position nobody here holds? Be honest about where the translation is doing smuggled work in either direction: do not let the halakhic framework's assumptions masquerade as translation, and do not let Kierkegaard's assumptions masquerade as halakhah.

A final epistemic request: as you go, flag how confident you are in each interpretive claim, and at the end single out the one point you are least sure of, with a sentence on what would change your mind about it.
