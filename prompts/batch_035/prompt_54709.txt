I cover science and ideas for a mid-size outlet, and the fights I actually have with my editors are never about fabricating things — nobody wants to print a false claim. The fights are about sentences that are technically true but built to make readers believe something stronger or just different: headlines like "chemical found in coffee linked to cancer" for a relative risk of 1.02, or "half of new teachers quit within five years" when that's counting retirements. The philosophy of language has this whole literature on the difference between lying (asserting what you believe false) and misleading (saying something true, or not asserting at all, while engineering a false belief) — and some people argue the misleading route is actually worse, morally, because it makes the reader do the deceiving to themselves. I want that turned into something usable. Please write a decision procedure I can run on a headline or lede in ten minutes before deadline: what to check, in what order, what counts as failing, and where the procedure has to stop and hand off to a human judgment call — especially the question of whether misleading-by-implication is as bad as lying, which I suspect is a values question no checklist can settle. It needs a few test sentences I can hold new drafts against, and it has to be honest about the case it can't handle: I can never know for sure what a reader will infer. Then run it on three real-ish examples: the coffee/cancer headline, a lede that says a startup "has yet to rule out" a safety problem that no one has ever alleged (true! also pure insinuation), and one where I expect the answer I won't like — our house style of hedged clickbait like "what X may be doing to your brain," which is carefully designed to pass every fact-check while doing the work of a false claim. Say plainly what the procedure concludes on that last one and whether I should take it up with my editor or live with it.
