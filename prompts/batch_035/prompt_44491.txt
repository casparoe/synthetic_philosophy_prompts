I'm a distributed systems engineer, and I've gotten deep into the philosophy literature on the extended mind thesis lately -- the Clark/Chalmers idea that cognitive processes can run partly outside the skull, with the famous case of Otto's notebook standing in for biological memory. I know it well enough to be dangerous but I keep reading the arguments and feeling like the two sides are talking past each other in ways I can't quite pin down, and I have a suspicion that my day job's vocabulary would either sharpen the debate or show me it's mostly verbal. So here's the request.

Restate the extended mind thesis in the vocabulary I actually use: primary and replica state, consistency models (strong vs eventual), read/write latency, failover, quorum, partition tolerance, and the question of where you draw the boundary between the application and the backing store. Do it as faithfully as you can. Otto's notebook looks to me like a cold replica with high read latency and no automatic failover; Otto-plus-notebook looks like a system whose working set is partly on slow storage; and the dispute about whether the notebook is "part of Otto's mind" looks like it might reduce to a boundary-drawing convention about whether the database belongs to the application. I don't know if that gloss is right or if it smuggles in assumptions, so part of your job is to tell me honestly where it holds up and where it distorts.

Then I want the residue report, and this is the part I care most about. For each of the main moving parts of the debate, say whether it translates: the parity intuition (that if the notebook's role were played by an internal process we'd count it as memory without hesitation), the trust/reliability conditions people put on Otto's access to the notebook, the "coupling" language that's supposed to distinguish a genuine part of the cognitive system from a mere tool, and the objections that say we're confusing causal dependence with constitution. Which of these have real counterparts in my vocabulary and which come out as artifacts of the translation? Specifically: is the internalist position ("cognition stops at the skin") expressible in my vocabulary as anything more than an arbitrary system boundary, or does it make a substantive claim about, say, latency budgets or failure domains that survives translation? And is there something in the debate -- the normative stuff, maybe, like whether Otto is responsible for what's in his notebook in the way he's responsible for his beliefs -- that my vocabulary simply cannot say, and if so, is that a gap in my vocabulary or a sign the philosophers' claim is less clear than it looks?

One more thing. I've noticed that once you phrase it my way, a natural follow-up appears: in my world we sometimes treat a slow-but-reliable replica as part of the service and sometimes as an external dependency, and which one it is depends on who's asking and for what purpose -- SLOs, blast radius, billing. That makes me suspect the internalist/extensionist fight is a fight over a boundary that's drawn relative to a purpose, not discovered. If you think that's right, say so and tell me what it gets right and wrong; if you think it flattens a real disagreement, tell me what the disagreement is in terms I can evaluate.

I'd like you to be careful to distinguish what's basically settled among people who work on this from what's genuinely contested -- I know enough to know that things like the trust conditions on Otto's notebook and the whole "mark of the cognitive" business are live arguments, not textbook results, so please don't hand me one side's framing as if it were the consensus view. And if my read-the-debate-through-ops-glasses gloss ends up presupposing one side's position (I worry the boundary-is-conventional thing is just extensionism wearing a hoodie), flag that too.
