At work my manager keeps quoting "fortune favors the bold" and attributing it to Machiavelli, most recently to get me to sign off on shipping a feature before our competitor does — because waiting is what cautious people do, and Machiavelli supposedly says cautious people end up ruined. I finally read chapter 25 of The Prince last weekend and... he never says that. What he says is way stranger. Near the start: "Fortune is the arbiter of one-half of our actions, but that she still leaves us to direct the other half, or perhaps a little less." And near the end: "fortune being changeful and mankind steadfast in their ways, so long as the two are in agreement men are successful, but unsuccessful when they fall out."

So now I'm confused about what the slogan — my manager's version, but maybe also Machiavelli's own — is even claiming. Can you lay out the different precise theses "fortune favors the bold" could be expressing when people deploy it, and say for each whether it's plausible, whether it's actually Machiavelli's view, and — the part I actualy need — whether it would justify shipping early?

Some candidate readings rattling around in my head; feel free to correct or add to them:

1. The tautology: boldness by definition is action under uncertainty, so of course luck operates on the bold more visibly — empty but sounds wise.
2. The probabilistic claim: bold actions have higher expected value, all else equal. Testable, possibly false.
3. Machiavelli's river: in chapter 25 fortune is a flooded river, and "she shows her power where valour has not prepared to resist her" — which sounds like the slogan has the causation backwards, since the winning move there is building barriers, not being impetuous.
4. Machiavelli's actual conditional: he says "he will be successful who directs his actions according to the spirit of the times" — boldness works only when the times call for boldness, which as unconditional advice guts the slogan entirely, since it drops the antecedent.
5. The game-theoretic one: boldness works because most people aren't bold, so it's a strategy that stops working once everyone adopts it (my manager also says "first-mover advantage," which I suspect is this).
6. The commitment one: the Julius II story in the same chapter — the pope charges off to Bologna before Spain and Venice can get their story straight, and his impetuosity is what made them "stand irresolute and passive." Boldness as moving first so that you rewrite everyone else's options before they coordinate.

Also, does the weird fake precision of "one-half, or perhaps a little less" do any real work, or is he just hedging with a number to sound scientific? That part confuses me the most.

Give me a menu I can bring to our next one-on-one: for each reading, what it claims, whether it's true or defensible, and whether my manager's "ship now" conclusion actually follows from it. And if none of the readings gets him his conclusion, tell me which one he'd have to be relying on, and whether that one is the defensible reading or the false one.
