I teach intro sociology, and every few years the Weber debate comes up and every few years it's exactly where it was when I was a grad student. You know the one: the Protestant ethic thesis — the claim, roughly, that ascetic Protestantism, especially in its Calvinist forms, made a life of disciplined labor, saving and reinvestment psychologically tolerable and then actually virtuous, and that this helped produce "the spirit of capitalism" and, eventually, capitalism itself as a system. What's funny is that the empirical side has genuinely moved a lot — econometric work comparing Protestant and Catholic regions within Germany, studies of whether the pattern travels outside Europe, people controlling for literacy and for which lord happened to convert in the Reformation — and yet the argument about whether culture can be a cause has gone nowhere for a century. The pro-Weber side says the numbers keep showing Protestant regions ahead; the skeptics say either the correlation is confounded (regions that went Protestant already had different institutions, or the monasteries getting dissolved was the real windfall) or that even a clean correlation wouldn't touch what Weber actually cared about, because his question was about meaning — why did these people experience endless work as a calling instead of a curse — and no regression can answer that.

Here's my working hypothesis, and tell me if it holds up: the two camps are answering different questions under one name. One side is doing causal-inference social science — did changing a region's religion change its economic outcomes? — and the other is doing something more like interpretive explanation — what made capitalist behavior intelligible and livable for the people actually doing it? If that's right, the endless rounds of new data are wasted on each other, becuase no finding could move a disagreement that's really about whether "cause" is even the right relation between a value and an action. But I'm honestly not sure the camps split that cleanly: a lot of the quantitative people take Weber's meaning-question seriously, and some interpretive types are perfectly happy to say culture causes things, just slowly and diffusely, the way climate does.

So diagnose the stall for me. Is this a verbal dispute about the word "cause," a substantive but hard-to-test difference about the mechanisms by which values get into behavior (selection, habituation, legitimating, vs. actually constituting the goal itself), or just an empirical question that's genuinely hard because historical data is messy? And given the diagnosis, what would count as progress — a sharper typology of the ways values can matter, a way of splitting the thing into two research programs that stop stepping on each other, or should I just accept that the original question was doing two jobs at once and neither job can be quietly dropped?
