I'm taking a graduate seminar on decision theory, and we've just spent two weeks on the absent-minded driver: the driver who must exit at the second of two identical intersections to get home (payoff 4), exits at the first or drives past both end up with payoffs 0 and 1, and who has no memory of how many intersections she has passed, so at each intersection she must act as if she doesn't know which one it is. Before I write my term paper arguing that one or another decision theory handles the problem best, I want the standard of success fixed first, so that I'm not grading causal decision theory and evidential decision theory against a list that was reverse-engineered to favor whichever one I already like.

So: write the desiderata -- the requirements any adequate decision theory must meet to count as solving the absent-minded driver problem, each with the case or consideration that motivates it. At minimum I want the list to confront the following, which we discussed in seminar, though you should add requirements I haven't thought of:

- What the theory owes us in the basic case: whether it must simply output "continue with probability p at each intersection, where p is chosen to maximize expected payoff under the self-locating uncertainty," or whether it owes us a derivation of that answer from more general principles -- and whether that demand is fair, given that some might call it a demand for idle architecture.
- The variant where the driver receives a piece of evidence at an intersection that is correlated with which intersection it is (say a billboard she is more likely to have noticed at the second one). This is where the two standard theories famously pull apart, one toward an action that looks like double-counting evidence and the other toward an action that looks like ignoring it. A theory that only handles the memoryless case hasn't done the work.
- Whether the theory must explain, not just permit, the role of randomization: why the driver does better with a mixed strategy than with any pure one, what exactly she is choosing when she "chooses a mixed strategy" in a problem where she cannot distinguish the choice points, and whether the theory's treatment of this generalizes to agents who can make binding commitments before getting in the car versus agents who cannot.
- The structural similarity to self-locating belief problems like Sleeping Beauty, and whether the theory should be required to handle both with one mechanism or whether that unification is a methodological preference, not a requirement.
- A constraint against solutions that only work for two intersections or for this payoff structure: whatever the theory says should extend to n intersections, to correlated uncertainties, and to cases where the agent's earlier actions are evidence about her later decision situation.

For each requirement, mark whether it is non-negotiable or a preference that particular camps happen to hold, and flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied -- my suspicion is that a demand for per-node act rationality and a demand for ex ante policy optimality may be jointly unsatisfiable in this setting, and if so I want to hear exactly why that is the result and not a defect in my list, though I am not sure of this. Then, briefly, say how you would expect the causal and evidential theories to do against the list without giving a full scorecard.

One more thing: I want the response to be self-critical about the list itself, not just confident in it. Mark which requirement you are least confident is a genuine desideratum rather than a prejudice in favor of one camp, and say what argument or variant case would lead you to drop or revise that requirement -- where is the reasoning behind the list most likely to go wrong? I would rather submit a term paper that exposes the standard's weakest joint than one that defends a list I never stress-tested.
