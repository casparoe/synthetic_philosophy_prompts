I'm in an upper-level seminar on theories of punishment, and last week's discussion of retributivism was effectively shut down by a classmate's one-liner: 'two wrongs don't make a right.' As best I can reconstruct her argument, it was this: the offender commits one wrong; the state's deliberate infliction of hard treatment is a second wrong; retributivism says the second is justified *because of* the first; so retributivism tries to make a right out of two wrongs and is self-defeating. The TA nodded, we moved on, and I left unsure what, if anything, had been established.

For a short paper I'm meant to precisify a piece of philosophical rhetoric, and I'd like to use this one. What distinct precise claims could 'two wrongs don't make a right' be making when aimed at retributivism? It seems to me the readings range from something nobody disputes (two wrong acts don't sum to a right act — true, but irrelevant if the retributivist denies that punishment is a wrong at all) to something genuinely substantive: that the state's intentionally harming a person is a wrong no matter what that person deserves, or that one wrong cannot alter the deontic status of a separate act, i.e., that desert is just not a justificatory property. There may also be readings in between — e.g., that a wrong act can be redeemed but only by consequences (which would push toward consequentialism), or that the slogan is not a refutation of retributivism but a constraint on it, something like a proportionality ceiling or a prohibition on certain *methods* of punishment.

Please lay the readings out as a menu rather than picking a favorite: for each, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or simply false, and then say which readings would actually support my classmate's conclusion — that retributivism is self-defeating, incoherent, or self-contradictory — as opposed to merely criticizing it. Two things I'd especially like your judgment on: first, whether the retributivist's cheapest answer is that the deserved cannot be wronged, so punishment is not a 'wrong' in the operative sense — and whether that answer is principled or question-begging; second, where to place something our professor mentioned only in passing, the old Hegelian idea that in being punished the criminal is treated as a rational agent and so, in a sense, gets his own right rather than a wrong done to him. I can't tell whether that is a way of denying a premise of the slogan or a separate theory the slogan simply isn't addressed to.

Finally, please flag how confident you are in each verdict, and single out the point in the whole analysis where you are least sure of yourself — I would rather know where the ground is soft than receive a flat list of conclusions.
