I have followed the debate about aggregation in Scanlonian contractualism for roughly two decades, and it now strikes me as thoroughly ritualised. The cycle is familiar: a critic presents a case in which contractualism's individualist restriction — the rule that principles are to be rejected on the basis of individual-person complaints, so that complaints cannot simply be added together — appears to yield the wrong verdict, or appears to get the right verdict only by covertly aggregating. The classic shapes are the numbers case (several people each facing a serious harm that one rescuer could avert for only one of them), the transplant case (one death imposed on an innocent to save many), and the risk case (a large risk to a few versus tiny risks spread across a very large number). The contractualist responds with a new distinction: claims of different strengths within a life, comparisons between threats and unfairness, tie-breaking when claims are equally strong, or a shift from claims about final outcomes to claims about the imposition of risk ex ante. The critic calls the new distinction a gerrymander, produces a case engineered to slip between its clauses, and the cycle begins again. As far as I can tell, almost nobody has changed their mind since the mid-2000s, and the growing pile of distinctions has not produced anything resembling convergence.

I want a diagnosis of this stall, not another round of it. My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis only, is that the debate is not really about contractualism at all, but about whether "aggregation" names a single phenomenon. It seems to me that at least three different operations travel under that name: summing benefits and burdens within a single life; counting across lives that each have the very same complaint; and weighing complaints of unequal strength across lives. If these are genuinely distinct operations, then perhaps the contractualist is entitled to accept the first two while rejecting the third, and the critics' real complaint — that this position is ad hoc — rests on conflating them. But I can see two ways this could go wrong. Perhaps the disagreement is instead substantive and lies elsewhere: a hidden commitment about whether the separateness of persons is a constraint on the content of moral reasoning itself, or merely a constraint on the form in which reasoning must be presentable to each person — a difference between what may count in deliberation and what must be shown to whom. Or perhaps the two sides are answering different questions under one heading: the contractualists addressing a theory of what we owe to each other, and the critics addressing a theory of what there is most reason to do, with the cases merely masking the divergence.

Please diagnose the structure of the disagreement using what the participants actually say and do in the debate, rather than by surveying positions. Tell me which, if any, of the three readings above is right, or offer a better one if none is. In particular, I would like to know: whether the stall is a sign that the underlying question is ill-posed, or merely that it is hard; whether the accumulating distinctions are genuine progress being misdescribed, or noise; and what, concretely, would count as movement — a new kind of case, a formal constraint on any acceptable resolution, a distinction that both sides would have independent reason to accept, or the admission that no progress should be expected here and why. Please write your answer in British English.
