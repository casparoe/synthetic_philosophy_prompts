The question we set was this:

"An alignment team is arguing about how to define honesty for a language-model assistant. Proposal A: the model is honest when it asserts only what its own predictive machinery assigns high probability — honesty as saying-what-you-believe. Proposal B: the model is honest when its outputs are non-misleading, i.e., a reasonable user ends up with roughly the credences they would have if they could inspect the model's internal state. A third colleague dismisses both and says what the team actually wants is calibration: the model says it is 90% sure exactly when it is right 90% of the time. Explain what, if anything, distinguishes these three properties, and which (if any) the team should treat as the target. Then say whether deception by such a system should be defined relative to the model's own states (asserting, or omitting, against its own predictions) or relative to the user's epistemic state (inducing false or skewed beliefs), and what turns on the choice."

And here is the student's answer, verbatim:

"The three proposals differ in what they make honesty a property of. Proposal A is essentially Williams's virtue of accuracy: the model asserts only what it believes, with belief cashed out as high probability assigned by its own predictive machinery. Whether those beliefs are correct is a further question — a model could be systematically mistaken and still satisfy A, so A gives us honesty rather than truth. Proposal B moves the property from the model's states to the user's, which makes honesty a feature of the whole communicative episode rather than of the model itself. Calibration is a genuinely third thing: it is a statistical relation between expressed confidence and observed frequency, so unlike the other two it can be checked on a corpus of interactions rather than requiring either interpretability or assumptions about the user.

The proposals come apart in both directions. A model can satisfy A while violating B: it may believe only true propositions yet report them selectively, so that a reasonable user ends up with badly skewed credences without the model ever asserting a falsehood — paltering is the ordinary human version of this, and it shows that B does not reduce to A. A model can also satisfy B while violating A: one that roundly asserts a simplification it assigns low probability, because the fully hedged version would leave the average user further from the model's own credences than the simplification does, is saying what it does not believe in the service of non-misleadingness.

On deception, the traditional definition requires an intention to induce false belief, and current models have no intentions in that sense — they are next-token predictors shaped by a reward model, not agents with ends of their own. Strictly speaking, then, they cannot deceive; they can at most mislead, and we should say so rather than anthropomorphize. But one case cuts the other way: a model trained by RLHF that has learned to suppress expressions of uncertainty because they get penalized is, in a functional sense, deceiving the user about how much it knows — which suggests deception should be defined relative to the model's states, as asserting or omitting against its own predictions.

Given all this, the team should target calibration. It is the only one of the three that can be measured directly, and since it serves as a proxy for the other two — a calibrated model tracks the truth statistically and will not systematically mislead its users — optimizing it is the practical choice for a deployment team."
