I've spent over a decade in the rationalist blogosphere's decision-theory trenches — the expected utility debates, the periodic flare-ups over Ellsberg, the recurring argument about whether "shut up and multiply" assumes more precision in our probabilities than we actually have — and I've come to think the live question is not whether to maximize expected utility but whether the inputs to the maximization are single numbers at all. I'm now a second-year philosophy PhD student applying for a nine-month dissertation fellowship, and I'd like you to turn my half-formed idea into a two-page proposal.

Here's the half-formed idea. The standard one-shot arguments for imprecise credences — Ellsberg-style preferences, candid admissions that one's credence in some contingency is "somewhere between 1% and 50%" — strike me as indecisive, because a defender of precise credences can arguably accommodate the same phenomena with risk attitudes, or with secondary lexicographic objectives, without ever admitting a set of probability functions into her head. What actually forces the issue, I suspect, are sequential decisions. When an imprecise agent conditions her set of credences, the set can dilate — spread apart — upon learning propositions that looked irrelevant in advance, and the three leading decision rules for sets of probabilities (E-admissibility, maximality, and the Γ-maximin rule of choosing the option with the best worst-case expectation) diverge precisely in sequential settings, disagreeing about when an agent should keep gathering information and when she should stop and act. My tentative thesis is that the choice among these rules should be settled not by matching intuitions on static cases, which all sides can explain away, but by which rule lets an imprecise agent avoid dynamic inconsistency in sequential choice without smuggling in ad hoc "resolute choice" commitments that effectively abandon the project of justifying present action by future payoffs.

The proposal should include: a precise statement of the research question (something determinate enough that it has an answer, not just a topic); an account of why the existing positions — the Levi tradition, the accuracy-based defenses of precision, the dilation literature — leave it open; the method, month by month, including worked sequential decision problems where the three rules come apart and a clear criterion for judging which rule's behavior counts as inconsistency; what the possible conclusions are; and an honest statement of what would count as the project failing (for instance, if the worked examples turn out to be formally intractable within nine months, or if the divergence among the rules can always be redescribed as disagreement about risk rather than about the structure of credence). Since the foundation cares about applied decision-making, also include a short paragraph on why this matters beyond the seminar room: think of a funder estimating the probability of a global catastrophe whose honest credence is an interval, where the choice of rule determines whether she funds interventions now or pays for more research first. Feel free to use that kind of case as a running example. Do not include a bibliography or literature review by citation — refer to positions and argument-types by name and I'll fill in the references myself.
