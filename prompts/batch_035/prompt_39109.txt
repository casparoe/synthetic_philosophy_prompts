I finally got around to Simone Weil's On the Abolition of All Political Parties, the short essay from 1943, and I keep getting stuck trying to explain to a friend what's actually interesting in it. Everyone boils it down to "parties pressure their members into conformity, groupthink, whatever" — which is true but banal, and it doesn't explain why she thought the problem was severe enough to warrant abolition rather than reform. As far as I can tell the real engine of the argument is something like: collectives as such cannot think, only persons think, and a party is a machine built to produce a simulacrum of collective thought — so the problem isn't that parties happen to corrupt debate, it's that a party's essential function (self-preservation, growth, winning) makes honest inquiry structurally impossible within it, sort of like how an advertisement can't be an honest investigation into whether the product is any good.

So what I want explained:

1. How exactly is that mechanism supposed to work, especially the role of love or affection — from what I remember it's doing something weirder than discipline or careerism, and I don't fully get how she gets from caring about the party to the corruption of thought.
2. If the mechanism is really about collectives in general, why does she single out parties? Why doesn't the same argument abolish unions, churches, the resistance networks she was personally involved with, even informal intellectual factions? Is there anything in the essay that actually distinguishes a party from any other organized grouping with a shared commitment, or is that just a gap?
3. How does this fit with her broader stuff — her whole view that collectives are these big anonymous things that no one can actually face, and that only individual thought is real? Is the party essay just a special case of that, or does it add something new?

Also feel free to push back on my framing if I've misread her. I care more about getting the actual mechanism straight than about whether parties should really be abolished.
