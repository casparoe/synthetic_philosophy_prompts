I'm advising a small research group — four researchers, roughly three years, conceptual and formal work rather than empirical ML — on how to divide its effort across open problems in AI control: the family of safety measures premised on the possibility that the system you are deploying is already misaligned, so that acceptable outcomes are achieved by structuring what the model can do, see, and influence rather than by verifying what it wants. The current picture, as I understand it, rests on a few load-bearing pieces: control evaluations (red-teaming-style trials in which a deliberately adversarial model attempts to cause some catastrophe, to test whether the surrounding protocol would catch it), safety cases (structured arguments that the probability of catastrophic harm under deployment is below some threshold, typically by forcing any successful attack through a monitored bottleneck), and the decomposition of trustworthiness into task-specific and time-limited properties rather than a global attribute of the system.

Which open questions in this area most deserve the group's attention, and in what order? I care less about the list than the reasoning, so please defend the ranking explicitly: state your criteria (importance, tractability for researchers without compute, neglect relative to where the field's attention already goes, and how many other questions depend on each), say where the criteria pull in different directions, and indicate what developments over the next few years would move a question up or down.

To give a sense of the level I have in mind, here are some candidate questions, phrased roughly — feel free to replace them with better ones:

1. The generalization problem. A control measure validated against red-team attacks protects against a real adversary only if the evaluation's attack distribution is in some sense harder, or at least covering, relative to what a genuinely misaligned system would attempt. Is there a principled account of when this holds, or does control quietly retain the trust-based step it was designed to eliminate?

2. The recursivity problem. Control protocols are usually framed for deploying models in the world, but the most safety-relevant near-term use is inside the lab, where the system being controlled is doing AI research — including research on control, and possibly on evading it. What would it even mean to evaluate a protocol whose test cases are partly generated by the thing being tested?

3. The deferred-oversight chain. Many control measures bottom out in a trusted human or trusted weaker model checking an action. But checking is itself a task that can be delegated, monitored, or gamed. Is the "trusted" relation transitive across a chain of delegations, and if not, where does the chain have to terminate and what guarantees can exist there?

4. The complacency objection. If control works well enough, it reduces the incentive to solve alignment, and one can picture a world of ever-more-capable systems held in ever-more-elaborate check. Is that a fact about deployment incentives that no amount of control research can change, or are there designs for control regimes that structurally avoid it?

Please also address two meta-questions. First, which of the questions you rank are genuinely conceptual — the kind a philosophy-adjacent group can move — and which are secretly empirical, in the sense that three years of clear thinking would be dominated by a few well-designed evaluations? Second, what would have to be true for the entire control framing to be the wrong abstraction — for instance, if the trustworthiness of a system turned out not to decompose along task lines at all, or if the bottleneck-forcing property of safety cases were unachievable for the kinds of deployment that actually matter? I'd personally find most persuasive an ordering that confronts that last possibility rather than assuming the program's foundations are sound.
