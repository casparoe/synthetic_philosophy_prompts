Ok so last night me and my flatmate had one of those arguments that runs way past midnight, and I've been chewing on it all day because I think she was onto something and neither of us could say what.

Her side: our friend Dana has basically been in love with our other friend Tom for years, would do anything for him. My flatmate kept insisting that at some point that devotion itself becomes a reason for Tom to love her back. And I kept feeling that's off without being able to pin down why. It's not that devotion counts for nothing -- it's obviously a reason to be kind to Dana, to not string her along, maybe to feel gratitude. But the moment you try to use it as a reason to love her it just... doesn't take. I can watch it push around all the neighboring attitudes (be gentle, feel bad, don't ghost her) while the one attitude it was offered as a reason for stays exactly where it was.

Then on the walk to work I started seeing the same shape everywhere. My boss is big on "the team performs better if everyone believes the launch will land," and he treats that as a reason everyone has to believe it -- and again, it's clearly a reason to *want* the team to believe it, maybe a reason to project confidence, but when I try to use it on myself, as a reason for my own belief, it slips through my fingers. There's an old story my poker friend tells too: a stranger offers you $500 straight up if you'll come to believe his team wins the cup this year. The $500 is a reason to want the belief, arguably even a reason to *try* to talk yourself into it, but it's not, by itself, one scrap of evidence about the team. And there's a version with fear: "this dog is dangerous" is obviously a reason to be scared of the dog, while "the owner gives you space if you're visibly scared" is a reason of some sort, but it feels like it arrives through the wrong door.

I half-know some distinctions that live in this neighborhood and none of them cut here. Epistemic vs practical reasons -- no, that's reasons to believe vs reasons to act, but all my cases are reasons *for attitudes* (love, fear, belief) where the question isn't believe-or-act, it's what can count in favor of the attitude itself. Wishful thinking -- that's the *failure mode* of using the bad reasons, not a description of their weird status. And it can't just be about belief, because the whole Dana fight was about love and the dog one is fear, so whatever the split is, it crosses attitudes.

So, questions. Does this thing -- considerations that successfully count in favor of everything *around* an attitude but go dead the moment you aim them at the attitude itself -- have an actual name in philosophy? If there's literature, I want to know how they draw the line, because my own attempts keep springing leaks. Is the mark that live reasons are about the world and dead ones are about the *having* of the attitude? That feels right for the $500 and my boss, but Dana's devotion is a fact about *her*, not about Tom's loving her, and it still feels crooked, so maybe that formulation is wrong too. And here's the one that's really bugging me: Tom is planning to go to Dana's family thing partly because he knows how crushed she'd be if he skipped -- is "she'll be devastated" a dead reason for intending to go, or a live one? I genuinely can't tell, and it makes me suspect the interesting stuff is all in the boundary cases. So: name if there is one, but a bare name won't settle this. What exactly is the phenomenon, what is it NOT, where does it break, and if nobody's named it yet then help me name it -- but only after drawing the edges.
