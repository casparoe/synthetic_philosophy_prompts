I manage the day-to-day operations of a university comparative cognition lab. Our subjects rotate: at the moment, cleaner fish, pigeons, jays, cuttlefish, and honeybee colonies, plus a new collaboration with a robotics group whose robots are driven by large language models. My recurring problem is a mismatch between paradigm and subject: students run insight or planning tasks on animals that have only ever shown trial-and-error learning, burn a semester, and then conclude that the species cannot plan, when the honest finding is that the design never gave a merely associative learner any route to the answer. The lab director wants an intake procedure that we run on every new species, system, or paradigm before it enters the building, based on Dennett's hierarchy of Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures — the ladder running from systems that improve only across generations, to those that reshape their own behavior within a lifetime through operant conditioning, to those that test candidate moves against an internal model before staking anything on them, to those that import thinking tools from their culture, language chief among them. The procedure has to be runnable by a first-year student with twenty minutes and no philosophy background, and defensible enough that I can hand it to a skeptical PI.

Please write it as continuous prose only — a memo I can read aloud at a lab meeting, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. It should give the ordered questions to ask about a system, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and which kinds of behavioral findings mark each boundary — for instance, what would justify calling something Popperian rather than merely a fast Skinnerian, and how one-trial learning, reversal learning, and detour tasks bear on that line. It should say where the procedure's output is simply that a proposed paradigm must not be run as designed, because it presupposes a higher kind than the evidence establishes, and where it must stop and hand the question to a person because it is no longer a question of competence — above all whether an animal suffers, which the hierarchy was never meant to settle. Please also flag the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as checks.

Then run the procedure on three cases. First, our jays in a food-caching and future-planning paradigm — and I warn you that if the procedure tells us our flagship experiment cannot distinguish genuinely model-based planning from heavily shaped association after months of training, we need to hear that. Second, a cuttlefish given a puzzle box, where the live question is what would count as one-trial insight rather than rapid trial-and-error, and what observation would settle it. Third, the robotics group's language-model robot, which narrates its plan out loud before executing it; I suspect the procedure will hand them a conclusion they will not like, namely that fluent self-description together with internally previewed moves makes it a candidate Popperian, and arguably a Gregorian one, given that language is the paradigm Gregorian tool, while saying nothing at all about whether anything is experienced — so be explicit about where that reading outruns what the hierarchy licenses. Finally, tell me where the ladder breaks as a description: which real systems do not sit at a single rung — a honeybee colony whose scouts may make the colony as a whole behave like one Popperian creature while individual bees stay near the bottom, or an animal whose edge is dense memory rather than an internal model — and what the procedure should say when it meets such a case, so that we do not mistake the ladder's failures for the animal's.
