Having spent a working life in experimental physics, I have recently taken up political philosophy, and I would be grateful for some help evaluating a passage I encountered in a popular introduction to the subject. The book summarizes the debate about the proper metric of equality as follows: "Utilitarians held that equality is about welfare — ensuring that everyone is equally happy. Rawls showed this was unworkable and proposed instead that society distribute primary goods equally: rights, opportunities, income, and wealth. Amartya Sen then proved Rawls's metric also failed, because two people holding the same bundle of goods can achieve very different levels of well-being — someone born with a disability, for instance, needs far more resources to accomplish the same things. The question is therefore settled: equality must be measured in capabilities, by which is meant what people actually manage to do and to be, and contemporary social policy is now built on this insight."

Something about the arc of this summary — successive refutations culminating in a settled doctrine — strikes me as suspiciously tidy, but I do not yet know the literature well enough to say where it goes wrong. Would you assess it piece by piece: which claims a careful utilitarian, Rawlsian, or capability theorist would accept, which conflate distinct positions (I gather distinctions may be being blurred between technical terms and their everyday senses), and whether the concluding claim that the question is settled actually follows from the accurate parts? In particular, please keep separate what is genuinely settled among people who work on this from what remains contested — about, for instance, how the rival metrics are to be measured, or how they bear on actual policy — and do not present any contested resolution as the consensus view. In my old profession we were taught to distrust tidy stories, and I would like to carry that habit over.
