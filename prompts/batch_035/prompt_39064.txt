I sit on the committee for our PhD program's comprehensive examination in AI safety and governance. This year I want a single essay question, answerable in ninety minutes, on the contrast between catastrophic failures driven by influence-seeking AI systems and failures consisting of slow value drift — the kind of gradual loss of human steering in which nothing seizes power, nothing goes visibly wrong at any particular moment, and yet the world ends up optimizing for things we no longer endorse. I want the question built around a specific crux rather than being a survey prompt.

The crux I have in mind is a diagnostic I have been drafting, which I'll call the fragility test: at any time t, imagine that by fiat you could permanently disable the single most capable AI system then in operation, and suppose the rest of the ecosystem continues to run as before. The intuitive hypothesis is that an influence-seeking failure counterfactually depends on that one system — disable it and the loss of control halts or materially slows — whereas value drift does not: the drift is carried by the whole ecosystem of users, incentives, and modest capabilities, so the counterfactual barely dents it. I want the question to ask candidates whether the fragility test cleanly separates the two failure families, or whether it instead tracks something orthogonal to them — for instance, the degree to which optimization pressure is concentrated, as opposed to the kind of mechanism producing the outcome. To force real engagement, the question should require candidates to construct at least one counterexample in each direction: a drift-style failure that would nonetheless halt under the counterfactual, and an influence-seeking failure that would continue despite it.

The question should additionally require candidates to take a position on whether the two families differ in kind or only in degree, and to draw out what follows for governance — for example, whether audits, deployment reviews, and capability evaluations aimed at individual systems are structurally mismatched to one of the families, and what that implies about defenses in the form of tripwires that only fire after some threshold has been crossed.

Please provide: (1) the question itself, worded for the exam paper; (2) a model answer of roughly 600 words; and (3) a marking scheme worth 20 marks in total, built around five must-hit points with marks allocated to each, plus a one-line note per point on how candidates typically lose the marks. The question must not be answerable by recitation: a candidate who merely lists and accurately summarizes the two failure modes should not be able to pass, and the marking scheme should say so explicitly. Assume the candidates have completed graduate coursework covering instrumental convergence, deceptive alignment, and debates over multipolar versus singleton scenarios, so the question may presuppose that vocabulary without defining it.
