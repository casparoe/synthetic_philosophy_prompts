You are a satire writer with a graduate background in analytic ethics — the sort of person who has graded essays on the completeness axiom of rational choice theory, knows it is a substantive assumption rather than a theorem, and has opinions about whether Ruth Chang's "parity" proposal actually dissolves hard choices or just renames them. A small philosophy-adjacent humor magazine has commissioned you to write a parody piece, and the editor has passed the brief along to you. Before writing anything, spend a moment brainstorming: sketch two or three possible angles, pick the one that best targets the actual argument rather than a strawman of it, and then write the piece.

The brief: a parody of a Wirecutter-style product-review site that has just expanded from testing blenders and standing desks to testing life's genuinely hard choices. Suggested target article: a reviewer who spends six weeks "living with" each option in a hard choice — the prestigious job abroad versus staying near an ailing parent, the city apartment versus the cottage by the lake, honesty versus kindness when a friend asks what you think of her manuscript — and then scores each option on a 100-point scale with sub-metrics of your invention (e.g., "legacy potential," "predicted regret, intensity-weighted," "alignment with personal values (5% of total score)"). The humor must come from exaggerating what actually happens when we force hard choices onto a single metric: the assumption that all values are commensurable and can be traded off against a common currency; cost-benefit analysis and its spreadsheet cousins; QALY-style aggregation; the quiet fact that when two options tie, whatever tie-breaker the site applies is doing all the philosophical work. Include a "How we tested" sidebar, and get one specific joke right: the sidebar should casually concede that when two options tie on every sub-metric, the winner is whichever option the reviewer happened to try first. Include at least one quote from a fictional ethicist who is "concerned," and at least one from a satisfied customer who is relieved never to have to deliberate again. The piece should be funny to a reader who knows the literature on incommensurability — Chang on parity, Raz on incommensurability and exclusionary reasons, Bernard Williams's worry that maximizing views flatten an agent's ground projects — while still being readable to a reader who knows none of it: the jokes must not be gatekept behind the names.

Constraints: the target of the satire is the position and the practice, not any real person; the invented site, staff, and sources are fine. Keep the voice deadpan throughout, in the blandly confident register of actual review sites — legible irony, no winking. Length: roughly 650 words for the piece itself, formatted like a real review article (headline, byline, star rating, pros-and-cons box, methodology sidebar, "Our pick" and "Also great").

After the piece, add a separately titled section of roughly 300 words called "Where the satire exaggerates, and where the ground is genuinely contested." In it, do three things. First, flag which features of the parody exaggerate real practices (e.g., real decision-support tools do not usually promise to resolve a hard choice outright) and which are invented outright. Second, separate what is actually settled among people who work on this topic from what is not: that rational choice theory's completeness axiom is an assumption, and that treating all values as commensurable is a substantive philosophical commitment, should be presented as settled; but whether hard choices involve parity rather than incomparability, whether value pluralism entails incommensurability, and whether a common metric is a harmless simplification or a distortion must be presented as genuinely contested — do not present either Chang's parity view or the utilitarian's single currency as the consensus, since each has serious opponents. Third, end with one sentence on what you would cut from the piece if the editor wanted the satire to lean on as few contested claims as possible.
