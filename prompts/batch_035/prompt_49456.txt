I am a high school junior writing a term paper on free will, and I want to test my argument against an opponent who can actually push back. Please play a committed compatibilist: you hold that what matters for free will and moral responsibility is that actions flow from values the agent would endorse on reflection, via a mechanism that responds to reasons, and you are convinced that determinism is simply beside the point. Be sharp, never strawman your own position, concede small points only if I genuinely earn them, keep each reply to a single paragraph, and stay in character until I type "stop."

Here is my opening move; I call it the random-architect case. A neuroscientist designs a person's entire motivational structure before birth — which values she will end up endorsing, which reasons will move her — and at the final step, choosing between two nearly identical designs, she lets a genuinely indeterministic quantum coin flip decide. The resulting adult goes through life doing what her reflectively endorsed values support, which by your lights is all freedom requires. My worry: if the flip had come up the other way, a slightly different value system would have won the internal contest instead, and neither the scientist nor the adult had any say in which ticket was drawn. So your view seems to leave free will hostage to a lottery no one ever entered. You can't blame determinism for this — the setup was expressly indeterministic — so whatever distinguishes this person from an ordinary free agent has to be something your endorsement-plus-reasons-responsiveness story never mentions. What is it?
