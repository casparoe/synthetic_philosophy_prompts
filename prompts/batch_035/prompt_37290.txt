The answer:

"The paradox is that if you grant that a man with zero hairs is bald, and that removing a single hair from a bald man leaves him bald, then repeating that step over and over gets you to the conclusion that a man with 500,000 hairs is bald, which is absurd. So something has to go, and the question is what.

Epistemicism, associated with Williamson, says the whole thing is a trick question: there really is a sharp cutoff — some exact number n such that n hairs counts as bald and n+1 does not — we just can never know where it is. His argument for the unknowability is a margin-for-error principle: knowing of some particular case that it falls on the bald side would require being able to discriminate cases that differ by a single hair, and no matter how good you are, there will always be nearby cases you can't tell apart, so knowledge of the cutoff is impossible in principle. On this view the sorites argument is unsound because one of its conditionals is false, though we can't ever know which one.

Supervaluationism says that once we reach borderline cases, 'he is bald' has no determinate truth value. We consider all the ways of making 'bald' fully precise that respect the clear cases (the admissible precisifications), and a sentence is supertrue if it comes out true on every one of them. Borderline claims fail this test and are neither true nor false. A nice feature is that 'there is no sharp boundary between bald and not bald' itself comes out true, which is what we all wanted to say anyway. In effect the supervaluationist assigns borderline sentences partial truth values, like fuzzy logic does, just with more elaborate machinery. And since supervaluationists reject bivalence, they've abandoned classical logic, which means they owe us a rewritten story about which of our everyday inferences still hold.

On the balance analogy: I think it fits the epistemicist position perfectly. The sample really does have a true mass, and the balance's coarse resolution merely hides it from us, just like the true cutoff is there and our concepts are just too coarse to pick it out. That suggests the analogy actually goes further than the epistemicist wants — if we had a better instrument, say a perfect follicle-counting device together with a perfect grasp of how the community uses 'bald', we could in principle locate the cutoff. So the analogy shows the unknowability is really just a practical limitation of our measuring tools, not something deeper.

I find epistemicism more satisfying overall, mainly because it doesn't tamper with truth values and keeps our logic classical, whereas supervaluationism has to start revising the rules."
