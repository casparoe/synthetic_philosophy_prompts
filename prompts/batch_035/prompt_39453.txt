Retired geophysicist here, got into philosophy a few years back after my daughter insisted I needed something besides golf. Lately I've been reading about the foundations of probability and something's bugging me. My whole career I never quoted a single number for anything — you give an estimate and then error bars. So naturally I like the sound of "imprecise probabilities," where instead of one sharp credence you carry around an interval or a whole set of them. That just seems like honest error bars for belief.

But I keep tripping over a second move that people make instead: keep every probability perfectly sharp, but stack another probability distribution on top, so you're uncertain about the probability itself. My question: are these actually two rival views, or is the imprecise one just the two-level view with the top layer ripped off and thrown away? If I say "the chance of a big quake on this fault in the next 30 years is somewhere between 1% and 5%," versus "it's 3%, but I only have weak confidence in that 3%" — is one of those strictly more expressive than the other? From what I can tell the interval person can do something the stacked person can't, like refusing to bet at any odds in the middle (that sure sounds like my old error bars refusing to pick a point), whereas if you really have a full distribution over probabilities, you can always average it out and get a single fair price. And apparently the intervals can also do weird things like get *wider* after you condition on new data, even on irrelevant-sounding evidence — some phenomenon called dilation, wich nobody with sharp probabilities has to worry about.

So please compare the two approaches: where do they genuinely come apart, what can each express that the other can't, and which one actually deserves the error-bar analogy I keep wanting to make? Also tell me if the difference is just about representation or whether they force different practical behavior, since in my old world that was usually what separated two "equivalent" formalisms.
