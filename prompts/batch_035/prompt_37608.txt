I keep getting into arguments about affirmative action and quotas with my brother-in-law, and I've realized my gut verdicts on individual cases don't line up into anything I could defend. So I want to know if there's a principle, or set of principles, that captures how I actually judge these things, and which of my verdicts needs to go if there isn't. Here's where I land on a bunch of cases, concretely:

- Norway requiring boards of publicly listed companies to be at least 40% women, on pain of dissolution: fine. I remember the business world predicted disaster, companies complied, life went on, and I never felt bad about it.
- India reserving seats in legislatures, universities, and government jobs for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes: fine in principle, though I get uneasy when I imagine the same well-off family benefiting generation after generation.
- India also reserving things for Other Backward Classes, with courts carving out the well-off among them (the creamy layer, as they call it): that feels right, and honestly the carve-out is what saves it for me.
- US universities using race as one factor among many in holistic admissions, a sort of tiebreaker-ish plus factor: uneasy but okay.
- A US university setting aside a fixed number of seats by race, an actual quota: clearly not okay.
- The Supreme Court ruling a couple years back that ended race-conscious admissions in the US: I'm less bothered by it than I expected to be, which confuses me given the two verdicts above.
- Legacy preferences, where a university boosts applicants whose parents went there: obviously not okay, and possibly worse than any of the racial stuff, since it's a preference that points in exactly the wrong direction.
- Veteran preferences in government hiring: totally fine. It doesn't even feel like it belongs in the same conversation.
- Reserving a share of village council seats for women in India, with the reserved seats rotating between elections: fine, maybe even the best-designed one on this list.
- Replacing selective admissions with a pure lottery: fine, almost refreshing.

So what's going on? My brother-in-law says my pattern is just "preferences for groups I like," which I don't accept, but I notice it's hard to answer him. My tentative thought is something like: a preference is okay when it responds to a history of exclusion and it self-undermines once it's worked, i.e., it's supposed to eventually not be needed. But the veteran verdict doesn't fit that at all (veterans weren't excluded, and the preference was never meant to expire), and yet it's my most confident one. And I accept hard quotas for gender while rejecting hard racial quotas, which seems like the same mechanism getting opposite verdicts, unless the rotating feature of the panchayat seats is doing all the work -- but Norway's quota doesn't rotate.

Can you help me out? I'd like one or two candidate principles stated precisely enough to bite, with the case that motivates each clause, plus an honest accounting of which of my verdicts each principle just cannot accommodate and would have to overturn. And please tell me what the best candidate says about cases I haven't listed: preferences for first-generation college students, disability hiring targets, quotas meant to hit a class/income mix, and geographic diversity (the rural-student discount at fancy schools). If my verdicts turn out to be a genuinely inconsistent mess rather than an under-theorized principle, I'd rather know that than get a principle contorted to save them all. Answer in American English, please.
