I'm helping direct a student production of Measure for Measure next term, and the scene I can't stop chewing on is the ending. You've got Isabella, who spent the whole play refusing to compromise her principles even to save her brother's life, being asked by the disguised Duke to plead for Angelo's life — and she refuses. It's Mariana, the woman Angelo abandoned, who has to beg on her knees, and even she only gets Isabella to kneel with her after the Duke orders it. Then the Duke pardons Angelo anyway, proposes to Isabella (she never actually says yes, which our dramaturg keeps insisting is the point), and wraps everything up. Meanwhile the whole play has been about Angelo the hypocrite: a man who sentences Claudio to death for fornication while trying to extort sex from Isabella as the price of Claudio's life.

I've been reading some philosophy of blame lately — the stuff on standing, the idea that even accurate blame can be inappropriate coming from certain people, hypocrisy being the textbook case since the hypocrite commits the very wrong he denounces. And it strikes me that the play runs at least three different characters through this machinery in the final scene: Angelo, whose condemnation of Claudio the play clearly treats as polluted by his own conduct; Isabella, who won't plead, maybe because she thinks she has the standing of the wronged party and shouldn't have to, or maybe because she thinks no one should pardon him; Mariana, who pleads anyway; and then the Duke, who's spent the entire play deceiving, testing, and manipulating everyone, and who ends up handing down judgments and pardons from on high as if he's entitled to. Does the play notice that the Duke might be the least qualified person in Vienna to be distributing moral verdicts, or is the text oblivious to its own governor?

So I'd like a reading of the play through the philosophy of standing to blame, and I want it faithful in both directions — don't bend the philosophy to make Shakespeare look prescient, and don't pretend the play settles questions it doesn't. Some things I'd specifically want you to take a position on: whether Isabella's refusal to plead reads as an exercise of standing (the victim declining to do what only the victim may do) or as a failure of mercy that the play quietly criticizes; whether Mariana's pleading counts for the philosophers as the interesting hard case, since she's both victim (jilted) and something like Angelo's wife (committed to him), two standpoints pulling opposite ways; and whether the ending, where forgiveness arrives from a deceiver with dirty hands, is the play's answer to the philosophers or its unresolved problem. Point me to the scenes we should rehearse with this lens in mind, and be honest about where the play resists the reading — I'd rather know that before opening night than read it in a review after.
