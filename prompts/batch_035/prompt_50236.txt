Here's the pattern that bugs me. Across very different health systems -- nationalized ones, insurance-based ones -- you find roughly the same coverage decisions. We'll pay to give growth hormone to a kid whose pituitary isn't producing it, but not to a short kid with a normal pituitary who just sits at the bottom of the curve. We'll pay for a cochlear implant in a deaf child, but nobody would fund an implant that gave a hearing child ultrasonic range. We'll pay for an SSRI prescribed for depression, but if a healthy patient wants the same drug just to be a bit brighter than baseline, that gets labeled "cosmetic" and it's on their own dime. Lasik to correct myopia, yes; Lasik to give 20/10 vision to someone who's already at 20/20, no.

So my question: what does this pattern presuppose? It looks like these systems are committed to some notion of species-typical functioning as a normative baseline -- treat up to it, never beyond it. But I want to know whether that's really the implicit theory, whether it's coherent (statistically average functioning is honestly pretty bad -- most adults need reading glasses by 45, so is the baseline the statistical norm or some idealized design?), and crucially whether the practice actually needs that theory or whether several rival views of medicine or justice would license the exact same pattern. In other words: where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, such that the coverage decisions underdetermine the theory behind them?
