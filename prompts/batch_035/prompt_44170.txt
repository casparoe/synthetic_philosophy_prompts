Every other month I meet with a friend from my grad school days — she's now a legal philosopher who works on sentencing, and over the years our dinners have turned into a running game: I bring her cases, she rules on whether the agent acted freely and is blameworthy, and we try to reverse-engineer her theory. She refuses to name one. Her verdicts so far: the bank teller who opens the vault at gunpoint is not responsible for the loss; the driver who kills a pedestrian while texting is fully responsible; an employee who stays in a job she hates because quitting would leave her children without health insurance is responsible for staying (she called it an unwelcome necessity, but still her choice); the longtime smoker who sincerely wants to quit, has tried for years, and lights up anyway is responsible, but with diminished blame; the man who makes a damaging decision in a blackout state is not responsible; and a volunteer who, while under hypnosis, does something outrageous — and even says afterward that he'd have chosen it — is not responsible, which she said was the easiest call of the lot.

Next dinner, I'm planning to bring the case of a woman I'll call Maya. She joined a somewhat cultish self-improvement program at nineteen; twenty years later she is calm, reflective, articulate about her values, and successful by her own lights. The program taught her that family obligations beyond the immediate household are sentimental weakness, and on that basis she has refused all contact with her estranged sister, declining to help even when the sister was seriously ill. Maya explains the refusal exactly as the program taught it, appears entirely at peace with it, and has never shown any sign of wavering; the program still sends her a daily message reinforcing its outlook, which she reads each morning. She is responsive to reasons in the ordinary sense — she holds a demanding job, adapts plans when circumstances change, and once delayed a decision for a week to think it through. My question for you: will my friend rule that Maya is responsible for the refusal, and if so, with full or diminished blame?

I'm less interested in the answer than in the method. It seems to me at least two rules fit her track record equally well: one on which blameworthiness requires that the action issue from the agent's own judgment without another agent bypassing it at the time (which explains the gunpoint and hypnosis verdicts and would likely make Maya responsible, since nobody is bypassing anything when she declines the call); and one on which the relevant attitudes must be the agent's own in some stronger sense — made hers through reflection she isn't merely having maintained for her — which also fits the track record but seems to point the other way for Maya, since the daily message looks like the hypnosis verdict stretched over twenty years. So: lay out the candidate rules that fit her verdicts, say what each predicts for Maya, give your best guess about what my friend will say and why, and — since I get to design one more case for the dinner after that — tell me which further case would best force the candidate rules apart. For that purpose I've been considering a version of Maya in which the program went defunct fifteen years ago and she has maintained its outlook entirely on her own, but tell me if some other case would discriminate better.
