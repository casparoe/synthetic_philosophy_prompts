Retired geophysicist here -- thirty years of dating rocks and modelling groundwater, and now that I've got time on my hands I read philosophy and argue with my son-in-law, who does climate economics for a living. Last weekend we got into it about discount rates. He mentioned, almost in passing, that a nonzero "pure" rate of time preference is a live position in his field: welfare a hundred years out counts for less *simply because* it's a hundred years out -- not because future people will probably be richer, not because the policy might fail, just pure temporal distance. That struck me as insane, so I built an argument to refute it, and now the arguement worries me because it seems to prove way too much, and I can't find the flaw.

(1) In public planning, a harm of a given size landing in 2125 counts less than the same harm landing in 2025. (2) On the "pure time preference" view, the only thing distinguishing the two harms is how far in time each sits from the decision. (3) So the weighting rule must be that distance from the decision-maker is itself a reason to count welfare less -- that's what "pure" means here, no other justification is on offer. (4) But distance is distance; the temporal case has no feature the spatial case lacks. (5) So a rule that discounts temporally distant welfare must, on pain of arbitrariness, also discount spatially distant welfare -- a famine in Bangladesh should count for less in British planning than an identical famine in Britain. (6) Nobody accepts that, including the economists who discount the future. (7) Therefore pure time preference is false.

I suspect the problem is step (4) -- "distance is distance" feels like exactly the kind of slick move I'd have flunked a student for -- but when I try to articulate the disanalogy I keep coming back to things like "the future is different because we can affect it and then it affects us back," which sounds causal rather than about distance at all, and I'm not sure whether that rescues the discounters or just changes the subject from their actual view. There might also be a slide somewhere between (3) and (5) about whose distance is being measured -- the decider's temporal distance to the harm versus a country's spatial distance to it, which aren't obviously the same relation. Don't write me an essay. Give me a numbered list of exactly five items, no intro or conclusion, each one or two sentences: the five most promising places to attack the argument (a premise, an equivocation between two steps, a suppressed assumption -- whatever you think), and for each, what rejecting or repairing it costs you.
