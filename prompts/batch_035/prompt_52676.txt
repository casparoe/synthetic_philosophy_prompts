I'm about two years into a PhD dissertation on teleosemantics — the project of naturalizing mental content by grounding it in biological function, "proper performance" norms, that kind of thing — and my committee keeps telling me to stop surveying and actually pick a problem to dig into. Trouble is, the literature has a whole pile of open problems, and I genuinely can't tell which ones are substantive and which are just four decades of people talking past each other.

So here's what I want: a ranking of the main open problems in teleosemantics by how much they matter, with the ranking defended, not just presented. The candidates as I see them (add others if im missing some): (1) the indeterminacy worry — that biological function is too mushy to determine fine-grained content, since it's underdetermined how many times a mechanism must contribute to fitness, over what timescale and population, for its function to get fixed; (2) the malfunction/mismatch problem — how a system that mostly fails at its function (a frog snapping at BB pellets as much as at flies) can still determinately represent flies rather than small dark blobs; (3) the etiology question — whether we need selected-effects functions at all versus some non-etiological account, including what happens with novel traits or a swampman-type creature lacking the right selection history; (4) the scope problem — whether any of this scales from frog-snap-level content up to abstract human thought, structured propositions, misrepresentation about math, etc. For each, I want to know why it ranks where it does: is it upstream of the others, such that settling it would make the rest easier or moot? Is it tractable for someone doing armchair philosophy plus reading biology secondhand, or does it really need empirical work I can't do? And is it neglected, or has everyone already had their say and it's just circling?

I'd also like your honest take on a hunch of mine, which is that (1) and (2) aren't really two problems but two symptoms of one underlying issue — namely that function talk fixes a norm for success/failure but never fixes a granularity, so the same facts can be described at multiple levels of coarseness, and no level is privileged. If that's right, it would change my whole plan, since I'd be writing one chapter instead of two and could spend the saved year on the scope problem, which honestly scares me the most. For context: I've got about two and a half years left, no serious background in evolutionary biology, and a strong preference for problems I can make progress on with cases and arguments rather than by mastering a bunch of empirical literature. Lead with your bottom-line ranking in the very first sentence, then give the reasoning — no preamble, and don't restate my question back at me.
