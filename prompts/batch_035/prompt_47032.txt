For a graduate seminar paper on the unity of the proposition, I want the specification settled before anyone reaches for a theory. Write out the desiderata that any adequate account of propositional unity must meet, with the case or puzzle behind each, and mark which are non-negotiable and which merely reflect the prejudices of one camp.

The ones I can see so far, roughly, are these. The account must explain what distinguishes the proposition that Socrates is wise from the bare plurality Socrates, wisdom — the difference between something that can be true or false and something over which truth is not so much as defined. It must say something about the relation Bradley raised: if the unity of the complex a-R-b is explained by a further relation tying a, R, and b together, that further tie appears to need the same explanation, and an account that answers every request for unity with another unifier never delivers any. So the account must either bottom out in a genuine primitive or explain why no further unifier is needed at that point — and it must be explicit about which of the two it is doing rather than relabelling the problem at each level. It must handle false propositions symmetrically with true ones: a view that identifies propositions with facts, or grounds their unity in whatever makes them true, owes an account of the unity of the false ones, or an honest admission that they fall outside its scope. It must permit the logical operations — negation, conjunction, embedding under attitude verbs — since a proposition that could not be negated or disbelieved would not be a proposition. It must either distinguish the unity of a proposition from the unity of the corresponding state of affairs or explicitly collapse the two, rather than trading on the ambiguity. And it should not quietly identify the proposition with the sentence expressing it; if a view does so, it should say plainly what is lost by that.

Then tell me which subsets of these are jointly unsatisfiable — that is the part I most want, and I suspect the demand that unity be explained and the demand that the explanation not simply regress cannot both be met in full, in which case the list itself is a result. If so, say which requirement you would relax and at what cost. After that, score three familiar contenders briefly against the list: the structured account on which a primitive exemplification or instantiation ties the constituents into a complex; the deflationary view on which propositions are whatever that-clauses stand for, their unity inherited from the sentence; and the primitivist view that unity is simply a feature certain complexes have, not further explicable. Keep the scoring brief — the criteria are where I want the thinking.

Throughout, please state how confident you are in your claims and single out the point you are least sure of; I would expect it to be the joint-satisfiability judgement, but say so if it is not. Please write in British English.
