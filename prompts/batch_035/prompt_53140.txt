I teach a 200-level phil of language course, ~15 students, one 90-minute session in a regular classroom, and the only materials I can count on are paper, printed cards, and whatever happens to be lying around the room. We've just finished Putnam's Twin Earth. Next unit is externalism and self-knowledge, and instead of lecturing on it I want an in-class game where the students run into the slow-switch problem before anyone names it: the environment fixes what your words mean, so if it changes quietly enough, your words change meaning with no noticable difference on the inside — and you end up unable to tell, from memory alone, whether your past uses of a word meant what your present ones do.

Rough shape I'm imagining (improve on it freely): students learn a made-up word near the start of class, its referent fixed by something physical in the room (a jar, a card taped under their chair, whatever works). At some point some of them get switched without being told — and there has to be a cover activity for this, or the whole thing collapses the second someone notices a jar moved. Later they have to judge or bet on statements about past and present uses of the word, and they should hit the weirdness themselves first: they're positive they've meant the same thing all along, and then the reveal shows they can't have been. Only afterwards does anything get a name.

Please give me: rules and materials, a 90-minute timeline, a scoring scheme that doesn't involve real money, what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it (my guess is students just ask each other what the word means early on and blow the cover — how do I prevent that without making the rules feel fake?), and an honest note on which parts of externalism the game can't actually reproduce and I'll just have to assert in the debrief. I already suspect two gaps: a card that stipulates a referent isn't the same as a natural kind term getting it's meaning from the world, and the game's memory spans minutes rather than years. Tell me if there's more I'm not seeing.

One instructor, no TA. No lecture up front.
