I serve on the board of the housing cooperative where I've lived for six years, and last month's meeting left us with a dispute that has since spiraled into a philosophical argument I can neither resolve nor stop thinking about. One of our members installed solar panels on her roof a few years back; her neighbor, acting entirely within his rights under our bylaws, has begun an extension that will shade her panels for most of the afternoon. She maintains she has been harmed. He replies that he has merely declined to keep benefiting her — the afternoon sun was never hers to begin with, and she has no more been harmed than a shopkeeper is harmed when a competitor opens across the street. The board split evenly, which was embarrassing enough. But the real trouble is the argument my friend, a second-year law student with strong Millian sympathies, constructed from our stalemate, because I think if it works it doesn't just decide our solar dispute — it unravels the harm principle altogether.

Here is his argument, as I wrote it down afterward. (1) Mill's harm principle holds that coercing an individual is justified only to prevent harm to others, and only this can justify coercion. (2) Harm is comparative: to harm someone is to leave them worse off than they were entitled, or had a legitimate expectation, to be. (3) Entitlements and legitimate expectations are fixed by an independent body of norms — property rules, conventions, rights — which is precisely the sort of thing we consult the harm principle in order to evaluate. (4) So whenever we ask whether some conduct is harmful, the answer has already been settled by the background theory of entitlements, before the harm principle is consulted at all. (5) A principle whose application is wholly determined by some other set of norms does no independent justificatory work; it merely certifies in advance whatever those norms license. (6) Therefore the harm principle is not the substantive principle of political morality it presents itself as: either it is an empty formula that rubber-stamps whatever the background norms happen to permit — including, if those norms are illiberal, censorship and paternalism — or, to avoid that result, it must smuggle in its own substantive theory of what people are entitled to, in which case it is not the sole and fundamental test it claims to be. My friend concludes that when our board asks whether the neighbor harmed the panel owner, we are really just asking whose entitlements the bylaws created, and Mill's principle contributes nothing to the answer.

I find the conclusion too strong — the harm principle does at least seem to be doing something when it tells the board we may not fine a member for her religion or her diet, even if the bylaws somehow permitted it. But I cannot locate the defective step. Premise (2) seemed suspicious to me for a while, since we call death a harm without comparing it to anything, though I notice it's much harder to say whether lost sunlight is a harm without some baseline. And there may be something slippery between (5) and (6), though I can't articulate it: perhaps a principle can fail to settle cases on its own and still constrain which background norms are acceptable. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? For what it's worth, I would like to hold onto (1) and onto my sense that the principle genuinely does some work — but I want to know exactly what I have to pay, and to whom, if I do.
