Write a parody of a peer-review rejection letter, roughly 600–700 words, from the editor of the fictional *Journal of Strict Demarcation Studies*, declining a manuscript that reports a thirty-year field study documenting natural selection in a wild population of lizards. The editor is a scrupulous, almost apologetic Popperian: he is unstinting in his praise for the study's rigor, the author's patience, and the admirable behavior of the lizards themselves, and he rejects the paper anyway, on the grounds that natural selection, understood as the survival of the fittest, borders on tautology — the fittest being, in the end, those that survive — and that in its general formulation the theory functions as a metaphysical research programme rather than a testable scientific one. The humor should come from getting the philosophy right rather than from caricature: the editor should apply the demarcation criterion consistently and entirely in good faith, noting for instance that the study's apparent falsifications can always be absorbed by auxiliary hypotheses or by redescribing the observed trait as adaptive after the fact; comparing the paper's proposed falsifiers unfavorably with what he regards as the ad hoc immunizing stratagems that rescued psychoanalysis and various historicist doctrines from refutation; conceding, with visible discomfort, that on his criterion the Newtonian research programme would also have required delicate handling; and reminding the author, kindly but repeatedly, that calling a theory metaphysical is not an insult, since metaphysical research programmes may be heuristically fertile and rationally discussable even though they make no risky predictions. At some point he must acknowledge that Popper himself late in life announced that he had changed his mind about the testability of natural selection, and he should dispose of this awkward fact in character — for instance by judging the recantation insufficiently corroborated, or by speculating that the master's fallibility was here on display. He should offer, in all sincerity, to forward the manuscript to the journal's metaphysics section, which he insists operates a review process just as rigorous as the empirical one, only different. Close with a postscript in which the editor's own criterion catches up with him in some way — for example, he reports that a colleague has wagered that no conceivable rejection letter could count against the demarcation criterion, and that the editor, after due consideration, has judged this observation unfalsifiable, and therefore profound. Keep the tone deadpan and courteous throughout: the satire targets the demand for falsifiability as a boundary of science and the tautology objection to Darwinism, never the study, the author, or the lizards, all of whom the editor respects enormously.
