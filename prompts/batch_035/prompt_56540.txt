I write about science and ideas for a mid-size outlet, and I'm working on a piece about whether health systems should be allowed to factor personal responsibility into who gets treated first — think smokers or the obese getting bumped down the queue for elective surgery. Philosophically I guess this dumps you in luck egalitarianism: inequalities from choice are fine, inequalities from brute luck aren't. My problem is that almost everything people call a "choice" in health looks manufactured by things nobody chose — whether you started smoking tracks your parents, your age cohort, and tobacco marketing; addiction rewires the reward system; even adolescent brain development was running the show when most smokers started. So I want your actual numbers, and to keep it tight give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, no intro or conclusion: (1) your overall credence that it's sometimes permissible for a public health system to give lower priority to a condition the patient knowingly brought on; (2) your credence that at least one real-world case involves a choice genuine enough to ground responsibility even after you trace its full causal history back through addiction, marketing exposure, and the age at which the habit formed; (3) your credence that, granting such a genuine choice, responsibility is enough to justify priority differences in a system funded by compulsory insurance rather than by the patient directly; (4) your credence that any rule a health system could actually administer at the bedside would track genuine choice well enough to avoid mostly punishing bad luck; and (5) which of those four moves the overall number the most, and one piece of evidence or argument that would shift it.
