I'm taking a graduate seminar on free will, and for my final paper I want to work out what Immanuel Kant would have made of Frankfurt cases — the thought experiments, introduced by Harry Frankfurt in his 1969 paper on alternate possibilities, in which someone cannot do otherwise (because a counterfactual intervener would have prevented any alternative) yet seems morally responsible for what they did. The received answer is that Kant, as an incompatibilist about freedom and moral responsibility, would simply have rejected the cases: he was scathing about Leibnizian compatibilism, mocking the idea that freedom could be secured by mere spontaneity of action in the manner of a mechanical turnspit carrying out its motions once wound up, and dismissing such reconciliations as a subterfuge. On the standard reading, if you show Kant an agent who could not have done otherwise, he tells you that agent isn't free in the sense required for imputation, and no intuition-pumping about counterfactual interveners will move him.

But I think there are at least two wrinkles that make the prediction less obvious, and I'd like your help developing them. First, Kant had his own solution to a structurally similar problem: divine foreknowledge. If God infallibly knows what I will do, it seems I cannot do otherwise — yet Kant never took this as an excuse for wrongdoing, arguing instead that God's knowledge is not an event in my causal history and does not determine me in time. That is strikingly close to the Frankfurtian point that a counterfactual intervener who never actually intervenes is not part of what produces my action. Second, Kant's account of moral worth seems to detach appraisal from alternatives in another way: the good will would shine like a jewel even if it accomplished nothing at all, and an action's moral worth depends on its maxim, not on what else the agent might have done. Might Kant, then, concede that responsibility does not require alternative empirical possibilities, while insisting that this is only because freedom was never an empirical matter in the first place — that the "can" in "ought implies can" concerns the agent's standing as an intelligible cause rather than the availability of alternate empirical paths? Or would he instead see Frankfurt's positive, hierarchical account of the will — freedom as alignment between first-order desires and second-order volitions — as yet another piece of empirical psychology masquerading as freedom, and hence as the turnspit fallacy all over again, just dressed in more modern clothes?

Please write a substantive response (roughly essay length) that predicts Kant's reaction to Frankfurt cases. Develop both horns: the strongest case that Kant would treat Frankfurt's conclusion as, in effect, a corollary of his own transcendental idealism, and the strongest case that he would reject it wholesale. Address specifically how the Wille/Willkühr distinction might bear on whether Frankfurt's second-order-desires picture could count as genuine practical self-determination for Kant, and whether "ought implies can" really does commit him to the principle of alternative possibilities. You are, of course, free to conclude that the question is in some way ill-posed — e.g., because Frankfurt cases concern responsibility while Kant's central notion is transcendental freedom, and these come apart — but if you take that line, explain what the resulting disagreement between Kant and Frankfurt would actually be about. No citations or references are needed beyond what you can reconstruct from the philosophical content itself.
