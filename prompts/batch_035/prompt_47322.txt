I sit on the animal care and use committee at a mid-size university (the IACUC, if your american about it), and I've been tasked with drafting guidance on how we decide the level of welfare scrutiny a protocol gets based on how likely the animals in it are to be sentient — likely to actually experience pain, fear, distress, not just detect and respond to harmful stuff. The committee meets monthly, we see maybe 15 protocols a meeting, and the scientists on it hate philosophy and will not read anything longer than a page, but they WILL follow a checklist. Write me a decision procedure they can actually run in about twenty minutes per protocol.

Some background on what the procedure has to grapple with, so you know which distinctions have to survive the simplification. We keep getting stuck on what you could call the distribution question: is sentience spread densely across the animal kingdom — most animals with any kind of nervous system get some welfare standing — or sparsely, only showing up in a few cognitively complex lineages? Different members default to different answers and the argument eats the whole meeting. Related to that: the distinction between nociception (detecting and reacting to noxious stimuli, which basically any animal can do) and the felt experience of pain, which our checklist has to somehow operationalize without turning into a neuroscience seminar. And there's the precautionary problem: the costs of a false negative (treating a sentient animal as insentient) versus a false positive (heavy paperwork and delays for something that feels nothing) aren't symmetric, but the committee disagrees about how much asymmetry to bake in, and that part is a values question, not an evidence one, so the procedure needs to hand that back to us rather than decide it.

What I want: a procedure with the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where it terminates in a verdict (full review / moderate safeguards / basically exempt), and where it has to stop and hand things to the committee because the remaining question is one of values. Then run it on three protocols: (a) invasive nerve recordings in octopuses, (b) catch-and-release tagging of wild fish with no other procedures attached, and (c) a drosophila genetics study that includes heat-stress assays where flies are held at temperatures that impair them. At least one of these should come out in a way I won't like — and flag it, don't fudge it to make me comfortable. And then tell me what the procedure can't do: where it will misfire, and what it will never settle no matter how well it's built.

One more constraint: keep the philosophy out of the deliverable where you can. If a step can only be run correctly by someone who knows what phenomenal conciousness means, it's probably badly designed.
