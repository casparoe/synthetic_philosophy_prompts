I follow weather forecasts way too closely as a hobby (model watching, storm chaser forums, the whole deal) and something has been buging me for years. The forecast always comes out as one number: 40% chance of rain tomorrow. But I know from the forums that some days that 40% comes from all the models agreeing on a kind of meh, could-go-either-way day, and other days half the models say we get absolutely soaked and the other half say it stays bone dry and sunny, and the average happens to work out to 40% too. Same number on my phone app, completely different situations. My gut says those two days are not the same risk at all — on the second kind of day what I really want to know is that the situation itself is unsettled, not just that rain is unlikely-ish.

I tried reading a bit of philosophy of probability on my own (no background at all, be gentle lol) and somewhere I picked up the idea that not everyone thinks probabilities have to be exact single numbers in the first place — that there's a view where you're allowed to only pin something down to a range, like somewhere between 20 and 60 percent, when the evidence is genuinely split like that. So heres my question: what does the whole practice of publishing one probability per day actually presuppose? Does it need to assume every chance situation has one true sharp number, or is it more like a formatting convention that would be compatable with the range view anyway? And if forecasters took the range view seriously, what would actually have to change — would my app show a fuzzier number on split-model days, and would that even help, or do people just read "somewhere between 10 and 70" as "about 40" and we're back where we started? I'd love it if you could tell me what the practice strictly requires versus what it just does out of habit.
