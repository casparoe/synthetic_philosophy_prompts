I'm a grad student in philosophy of mind, and for about two years now a running argument in my cohort about split-brain patients has been totally static, and I've started to suspect the problem isn't the evidence, its the question itself. So what I want is a diagnosis of the disagreement, not a verdict on who's right.

Here's the shape of it. On one side there's the two-subjects reading: after the corpus callosum is severed you've got two conscious streams, and in a sense the pre-surgery person is gone — the old experiments where one hemisphere reports something the other denies knowing anything about, the hand that contradicts the other hand, that whole body of work from Sperry and Gazzaniga onwards, gets read as straightforward evidence of two experiencing subjects. On the other side there's the one-subject camp: they point out that the patients don't live like two people — they walk, talk, and carry a single autobiographical sense of themselves — and they lean on some newer experimental work suggesting both hemispheres stay aware of alot more of the same things than the classic stories implied, so the disconnection shows up as an integration failure inside one person rather than a multiplication of selves. And then there's a third group, smaller and smugger, who say the question "one mind or two?" is just confused, because counting minds assumes minds are the kind of thing that come in integer quantities, and at that point they fold their arms.

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the two main camps aren't disagreeing about the neuroscience at all — they basically accept the same data — but about which relation to experience is the one that matters for being a subject. The two-subjects people weight synchronic unity of content (two non-overlapping awarenesses right now means two subjects right now), the one-subject people weight diachronic and practical unity (one continuous agent navigating the world over time), and the smug third group is right that the counting question is ill-posed but wrong to think that settles anything, because the interesting question then becomes which of those two relations we should care about and why, and nobody ever addresses that head-on.

So: is the impasse verbal, is it a hidden normative question about what makes a life matter wearing a metaphysical costume, or are the sides genuinely answering different questions under one heading? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here — a sharper distinction, some further kind of experiment, a change of question — or is this a case where no progress should be expected and I should just pick a side and say why? I'd honestly also accept "your third group is more right than you're allowing" if you can actually argue it.
