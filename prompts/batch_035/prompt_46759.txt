I build quantitative risk models for industrial equipment, and a recurring embarrassment in that work is the unobserved failure mode: a component that has never failed in the field, operated in a new regime, where the analysis nonetheless requires a numerical probability. Analysts on my team reach for some version of spreading probability evenly over their ignorance, and they get different answers depending on which quantity they spread it over — uniform over the failure rate, or over its logarithm, or over the mean time between failures — and I have stopped believing that any of them can justify their particular choice. Before I read any more about the principle of indifference, I want the specification it is supposed to be measured against. Write me the desiderata: what any rule for assigning probabilities in a state of ignorance has to deliver, with the case or consideration behind each requirement.

At minimum, the list should say something definite about the following: whether the rule must yield a unique answer at all, or whether a range or set of distributions counts as a legitimate output; whether it must give the same answer under reparameterization of the variable and under different but equivalent descriptions of the same setup; how it must treat problems with genuine physical symmetries — if the symmetry of a die forces one-sixth on each face, the rule had better return that, and if the problem has no such symmetry, the rule had better not pretend it does; what to do about the chord problem, where drawing a random chord in a circle by three equally natural procedures gives 1/2, 1/3, and 1/4; and how to handle the water-and-wine case, where spreading evenly over the volume of one constituent gives a different answer than spreading evenly over the ratio between the two. Then there are the requirements my use case imposes: the output must be usable downstream in a risk model — a number or a distribution, not a lecture on the philosophy of probability — and it must not manufacture information, since in my line of work a number that merely looks informed is actively dangerous.

For each desideratum, say what makes it a genuine requirement rather than a prejudice of one camp, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are negotiable for my purposes. If some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, say so and identify the subset — that is the part I most want to see, because it would tell me whether the disagreement on my team is substantive or just everyone violating the same constraint in different directions. Then, briefly, hold the main contenders up against the list: naive indifference over a chosen partition; Jaynes-style invariance under transformation groups; maximum entropy relative to stated constraints; reference priors; replacing the single distribution with a set of them; and the deflationary view that ignorance simply does not determine probabilities, so the demand for a rule rests on a mistake. Keep the scoring to a short paragraph — the list is the deliverable, and the scoring is only there so I can see which requirements do the most exclusion.
