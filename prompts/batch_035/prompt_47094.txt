My philosophy TA has this habit of ending arguments by saying that truth is whatever works. Last week someone asked whether it's actually true that there were an even number of stars in some distant galaxy (the question came up because we were arguing about whether every statement has to be determinately true or false, even one no one could ever check), and the TA just sort of waved his hand and said the question was empty, because a claim like that doesn't "work" for anyone, so there's no fact there. A couple of days later, someone asked whether we can really say our current physics is true if a better theory might completely replace it in a hundred years, and there the slogan apparently did the opposite — he said our physics counts as true insofar as it works for us now. So in one case "what works" made a fact go away, and in another it made a fact come and go with our theories. I pointed this out and he said I was being obtuse, which, maybe! But I genuinely can't tell what principle he's using.

I know this is supposed to be pragmatism, and I've been told Peirce and James meant pretty different things by talk like this, something about inquiry converging in the long run versus what's good for the believer, but I can't reconstruct the details well enough to tell which one (if either) my TA is running, or whether he's just mushing them together. Also worth noting he brought up religion at some point — I think the line was that believing in God could be "true for you" if that belief makes your life go better — and the rest of the class nodded along while I sat there wondering if this licenses wishful thinking flat out.

So here's what I want, and I want it as a list I can bring to seminar: lay out the different precise claims that "truth is what works" could actually be expressing, ordered from the most trivially true to the most radical. For each one, say whether it's a platitude, whether it's substantive but defensible, or whether it's flatly false, and then say which readings would support (a) dismissing the distant-galaxy question the way he did, and (b) calling our current physics true-for-now, and (c) the "true for you" move about God. My suspicion is that no single reading does all three jobs, but I'd like to know which reading he'd have to be running for each, and which of the dinosaur-type cases (claims with no practical upshot either way) would genuinely separate the readings from each other. Also, if the Peirce-style reading really is different in kind from the James-style one, flag where on the list each of them would sit, so I can ask him which one he thinks he's defending.
