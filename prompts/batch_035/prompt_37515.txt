At a family dinner a few weeks back the conversation drifted onto human rights, and my cousin's husband kept deploying this one phrase like a trump card. My aunt said that whatever you think of certain governments, locking people up over their sexual orientation is wrong, full stop. He said 'that's a Western value — you're taking your own morality and dressing it up as universal.' When she pushed back he said the whole idea of universal rights got its modern form in Europe a couple centuries ago, that other civilisations have their own goods, and that acting like ours are everyone's is just cultural imperialism with a friendly face. She tried 'arithmetic was also invented in a particular place, and it's still true everywhere,' and he waved it off with 'values aren't math, they only have force inside a tradition that shaped you.' Neither moved an inch, and I honestly couldnt tell which parts of his claim were doing the work. It's been bugging me since.

Can you lay out the distinct precise theses 'Western values' might actually be expressing? I want a menu I can use next time this comes up. Some readings I can half see already: values that most Westerners happen to hold; values that originated in the West; values such that being Western *entails* holding them (so a European who wants censorship back isn't really 'Western'?); values that only have grip inside Western traditions and can't be transplanted; values whose export is inherently coercive given how they arrived (colonialism etc.). But I can't tell which of these are trivial, which are substantive and defensible, which are just false — and, the part I care about most, which ones would actually support the move he kept making with the phrase, i.e. from 'Western' to 'not binding on anyone else' or 'just one civilisation's preference among many'. For each reading, say whether it gets him that conclusion or whether it's a plittle stronger than what he needs.

Two more things. First: is 'the West' even a stable referent here? If the category has to stretch from ancient Greece through medieval Christendom to the Enlightenment to modern secular liberalism, what's left over that excludes, say, the Islamic philosophers who kept Aristotle alive, or Japan? And does it matter for his argument — i.e., if the referent is fuzzy, does that weaken the slogan or is he fine? Second, was my aunt's arithmetic analogy any good, or did it trade one confusion for another? Definately want to be better armed next holiday season.
