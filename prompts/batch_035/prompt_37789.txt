I edit a tiny print zine about prediction and forecasting (maybe 400 subscribers: mostly actuaries, a few statisticians, and one guy who mails in a hand-written letter about the Doomsday argument every single issue). For the next issue I want a satirical advice column, and since about half the letters I get are complaints about reference classes, I want the column built around that. The setup: a fictional country where every citizen is legally required to keep a personal longevity forecast on file with the Bureau, and the Bureau derives it by Copernican-style reasoning — you're told you're probably neither very early nor very late in your reference class's lifespan, so your remaining time gets estimated from where you fall in the class, which means everything hangs on which class the clerk selects. The Bureau has abolished ordinary actuarial life tables on the grounds that they were just reference classes with better branding. The column should print three or four short letters addressed to "Dear Actuary," each with a deadpan reply, around 600-750 words total.

The letters should each turn on a real feature of this kind of reasoning, not an invented one. One from a 63-year-old trying to get a favorable mortgage rate, asking to be refiled under "entities that have already survived sixty-three years" — and the actuary's reply should grant that this is tempting but explain, as routine Bureau policy, that the whole point of the method is to use only where you find yourself now, since conditioning on what you already know about your own past is exactly the bias it refuses. One from my recurring Doomsday correspondent, who was filed under "all humans who will ever live," got doom fairly soon, then refiled under "entities that have lasted as long as humanity has" and got a forecast different by orders of magnitude, and wants to know which is binding — the reply should treat this as a mundane administrative question, no criterion privileges either class, so the Bureau's policy is something deadpan and absurd but fair (issue both and let the citizen choose, or average them, or bill him twice). Somewhere there should be a fine-print policy update gesturing at the self-indication idea — the Bureau tried counting each citizen into every class in proportion to the class's size and doom quietly moved off the books — presented as a procedural note, not as a resolution anyone celebrates. And the last letter should just ask which class is the right one; the honest ending is that the actuary doesn't know, because that's genuinely where the reference class problem still stands, but she has to stay in character — maybe offering the correspondent the class "entities currently being classified" as a courtesy, since it makes everyone's forecast identical and is therefore economical.

Keep it deadpan throughout, no winking at the reader, and don't let the column resolve the problem unless the resolution is itself the joke. The humor has to come from the actual features: arbitrariness of class choice, divergence by orders of magnitude between equally licensed classes, observer-selection effects, the refusal to condition on known facts. Someone who knows this literature should find it fair, and someone who doesn't should still find it funny. Please end the whole piece with a single line starting "Summary:" — one plain sentence, no jokes, stating what the column has actually established about refrence-class forecasting. (Also if you could keep the actuary's voice consistent across the replies, our copy editor will owe you a favor, since she still hasnt forgiven me for the limerick issue.)
