A friend of mine has spent the past year reading Camus, initially as background for a jurisprudence seminar on punishment and then, as she puts it, for real. She started with his long essay against the guillotine, went on to the play he built out of the 1905 assassination of a Russian grand duke, and has now assembled what amounts to a casebook of verdicts on killing, which she delivers with unnerving confidence. So far I have her down as follows. The state executing a murderer after a fair trial, with full procedural protection: never justified, not even for the most appalling crimes, and she thinks the deterrence argument is a dodge. The revolutionary in the play, who throws his bomb at the grand duke's carriage only after making sure the children aren't riding with him, and who then goes to the gallows without asking anyone's pardon: justified. The same man refusing to kill when the children are in the carriage, even though it means betraying the conspiracy: right, and she treats it as the hinge of the whole thing. Ordinary suicide: condemned flatly, as desertion from the community of the living. Killing someone who is about to kill you, right now, with no way out: permissible, though she says it with visible reluctance. And she keeps returning to one thread from the essay: that a society has no right to kill if it can't bring itself to look at what it's doing, that the whole apparatus exists to keep the deed hidden and the executioner anonymous.

Three new cases have come up, and I want to place bets before she rules, but I've discovered I can't, because I can't figure out what rule she's actually running. Case one: a government carries out a targeted drone strike on a general who is directing an ongoing campaign of massacre, the strike is approved through layers of legal review, no bystanders are nearby, and the pilot who fires sits in a shipping container on another continent, at no personal risk whatsoever, before driving home for dinner. Case two: a friend of hers, terminally ill and in constant pain, asks for a lethal dose of medication, administers it herself, and does so only after months of reflection and repeated, unwavering requests. Case three: a state abolishes the hired executioner and instead conducts executions in public, performed by a citizen selected by lot, with the option to decline replaced only by a fresh draw -- so that the killing is visible, personal, and done in the citizenry's own name.

Predict her verdicts on the three cases. More importantly, tell me which rules are consistent with her record so far, since I can see at least two that fit everything she's said -- one under which killing is permissible only when the killer stakes his own life and acts in his own name, and one under which the distinction that matters is not the killer but the target: punishment is never a reason to kill, while resisting ongoing tyranny sometimes is -- and these come apart on the drone strike. Are there other rules I'm missing that fit her record equally well, and which of the three cases does the best job of separating them? And if her verdicts on the three cases still leave the rules underdetermined, describe the further case that would finally force her hand.
