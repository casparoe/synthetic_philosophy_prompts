I clerk for one of the judges on our post-transition review commission — since the regime fell, we're the tribunal that goes through the old regime's laws and figures out what to do with the prosecutions and convictions that happened under them. Our precedents so far:

1. The secret decrees. Never gazetted, circulated only as internal ministry memos, people convicted and sentenced under them anyway. Held: not law at all. The officials who enforced them got no legality defense and the convictions were vacated.
2. The "insulting the Leader" statute. Published, clear, prospective, enforced through the ordinary courts in the ordinary way. Held: valid law at the time — wicked, but law. So the officers who enforced it in the routine course couldn't be prosecuted for it, though we vacated the convictions going forward under the new bill of rights. This one went 4–3, and the dissent's position was basically that a law this rotten stops being law.
3. The decree against the Hallor family. Named a single family and stripped them of citizenship. Held: not law — law has to be general, you can't have a statute aimed at specific people.
4. The retroactive tax statute. Held: not law, law has to be prospective.

Now the new case, and its a nasty one: the Flogging Amendment from the regime's last years. Published in the gazette, crystal clear, prospective, and general on its face: any member of the Marsh community convicted of a misdemeanor shall be sentenced to public flogging, where everyone else got a fine or short jail term. The regime's courts applied it around 300 times. Two questions before us: (a) can we prosecute the prison officers who carried out the floggings, whose defense is that they were applying valid law just like the insult-statute officers, and (b) do we vacate the old convictions as void, or leave them standing and pardon?

So: what should we do if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Which precedent is the closest analog — the insult statute, which this matches formally, or the secret decrees, which it matches in how its leaving our docket? And be honest about what we'd be committing to either way. If we call this law, we're saying the line between law and not-law gets drawn entirely by the formal stuff — publicity, generality, prospectivity, clarity — and the content never disqualifies, no matter how disgusting, as long as its drafted properly. If we say its not law, we owe an answer for what the insult statute had that this lacked, since formally they're twins. My suspicion is our precedents genuinely underdetermine this, so I'd also like to hear: what principle is each choice adopting, is there a reading that makes the insult statute and this one come out differently without just pointing at how bad each one is, and what case would we want to have on the books to make a case like this easy next time?
