I help run a workshop on animal ethics + decision theory, about 10 people (grad students and a couple of researchers, so they can handle expected-value talk fine but they're not all philosophers). Two participants have been fighting for months about insect welfare and it's getting old.

One is a scale-argument person. Their line: even if the probability that any given insect has valenced experience is low, and even if the moral weight per insect is small, you multiply by roughly 10^19 animals alive at any moment and something like a trillion deaths per day, and insects swamp every other animal-cause in expectation. So we should be putting real money and research effort into this.

The other is a skeptic. They think the probability that anything morally significant happens inside a housefly is genuinely tiny (they keep saying nociception without subjective experience can do all the behavioral work evolution needs), and they accuse the first person of pseudo-precision — multiplying a made-up point estimate by 10^19 and calling the product a finding. They also suspect the whole thing is Pascal-mugging territory: tiny probability times astronomical stakes gets you to "must act" no matter what the evidence ever does.

Instead of letting them argue past each other for another session, we're making them do a formal adversarial collaboration. Write the joint statement they should come out with. It needs:

- a numbered section of claims both can sign — including about the actual behavioral literature (motivational trade-off experiments, analgesia/self-administration designs, the bumblebees rolling balls around apparently for play), and about what things like neuron counts and missing brain structures do and don't settle
- the precise disagreements, phrased so each side would accept the wording of their own view — I want the skeptic to read the disagreement section and say "yes, that's my position"
- the cruxes. I'd guess one is how much the trade-off/analgesia experiments should move the probability of sentience, one is whether you're allowed to multiply through a whole credence distribution versus a point estimate, and one is what "worth prioritizing" even means when expected value is astronomically large but the practical action-guidance is thin
- for each crux: a concrete experiment, observation, or argument both agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance, so we can check later who called it
- and if part of the fight is verbal (e.g., "insects matter morally" vs "insects should be near the top of the funding stack" — these come apart once you factor in tractability and opportunity cost), say which part and why

Format: numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table with the cruxes as rows and predictions as columns. Keep it honest — if the scale person has to concede something (like treating a 10% credence as if it were knowledge, or admitting that expected value alone doesn't tell you where to send money tomorrow), and the skeptic has to concede something (like that "we probably can't tell" is different from "probably no"), the document should come out and say so. Around 1500-2000 words for the statement itself.
