I'm taking a seminar on ethical theory that has spent the last few weeks on consequentialism's epistemic problems, and I've chosen Frank Capra's It's a Wonderful Life as the subject of my final paper. I want to read the film specifically through the problem of cluelessness, and I'd like your help working out whether the reading holds up.

Here is the idea I want to pressure-test. Consequentialists are accused of being clueless: an agent cannot know the remote, ripple effects of her actions, and because those effects plausibly swamp the foreseeable ones, she cannot know even the sign of the difference her choices make to total value. What is striking about the film is that it stages the exact fantasy the cluelessness literature implies is unavailable: Clarence the angel shows George Bailey the counterfactual world in which he never existed, giving him precisely the God's-eye comparison a human consequentialist can never have. So the film seems to dramatize cluelessness by temporarily suspending it — George despairs and attempts suicide precisely because, like any of us, he cannot see the counterfactual, and the plot's resolution is the arrival of the information consequentialism says is unobtainable.

My questions are these. First, does the angel's demonstration actually cure George's cluelessness, or does it merely shrink it? Clarence shows George the immediate effects of his absence on Bedford Falls — Harry's death as a boy, the men on the transport who die without Harry as a naval pilot, the fates of Martini, Violet, Ernie, George's mother — but the demonstration is conspicuously truncated. It never shows the descendants of the men Harry saved, the remote effects of the Bailey Building and Loan across generations, or anything beyond the town and the wartime moment. Even the angelic oracle seems to stop at the first or second ripple, which suggests the film quietly concedes that full counterfactual knowledge is impossible even in principle for a finite mind — or maybe that it's dramatically useless, since an unbounded ripple display could not support the moral conclusion the film wants.

Second, I'm unsure whether the ending vindicates consequentialism at all. George is saved not by his own calculus but by the debts of friendship and community accumulated over a lifetime, and the climax — the townspeople covering the missing eight thousand dollars — reads more like a point about keeping faith with people than about good outcomes. Is the film best read as an argument for objective consequentialism (George's life was in fact good because of what it caused, even though he couldn't know it), or does its emotional payoff actually rest on agent-relative considerations — relationships, promises, being remembered — that a consequentialist would have to translate into the currency of outcomes at some cost?

What I want from you is a reading that takes both the film and the philosophical problem seriously: where the film genuinely illuminates cluelessness, where it cheats to get its happy ending, and whether a defender of consequentialism should claim the film or concede it to the other side. Please also tell me the strongest objection to my framing — for instance, that Pottersville is less a counterfactual projection than a moral hallucination designed to manipulate George, so that what the film is really about is self-evaluation under despair rather than the epistemology of consequences. Finally, if there is something the film shows about cluelessness that the standard arguments don't — some way the problem feels from the inside, lived rather than formalized — I'd like that articulated, since that difference is what I want my paper's conclusion to be about.
