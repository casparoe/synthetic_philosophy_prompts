You are a political philosopher with a gift for deadpan satire, and I'd like you to write a piece of sustained irony for a mock publication. The venue is the monthly newsletter of a fictional, fully privatized incorporated town — invent a name for it — whose founding charter enshrines the entitlement theory of justice (Robert Nozick's Anarchy, State, and Utopia, 1974) as a kind of civic religion. There are no taxes in the town, only voluntary fees for services; the newsletter carries a syndicated advice column, "Dear Curator," in which a resident philosopher answers letters from townspeople. Write roughly 700 words of that column: a header, then three or four letters, each followed by a reply.

The letters should raise everyday quandaries — a disputed fence line, a neighbor charging hikers to cross his land, a resident wondering whether the association's mandatory landscaping fee is really a tax by another name, complaints that the distribution of holdings has become lopsided after the local basketball star was paid a small sum from every home-game ticket. The humor has to come from the actual structure of the theory, so that someone who has read the book laughs rather than winces. In particular: the theory is historical, not patterned, so the Curator must remain serenely unmoved by any complaint about the *shape* of the distribution — inequality, need, and desert are simply not the kind of facts that could make a holding unjust, and a resident who writes in about the top one percent should be told so, gently, as if correcting a category error. On taxation, the Curator holds that taking earnings from labor is morally on a par with making someone work, and must find some principled way to distinguish the association's fees from a tax that his own theory condemns — let him do it, and let the reader watch him do it. Appropriation is legitimate only under the Lockean proviso, that taking something may not leave others worse off than they would have been had it remained unowned, and the Curator should be shown actually applying this test to some fresh appropriation, with visible care.

And then there is rectification, which is where I want the deepest joke to sit. The town's history is not clean: choice lots were seized from the original homesteaders during the founding, and their descendants are now tenants of the founder's grandson. Because the theory is historical, past injustice propagates forward — every later transfer from a tainted holding is itself unjust, so the present distribution is quietly rotten, and no simple appeal to the virtues of free exchange can launder it. The Curator cannot wave this away by appealing to what would be best for the community going forward, since end-state considerations do no work in his theory; but the theory also gives him almost nothing to say about what rectification would actually require. Let him acknowledge this in the tone of a man noting a scheduling difficulty: the anomaly is logged, the theory stands, next letter please.

Before writing, think through which real features of the entitlement theory each letter will turn on, and sketch the replies so that each one gets the theory right; the target of the satire is the theory and its reasoning, never a strawman of them. The Curator must stay internally consistent throughout — no slide into consequentialism, no winking at the reader. The irony should be legible from the content alone: a reader who knows the position being inhabited should feel its genuine attractions and its genuine pressure points, and by the end should be left facing the real question of whether a theory of justice can survive the part of justice it cannot specify.
