My housemate has been reading Paul Churchland and has distilled the case for eliminative materialism into four steps. I have walked through it a dozen times; each step still looks defensible to me on its own, yet the same reasoning, applied elsewhere, delivers conclusions I regard as absurd. I would like a diagnosis.

(1) The entities a theory posits exist only if that theory is at least approximately true within its home domain. He presents this as uncontroversial: phlogiston does not exist because phlogiston theory was not even approximately true of combustion, whereas terms that belonged to roughly correct theories survived theory change as terms for real things.

(2) Beliefs and desires are posits of folk psychology — the everyday framework by which we explain and predict one another's behavior by attributing contentful states.

(3) Folk psychology is not approximately true. The supporting case: it offers no account of sleep, memory, learning, mental illness, or imagination; its central principle — that people act so as to satisfy their desires in light of their beliefs — admits exceptions no one can enumerate in advance; and it has seen no substantive development in all of recorded history, which he says is the signature not of a roughly correct theory but of a moribund one.

(4) Therefore beliefs and desires do not exist.

Here is my problem. If I continue in his register:

(5) Folk biology, which placed whales among the fish and treated species as fixed, is not approximately true by the standard applied in (3) — it misclassified whole orders of organisms, embedded everything in a false picture of fixity, and stagnated equally long.

(6) So fish do not exist — and, running the parallel against folk physics, neither do sunrises or solid objects.

He anticipates this objection and answers that folk psychology is like demonology rather than folk taxonomy: demonology was false through and through, its entire explanatory machinery wrong, whereas pre-scientific fish-sorting got most classifications right and needed only boundary corrections. But I cannot see how to apply the distinction between false-through-and-through and right-in-outline without begging the question. Demonology got some things right too — the episodes it pathologized do cluster, remit, and call for explanation — and folk psychology arguably tracks the gross patterns of human action quite reliably. If the weighting of right calls against wrong ones is left to intuition, then the intuition that fish exist is doing all the work, and the intuition that beliefs exist should get a vote as well.

Where I stand: I have no deep attachment to beliefs and desires, and if (4) is the price of the argument I will consider paying it. What I cannot accept is (6), and I also cannot accept the obvious escape of rejecting (1) outright, since (1) is what makes elimination possible at all — I want to keep saying that phlogiston never existed, and that the chemists who dropped it were responding to how the world is rather than merely changing the subject. So: which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost? If your diagnosis rests on a disanalogy between the folk-psychology case and the fish case, state it in terms that don't simply restate the conclusion; and if you think the flaw is in how (3) supports itself, say why the friend's three complaints — silence on neighboring phenomena, exception-ridden laws, stagnation — don't show what he thinks they show.
