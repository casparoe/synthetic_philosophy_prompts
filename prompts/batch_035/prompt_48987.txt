In my economic history seminar we have spent most of the term on a question that never seems to get resolved: can culture be a cause of economic outcomes? We started with Weber's thesis that Protestantism, and especially its anxiety about salvation, helped produce the spirit of capitalism, then read critics who said the causality runs the other way, that commerce produced the theology, and then moved on to the bigger fight about why northwest Europe industrialized first, where some people appeal to cultural traits like thrift, trust, or work discipline and others insist on geography, coal, colonies, and factor prices. The argument has the shape of a stalemate: both sides concede that culture and material conditions co-vary, both can tell a plausible story in their preferred direction, and after a century of this nobody seems to have moved. My working hypothesis is that it is not really an empirical dispute at all, but a disagreement about what an explanation of a large economic event has to look like — whether citing a shared value system explains anything, or merely re-describes the pattern that needs explaining, and whether that standard is itself something the two sides differ over. But I am not sure whether that is right or whether there is a genuine factual question hiding underneath, perhaps about whether cultural values persist and transmit independently of the material conditions that produced them. Could you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is — substantive, verbal, or two camps answering different questions under one heading — using what the cultural and the materialist sides each actually claim, and then tell me honestly whether anything (a natural experiment, a sharper definition of a cultural trait, a distinction between explaining an event and explaining its persistence) could count as progress, or whether the question is ill-posed and should be reformed?
