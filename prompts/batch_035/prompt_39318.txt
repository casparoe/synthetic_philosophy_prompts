I've settled into a position on whose values an aligned AI system should serve, and what I want is a map of its exposure rather than further support for it. The position: each AI system should be aligned to the values of its principal — the individual who deploys it — with no attempt to aggregate across principals, because any aggregation function (majoritarian, broadly utilitarian, bargaining-theoretic) smuggles in a substantive ranking of ways of life that a neutral technology has no business imposing, in roughly the way a liberal state refuses to rank its citizens' conceptions of the good. Call it one-principal alignment. On this view, the fact that your neighbor's assistant serves values you find abhorrent is no more your business than the fact that your neighbor worships at the wrong church.

What would count against it? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I'd like the following addressed in particular. First, whether the position merely relocates the aggregation problem inside the principal: a single person's values are themselves a messy mixture of first- and second-order preferences, adaptive preferences, and diachronic change, so "the values of the principal" is not a ready-made input but the output of some interpretation or filtering procedure — and it's not obvious that this inner procedure can be neutral in the way the outer refusal to aggregate is supposed to be. Second, externalities: cases where individually aligned systems interacting in a market or an information ecosystem produce collective outcomes that no principal endorses, and whether that shows the individual-alignment framing misdescribes what the technology actually is. Third, whether liberal neutrality is the right analogy at all — a state governs people who can contest and replace it, whereas an assistant acts on one person's behalf in a world full of third parties who can do neither — and where exactly that disanalogy bites. Fourth, principals whose values include the domination of others: does excluding them commit me to precisely the substantive ranking I claimed to avoid, or is there a principled line between neutrality and what the state analogy would call rights protections? Fifth, whether the principal is even the right unit: children, people with severe cognitive impairments, corporate principals, and a user's own future self all strain the notion that there is one person whose values are simply there to be served. For each defeater, tell me what a committed defender of one-principal alignment would concede as counting against it, what they would reframe, and what they could only deny at the cost of the position's original motivation. Finally, give an honest verdict on whether the position has already been insulated: if the slide from "each person's values" to "each person's suitably processed values" quietly does the work I refused to let the outer aggregation do, I want to be told that plainly.
