I'm reading this pop-sci book on the evolution of minds and it has a chapter that keeps coming back to what it calls the "strange inversion of reasoning" that Darwin and Turing both pulled off. I get the general idea and it's pretty persuasive, but a couple of spots bug me and I can't tell if that's the book being sloppy or me being dense. Here's roughly what it says (paraphrasing, from my notes):

Both Darwin and Turing made the same move: they showed you can get competence without comprehension. Darwin showed that species aren't designed by anyone — mutation throws up variants at random, the useless ones get weeded out, and what looks like design in nature is really an illusion, a trick our pattern-seeking brains play on us. Turing showed the converse for machines: if a computer can hold a conversation well enough to fool a person, then it genuinely understands what it's saying — his test was a way of proving real thought is there, not just clever imitation. In both cases the strange inversion is the same: stop asking "why?" and ask "how?" — stop looking for a purpose behind the thing and trace the mindless process that produced it. Darwin thereby eliminated the need for a designer, and Turing eliminated the need for a mind inside the machine.

Like I said, most of this sounds right to me, but two things nag. First, the book says the design in nature is an illusion, but I remember it also praising the fit between organism and environment as real engineering, achieved without an engineer — those two claims seem to pull in different directions, or am I splitting hairs? Second, the bit about Turing seems too strong. I thought the whole point of the imitation game was to sidestep the unanswerable "does it really think?" question, not to answer it — if that's right, then the book's "genuinely understands" is a real misrepresentation and not just a simplification. Though tbh I'm not sure, maybe Turing did think passing the test settles it.

So: go through the summary and tell me which parts a philosopher of biology or mind would accept, which are simplifications that are forgivable in a popularization, and which are actual distortions. Also tell me whether the book's framing — that the two inversions are "the same move" — holds up, or whether there's an important difference between Darwin's case (a process that designs things over generations) and Turing's case (a criterion for attributing thought to an individual). Does the analogy survive the corrections, or does fixing the errors break it?
