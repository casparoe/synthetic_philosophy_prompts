A member of our decision-theory reading group insists that the Ellsberg preferences are nothing but a bias, and she has an argument for this that seems to me to prove far too much. The setup, in case it matters: one urn is known to contain 50 red and 50 black balls; a second contains 100 balls, red and black in some unknown proportion. Most people prefer betting on red from the known urn over red from the unknown urn, and likewise for black — the pattern Ellsberg made famous. Her argument:

(1) A rational agent evaluates every bet by expected utility, computed from a sharp credence for each event; anything less exposes her to sure-loss combinations of bets.
(2) The unknown urn's composition is a perfectly determinate fact: it contains some specific number k of red balls, so the chance of drawing red is exactly k/100 — an ordinary unknown quantity, no different in kind from the number of eggs in my refrigerator.
(3) Rational credence about unknown quantities tracks expectation, and by symmetry your expectation of k/100 is 1/2, the same as the known urn's chance.
(4) So a rational agent is indifferent between the two urns' bets at equal stakes, and, more generally, between any two bets that match in payoff structure and in the credence they warrant.
(5) So the Ellsberg pattern is not a defensible stance but a bias, on a par with overweighting vivid but negligible risks.
(6) And since rational agents are indifferent between matched bets whose chances are merely expected rather than known, transparency has no value for them: such an agent should be willing to swap, for no compensation, a position in an audited fund for one in a fund with sealed books but identical claimed expected returns, and a structural engineer with only rough data on soil strength should add no safety margin beyond what she would use with exact data, since the soil's strength, whatever it is, is already a determinate number.

I can live with (5). What I cannot accept is (6), which seems to imply that due diligence, audited accounts, and safety factors are all marks of irrationality — that rationality demands treating a black box exactly like an open one. Yet each step looks fine to me in isolation. Which step should I reject, and what do I pay for rejecting it?
