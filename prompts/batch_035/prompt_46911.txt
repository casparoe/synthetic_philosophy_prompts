I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background — I work on distributed systems — and I need your help with something my best friend and I have been arguing about for the better part of a year. The dispute: whether space (or spacetime) is a real thing in its own right, or whether only physical objects and the relations among them exist, with space being a bookkeeping abstraction.

The container side of the argument goes like this: space is something like the global coordinate frame an engineer would assume exists under the hood — a genuine structure in which every object has a location and motion, and which would still be there even if the universe were nearly empty. The relations-only side goes like this: the only real facts are relational facts, the way a peer-to-peer network has no privileged master node — there's just which nodes exist, how they're connected, and how their distances change over time; talk of a "background container" is a convenient fiction, not a description of what there is. Newton's rotating bucket (and the related thought experiment with two globes connected by a cord) keeps coming up in our arguments, and each time we each walk away convinced the bucket decisively proves our side, which after a year I take as evidence that neither of us actually understands what it does and doesn't establish.

Here's what I want from you. Act as a professional philosopher of physics who has been asked to moderate and draft the closing document of an adversarial collaboration between the two of us. Please think carefully before writing, and begin with a short brainstorming section in which you map out the terrain before committing to formulations. Then draft the joint statement itself, in American English, structured as follows:

1. A numbered section of claims both sides can sign. These should include claims about what Newton's bucket and globes thought experiments actually demonstrate (and fail to demonstrate), what each side owes an account of, and any points where you think we've been talking past each other.
2. A disagreements table: each remaining disagreement stated in wording its own side would accept as a fair statement of its position — the container side must be able to read its row and say "yes, that's my view," and likewise for the relations-only side.
3. The cruxes: the specific sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of us, rather than just producing more shouting. Candidates you might consider include what, if anything, is true in a universe containing nothing but a single rotating bucket; whether "acceleration is absolute even though position and velocity are not" is a coherent position the container side must hold and the relations-only side must attack; and what the two-globes-with-a-cord scenario is supposed to show about the cord's tension.
4. For each crux, a concrete case, thought experiment, or argument both sides would agree bears on it, together with each side's prediction about that case, written down in advance so we can't later claim we believed it all along.

Two constraints. First, wherever you think one of our disagreements is merely verbal rather than substantive — for example, if part of our fight dissolves once we agree on what counts as "existence" or "real structure" — say so explicitly and explain which disagreement it is and why it's verbal. Second, assume neither of us has read anything technical in the philosophy of physics beyond pop-science accounts of Newton and Mach; you may use our engineering vocabulary (frames, synchronization, state, bookkeeping) where it genuinely helps, but you must not smuggle in jargon without explaining it in a sentence.

Please be rigorous rather than diplomatic: if the container side has a stronger reply to the bucket argument than we've been giving it, or if the relations-only side has resources for absolute acceleration that we haven't noticed, say so. The point of the document is to make our remaining disagreement exact, not to make it go away.
