For an upcoming graduate workshop on population ethics, participants have been asked to circulate a short written statement of their considered positions, to which two respondents will reply directly. I have drafted mine, but I would like a rigorous audit before I circulate it, since I suspect that some of my commitments do not sit comfortably with the others — though I genuinely cannot tell which ones, or how badly.

Here is the set of positions as I have written them down. First, I accept the procreation asymmetry in both directions: a couple who could, at no cost to anyone, conceive a child guaranteed a flourishing life has no moral obligation to do so, while a couple who would conceive a child whose life will be wretched does something pro tanto wrong in conceiving if they could avoid it. Second, I endorse a person-affecting view: moral claims ultimately concern making people better or worse off, and there is no impersonal value in the total amount of happiness in the world — happiness matters because it is somebody's happiness. Third, I believe we now act wrongly in pursuing policies that will leave future people with badly degraded lives, and I do not think the fact that those future individuals will owe their very existence to our policies gets us off the hook. Fourth, in a case where parents can have either (though not both) a child whose life will be barely worth living or a child who will flourish, and all else is equal, I judge it pro tanto wrong to choose the former — even though the child who is born is not worse off than it would otherwise have been, since otherwise it would not exist at all. Fifth, despite that verdict, I deny that parents using embryo selection are obligated to pick the child with the best expected life; selecting against predictable misery is required, but selecting for the maximum is not. Sixth, I reject the claim that a very large population whose members each have lives barely worth living could be better than a much smaller population of flourishing people, even if the former contains more total wellbeing.

What I want from you is not a survey of population ethics but an audit of this particular package. Which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension, which merely appear to be, and how severe is each tension once spelled out? For each genuine tension, tell me what would have to give and what that concession would cost elsewhere in the set. Then tell me which single revision, if I made it, would clean up the most — and whether the resulting position would still be recognizably mine. I have deliberately not flagged which commitment I would be most willing to abandon, because I don't know, and I would rather find out where the real pressure is than be flattered about my starting point.
