Everyone remembers Bentham as the panopticon guy, but people forget the same man wrote a whole essay (in his Political Tactics, printed under the heading Of Publicity) arguing that the great security against misrule is publicity aimed at the governors -- open courts, published proceedings, the public watching the powerful. And he basically thought secrecy was the favorite tool of what he called sinister interests. Oh, and he considered natural-rights talk to be nonsense, so the whole German constitutional-dignity framework would cut no ice with him on its own terms.

So here's what I want: teleport him to the present day and walk him through three regimes. (1) Germany: strong data protection, informed-consent requirements, the right to be forgot, all built on the idea that citizens get to control and conceal information about themselves, both from the state and from companies. (2) The US: openness as the default -- court records, property records, and a lot else public by design, plus a fairly leissez-faire attitude toward firms trading data around. (3) China's social credit experiments: not the cartoon version, but the real pilots -- the state aggregating everyday behavior into scores, with blacklists and joint rewards-and-punishments.

I don't want a both-sides tour. Take a definite position: which of the three would he feel most at home in, and why? Then the hard part -- everyone assumes he'd shrug and endorse the surveillance, since the panopticon looks like social credit in miniature. Does that actually follow? Notice his own track record: his publicity principle ran downward at rulers, while his inspection regimes ran at prisoners, paupers, and schoolboys -- people he'd already stripped of the standing to govern themselves. So which way does he jump when it's ordinary citizens being scored and sorted? Does he say German-style concealment is just a shield for sinister interests, or does he notice that in a modern state the lens has flipped direction, and conclude that the panopticon and the publicity principle point in opposite ways -- and if they do, which one was the real Bentham?

Give me your single best answer, argued from things he actually held (the consequentialism, the hatred of secrecy, the contempt for rights talk, his obsession with record-keeping and ledgers), and tell me honestly where you're extrapolating past anything he wrote. And if you think he'd land somewhere that fits none of the three regimes, commit to that too and sketch the arrangement he'd design instead.
