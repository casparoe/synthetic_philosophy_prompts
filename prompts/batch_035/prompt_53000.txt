the question:

"Bentham's hedonism treats pleasure as differing only along quantitative dimensions. But pleasures seem wildly different from one another. State the heterogeneity problem for hedonism, explain one classical and one contemporary hedonist response, and assess whether either response succeeds."

the answer I have to grade:

The heterogeneity problem is that hedonism says pleasure is the only thing that is intrinsically good, which means all pleasures must share some one property in common — the property that makes them good. But when you look at actual pleasures, they seem to share almost nothing. The pleasure of cold water after a long run, the pleasure of finally understanding a proof, the pleasure of just lying in the sun — these don't feel alike at all. If there is no common property, then pleasure is just a grab-bag of unrelated states, and hedonism can't say what exactly it is that is supposed to be good. So the theory loses its main claim, that there is one simple thing, pleasure, that is the good.

Bentham is the classic quantitative hedonist. He held that pleasures differ only in their quantitative dimensions: intensity, duration, certainty, how soon they will occur, fecundity, purity, and how many people are affected. He also famously held that the game of push-pin is just as valuable as poetry, since as pleasures they sit on the same dimensions and only differ in amount. This is exactly the kind of view the heterogeneity problem targets.

Mill's response is the qualitative one. Some pleasures are higher (intellectual and aesthetic) and some are lower (bodily), and which are higher is settled by the competent judges test: if those who have experienced both kinds prefer one, even when they know it comes with less contentment, the preferred one is higher in quality. So the competent judges show that higher and lower pleasures do share a common property after all, namely being preferred by experts, and this is how Mill solves the heterogeneity problem.

The contemporary response is Feldman's attitudinal hedonism, laid out in his book Pleasure and the Good Life. Feldman says pleasure is not a sensation but an attitude you take toward things, like being pleased that something is the case. On this view, the thing all pleasures have in common is simply that they are attitudes of the same type. This is a version of motivational hedonism, because it says pleasure attitudes are what drive our choices. With this move, the heterogeneity problem is completely solved: no sensations ever needed to have anything in common, only the attitudes did.

Overall, the classical hedonists fail the problem, but Feldman succeeds completely.
