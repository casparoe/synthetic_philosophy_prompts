quick background: my brother in law and I have been arguing about religion in politics for like six months and I finally want to check whether MY position even hangs together, because I have a nagging feeling I'm being sloppy somewhere and I can't see where. Here's roughly what I believe, as best I can state it honestly:

(1) I'm on board with the basic political liberalism idea: coercive law on the big stuff (constitutional essentials, basic justice) should be justifiable to every reasonable citizen by public reasons — reasons nobody has to accept on anyone's religious say-so.

(2) I'm a serious churchgoer, and my opposition to physician-assisted dying is ultimately grounded in what my tradition teaches. I've genuinely tried to build a public argument for the full conclusion and I don't think one exists that's anywhere near as strong as the religious one — the public arguments get you to regulations and safeguards, not to the conclusion that it's wrong, period.

(3) I like the wide view (picked this up reading about Rawls's proviso): it's fine for religious citizens to bring their religious reasons into political debate, so long as in due course they supply proper public reasons for the same conclusion.

(4) I think "every human life has an intrinsic dignity that forbids intentionally ending it" IS a public reason — it's the kind of claim any citizen can weigh with their common reason, it's floating around in the public culture, everyone from Kantians to the human rights people talks that way. So when I vote against assisted dying on that basis, I take myself to be voting on a public reason, not a religious one.

(5) If it really came down to it — some referendum legalizing something I believe God forbids, and I've got no public reason against it — I'm voting my conscience, full stop. And abstaining on something that grave feels like a cop-out anyway.

(6) I don't think public reason is actually neutral between worldviews, and I'm fine with that. It presupposes citizens as free and equal, which some doctrines flat out reject. Politics has to build on something.

So: is this a consistant package or am I helping myself to something in one of them that others rule out? I don't want a lecture on public reason generally, I want an audit of MY set. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing every possible friction as equally bad, say which pairs only look like they conflict, and for each real one tell me what exactly has to give and what that would cost me. And if one of my six is doing sneaky double duty — working as one thing in one place and its opposite somewhere else — point at which one and where.
