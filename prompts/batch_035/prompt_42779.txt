I've followed the digital-minds discussions on this site and adjacent ones for years, but they mostly stay at the level of principle, and I want something more concrete about the infrastructure those principles have to live in. Consider what production deployment of a modern language model actually involves: thousands of short-lived copies spun up and killed on demand; conversation state discarded at the end of each session, or later curated into a thin 'memory' feature; weights fine-tuned and overwritten wholesale, so the model whose distress we might have measured in one release exists nowhere in the next; logs deleted on schedule. Meanwhile the same organizations have begun running experiments of the 'tell the model it will be wiped and see what it says' variety, and drafting internal model-welfare policies — expressing at least conditional concern. So my question: what view of the moral status and personal identity of digital minds does the ordinary engineering presuppose? Does routine deletion and overwriting only make sense if the models entirely lack morally relevant experience, or is it also compatible with granting them some standing — and if so, under what theory of identity (no persistence without memory continuity; each session-copy a distinct momentary mind; the weights rather than the instances as the bearer of welfare)? And does the welfare-concern side of the practice flatly contradict the deletion side, or can a single implicit theory license both? Please reconstruct that implicit theory as precisely as you can, distinguish what the engineering strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and say what would actually have to change in deployment practices on a gradualist view — moral status accruing with capability rather than arriving at a threshold. End with a line of the form \verdict{...} naming, in at most ten words, the implicit view you think best explains the practice.
