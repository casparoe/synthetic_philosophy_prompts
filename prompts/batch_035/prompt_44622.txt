quick background for the prompt: in a reading group on control architectures we've been talking about sphexishness — the digger wasp that mechanically re-checks her nest every single time you displace the cricket, no matter how many times you do it. Someone in the group made an argument I can't stop chewing on: the wasp is fully sphexish, humans are sphexish only to a degree, so free will just IS the inverse of sphexishness, i.e. free will = whatever mechanism strips the rigidity out. On that view, if neuroscience could catalogue all the wasp-like fixed routines in a human brain and we deleted them one by one, the system would converge on maximal free will at the point where no rigid routine is left. So my actual question: at that endpoint, isn't whatever remains of the system just noise? And if free will bottoms out in noise, doesn't that mean the wasp's rigid routines were doing all the work we actually cared about all along (the steering, the goal-directedness, etc.)? I feel like something in this setup is pretty badly off but I can't quite pin down where the argument goes wrong — is the problem the identification of free will with non-rigidity, the assumption that sphexishness is one scalar thing, or the last step about noise? Would love to have the confusion untangled.
