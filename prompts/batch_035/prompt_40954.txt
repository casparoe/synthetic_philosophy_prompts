Hi! So I'm a novelist — thrillers, mostly unpublished, don't judge — and my current manuscript has the classic ticking-bomb chapter: my detective has the bomber cuffed to a chair, the device is real, the timer says 40 minutes, and there's a car battery in the corner and a very bad concience forming in her chest. My critique partner (philosophy PhD dropout, loves doing this to me at 1am) walked me through an argument last night and now I can't write the scene until I know where it goes wrong:

(1) Suppose it would be permissible for my detective to kill the bomber — say he's lunging for the detonator and shooting him is the only way to stop the blast. Most readers, and honestly most of us, say yes.
(2) Torture is less harmful than killing. The tortured man survives, can in principle be compensated, gets a trial; the dead man gets nothing.
(3) If harming someone to a certain degree is permissible in a situation, then harming them to a lesser degree, for the same end, in that same situation, is also permissible. This just seems like common sense — if you may break his leg you may certainly bruise it.
(4) So wherever killing is permissible, torture is permissible too — including all the ordinary cases: enemy combatants in war, a fleeing murderer the police could lawfully shoot, a suspect who could lawfully be killed to stop an imminent attack.
(5) Which means a beat cop who'd be justified shooting a fleeing serial suspect may instead wire him to a car battery to find out where the bodies are buried — and that seems to prove WAY too much.

My gut says the sleight of hand is somewhere around (3) or (4), but every time I try to pin it down the steps look fine to me. My critique partner says if I can't name the slip, I can't write the scene honestly — either my detective is a utilitarian or a monster and I have to know which before I type another word. Also, if you tell me the argument is actually valid and it's (5) that has to go, I kind of need to hear that too, weirdly.

For what it's worth, I'd most like to keep (2) and (3) — they both feel obviously true — but I suspect that's exactly where I'm being lazy.

Please answer in American English, with these sections in this order:

- "The broken step" (~150 words): name the exact step to reject and what's wrong with it.
- "Why it fooled me" (~100 words): why that step looked fine at first glance.
- "The alternatives, priced" (~200 words): what it costs to keep the step I should reject and give up something else instead — what absurdities or costs follow.
- "What my detective should do" (~150 words): given all this, how do I write her choice so it's philosophically honest instead of just cinematic.

Don't be wishy-washy on my account — spoilers welcome, it's a first draft anyway.
