My brother in law does moving jobs on weekends and we got into it about whether "the two of us carried the couch" is ever literally true, and I've now argued myself into a corner I can't get out of. Here's the chain as best I can write it down:

(1) Everything that actually happens when two people carry a couch is made of my bodily movements and his bodily movements. There's no third pair of hands hovering over the thing.
(2) My movements get caused by my intention to do my half (grip, lift, back up the stairs), and his by his intention to do his half. Neither of us can intend the other guy's arms — you don't intend what you don't control, and I don't control his grip.
(3) Here's the wrinkle though: I only intend my half because I expect him to do his, and he only intends his because he expects me to do mine. So the "togetherness" is a loop — his intention and mine each exist because of the other one.
(4) Now take the loop apart and count what's in it. Two individual intentions, each of them a fact about one guy, plus two expectations about the other guy. That's it. A circle of expectations is still just expectations — going in a circle doesn't summon a third thing, a "we," that starts doing stuff.
(5) So there's nothing in the world for "we carried the couch" to be true OF, over and above: I moved my end, he moved his, each one expecting the other to. The couch got moved, in a manner of speaking twice, by two solo acts of moving-an-end.
(6) And the same reasoning applies to everything two or more people do, so nobody has ever sung a duet, played on a team, or fought a war. Just individuals, side by side, each doing their own thing while having expectations about the others.
(7) But that's crazy, and it's crazy in a way that matters. If (6) were right, then when the couch falls on the stairs because he dropped his end, I'd bear zero responsibility — my half went fine, my intention did exactly what it said on the tin.

Something has to give and I genuinely can't tell what. If I give up the little rule in (2) about not intending what you don't control, then it looks like I intend things I have no power over, which sounds like wishing rather than intending. If I keep it, the loop in (3) and (4) feels airtight to me. And the one thing I really don't want to give up is the responsibility bit in (7) — he shouldn't be able to point at (5) and say the drop was all on him. Tell me which step to throw out and what I'm stuck with once I do. Lead with your answer in the very first sentence, then walk me through it — no preamble, no restating my argument back at me.
