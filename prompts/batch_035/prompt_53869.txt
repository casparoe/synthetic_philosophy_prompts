I've been reading Aristotle's Politics on my own — no background in philosophy or economics — and there is a pattern in his discussion of wealth that I can't find a name for. He allows one way of making money that stops once the household has what it needs, and he condemns another way that has no stopping point, because money, which was invented to serve life, has become the thing being served. What strikes me is that the condemned kind doesn't seem to be just the first kind carried further: the same acts — buying, selling, lending — count as a different sort of activity depending on what they are for.

So I have two questions. First: is there an established term for classifying one and the same behavior as two different kinds of thing according to its purpose rather than its outward form? Second: is there an established term for the failure he is diagnosing, where an instrument comes to be pursued as an end — the way someone who starts working in order to live can end up living in order to work, or the way a like-count introduced to measure approval comes to be chased directly?

For each, tell me what the nearest concepts are and how they differ from what I've described (I can imagine "greed," or something about valuing things over people, covering part of it, but neither seems to capture the inversion). If nothing established fits, coin a term, give a definition, and supply boundary cases. Please say how confident you are in each identification and single out the one you are least sure of.
