My roommate's a chem major and we got into it after philosophy of science, and now she has an argument that seems to prove way too much and I can't find where it slips:

(1) A reduction of some higher-level property M to a lower-level base B is supposed to be complete: once you've fully described what's going on at the base level, there's nothing left over about M's behavior that needs its own explanation.
(2) If the base-level story is complete, then for every event we'd normally explain by citing M, the base story already suffices to bring that event about.
(3) If the base can entirely take over M's causal work like that, then M is doing nothing of its own — it's causally idle.
(4) Causally idle properties should be eliminated from our ontology, the way phlogiston was.
(5) So every successful reduction eliminates the property it reduces.
(6) But the textbook reductions — temperature as mean molecular kinetic energy, genes as DNA sequences — didn't eliminate anything. We still use temperature, and the reductionist's whole pitch is that reduced things get vindicated, not deleted.
(7) Therefore either no reduction is actually complete, so reductionism is just a promissory note, or reduction is really elimination, in which case the reductionist's favorite cases are exactly the ones where the reduced property survived. Either way the reductionist loses.

She thinks this is just the causal exclusion point generalized from minds to all the special sciences, but something feels off between (3) and (5). Like maybe "causal work" means one thing when we say the base does it and another when we say M doesn't? I'd rather keep (6) than anything else. Which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? Also if I keep (6) by saying reduction isn't elimination, I feel like I owe her an account of why phlogiston went away but temperature didn't, and "the base story is complete" doesn't obviously seperate them.
