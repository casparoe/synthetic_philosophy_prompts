I recently transferred within my company from a testing group to the team that builds the planning stack for our warehouse robots, and my new lead handed me a popular book chapter to read as background on something she called the frame problem. She also gave me her own two-minute version of it, which she uses to explain to managers why our planning component is harder than it looks. Before I repeat any of this in a design review, I want to know how much of it I can rely on.

Here is her summary, as close to verbatim as I can reconstruct it. "The frame problem was identified by philosophers in the 1980s: when a robot performs an action, like picking up a crate, it has to update everything in its database that the action affects, and enumerating all of the things that are not affected is combinatorially explosive. Dennett's famous robot shows that this is really the problem of relevance, of deciding what a system can safely ignore, and that is what made it a genuinely philosophical problem rather than just a programming inconvenience. At bottom it's the same as the symbol grounding problem: the robot doesn't know what its symbols mean, so it can't tell which ones matter. The good news is that the technical version was largely solved in the 1980s with default assumptions in nonmonotonic logic, so it's mainly of historical interest now, and it's one reason deep learning systems don't face it at all. Fodor drew exactly this conclusion: the frame problem shows that general intelligence can't be computational." Something about the arc of this from deep problem to solved problem to non-problem for modern AI struck me as too tidy, but I don't have the background to say where it goes wrong, and the chapter she gave me is by a general science writer, not a specialist.

How much of this is right? Please go through it sentence by sentence and tell me which parts a researcher working in knowledge representation or philosophy of cognitive science would accept, which ones they'd wince at as rough but serviceable, and which are just wrong, including any places where two distinct problems are being run together or where a conclusion is being passed off as settled. Then tell me whether her practical takeaway for our situation, that the frame problem is a historical curiosity for us because we use learned components rather than logical ones, actually follows from whatever in the summary is true. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the sentence-by-sentence reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
