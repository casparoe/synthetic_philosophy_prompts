so I've gone and done it: accepted an invitation to speak at the grand rounds of a psychiatry department (mostly attending psychiatrists and residents, plus two philosophers of science who actually know the looping effects literature) defending a position I've basically worked out in the comments section over the years. the thesis: Hacking-style looping effects mean DSM categories are structurally self-consuming, not just imperfect. once a diagnosis is in circulation, the people it sorts know they're being sorted -- some perform the symptoms, some mask them, some reinterpret their whole past through the label -- and this feedback shifts the very symptom clusters the category was validated on. so we shouldn't expect any psychiatric category to converge on a natural kind the way, say, infectious disease categories did, and the dream of eventually finding the biomarker for "major depression" is category-confused: by the time the neuroscience matures, the human kind it was supposed to ground will have moved. my rough argument runs: (1) these are interactive kinds, not indifferent kinds -- the classified population responds to classification; (2) the DSM's own revision history is mostly an empirical record of loops running their course, not steady progress toward joints; (3) so the field should treat diagnostic categories as having a shelf life by design rather than pretending each revision is getting closer to the truth.

prepare my Q&A prep sheet. the objections I'll actually get from forty clinicians who prescribe off this manual every day and two philosophers who've read everything I'm gesturing at -- ordered by how much damage they'd do, phrased the way that audience would phrase them, each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. cover at least the obvious ones: "so what am I supposed to bill insurance for," "lithium for mania keeps working no matter what the patients think they are," and "didn't you just describe ordinary measurement reactivity, dressed up." make sure to include the one question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks. if it matters for calibration, I've given versions of this talk to rationalist-adjacent audiences where everyone already agrees the DSM is a catalog of "things that sometimes co-occur" and I've never had to defend the step from "categories are historically unstable" to "they're self-undermining", which is exactly the step the philosophers will jump on.
