I'm a PhD student working on formal decision theory, and I've run into an argument that seems to show updateless decision theory is self-defeating. The premises each look defensible to me, the conclusion is one I cannot accept, and I cannot locate the exact point where the reasoning slips. I'd like you to tell me which step to reject, why that step looked fine, and what rejecting it costs — and I want a committed verdict: name the step, take the position, and defend it; don't lay out the exits and leave me to choose among them.

Some background so we're aligned, with the salient facts supplied. Updateless decision theory — the family descending from UDT in the LessWrong-adjacent decision theory literature — says, roughly, that an agent should choose its policy as if it had precommitted before some observation, evaluating expected utility from the epistemic state it occupied before receiving that observation. The canonical illustration is transparent Newcomb: a near-perfect predictor places $1,000,000 in a transparent box if and only if it predicted you will take only that box, and nothing in it otherwise; you can see whether the money is there before choosing. Conditioning on what you see, two-boxing is obviously right when the box is full. But from the earlier state — before you knew what you'd see — one policy dominates: precommit to one-boxing, because that is exactly what the predictor foresaw, and the earlier evaluation is the one that tracks how your choice is coupled to the box's contents. Updatelessness says the earlier state wins.

Now the argument, which a fellow student pressed on me:

(1) In cases like transparent Newcomb, the updateless recommendation is justified by the precommitment point: you should evaluate from the latest epistemic state at which you could still have influenced your response to every possible observation in the problem — the state before you saw what you saw, in which both observations were still open.

(2) Call being updateless with respect to an observation O: choosing the policy that the earlier, pre-O version of you would have endorsed for the post-O situation.

(3) Whether to be updateless with respect to O — which epistemic state to evaluate from — is itself a decision, and it is made from a state in which you have already learned a great deal: that the predictor is accurate, that the payoffs are what they are, that the box is transparent, that decision theory is a going concern. Every one of those items was, at some point, an observation that could have been otherwise; each has an earlier state from which you could have precommitted regarding it.

(4) By the reasoning of (1) and (2) — applied now to the choice of evaluation state rather than to the choice within a fixed problem — you should be updateless with respect to each of those observations too, and so on for everything you have ever learned. Consistent updatelessness requires evaluating from the earliest epistemic state you ever occupied.

(5) From that state, no evaluation is possible: you do not yet know which decision problems exist, what the acts and payoffs are, or that you are an agent at all. Expected utility is undefined or flat, so the mandated point of evaluation is one at which nothing can be justified.

(6) So either updatelessness, consistently applied, prescribes nothing — the regress horn — or it is permissible to stop the regress somewhere and condition on some prior knowledge, in which case the stopping point looks arbitrary, and arbitrariness in prescription is precisely what the theory was built to eliminate — the arbitrariness horn.

Conclusion: updateless decision theory is either empty or arbitrary, and in both cases self-undermining. I can't accept that; the one-boxing intuition at transparency has content, and updateless agents in the toy models demonstrably act. But I can't see which step does the damage. My instinct is that the mistake is somewhere in (3) or (4) — something about the transfer from choosing within a problem to choosing the state from which to choose — and I'd rather keep (1) and (2) than abandon them. But I want you to make the call: identify the step you reject, explain why it nevertheless looked unproblematic to us, and then price the alternatives honestly, including the horn you don't take. If you think the argument is valid and something I consider obvious has to go instead, say that and defend it — but commit to something.
