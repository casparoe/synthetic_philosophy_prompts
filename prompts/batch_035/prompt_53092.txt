I'm taking a third-year course on the philosophy of mind, and we've just finished a unit on the split-brain cases. I can follow the textbook version well enough: severing the corpus callosum leaves each hemisphere with access only to the opposite half of the visual field, the left hemisphere controls speech, and so the patient can name what was shown on the right of the screen but not on the left. What I'm struggling with is a cluster of later complications the textbook glosses over, and I'd like a careful explanation of what is actually at stake in them.

As I understand it, from the 1980s onwards researchers noticed that the disconnection is not total — some information appears to cross subcortically — and split-brain patients behave in everyday life as one person rather than as two quarrelling halves. More recently, some neuroscientists have gone further and argued that under forced-choice conditions, these patients can respond accurately to stimuli presented to the right hemisphere, and can even locate them with the left hand while denying any awareness of them, which has been taken to suggest that each hemisphere perceives the whole visual field, and that what the surgery divides is something other than consciousness itself. Meanwhile, the philosophical literature I've read — Nagel's suggestion that these patients may have two centres of consciousness, and Parfit's use of the cases to argue that personal identity comes in degrees — leans on the older, sharper 'two minds' reading.

So here is what I'd like explained. What exactly is the difference, conceptually, between saying that a split-brain patient has two streams of consciousness, one stream whose access is divided, and one subject who is systematically unable to report what she nonetheless knows? Which of these proposals are competing empirical hypotheses about the same phenomenon, and which are really competing accounts of what 'a consciousness' or 'a stream' is, so that no experiment could decide between them? And how do the familiar marks of unity — the co-possession of experiences by one subject, their integration into a single perspective, the availability of a unified report — come apart in these cases, if they do come apart at all?

Please be disciplined about epistemic status throughout: distinguish the empirical findings that everyone in the field accepts from the genuinely contested interpretative questions, and don't present either the recent 'one undivided consciousness' reinterpretation or the traditional 'two minds' reading as settled consensus if it isn't. My tutor suspects the recent work is often over-described in popular articles as having simply overturned Sperry's picture, and I'd like to know whether that suspicion is fair. I'm writing an essay on this, so I want to build on the solid parts rather than repeat a contested claim as fact.
