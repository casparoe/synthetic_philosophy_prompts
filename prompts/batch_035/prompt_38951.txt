I'm a novelist, and I've gotten stuck on a question that started as a character problem and turned into an actual philosophical sinkhole. The book I'm drafting has two sisters. One gives away a serious chunk of her income to famine relief because she genuinely can't stop thinking about the children she saw on a news segment; the other gives exactly the same amount, but only because giving is what people in her circle do and she likes the way it looks — the donation is public, there's a plaque. My editor says readers will obviously like sister one better, and she's probably right, but when I tried to write the scene where a third character, a hard-headed utilitarian type, tells them both that morally speaking they've done exactly the same thing, I realized I don't actually know what I think. Does the second sister deserve less moral credit? Is moral credit even a real quantity, or just a feeling readers have that philosophy shouldn't dignify with a theory? And there's a wrinkle that keeps bugging me: the status-motivated sister was raised from birth to care about her standing in exactly that way — if her motives are just as non-chosen as the first sister's compassion, does that change the comparison, or just relocate the problem?

Here's what I want from you: your credence that acting for the right reasons is a genuine determinant of the moral worth of an action — that the two sisters' donations are not morally equivalent, and not just strategically or emotionally different — decomposed into the pieces that credence hangs on. Please write it as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, no introduction and no conclusion: (1) your overall number, with the claim stated as precisely as you can state it; (2) through (4) the rough probabilities for the three sub-questions you think it actually decomposes into — for instance whether moral worth is a coherent notion at all as opposed to just the rightness of what was done, whether an agent's reasons can be evaluated separately from the act they produce, whether there's a fact of the matter about which reasons are the right ones or whether it bottoms out in whatever a given community treats as praiseworthy, or whatever you think the real load-bearing questions are; and (5) the sub-question your overall number is most sensitive to, and what evidence or argument would move it the most. Give me actual numbers, not "depends on your metaethics" — my utilitarian character would eat that for breakfast and I need to know whether the scene works.
