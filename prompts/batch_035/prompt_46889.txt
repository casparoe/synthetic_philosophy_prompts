Last weekend I was at a going-away dinner for my friend Priya, who's leaving for New York to work at a quant fund and has publicly committed to giving away most of her bonus to charity. Somewhere between the second and third bottle of wine, two of my other friends, Marcus and Elena, got into it about where that money should actually go, and they eventually dragged me in as referee, which I am plainly not qualified to be, so I'm outsourcing it to you. Priya has it narrowed down to two options: a deworming charity that treats kids in Kenya (they've got randomized trials, cost-per-treatment figures, the works) or a local tenant-organizing group that Elena volunteers with, which fights evictions and slumlord conditions in our own city. Priya herself stayed out of it mostly, she just kept saying "convince me."

The argument went like this. Marcus said it was no contest: the deworming charity can tell you exactly what a dollar buys, the trials show real effects on health and school attendance, and with the tenant group you're flying blind — nobody can tell you what a hundred thousand dollars of organizing accomplishes, so you can't responsibly pick it. Elena said that's just measurability bias: nobody runs randomized trials on organizing, so of course the countable stuff looks better, but absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence, and besides the tenant work attacks the upstream cause of the very poverty the deworming charity patches up downstream. Marcus replied that if you can't compare anything then you can't choose anything, and "under uncertainty" doesn't mean "give to whatever feels righteous"; measurability is a discipline, not a bias, and the deworming dollars are near-certain to do some good while the organizing drive might do literally nothing, since those fail all the time. That's when Elena got a bit sharp: she said the whole "effective giving" apparatus is rigged toward whatever can be counted, that the countable stuff is systematically the downstream band-aid stuff, and that Priya's hedge fund friends love deworming because it makes earning-to-give look like a clean conscience purchase instead of what it is — profiting from institutions that produce the harms you're then donating to soften. She also said if everyone reasoned like Marcus, all charitable money everywhere would flood toward whatever happens to have a number attached.

Marcus said that was unfair twice over. First, the same argument proves nothing is ever better than anything else, which is absurd — if we really couldn't compare at all, Elena couldn't even say the tenant group is better than burning the money. Second, the downside on the deworming side is bounded and known, while the tenant group's downside includes quietly accomplishing nothing for years, and at least priya can sleep knowing what her bonus did. He also pointed out that "you can't quantify it" has historically been the refuge of charities that don't work. Elena conceded that "nothing is comparable" is too strong a conclusion, but said the right lesson is to compare honestly under uncertainty — including uncertainty about what measurement itself leaves out — and that Marcus's position amounts to defaulting to the measurable whenever measurement gets hard, which just re-creates the bias she was complaining about. By then it was midnight and nobody had convinced Priya of anything. Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right at each step (not just who wins overall), including whether Elena's "rigged apparatus" point is fair or just a mood, and whether you see anything both of them are missing that would actually help Priya choose? Please write it as continuous prose, like an email to a friend — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
