I'm part of a small reading group on the philosophy of biology that has spent the better part of a year on the units of selection debate, and we've hit a wall I'd like your help diagnosing. The arc we followed is presumably the familiar one: Wynne-Edwards's old-style group selection for population regulation, demolished in the 1960s by Williams's attack on "the group selection fallacy," the rise of the gene's-eye view with Dawkins, and then the unexpected revival of multilevel selection through Price-equation-based trait-group models in the 1990s and 2000s. What has stalled us is not any particular argument but the meta-question: after all the equivalence results showing that group-selectionist and gene-selectionist (or inclusive-fitness) descriptions of the same scenario can be transformed into one another, the disputants have not converged at all. Some of us read the equivalence results as proving the dispute was verbal all along — a matter of bookkeeping preferences — while others insist that the two frameworks still disagree about causal structure: about which level the selection process actually operates at, as opposed to which statistics you choose to report, and about whether the so-called equivalences smuggle in assumptions (about additivity of fitness effects, or about what counts as "the same" model) that break precisely in the interesting cases.

My working hypothesis, offered as just that, is that the disagreement has quietly become a disagreement about what a scientific explanation is supposed to do: the gene-centric side seems to want the unit that makes the dynamics transparent and predicts resistance to subversion from within, while the multilevel side seems to want the level at which the causal difference is actually made, and these two desiderata can come apart. But I'm not sure that's right, and part of me suspects the dispute is really about a hidden formal question — something about how to partition the Price equation, or about whether "selection at level X" is even well-defined independently of a chosen decomposition — that no one has stated cleanly. It also matters to us whether the recent empirical cases people argue about (cooperative microbial biofilms, eusocial insects, cancer as within-organism selection) genuinely bear on the dispute or just give both sides fresh rhetorical material.

So here is what I'd like from you: a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is — verbal, substantive-but-empirical, a clash of explanatory values, two different questions traveling under one name, or something I haven't listed — supported by what each side actually says rather than by caricature. Please tell me what, if anything, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: a sharper formal distinction, a class of empirical systems where the frameworks make different predictions, a change in the question itself, or nothing. And please be honest about the possibility that the reason this debate has run for sixty years is that it is unresolvable in its current form, and explain what would have to change for that verdict to be more than fatigue.
