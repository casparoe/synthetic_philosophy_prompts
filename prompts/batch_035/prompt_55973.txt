I'm a retired chemist who fell into philosophy a couple of years ago, and after reading Hume's essay on miracles I've basically adopted his position as my own: no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle unless the alternative (that the testimony is mistaken or fraudulent) would be even more miraculous than the event itself. To me this just seems like proper Bayesian reasoning before Bayes -- you weigh the prior improbability of the event against the reliability of the witnesses, and since a violation of a law of nature has about the lowest prior imaginable, testimony basically never wins. It also matches how I treated anomalous results back in the lab: one spectacular reading against a background of millions of confirmations meant equipment error, period.

But I've noticed that when I bring this up with philosophy-minded friends, they don't just disagree -- they claim the argument is circular, since the prior you assign to the miracle already assumes laws of nature can't be violated, which is the very thing in dispute. And my wife, who is religious, says I'm ruling out her evidence before she even gets to present it. So now I'm honestly unsure whether I hold a solid epistemological principle or just a very confident prejudice dressed up in probabilities.

What would actually count against the Humean position? I don't want the standard rebuttals rehearsed -- I want to know which objections should genuinely move a committed Humean (maybe the circularity charge has real bite?), which are merely awkward, and whether the position can be qualified into unfalsifiability without that being a fatal flaw. Also, if you can, address whether the lab analogy I keep using is doing illegitimate work -- miracles and malfunctioning spectrometers might not be comparable cases in some way I can't quite pin down.
