Some years ago I came across Daniel Dennett's four-way sorting of minds, and I have been chewing on it ever since. In case the terms are not familiar: a Darwinian creature has its good tricks installed by blind variation and natural selection — the tricks were chosen by differential survival, with no learning by the individual. A Skinnerian creature can also learn by trial and error in its own lifetime, but the trials are real: it acts, gets rewarded or punished, and bad moves cost it in flesh rather than generations. A Popperian creature can run candidate moves through an inner model of the world, so that bad hypotheses are discarded instead of the creature. And a Gregorian creature imports extra mind-tools — language, maps, arithmetic, other people's ideas — that let it redesign its own thinking, with culture as an outside source of designs.

I have gone through a list of systems and sorted them by gut feeling, and I would now like to know whether my gut is following one coherent rule. Please take the role of a philosopher of mind who takes toy cases seriously, and start by brainstorming rather than concluding: lay out two or three candidate principles first — for instance, a system is at whatever level its next unlearned move comes from; or, a system is at the highest level it ever reaches; or, you should credit the level of the process that produced a system's policy, not the level at which the system itself operates — and only then commit to one. My verdicts:

- A moth circling a light bulb: Darwinian, full stop. No learning, no inner test.
- A rat that learns a maze by running it and getting shocked on wrong turns: Skinnerian.
- A rat that watches another rat run the maze and then takes the correct route on its first try: I say Popperian, because the observation was a free trial. My brother says it is still Skinnerian, since the rat never tested anything inside its own head. We cannot settle it.
- A chess program that evaluates millions of move sequences inside itself before touching the board: Popperian, obviously.
- The development history of that same chess program — engineers generating many designs and keeping the ones that won: Darwinian? But then every engineered artifact seems to become Darwinian, which threatens to make the category useless.
- A self-driving system whose policy was shaped by millions of simulated crashes: I keep flipping between Skinnerian, because it was effectively punished for crashing, and Popperian, because the crashes happened inside a simulation rather than in the car's real world.
- A person who never tries the unfamiliar restaurant but imagines the evening and decides it would be disappointing, and skips it: Popperian.
- A person who writes a pro-and-con list and phones a friend before deciding: Gregorian, clearly.
- My GPS app rerouting me around traffic: my instinct says this must rank higher than a moth, yet during my drive nothing in it learns or imagines anything — all of its smarts were installed beforehand. So it comes out Darwinian on the same reasoning as the moth, and I cannot reconcile my opposite intuitions about the two.
- A person who memorized a rule from a book, such as checking the weather before painting outdoors, and applies it faithfully without ever testing or questioning it: I want to say Gregorian, because the tool came from culture, and simultaneously that they are behaving mechanically, no better than a well-installed Darwinian reflex. This is my hardest case.

So here is my question: is there one principle — one usable definition of which level a system is at — that delivers nearly all of these verdicts? Where it cannot, say which of my verdicts you would overturn and why, rather than quietly patching the principle to fit. Then state plainly what your principle predicts for the case I keep going back and forth on: a large language model, trained on oceans of human writing, that works through a problem step by step before answering — is it Popperian, Gregorian, both, or neither, given that all of its trial and error seems to have happened during training rather than in the conversation itself? Finally, be explicit about what would change your mind: which of my verdicts, if I dug in my heels on it, would sink your preferred principle, and where in your own reasoning you think you are most likely to have gone wrong.
