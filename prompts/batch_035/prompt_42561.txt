Something about the laws of war has been bothering me, and I'd like help untangling it. If a soldier is captured, the rules that nearly all countries have signed onto say he must be fed, housed decently, protected from violence, and released when the fighting ends. These protections don't depend on anything about him except that he was fighting for the other side: a teenage conscript dragged into a defensive war and a career pilot who volunteered to fly bombing runs for a country that started the war are treated exactly the same. At the same time, the very same international system treats launching an aggressive war as the gravest crime there is, and has put leaders in prison for it. So the men and women who actually do the killing get a blanket excuse for the killing, while the people who ordered it can be locked up for life. And a captured soldier can be prosecuted for shooting a prisoner or bombing a village, but not for the fighting itself. I've been trying to figure out what view of a soldier's moral position this whole arrangement only makes sense under. Is it that soldiers are presumed to have done nothing wrong because they had no real choice, or that fighting in a war simply doesn't make you liable to punishment even when you chose it, or is the humane treatment not about desert at all but just a reciprocal bargain — we treat your prisoners well so you treat ours well? It matters to me whether these pieces fit together, because the "no choice" story can't be right for the volunteer pilot, and the reciprocal bargain doesn't obviously justify protecting him from prosecution rather than just from mistreatment. I also wonder which rival views the arrangement quietly rules out — for instance, whether it's compatible with thinking that soldiers who fight for an unjust cause do forfeit something, just not enough to lose the protections.

Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two long, with no introduction or conclusion. Suggested coverage, though you may arrange it however works: (1) reconstruct the most plausible implicit theory of the soldier's responsibility; (2) say which parts of the practice that theory genuinely requires as opposed to merely fits well with; (3) identify a rival theory that would also license the practice, if one exists, or say why none does; (4) address whether the practice's treatment of war crimes versus the war itself is consistent with the theory you reconstructed, or marks a hidden second commitment; and (5) state what would have to change in the practice if the presupposed theory were replaced by the view that soldiers on the unjust side forfeit their protections. Please write in American English.
