Every fall I teach intro logic, and every fall the surprise exam paradox eats up a whole session I'd budgeted for something else. You know the thing: the teacher announces there will be an exam next week, on a day you won't be able to predict in advance, and then the students run the elimination argument -- it can't be Friday, because Thursday night you'd know it's coming; it can't be Thursday, because... -- and conclude the announcement is self-refuting, at which point the teacher gives the exam on, say, Wednesday and everyone is genuinely surprised.

Here's my question. My colleague in game theory keeps telling me the students' argument is nothing but backward induction, and that I'm making a mystery out of a solved problem. On his telling, the structure is identical to the reasoning that says you should defect in every round of a finitely repeated prisoner's dilemma, or grab the pot at the first node of a centipede game: start at the last stage, see what's forced there, then unravel backwards, and accept the result even if it feels odd. Backward induction is just sound reasoning, he says, so the student who dismisses the announcement is being perfectly rational, and the teacher's announcement is simply incoherent in a way that's well understood -- the paradox belongs in a lecture on common knowledge, not in a unit on paradoxes proper. He's even said the exam case is a nice excuse to teach the backwards-induction unraveling before we get to the repeated games.

I want an audit of the analogy before I let him take over my class. Please lay out the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of the game tree, the stages, the terminal node, the players and their payoffs, the common knowledge of rationality? Then tell me where the mapping breaks. Things I'd especially like you to think about: the announcement is self-referential in a way a game tree isn't (it's a claim about the students' future epistemic states, including states produced by reasoning about the announcement itself); the teacher arguably isn't a strategic player at all, just a truth-teller or not; "surprise" might be doing something that "can't predict" in the game-theoretic setup doesn't; and there's the fact that in the repeated prisoner's dilemma people actually cooperate, while in the exam case the exam actually happens and people are actually surprised -- do those real-world outcomes cut the same way, or differently?

Then separate the disanalogies that matter for my colleague's use of the analogy (his conclusion is that the student's reasoning is rational and the announcement is just incoherent, hence: no paradox worth teaching) from the ones that don't. And if there's a better analogy for the students' argument than backward induction, say what it is.

End your answer with a final line of the form

Verdict: [one short sentence saying whether my colleague's conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table]
