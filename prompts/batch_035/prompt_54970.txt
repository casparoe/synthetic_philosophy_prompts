I teach a graduate seminar on the philosophy of freedom, and we have just finished two weeks on the Grand Inquisitor episode from The Brothers Karamazov. My students split along lines I find instructive, and rather than simply gather impressions, I would like a quantified second opinion. Please respond in the persona of a professional philosopher with expertise in both nineteenth-century Russian thought and contemporary theories of freedom and well-being, and before committing to any numbers, take a few sentences to brainstorm through the chapter's structure — the three temptations and what Christ's refusal of each is supposed to purchase for human beings.

Here is the claim I want evaluated, stated as precisely as I can manage. The Inquisitor's position is correct, in both its parts: first, that the freedom Christ insisted upon by refusing the temptations of bread, spectacle, and earthly power is a burden the great majority of human beings cannot bear, and that they will reliably surrender it for security, mystery, and someone to obey; and second — this is the part my students dispute most — that an institution which confiscates that freedom in exchange for collective happiness acts better, all things considered, than Christ did.

Give me your credence that this compound claim is true, decomposed into at least the following terms, plus any others you judge to belong: (a) the empirical-psychological claim about what most people can in fact bear; (b) the evaluative claim that a life arranged for happiness at the cost of freedom is better for the person so arranged; (c) the interpretive question of whether Dostoevsky intends the Inquisitor's argument to stand on its own or to be undone by its frame — the Inquisitor's own evident torment, Christ's silent kiss at the end, the fact that it is Ivan, a man in the grip of a fever, who narrates it; and (d) the asymmetry that the Inquisitor excepts himself, keeping the burden of freedom precisely by stripping it from others, which may show that the trade between freedom and happiness is not the simple one he presents. Assign each term a rough probability with one line of justification, tell me whether the terms are independent enough to multiply, show the combination, and identify which term your final number is most sensitive to and what argument or piece of evidence would move it most.

For reference, my own credences are roughly 0.5 on the psychological half and 0.15 on the normative half. I would like to know where you diverge from me and, more importantly, where my two numbers most likely fail to capture what the chapter is actually arguing. Please think carefully and do not settle for the generic observation that freedom and security trade off; the deliverable is a probability with its derivation and its weak points marked.
