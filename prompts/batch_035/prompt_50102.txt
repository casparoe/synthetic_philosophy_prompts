I am a high school senior, and I have noticed a recurring move in arguments about fairness that I cannot find a name for. Twice recently I have encountered someone defending a change purely on the ground that it shrinks a gap between people, even when the change helps no one. In one online argument, a person said they would support a tax policy that simply destroyed the wealth of the very richest people, with none of the revenue going to anyone, because what mattered was that the gap between the top and everyone else got smaller. At my own school, there was a proposal to eliminate the honors track on the ground that having separate tracks is unfair to students in the regular one, even though eliminating it would not improve the regular classes in any way; the students defending the proposal freely admitted that the advanced students would learn less and nobody would learn more, and they still thought it worth doing because at least everyone would be in the same boat.

Is there an established concept in philosophy for treating a reduction in inequality as an improvement in itself, in cases where literally no one benefits and at least one person is made worse off? It seems related to envy, but the people making these arguments were often not the worse-off party and stood to gain nothing themselves, so envy does not seem to fit. It also seems different from the claim that inequality causes bad downstream effects, like resentment or political corruption, since in both cases the arguers went out of their way to say that the consequences were not the point — the flatness itself was the good.

If this pattern of argument has a name, I would not be satisfied with just the name. I would like to know what exactly the position amounts to — whether the claim is that the equality itself is good, or something about fairness in how a state of affairs is structured — and how philosophers have tried to answer it. Are there serious philosophers who actually bite the bullet and accept that making some people worse off, with no compensating gain for anyone, can still be an improvement? What do they say is the strongest defense of that? Conversely, I have a vague sense that there is a rival view on which what matters is not the gap at all but rather giving extra moral weight to benefits received by people who are badly off — a view on which destroying the wealth of the rich and gaining nothing for the poor would count as no improvement, or worse than nothing. Does that rival view have a name too, and where exactly do the two views come apart, if it is possible for a gap to shrink while the worse-off gain nothing but also lose nothing? Finally, are there positions in the middle, and if so, what would each of them say about my school's honors track?
