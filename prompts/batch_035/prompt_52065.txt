I am a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am at the outline stage of a long-form essay about whether digital life is quietly dismantling the conditions for cooperation. The thesis I want to pressure-test runs as follows: the classical results on the evolution of cooperation — the prisoner's dilemma, the difference between one-shot and iterated play, the finding that cooperation among self-interested agents depends on a long shadow of the future and on the ability to recognize and remember specific partners — were largely developed in a world of repeated encounters among people who knew each other. Yet an increasing share of contemporary economic and social life now takes place in settings that are effectively anonymous and one-shot: gig platforms matching strangers for a single ride or task, marketplace transactions mediated entirely by algorithms, online exchanges conducted under disposable identities. If cooperation really does require repeated interaction with recognizable partners, the digital economy may be eroding the very ground it stands on.

Please set out the most important implications of the one-shot and iterated prisoner's dilemma literature for evaluating this thesis. In particular, I would like to understand, first, what precisely iteration contributes to sustaining cooperation and why the one-shot version is supposed to be so much harder; second, which of the classic mechanisms for supporting cooperation — repetition, reputation, external enforcement, and small-group clustering — remain available when interactions become anonymous and unrepeatable, and which of them reputation systems can plausibly substitute for; and third, whether algorithmic ratings and scores are best understood as restoring the function of repeated play or as introducing a genuinely different mechanism with distinctive failure modes of its own, such as sellers who cooperate only until they plan to exit the platform, collusive rating arrangements, or strategic generosity timed to lifecycle endpoints rather than to the actual history of interaction.

I would also welcome a candid account of where the theory is silent or unhelpful here — for instance, whether the standard models, with their fixed payoff matrices and clean notions of defection, can really accommodate the diffuse, partial, and often unverifiable defections of platform life, or cooperation that spans many agents at once. Since my readers are intelligent non-specialists, please favor plain conceptual explanation over formalism, and please do not include reading lists or references; I only need the reasoning.
