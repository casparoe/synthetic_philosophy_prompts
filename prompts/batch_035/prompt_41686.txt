I am a twelfth-grade student who runs my school's philosophy club, and each spring I write a practice paper for the younger members preparing for a regional philosophy essay contest. This year our topic is thought experiments, and I have deliberately invented a scenario of my own so that nobody can look up the standard commentary on it. The scenario is this: a company sells a device called the Memory Launderer, which deletes the memory of a single wrongdoing while leaving everything else — including the pleasure that wrongdoing brought — fully intact. A customer who cheated a close friend out of money uses the device and thereafter sincerely, and without any detectable psychological gap, believes he never did it. He remains happy and well-adjusted, and by every ordinary measure he seems exactly as good as a person who never wronged anyone. When I present this to club members, nearly all of them have the strong intuition that he has somehow "gotten away with it," that something bad remains true of him. But I have noticed the intuition weakens when I strip out details I happened to include: I described the device as sleek and corporate, the customer as smug about his purchase, and the cheated friend as still quietly suffering. I am not sure whether my scenario is a good thought experiment or a manipulative one, and I want the exam question to make students grapple with exactly that.

Acting as an experienced philosophy examiner who has set many papers and knows precisely where students go wrong, and thinking carefully before you write — perhaps sketching for yourself what a merely competent answer would say before deciding what a genuinely excellent one adds — please produce three things. First, an exam question, suitable for strong high-school students with forty-five minutes to answer it, built around my scenario, that tests whether students can distinguish the features of a thought experiment that do real argumentative work from those that merely push the reader toward a conclusion, and that requires them to say what, if anything, survives once the manipulative details are removed. Second, a model answer of roughly six hundred words that actually performs everything the question demands rather than describing how one might. Third, a marking scheme out of twenty marks that names the specific moves an excellent answer must contain, explains which of those moves separate an excellent answer from a merely competent one, and identifies the standard mistakes that should cost marks — in particular the mistake of narrating the scenario back at length, and the mistake of labeling something a framing effect without showing which specific detail is doing the persuading and why. I would also like the marking scheme to say how a student could reasonably challenge the question's own presupposition that the persuasive details are inert, in case some detail I thought was decoration turns out to matter. Please write all three parts as continuous prose only, the way one would write a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables of any kind.
