I sit on the ethics committee at a mid-sized regional hospital, and thursday we're hearing a case I've been assigned to write up beforehand. Patient — call him D. — has schizoaffective disorder, six or seven hospitalizations over the years. When he was stable, about eighteen months ago, he sat down with his outpatient psychiatrist and signed a psychiatric advance directive: if he's ever admitted in an acute episode, he refuses haloperidol outright (it gave him severe akathisia once and he described it as feeling poisoned from the inside), he pre-consents to olanzapine, and he pre-consents to ECT before anyone goes to court, if meds fail. Now he's back, actively psychotic, refusing everything — including the treatments the directive authorizes — and insisting the document is a forgery planted by his brother. The brother wants it honored. D.'s ex-partner thinks that's absurd: you'd be overriding a person's currently stated will on the basis of a signature from someone who, in her words, no longer exists in any meaningful sense — the man in the bed didn't sign anything. Our hospital policy says these directives are "presumptively valid" and, helpfully, says nothing about what defeats the presumption.

Here's what I actually want the memo to do. I've been reading about Ulysses contracts and constitutional precommitment — the idea that a self, like a people, can legislate for its future, less-rational incarnation, the way the framers bound later generations with an amendment procedure, or the way Ulysses ties himself to the mast before the sirens are even audible. Our committee chair thinks that analogy basically settles it: D.-stable is the constituent power, D.-psychotic is the government acting ultra vires, honor the document. Another member thinks the analogy is precisely what's wrong with the whole framework — a constitution contains its own amendment clause, and the psychotic patient has no procedure at all through which his current will could count, so calling him "bound by his earlier self" is just ruling against him and dressing it up as self-rule. She'd add that at least a constitution gets ratified by people who are all still around to be governed by it. I want the memo to engage that fight directly, not to just restate autonomy doctrine or hand-wave about capacity.

So: write this as a philosopher-bioethicist brought in to advise the committee, someone comfortable moving between clinical ethics and political theory. Take a definite position — enforce the directive, don't, or a conditional version you actually specify — with a concrete course of action and time frame for this admission, plus a short closing paragraph on what our policy should say going forward. Before you write, do some actual thinking about which side has the stronger argument overall, and make sure the memo answers the strongest version of the losing side's case, not a convenient weaker one. And be upfront in the memo about anything where you're reasoning from plausible assumptions rather than established fact. Something like 700–900 words, the length a real circulate-before-the-meeting document would be — the committee members are clinicians, not philosophers, so no untranslated jargon.
