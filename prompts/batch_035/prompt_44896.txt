My friend and I got into a long argument about the "for want of a nail" thing after watching a doc on the assassination in Sarajevo (the driver taking a wrong turn past the deli). She's deep into the chaos theory of history and left me with this argument I can't quite shake:

(1) Chaotic systems are sensitive to initial conditions — a difference too small for anyone to notice at the time produces an unrecognizably different outcome later.
(2) Human history is chaotic in roughly this sense.
(3) So for any historical outcome, there are indefinitely many tiny earlier differences — a sneeze, a missed train, one voter staying home, a slightly different breakfast — such that if it had gone the other way, the outcome would have been wildly different.
(4) But then the things historians actually single out — the driver's turn, a general's decision, a treaty, an assassination — are not special. Each is just one small input among millions, and none is privileged, since for any of them you can find some trivial difference that would have undone the outcome just as thoroughly.
(5) So no historical outcome is attributable to any decision or identifiable event more than to any other.
(6) And if nothing caused anything more than anything else did, then "it could have gone the other way" loses its point — "the other way" isn't anchored to anything, since the outcome depended equally on everything and nothing.

Her punchline is that contingency, pushed hard enough, dissolves: if everything is contingent, nothing is. I said (4) feels like it slides from "everything matters a little" to "nothing matters much," but she says that's literally what sensitivity means and I'm just squeamish about it. She also pointed out that Tolstoy basically bit the bullet on (5) in War and Peace — Napoleon riding around thinking he's steering when he's a cork on the wave — which honestly stung a bit.

I want a proper diagnosis, not vibes. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd like to keep (2) if I can — I do think history is full of path dependence — and I'd like to keep the ability to say the driver's turn mattered in a way the archduke's breakfast didn't, but I can't currently say what grounds that difference. Is there a principled line between "everything the outcome counterfactually depends on" and "the things the outcome is attributable to," or is that just a fact about what we care about?
