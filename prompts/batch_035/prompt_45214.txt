I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training — backend systems by day, but I've spent years tinkering with genetic algorithms as a hobby, and a forum argument has pulled me into the question of whether natural selection "really" operates on genes, organisms, or groups. I've read enough popular science to know the names (Dawkins, Williams, group selection, multilevel selection), but the books all argue for a position rather than helping me find mine, and I'd rather work from cases than from labels. Since I think in debugging trees anyway, here's what I'd like you to build me: a decision tree that starts from a commitment I already have — that adaptations exist and need an explanation in terms of selection — and walks me, one branch node at a time, to a named position in the units-of-selection debate.

Constraints on the tree:

1. Every branch node must be a question I can answer from my intuitions about concrete cases, not from knowing which position it leads to. Please draw on cases like these, plus any others you think do real sorting work: a segregation-distorting allele that biases meiosis in its own favor while slightly harming the organism carrying it; cancer, where cell-level selection destroys the individual; an allele that spreads only because it sits on the same chromosome as a beneficial one, contributing nothing itself; eusocial insects with sterile workers; and, if you find it useful, my own hobby domain — genetic algorithms where I select whole candidate programs, yet parasitic subroutines that contribute nothing to the program's score can still accumulate in the population.

2. The leaves should be actual named positions where names exist (for example: the gene's-eye view, organism-centered selection, multilevel selection, and the view that the choice of level is a matter of bookkeeping convenience rather than a factual dispute) — and "unnamed combination" where a region of the tree has no occupant, with one sentence on whether you think nobody holds it because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

3. If two leaves can't actually be separated by any question a person could answer about cases — and I suspect the gene's-eye view and the bookkeeping view at least are like this — say so explicitly rather than inventing a spurious branch, and tell me what kind of consideration, if not a case, would move someone between them.

4. Define any technical term the first time you use it, and keep the whole tree, including the leaf descriptions and caveats, under 900 words. Present it as indented text with one question or claim per line; I want something I can actually trace myself down.
