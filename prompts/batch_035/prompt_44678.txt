I work on the technical side of AI alignment, mostly preference learning, and I've been asked to write a position statement for a workshop where it will get pushed back on. Before I circulate it, I want to know whether my views actually form a package or whether I'm holding positions whose usual motivations can't all be true at once. Here is roughly where I've landed:

(1) Inferring human preferences from behavior is, at bottom, a problem about the assumptions we make about human rationality, not a data problem: more demonstrations will not rescue an inference built on the wrong model of how preferences produce behavior.

(2) Treating humans as approximately rational with noisy choice — the Boltzmann-rational model that much of the modern IRL and preference-learning stack is built on — is a reasonable first approximation, and the systematic deviations (biases, fatigue, limited attention) can be treated as noise that washes out given enough data.

(3) There is a fact of the matter about what someone's preferences are, over and above a summary of their choice dispositions: two people could in principle share all their dispositions while wanting different things, and the whole preference-learning project only makes sense because of that gap.

(4) Human preferences are to some extent constructed rather than revealed by the process of eliciting them — the way you ask changes the answer — and they shift over time with circumstance and reflection, so the target of inference is not fixed in advance of the interaction.

(5) The right answer to worries about misattributing bias to preference is methodological pluralism: cross-check behaviorally inferred preferences against stated preferences, ratings, and pairwise comparisons, and trust the triangulation rather than any single channel.

(6) Assistance-game setups — where the machine treats the human as the authority on a latent preference it remains uncertain about — are the right safety story for preference learning, and their safety does not require the human model to be exactly right, only approximately right.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which tensions are real but survivable with a cheap patch, and which only look like tensions until the claims are stated more carefully. Rank them by severity rather than listing them flatly, and for each real tension, say what would have to give and what it would cost me to give it. One more request: keep separate what is settled among people who actually work on this from what is genuinely contested. I don't want to present a contested reading of, say, the noise model or of the assistance-game safety argument as if it were the field's consensus, and I would rather know in advance where I am taking a minority view.
