Now I've wandered into a debate in the alignment world that has a familiar smell to it. One camp insists safety work should aim at worst-case guarantees: the system has to be safe for every input in some class, including adversarially chosen ones, in a way you could in principle cash out as a proof or a bound. The other camp says that's pie in the sky for systems this complex and opaque, and that the realistic goal is average-case or high-probability safety — good behavior under realistic deployment conditions, assessed the way we assess reliability in other engineering fields. The frustrating part is that both sides seem to agree on the facts: current models are unsafe in various ways, a powerful misaligned system would be very bad, and exact mathematical guarantees for neural networks are basically out of reach. And yet the argument has been going for years with, as far as I can tell, nobody moving an inch. My working hypothesis — and I'm aware I may be smuggling in my own training here — is that this isn't a technical disagreement at all but a disagreement about what standard of evidence a safety claim has to meet before somebody is allowed to act on it, more like engineers arguing about safety factors than like scientists arguing about the world. But I could easily have that backwards.
