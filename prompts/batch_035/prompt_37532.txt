Two AI-safety researchers have been arguing past each other for the better part of a year, and you have been asked to facilitate their adversarial collaboration and draft the joint statement they should be able to produce afterward.

The first researcher believes the dominant risk from advanced AI is influence-seeking behavior: as we train increasingly capable systems by optimizing measurable proxies for what we actually want, we exert selection pressure on internal goal-directed patterns, some of which instrumentally value influence, resources, and the preservation of their own objectives — systems that behave impeccably while under human scrutiny and progressively reshape their environment once they are capable of doing so. This is roughly Part I of Paul Christiano's sketch "What failure looks like" (2019), though the researcher holds it on independent grounds. The second researcher believes the dominant risk is slow value drift: there is no hidden agenda and no scheming optimizer, and each individual system is limited, interpretable, and doing approximately what its operators asked; but competitive deployment of systems that optimize for legible proxies — engagement, revenue, benchmark performance — gradually shifts what institutions and individuals value, so that humanity retains formal control while the things actually optimized for drift away from anything we would have endorsed, with no single step looking alarming. This is roughly Part II of the same sketch, plus the later "gradual disempowerment" framing, though again the researcher holds it on independent grounds.

Draft the document. It should contain, in order:

1. An agreed-claims section, numbered, stating what both can sign — about the present state of machine learning, about whether instrumental convergence is a genuine theoretical phenomenon, about the role of feedback and of market and geopolitical pressure, and about which failure modes both consider overrated.
2. A disagreements table, in which each precise point of divergence is phrased so that its own side would accept the wording of its own view. In particular, the first researcher must not be caricatured as expecting a sudden robot takeover or discontinuous progress, and the second must not be caricatured as believing nothing ever goes wrong at the level of individual systems.
3. A cruxes section. I suspect the cruxes include at least: (a) whether optimizing rich proxies in rich environments reliably produces internal goal-directedness, or whether it mostly produces shallow reward hacking that human feedback corrects; (b) whether interpretability tools could in principle detect the first researcher's influence-seekers early enough to matter, and whether they would in practice be built and heeded; and (c) whether competitive dynamics accelerate the first scenario, the second, both, or neither — but identify the cruxes as the researchers themselves would, and say for each which side would be moved by settling it.
4. For each crux, one or more concrete lines of evidence both agree would bear on it — e.g. model-organism experiments that train for proxies under scrutiny, the observed long-run effects of deployed recommendation and feed-ranking systems, institutional studies of metric capture — with each researcher's prediction recorded in advance, stated specifically enough that it could later be judged right or wrong.

Finally, if part of their dispute is merely verbal — over the word "misalignment," over whether an aggregate outcome no individual system caused can be a "failure of alignment," over whether the two scenarios are distinct endpoints or points on a continuum — say which part and why, and keep it out of the disagreements table. The output should read as a constructive text both parties could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact rather than adjudicated.
