I teach an evening course called "Minds and Machines" at a community college. The students are mostly working programmers in their thirties and fortights — correction, thirties and forties — and next week's single ninety-minute session covers Searle's Chinese Room argument. The complication is that this audience arrives with a strong prior: most have absorbed a garbled pop-summary version on podcasts and are already convinced it's a bad argument that only people who don't understand computation could take seriously. What I want from you is not the argument itself but a plan for the session.

Specifically:

1. The order the pieces must go in — in particular, whether the thought experiment should be presented before or after the distinction it is usually taken to support, and what breaks if I get that order wrong with an audience that has already decided the conclusion is false.

2. The version of the scenario I should build the session around. My inclination is to describe the rule-following not as one man at a desk with a rulebook but as a shift of workers in a mail-sorting hall, each handling a single rule and passing cards down the line, with no one in the hall able to read a word of the language. I'd like your view on whether that variant helps or hurts, and why — especially whether it prematurely hands the students a reply they haven't yet earned.

3. The misconceptions to expect from this audience, in the order they will surface. My guesses: "Searle proved computers can't think," "the whole system understands — that's the reply, argument over," offered as a knockout blow, and "the man could just memorize the rules, so there's no difference worth discussing." For each, tell me the exact point in the sequence where it should be headed off, since with programmers a misconception left alone for ten minutes hardens into a position.

4. What I should openly decline to cover at this level — I assume the hard problem of consciousness is out; anything else? — and how to mark the boundary without seeming to dodge.

5. One question I could put to them at the end that would distinguish a student who has grasped the argument's actual target from one who has merely learned to stop dismissing it.

Note that I'm asking for the plan, not a draft lecture: what depends on what, where the friction will come, and what to leave out.
