I'm grading finals for a upper-level phil of math course I'm TAing, and the instructor gave no rubric for this question, just "write your own comments." The question was:

"Gödel's incompleteness theorems get borrowed far outside mathematics: to argue that the mind surpasses any machine, that there are truths that can never be proved, that no system can fully account for itself. Pick two such extra-mathematical uses (at least one of which you take to be mistaken), and for each say exactly what the theorems do and don't license, being explicit about which technical conditions do the work."

And heres one student's answer, transcribed as written:

"First, the theorems. (1) Any consistent, effectively axiomatized theory strong enough for arithmetic is incomplete: there's a sentence it can neither prove nor disprove (Gödel originally needed ω-consistency; Rosser got it down to plain consistency). (2) Such a theory, if consistent, cannot prove its own consistency. Both are results about formal systems, and the hypotheses do the work: consistent, effectively axiomatized, strong enough to express arithmetic.

(a) Lucas–Penrose. Let S be the formal system a machine realizes. Gödel gives a sentence G_S that, if S is consistent, S cannot prove. But G_S is true — it says 'I am unprovable in S,' and it is — so we know a truth no machine running S can know. Hence the mind outstrips any machine. I don't think this works. The move from 'unprovable in S' to 'true' goes through only if S is consistent, and by the second theorem S can't certify that itself; whether we can certify it from outside is exactly what's in dispute, and for an arbitrary machine we'd have no way of telling a consistent system from an inconsistent one that hasn't yet shown a contradiction. Also the mechanist can just switch to a stronger system and the trick starts over, so nothing follows about machines in general. So the anti-mechanist conclusion isn't licensed. What survives is much weaker: no fixed consistent system captures everything a mind that is itself consistent and can recognize the consistency of other systems would see.

(b) 'There are truths that can never be proved.' Too strong. The theorems don't show any truth is unknowable, period — for any particular Gödel sentence, we standing outside the system can prove it, in a stronger system. Gödel also showed that truth for arithmetic cannot be defined in arithmetic, and that is the deep reason truth outruns provability within any one formal system. What does survive: any consistent theory at all, provided it's effectively axiomatized, is incomplete. So if all of our knowledge could be captured in a single consistent formal system, there would be truths it couldn't deliver. But we're not a single fixed formal system, so nothing follows about human knowledge, and the theorems are a limit on formal systems, not on minds or on science."
