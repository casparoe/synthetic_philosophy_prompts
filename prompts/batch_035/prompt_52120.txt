Last week in my epistemology seminar our instructor, on Monday morning, announced that there would be a surprise exam sometime before the end of the week — an exam such that, on the evening before it took place, we would not be able to know that it was coming the next day. I went home and ran what I later learned is the standard elimination: the exam can't be on Friday, because Thursday night we'd see it must be Friday; but then it can't be on Thursday either, since Wednesday night we'd know Friday was excluded and deduce Thursday; and so on back to Monday. So no surprise exam is possible, I concluded, and I sensibly stopped revising. The exam arrived on Thursday morning and I was — genuinely, completely, viscerally — surprised. I also failed it.

Here is what troubles me. The elimination looked airtight as I performed it, and each individual step still does; the very first step, about Friday, seems to me beyond dispute. Yet the exam occurred and my surprise was not acted. So it seems that at least one of the following must be true, and I cannot determine which: my feeling of surprise was irrational, since a person reasoning correctly could have known the exam was coming; or the argument is unsound despite my inability to locate the flaw; or the announcement was somehow both refuted and fulfilled at the same time, which I can't even state coherently. My tutor told me the puzzle is famous and that there is no consensus resolution, which I find worse rather than better.

My working position, which I'd like checked, is this: the argument shows that someone who reasons correctly can never legitimately be surprised by anything announced in advance, and so my Thursday-morning surprise was a merely physiological event — the sort of startle one gets from a jack-in-the-box one has wound up oneself — rather than a genuine epistemic surprise. On this view my classmates who didn't run the argument and were equally surprised were doing fine, whereas I was irrational twice over: once in trusting the argument and once in feeling anything on Thursday. Is that right? And if it is not, I'd like to know exactly where the elimination goes wrong without being told merely that it 'must' go wrong somewhere. My seminar is about to reach backward induction in game theory, and if the lesson of this episode is that backward induction is generally untrustworthy, I would rather learn that now than after the midterm.
