I've bought into the Hofstadter and Sander line from Surfaces and Essences — the idea that analogy isn't just some neat trick minds do, it's basically the whole engine, and even boring everyday categorization (recognizing this mug as MY mug) is the same mechanism as noticing my new bug at work is structured like that bug I fixed three jobs ago. I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, so maybe I'm missing something, but it feels like a claim this sweeping could swallow any counterexample — you can always redescribe whatever the mind does as a "stretched analogy" if you're motivated enough. So what would actually count against it? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: stuff like dissociations where someone is good at categorizing but terrible at analogy (or the reverse), developmental data where infants categorize way before they plausibly make rich analogies, or ML systems that categorize fine without anything I'd recognize as analogy-making going on. For each, say whether a Hofstadter-style defender would concede it counts against the view or just absorb it, and be honest about whether the whole thing has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if so, whether that's fatal for a claim like this or whether its fine for it to work more like a research program than a testable hypothesis.
