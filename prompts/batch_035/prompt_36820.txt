I teach an upper-level seminar on intergenerational justice, and for our final session I want to close with a poem before the students scatter into futures I will mostly not live to see. The last weeks of the course were taken up with the ethics of discounting: whether the mere fact that a person exists later makes their welfare count for less (what economists call pure time preference), and the standard rejoinder that real-world discounting is not really about time at all but about risk and uncertainty — the chance that civilization does not persist, the likelihood that our descendants are richer and need our help less, the sheer haze that gathers over any prediction a century out. The seminar never settled whether that rejoinder is a rescue of discounting or a quiet concession that its moral core was indefensible, and I would like the poem to keep that question open rather than close it.

So here is the constraint. The poem should enact an exponential discount rate in its very form: each successive stanza roughly half the length of the last — say sixteen lines, then eight, then four, then two, then one, or some other audibly shrinking schedule — so that the further future gets exponentially less room on the page. Within that form, I want the shrinking to be genuinely ambiguous between the two readings we kept circling: at each stage, the reader should be unable to say whether the compression is a value claim (you matter less because you are later) or an epistemic one (I cannot see you clearly, my voice will not carry, the odds of my mattering to you decay). Let the earlier stanzas spend their length the way present generations spend theirs — on detail, on projects, on the speaker's own life — and let the discounting reveal itself gradually rather than being announced.

The closing single line carries most of the weight of the assignment. It has the least space of anything in the poem, and I want it to be the line that stays with the reader anyway — spoken by, or on behalf of, the far-future person the form has been squeezing out. It should not be a rebuke and not a plea; something quieter than either, which a student who has done the readings on pure time preference will hear as an argument and a student who has not will hear simply as a good last line. No fixed rhyme scheme is required, and I would rather the form's shrinking be audible to the eye and ear than grammatically exact. Above all, the poem should resist the sermon: if it simply indicts discounting, it has done less work than the form was asked to do.
