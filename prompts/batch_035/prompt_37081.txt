I'm a master's student in cultural history, and I'm preparing an essay for a module on the history of records and record-keeping. My draft argues for a fairly strong inversion thesis: that the classic "archives and power" framework — archives as instruments by which states decided whose lives left a trace and whose did not — was built for an era of scarcity, and that the move to born-digital records doesn't merely update it but partially reverses it. I'd like your help evaluating the draft. Please take on the role of a scholar who works at the intersection of archival theory, political philosophy, and the history of information: read the draft carefully, think about it before responding, and give me candid, detailed comments rather than encouragement. In particular, I want to know whether the central inversion claim actually holds, where the argument is at its weakest, and what a sceptical examiner would seize on.

Here is the draft:

"The archive has never been a passive storehouse. From the Domesday Book onward, the record was an act of sovereignty: a census is a claim about what counts and who counts, and the colonial state's files were as much a technology of rule as its railways. Against this, a whole literature — one thinks of Derrida on the archive, and of Trouillot's account of the Haitian Revolution, which Western contemporaries could record only as noise — has taught us to read power in the silences: the unrecorded, the misrecorded, the filed under categories designed to make some people permanently legible as subjects and never as agents. The operative mechanism was omission at the point of recording. Scarcity — of paper, ink, clerks, shelf space — meant that recording was a choice, and appraisal, the decision about what to keep, was the state's quietest and most consequential form of power.

My claim is that the born-digital era inverts this. Recording is no longer scarce; the default is capture, and the interesting exercise of power has migrated from the decision to record to decisions made after the fact — de-indexing, deletion on request, the quiet expiry of accounts, the shutdown of platforms. Consider two episodes. When Yahoo switched off GeoCities in October 2009, roughly fifteen years of amateur web history was slated for erasure, and it survived only because volunteers and the Internet Archive grabbed what they could before the deadline — a rescue operation, note, with no public mandate, performed by an American nonprofit. When MySpace admitted in 2019 that a server migration had corrupted beyond repair some twelve years of uploaded music — over fifty million songs from some fourteen million artists — no one pretended a national archive would have prevented it, because no such archive has custody of platform-held records at all. In the paper era, catastrophic loss was exceptional — a fire in a repository — and its causes were at least conceptually distinct from appraisal. Now the two collapse into one another: the appraiser of the digital record is a company deciding whether a migration is worth the cost, and the resulting silence is indistinguishable from an accident.

The new silence, then, is not the unrecorded but the de-recorded. And it has a class structure the old silences lacked: those most intensively recorded — the connected, the young, the poor in data-rich economies — are also those whose traces are most fragile, since they exist only inside systems they do not own. Meanwhile the oral traditions that the silences literature mourned turn out to have better survival properties than a social media account. If the old archive made subjects legible, the new archive makes them legible and then evanescent, and a framework built for reading omissions needs to be retooled to read disappearances."

So that's where it stands — roughly 500 words, meant to grow to about 2,500. Before you comment, please brainstorm the different kinds of objection an examiner with archival-training could raise, and structure your response around the following: (1) Is the inversion genuine, or is it just appraisal changing hands from state to corporation, with the underlying power structure continuous?
