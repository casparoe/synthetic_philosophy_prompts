I'm a lay member of the committee that adjudicates disciplinary cases at a state board of nursing licensure. We hear roughly forty cases a year in which a nurse is alleged to have done something wrong — documenting a medication she didn't actually witness, overriding a safety protocol, sharing a patient's information with someone who turned out not to be authorized — and the sanctions range from a written reprimand to revocation of the license. A hearing lasts one afternoon, we have a staff attorney but no philosopher, and the nurse's livelihood is on the line, so the stakes are high and the time is short.

More and more, the defense we hear is some version of "I couldn't have done otherwise": the unit was short-staffed and following protocol was physically impossible in the time available; my supervisor told me to falsify the chart or be fired; my ex-partner, who had threatened me, demanded I take the medication from the cabinet; I was in acute withdrawal and not in control. Half the committee thinks that if there genuinely was no permissible alternative, responsibility goes out the window. The other half thinks the nurse did the wrong thing regardless of what the alternatives were, and that excusing her punishes the colleague who reported it. We keep splitting these cases on vibes, and I want better than vibes.

Some years ago I read about the so-called Frankfurt cases in philosophy, and they seem to bear directly on our problem. As I understand the classic shape of it: someone performs a wrongful act entirely for her own reasons, while an intervener — in the thought experiments, a neuroscientist with electrodes implanted — stands ready to force her to do it if she shows any sign of wavering. She never wavers, the intervener never acts, and yet many people's intuition is that she's responsible even though she couldn't have done otherwise. The suggestion is that what matters for responsibility isn't the availability of alternatives but whether the actual explanation of the act runs through the agent or through the thing that removed the alternatives. I also gather there's a well-known objection — something like: if she was never going to waver, you can't tell a story about what she would have done if she'd tried, so the thought experiment never really removes the alternatives it claims to remove — and I don't know whether the objection defeats the cases or not.

What I want from you is a decision procedure the committee could actually run on a "no alternative" defense: an ordered list of questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which answers end the inquiry with no discipline and which end it with full discipline, and which points hand the decision back to the committee because the question is one of values rather than facts. The conceptual core should be the Frankfurtian one — distinguishing cases where the constraint explains the wrongdoing from cases where the constraint was mere backdrop and the agent's own reasons did the work — but translated into questions a non-philosopher can ask a witness under oath. Then run the procedure on three cases we actually face: a nurse who falsified intake times under a supervisor's explicit threat of termination; a nurse who diverted opioids while being coerced by an abusive partner; and a nurse who says short-staffing left no compliant option for a medication-error reporting deadline, where I suspect the procedure will return an answer the majority of my committee won't like. Tell me where the procedure will misfire.

Two requests about how you present it. First, wherever the procedure depends on a philosophical claim that specialists still argue about — including the question of whether Frankfurt-style cases succeed at all, given the objection I mentioned — say so explicitly and mark the branch of the procedure that rests on it, rather than building the whole thing on a contested reading presented as settled. Second, tell me plainly which steps of the procedure are real tests and which are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, since we'll be handing this to committee members who will follow it literally.
