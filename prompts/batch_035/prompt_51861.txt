I'm writing a piece on the "Moloch" framework — the idea, familiar from essays on multipolar traps and races to the bottom, that competitive dynamics can force every participant toward an outcome no participant wants, and that such traps are typically escapable only by coordination imposed from somewhere outside the game. My test case is the Partial Test Ban Treaty of 1963, and my grader is skeptical of my thesis, so I need the analysis to be genuinely rigorous rather than an illustration.

The episode, briefly: by the late 1950s the United States, the Soviet Union, and Britain had conducted well over two hundred atmospheric nuclear tests between them, each round yielding weapons data and prestige while scattering fallout — including strontium-90, measurable in milk and in children's teeth — across countries that had never tested anything. A voluntary moratorium held from late 1958 until the Soviets resumed testing in the autumn of 1961, a series culminating in the Tsar Bomba, the most powerful explosion in human history; the Americans and British soon followed back into the race. Then in August 1963 the three powers signed a treaty banning tests in the atmosphere, outer space, and underwater. France and China never signed and kept testing in the atmosphere into the 1970s and 1980s respectively, while the signatories simply moved their testing underground and continued it there for roughly three more decades, alongside an arms race that grew to tens of thousands of warheads.

Please structure the response in four sections:

**The game** (roughly 350 words). Map the episode onto the multipolar-trap framework with discipline: who the players are (is it three, or five once France and China are counted, and do the citizens of non-nuclear states function as players or only as payoff entries?), what each actor's options actually were (test, halt unilaterally, halt conditionally, defect on a moratorium), and what the payoffs were — weapons knowledge, alliance credibility, domestic politics — with special attention to why fallout, a cost borne diffusely by everyone, didn't discipline anyone's behavior for years.

**The exit** (roughly 350 words). The standard theory offers a short list of escape routes: coordination, a single dominating victor, or exhaustion. Identify which of these the treaty was, and evaluate my thesis, which my grader suspects is too neat: that the decisive mechanism was that atmospheric testing externalized its costs onto everyone at once and visibly — a common enemy that was nobody's ally — which converted an invisible trap into a publicly legible one and made coordination possible between rivals who trusted each other on nothing else.

**Where Moloch stops explaining** (roughly 400 words). Give an honest account of what the framework misses or misdescribes. At minimum: the signatories banned the atmospheric version of the race and then kept racing underground for decades, so did anyone escape anything, or was the trap merely relocated to a rung invisible to the public? France and China, as defectors from the coordination, faced essentially no punishment and suffered no meaningful strategic loss — what does that do to the claim that the trap compelled everyone? And the arms race the testing fed continued growing long after the treaty; if Moloch was in the room, the treaty evicted only one of his heads.

**Verdict: what the case says about the theory** (roughly 250 words). Say plainly whether this episode confirms the multipolar-trap framework, strains it, or reveals a mechanism its standard escape list never anticipated — and, if my fallout thesis survives scrutiny, whether it generalizes to other races to the bottom or was an accident of a harm that happens to cross borders and show up in milk.
