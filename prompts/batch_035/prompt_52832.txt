I teach an upper-level philosophy of science seminar, and I'm writing the take-home final for our unit on scientific thought experiments — the one that runs from Galileo's falling bodies and Newton's bucket through Maxwell's demon and Einstein's elevator. My students already wrote a midterm paper summarizing the standard debate between the argument view and the Platonist view, and they can recite both positions in their sleep, so a question that simply asks them to adjudicate between those two labels will tell me nothing about who actually understands the epistemology. Instead, I want to build the exam around a crux that both standard positions tend to gloss over, and that we kept circling in seminar without ever pinning down: whether the yield of a thought experiment is propositional at all. Call it the know-that/know-how problem. If the argument view is right that Galileo's falling-bodies case is just a reductio of premises Aristotle already accepted, then a student who follows the derivation acquires no new entitlement — she could have gotten the conclusion all along. Yet pedagogically that seems obviously false: something happens when a student finally sees why the theory of fall is unstable that doesn't seem reducible to adding a belief to her stock, and it looks more like acquiring the ability to run the scenario, notice where it strains, and rederive the result on demand. Whether that "something" is epistemically load-bearing — whether it constitutes knowledge of a kind the argument view can't capture, or whether it's merely psychological scaffolding around the real (propositional) work — is what I want the question to force them to confront.

Please write three things. First, a single essay question answerable in about 90 minutes, anchored throughout in the Galileo case — specifically the move where Salviati argues that, by Aristotle's own lights, a heavy stone fastened to a lighter one must fall both faster and slower than the heavy stone falling alone. The students know this case cold, so the difficulty must come entirely from the crux, not from the setup; the question should be unanswerable by someone who can only summarize the standard positions. Second, a model answer of roughly 600 to 700 words that takes a definite position — I genuinely don't care which one, but it must commit and argue for that commitment rather than surveying the options and leaving the examiner to choose. Third, a marking scheme out of 20 points built around five must-hit moves, with the marks for each and a one-line note on the mistake a B answer typically makes at that point — for example, gesturing at intuition as a mysterious source of a priori knowledge without saying whether the thought experiment's work is done by the intuition, the derivation, or something that is genuinely neither.

A few constraints on your end. Please act as an experienced philosophy of science examiner who has set this kind of question many times, and think through the design before drafting: identify what a memorizer could fake on this question and make sure the wording closes those exits. The question, model answer, and marking scheme should rely only on what was covered in a typical seminar on this topic — the big three standard views and the classic cases — and should not require any citations, bibliographies, or references to specific papers or page numbers, since neither my students nor the grader will be able to look anything up while writing or marking. Throughout, be willing to make definite choices and defend them — including about the question's framing itself, if you think the know-that/know-how crux is the wrong one and a different one would better separate understanding from memorization; but if so, say so explicitly and justify the substitution rather than quietly hedging.
