Please argue that the most defensible way to side with the dust specks in the torture vs. dust specks comparison is not to deny that harms aggregate across persons, but to posit a lexical threshold within each individual's life: there are harms so slight that no number of them befalling one person can add up, for that person, to a single instance of a grave harm like torture. The argument should proceed in two stages: first, show why denying interpersonal aggregation outright concedes too much, since it would also block the thought that a sufficiently large number of moderate harms (broken legs, say) could outweigh one torture; second, show that once intra-personal thresholds are in place, summing sub-threshold specks across persons amounts to adding zeros, so the astronomically large number in the case does no argumentative work. Then address the two most serious objections: that lexical orderings are non-Archimedean and generate pathological preference patterns elsewhere (no trading at any odds between threshold-protected harms and lesser ones), and that the threshold's location is arbitrary, since a hair's-breadth difference in harm size falls on either side of it. Conclude by stating precisely which classes of harm your view treats as aggregative and which as threshold-protected.
