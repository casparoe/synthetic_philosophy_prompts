I spent most of Saturday evening arguing about time travel with my friend Priya, a physics postgraduate who thinks the whole grandfather paradox is a non-issue, and I've come away realising my own reactions to time-travel stories are a mess that I'd like help sorting out. Here are my verdicts on a run of cases. First, the classic: a traveller goes back to shoot his grandfather, and the universe stops him through absurdly contrived means — the gun jams, he slips on a roller skate, a falling roof tile clips him on the head. It's formally consistent, and yet it strikes me as somehow cheating, as though the story has a thumb on the scales. Second, a self-consistent loop: the traveller goes back, fails for reasons that were always part of history, and it turns out his failed attempt is the "mysterious stranger" incident his grandfather used to tell stories about. That one feels fine to me, even elegant. Third, an information loop: the traveller hands the young Shakespeare the complete manuscripts of Shakespeare's plays, which Shakespeare then publishes, which are the manuscripts the traveller later carries back. Uncanny, but I can't find anything wrong with it. Fourth, a material loop: a watch is handed backwards around the same cycle forever, so the watch has no manufacturer, and it is objectively older on every trip through the loop. Here I balk, and I can't fully explain why — Priya points out that an information loop is just as circular as an object loop, and that my only complaint about the watch is entropy, which is a physics quibble, not a logic one. Fifth, branching timelines: the traveller kills his grandfather, spawning a new branch in which he is never born while the original branch carries on undisturbed. My instinct is that this is a dodge rather than a solution, and yet I grudgingly admit it's the only setting in which "going back to fix a mistake" means anything at all, and I do want to say that a grief-stricken person taking a time machine to save someone isn't thereby confused. Sixth, the straight overwrite, where the traveller kills grandfather and the photograph of his own birth simply fades out: that just seems incoherent to me — if he was never born, who is standing there in 1923 doing the killing?

I'd like you to take on the role of a professional metaphysician who works on time and thinks it matters that the answer be precise rather than vibes-based. Please start with a short brainstorming phase before you commit to anything, and then give me: (1) a principle, or a small set of competing principles, stated carefully — perhaps something in the neighbourhood of the idea that time travel is possible just in case the story is consistent with the traveller's personal time and external time being distinct, or perhaps something else entirely — that delivers verdicts on all six cases; (2) an honest account of which of my verdicts your preferred principle cannot accommodate, and whether the banana-peel case and the branching case are actually the same complaint of mine wearing different clothes; (3) an explanation of whether my treatment of information loops versus object loops can be defended, or whether Priya is right that it's arbitrary; and (4) your principle's verdict on a case I haven't mentioned: a traveller whose very motive for going back is produced by the trip — he goes back in order to thank the old man who once saved him as a child, and it turns out he is the old man, so the gratitude has no origin anywhere in the loop. Please write the whole answer in British English, and don't hesitate to tell me where my gut is simply wrong.
