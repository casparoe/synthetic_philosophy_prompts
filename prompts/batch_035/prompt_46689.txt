I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on ethics and the market at a large public university, and in two weeks I have a 75-minute session on commodification that I want to run as an in-class activity rather than a lecture. My own stake in this is unusual: before graduate school I spent three years as an operations manager at a plasma-collection company, where I watched compensation for plasma go from something my own relatives found vaguely shameful to something my donors treated as a routine side income, and I have never been satisfied that the arguments I heard on either side actually located what was or wasn't troubling about the practice. That experience is why I don't want my students to evaluate commodification cases from the safety of assigned positions; I want them to make real rulings, in front of each other, and then discover that the principles they confidently announced cannot do the work they wanted them to do. The class has 14 students, no lectern, movable desks, and a whiteboard; no real money may change hands; nothing may be graded; and I want the exercise to be fully self-contained, since none of them will have read the assigned literature before that session (I have accepted this about them).

Here is the scenario I have sketched and would like you to develop into a runnable design: the students are the internal ethics board of a fictional startup accelerator that operates a licensing platform and wants to expand into new markets; small teams of students each pitch a proposed new market to the rest of the class, and the board must approve or reject each one on the spot with a one-sentence rule stated in general terms. A rotating red team then gets a fixed number of minutes to construct a market the board just rejected but that their own stated rule licenses, or a market they approved but that violates the spirit of their own objections. I want the candidate markets to range across the familiar and the borderline — compensated kidney donation, vote markets, price gouging on generators after a storm, paying someone to stand in a queue, monetized apology services, compensation for risky pharmaceutical trials — and I want the payoff structure to make the board feel pressure to approve things, since the accelerator loses a licensing fee for every market it rejects; the exact mechanics of that pressure are yours to design, provided it uses no real money. The debrief is the part I care most about: it should map what actually happened onto the philosophical distinctions without me having to import them by lecture — the difference between objections grounded in harm to third parties and objections to the mere existence of the transaction, the question of whether unexplained repugnance is a placeholder for reasons we have not yet articulated or a response to something real about how markets change the practices they enter, and the role of background inequality in explaining which consent feels tainted. I would also like a section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — my guesses are that some students will smuggle repugnance into their rules under the word "dignity," and that "informed consent" will function as a magic wand until someone notices it approves nearly everything on the list, but I want your diagnosis, not confirmation of mine — and an honest note on which feature of the philosophical problem a one-shot classroom exercise cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself.

Please format the whole thing as a facilitator's plan I could read the night before: the pitch scenario as I would say it to students, the rules and timeline with minute counts, the list of candidate markets with a sentence on the work each one is designed to do, the debrief as an ordered sequence of questions keyed to what students will likely have just done, the predictable failure points, and the honest note. Keep the entire plan under 900 words — tight, but I would rather cut detail from the scenario than from the debrief.
