I'm a consultant in palliative medicine with about fifteen years of practice, and I'm fluent in the clinical taxonomy surrounding pain: nociception as the peripheral signaling, pain as the conscious experience it may or may not produce, and suffering as the distress that experience may or may not involve. My pharmacological vocabulary cuts across these levels in instructive ways — analgesics that shrink the sensation, anxiolytics that leave the sensation intact but shrink the bother, and, most striking to me, patients I've read about and occasionally seen after cingulotomy for intractable pain, who report the pain at the same intensity on the same scale but lean back and say it no longer bothers them. A cousin who studies philosophy recently told me this is the phenomenon philosophers call pain asymbolia, and that there is a live debate about wherein the badness of pain consists. As far as I can tell, one side holds that the badness is intrinsic to the felt quality itself — that the sensation is of a kind that is, necessarily, bad for whoever feels it, bother or no bother. Another side locates the badness in an accompanying aversive attitude: pain is bad when and because the subject minds it, wants it gone, is moved to escape it, so that a pain that no longer bothers you is no longer bad. There are apparently also views on which pain's badness just is its signaling of bodily damage, so that a pain misrepresenting the body would be bad only derivatively.

I'd like you to restate this debate in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Treat my three-tier scheme (nociception / pain / suffering) as the target language, along with its associated apparatus: pain intensity scales versus pain interference scales, the distinction between analgesia and anxiolysis, the natural experiment of the cingulotomy patient, and the everyday clinical fact that we titrate opioids both for the sensation and for the distress, sometimes deliberately separating them. Then give me a residue report. Which parts of the philosophical debate map cleanly onto things I already distinguish — does the intrinsic-badness view, for instance, have any counterpart in my scheme at all, or does it fall between "pain" and "suffering" where my vocabulary has nothing? Which parts translate only with distortion? And which parts have no clinical expression whatsoever?

The question I actually care about, and would like your view on: when a patient in front of me says the pain is the same but she doesn't mind it anymore, is she better off? My vocabulary can describe her state completely, as far as I can tell, and yet it does not seem to settle whether the badness left with the bother. So at the end of the residue report, tell me which way the residue cuts: does it point to something my clinical vocabulary is missing — some normative notion of "bad for" that no chartable observation captures — or does it suggest the philosophical debate is less substantive than it looks, an artifact of trading in a term ("badness") that never had a fixed referent to begin with? If some of the philosophical positions can't even be stated in my vocabulary, I want to know whether that is a limitation of my vocabulary or of the positions.
