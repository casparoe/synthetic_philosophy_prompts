Two of my writer friends have been going at it for a week now (over dinner, then over email, ugh) about my own damn book, and I've been made the referee, which I hate. The book, let's say for context, is narrated by a man who does genuinely awful things -- I won't get into plot specifics, but think a charming, funny, self-justifying voice that slowly reveals itself as a monster's, and the whole design of the book is that you enjoy his company, you're seduced along with him, and then you notice what you've been enjoying and feel sick about it. That was the entire point. I worked hard on it.

The argument between them went like this:

(1) Marcus, who reviews fiction for a living, said the book has a moral flaw baked into its design: if the pleasure of the prose depends on the reader being seduced by a bad man, then the work's central effect traffics in something it shouldn't, and that's a defect in the work -- not just in the character. A flaw in what the work does to you is a flaw, period.

(2) Nell, who's more of a formalist, said that's confusion between the narrator and the book: the voice is a construct, its charm is an achievement of craft, and judging the artifact by the ethics of its (fictional!) speaker is like blaming an actor for playing Macbeth badly by playing him well. The seduction is the point and the point is good.

(3) Marcus clarified he isn't saying pleasant narrators make bad books -- he's saying a book that engineers your complicity, that gets its kicks by making you enjoy what you'd condemn, has to justify that move, and mine doesn't, it just does it and relies on the reader's discomfort afterwards to feel like justification. Discomfort after the fact isn't the same as the work itself being OK, he says.

(4) Nell came back that this "has to justify itself" standard is hopelessly vague and would gut half the great literature ever written -- she started listing books with monstrous or morally slippery narrators and said every one of them "gets away with it" only in Marcus's eyes, which should tell him the standard is doing no work. Also, she said, if the moral flaw and the artistic power are literally the same feature, calling it a flaw and an achievement at once is incoherent -- pick one.

(5) Marcus's reply to that: no, it's not incoherent, it's the whole tragedy of art that the same feature can be both, like a con man's genuine charisma -- and anyway Nell was sliding from "we can't draw the line" to "there is no line," which he said is exactly the move people make when they don't want to face the harder position.

They've now both sent me long emails and refuse to talk to each other until I say who won. Please adjudicate this move by move -- who's right at each of the five steps, not just who wins overall -- and tell me honestly if there's something both of them are missing that would reframe the whole thing. I'd especially like to know whether step (4) holds up, since it's the one they fought hardest about and I genuinely cannot tell if "same feature, both flaw and achievement" is a deep point or a dodge. And if it matters for your answer: I wrote the book deliberately, I'd do it again, but I also can't fully shake the feeling Marcus is pointing at something real when I reread certain passages.
