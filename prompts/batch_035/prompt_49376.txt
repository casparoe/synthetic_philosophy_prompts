In my philosophy of mathematics seminar the platonism–nominalism dispute has stalled in a way that mirrors the field at large, and I would like a diagnosis of the impasse rather than a verdict on it. The students on both sides already know the standard moves: Quine's indispensability argument, Benacerraf's point from 1965 that nothing in our numeral-talk privileges one set-theoretic reduction of arithmetic over another, and Benacerraf's later epistemological challenge about how causally isolated abstract objects could be known. They can recite all of it, and yet after a full semester the platonists and the nominalists are exactly where they started: whenever the nominalist offers a paraphrase or a fictionalist reading of mathematical discourse, the platonist says it changes the subject from what our theorems say to what we say about them, and whenever the platonist gestures at the objectivity of mathematics, the nominalist hears a claim about the coherence and consistency of our practices dressed up as a claim about a realm of objects. My working hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that this is a verbal dispute about whether the English word "exists" is being used univocally, and that neither side can see this because each is silently answering a different question: the platonists a semantic question about what our quantifiers commit us to, the nominalists a metaphysical question about what there is in the most fundamental sense. But I am not confident, since the students on each side seem genuinely to disagree about arithmetic, not merely about words. Assess my hypothesis: is this disagreement verbal, substantive but non-empirical, a clash of bedrock intuitions about objectivity, or something else? Support the diagnosis with what each side actually says rather than with a survey of positions. Then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given that diagnosis: a distinction (for instance between asserting a theorem and asserting the existence of its subject matter), a program of the kind Field attempted, a reformulation of the question, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why. Please write the response in American English, as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, in the style of a letter to a colleague.
