Every spring I co-teach a little seminar with two folks from econ on prediction in the social sciences, and this year it ended in the best kind of disaster: a dinner argument afterwards that none of us could settle. I'm a philosopher of science, mostly, and I've been chewing on it for a week, so let me just hand you the exchange and you referee it.

The occasion: Leo (economist) had given a talk where he used Malthus as the great cautionary tale of long-run prediction. Population grows geometrically, food grows arithmetically, living standards get ground down to subsistence -- and then, Leo said, the exact opposite happened for two centuries: food per capita exploded. Case closed, theory falsified.

Marta (philosopher, works on scientific models) went after this at dinner. Her move: Malthus wasn't giving a dated forecast, he was describing an equilibrating mechanism -- whenever real wages rise, fertility responds and population presses back on resources. That mechanism arguably governed almost all of human history before roughly 1800; the modern era is the exception, and the exception runs on stuff (the demographic transition, fossil energy) that is contingent and not obviously permanent. So "right about the mechanism, wrong about the timing" isn't a rescue -- it's the honest reading.

Leo's reply: this is exactly the immunizing move Popper worried about. Any failed long-run prediction can be redescribed after the fact as "the mechanism is real but a contingent factor intervened." If living standards had stagnated, Marta would count it as vindication; since they rose, she counts the escape as the exception. What observation could EVER have counted against the theory? A theory compatible with both outcomes predicts nothing.

Marta: no -- if wages had risen somewhere, in some period, without any fertility response, that would have killed the mechanism. It has a real counterfactual profile. A model of bank runs isn't unfalsified-but-unfalsifiable just because we've had a quiet few decades.

Leo: quiet, sure, but then the theory tells you almost nothing about the specific window Malthus was confident about, and there's a suspicious asymmetry: nobody would be saying "right but early" if he'd been right on schedule. They'd just say right. The conditional reading only ever shows up to the funeral. And Marta's own example cuts against her -- seismologists don't claim to know when the big one comes, so they aren't on the hook; Malthus was on the hook, he even drew policy conclusions from the prediction (he was famously hostile to the poor laws). You can't have it both ways: either the prediction is weak and conditional, in which case it licenses no policy, or it's strong enough to act on, in which case it was wrong.

Marta's counter: predictions in the social sciences partly alter their own subject matter. If the warning contributed to delayed marriage, emigration, policy -- then non-fulfillment is partly evidence the warning worked, not that the mechanism was fake. She said Leo was applying physics standards to a domain where the forecast is one of the causes.

Leo: fine, but then successful non-prediction and failed prediction look identical from the inside, and you've handed a permanent alibi to everyone whose long-run forecast flops. EveryLimits-to-Growth-style revival gets to say "right but early" forever.

At that point it got loud and someone changed the subject to parking. For what its worth, they were both making points I half-believe, which is probably why I couldn't referee it myself.

So: go through the exchange move by move and say who's right at each point, not just who wins overall. And tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing -- I have a feeling there's a distinction sitting right there that neither of them reached for, something about the difference between predicting an event and describing an equilibrium, but I can't quite get it straight myself.
