I teach an upper-level seminar on probability and epistemology — twelve students, one 90-minute session, comfortable with conditional probability but no measure theory. Last night a friend and I argued until 2 a.m. about the doomsday argument. He accepts that finding myself roughly the hundred-billionth human is Bayesian evidence that humanity won't last for trillions more; I'm convinced something is rotten in treating my own birth rank as a random draw, but I couldn't say exactly where the reasoning goes wrong. Rather than lecture them about our impasse, I want my students to run into it themselves. Design an activity built around numbered slips representing birth ranks and two candidate worlds — one where history ends soon, one with trillions of future observers — in which students bet (imaginary) money on which world they're in. Please include a sequence of rounds that manipulates what counts as being in the sample: only students in this room, then everyone in the building, then a claimed population of artificial minds that also received numbers before the betting. For each round, specify the materials, the information revealed and when, and the payoff scheme. Then give me a debrief that maps the rounds onto the live moves — sampling from among observers, the reference class problem, the reply that extra future observers should dilute rather than doom the inference — while withholding the standard terminology until the very end. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about which part of the real argument no staged game can reproduce: my suspicion is that the move from 'a number was assigned to me' to 'I could have been someone else' is exactly where the activity goes silent and the facilitator has to speak.
