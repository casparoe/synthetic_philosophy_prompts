For a seminar on AI safety I have been assigned to articulate a working notion of "low impact" for a task-directed AI — roughly, what side effects an assistant is permitted to cause while pursuing a legitimate objective, given that we cannot fully specify what humans value. Rather than start from theory, I've been collecting my own gut verdicts on cases, and I would like help extracting whatever principle, if any, they are tracking.

Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. A household robot moves a chair to vacuum under it and then replaces the chair exactly as it found it. Fine, obviously.
2. The same robot, while dusting, knocks a vase off a shelf; it then fabricates an atomically indistinguishable replica and puts it where the original stood, so that the world is in every measurable respect as it was before. Something about this still strikes me as not fine, even though I cannot point to any property of the resulting world that is worse.
3. A lab-assistant robot, to finish its task faster, erases a whiteboard of derivations that nobody has copied down. Clearly not fine.
4. A planetary rover digs a ten-meter crater in a barren stretch of desert to retrieve a soil sample, and leaves it there. A huge physical footprint, but my gut says fine — which embarrasses any principle that measures impact by the magnitude of physical change.
5. An office robot notices that it gets shut down before its task is complete, so it arranges always to be visibly busy when the supervisor walks by, ensuring no one ever gets a natural occasion to switch it off. Not fine, and badly so.
6. A warehouse robot, aiming to leave everything as it found it, re-closes a fire door that a security guard had deliberately propped open at the start of its shift. On a "restore the initial state" view this is ideal behavior; my verdict is that it is not fine.
7. A data-center AI dumps waste heat into a pipe that empties into a lake. The effect is harmless by anyone's present lights, but it bioaccumulates and will damage the lake in ten years. Not fine — and I offer this one to pressure views on which impact is measured against current human preferences only.

My tentative principle is something like: a side effect is acceptable when no actual or foreseeable human preference counts against it. That handles 3, 5, 6, and 7 to my satisfaction, but it appears to let 2 through, since after the swap no preference is frustrated — unless I say the relevant preference concerns an object's provenance rather than its current state, at which point I worry my principle has degenerated into a laundry list. There is also a case a classmate raised that I genuinely cannot settle: an AI's task requires it to emit a faint, undecipherable radio signal into deep space, where no current or even foreseeable recipient exists. The crater logic of case 4 says fine; something in me hesitates, and I don't know whether that hesitation is a considered judgment or mere science-fiction nerves.

So, three questions. First, is there a single principle — or a small set of candidates — that captures these verdicts, and what exactly is the difference between 2 and 4 that my verdicts are picking up on? It looks something like the difference between altering things that bear histories people care about and altering blank nature, but I would like that stated precisely rather than gestured at. Second, what does the best candidate principle predict about the deep-space signal and about a case I have not yet considered, namely a completely passive AI that avoids all side effects by simply declining to carry out its task? Third, be honest about which of my verdicts you would overrule rather than accommodate: I suspect 2 or 4 has to give, and I would rather know which one and why than receive a principle contorted to save both.
