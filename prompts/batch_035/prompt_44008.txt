I spent most of my career in quantitative genetics — animal breeding, mostly dairy cattle, plus a stint in plant science — and since retiring I've been reading philosophy of science properly for the first time. The heritability debate is the one that keeps nagging at me, because I know the technical side cold but the two literatures barely talk to each other. On my side, everyone treats h² as a mundane breeding statistic: the fraction of phenotypic variance attributable to additive genetic variance, in a specific population, in a specific environment, and a thoroughly useful number when you want to predict response to selection. Then on the other side you've got philosophers and various social critics saying things like "heritability says nothing about the fixity of a trait" or "heritability estimates can't support claims about the malleability of human traits," and when this argument flares up every few years, nobody budges an inch. The biologists think the critics don't understand what a variance decomposition is; the critics think the biologists are sneaking normative conclusions in the back door. Each side has a list of stock responses, and they've been trading the same list for decades.

My working theory, for what it's worth: the two sides are actually disagreeing about a philosophical question that neither of them states — something like what a statistical measure is *for* — and the heritability coefficient itself is just the arena. When I say h² = 0.8 for milk yield in a herd, I'm making a claim that's explicitly conditional and instrumental: conditional on that herd, that ration, that management regime; instrumental in that I'll use it to pick sires. When someone reports h² for IQ or educational attainment, the number looks identical, but the implicit use is nothing like that, and maybe the fight is really over whether the second use is even coherent. But I might be wrong, and I don't trust my own diagnosis since I'm too close to the breeding side.

So here's what I want from you: act as a philosopher of science with a good grasp of quantitative genetics, and don't just re-explain the old points about gene-environment interaction and the equal environments assumption — I've read all that, I could recite it in my sleep. What I want is a proper diagnosis of why this debate never resolves. Is it verbal, with the two sides meaning different things by "heritable" or by "genetic"? Is it substantive but smuggling in an empirical question nobody's actually run the experiment on? Is it a clash about what would even count as evidence? Is it, as I half suspect, two different questions (a prediction question and an explanation question) living under one coefficient? Or is it really a normative/political disagreement dressed up in statistical clothing, in which case the whole "philosophy of science" framing is just decoration?

Please think it through carefully before answering — maybe start with a quick brainstorm of the candidate diagnoses, then argue for the one you find most plausible, and then tell me honestly what, if anything, would count as progress. If progress requires some new distinction or reframing, actually give me the distinction rather than gesturing at where one might live. And if you think the debate is intractable for a structural reason, say so and explain the reason, don't fudge. Concretely: I'd like to come away knowing whether it's worth my time trying to write something about this from the breeding perspective, or whether I'd just be another voice shouting across the same gap.

No citations or reading lists needed — just the arguments themselves. I'll chase up sources on my own if something sounds right. Length: whatever the argument needs, but I'd rather have four pages of real diagnosis than fifteen of survey.
