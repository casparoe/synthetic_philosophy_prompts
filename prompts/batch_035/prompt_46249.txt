I keep issuing different verdicts on failed long-run predictions, and I want to know whether there is a principled difference between the categories I'm implicitly using or whether I am simply playing favorites with Malthus. Here are my verdicts, case by case. Malthus's claim that population, if unchecked, grows faster than subsistence, so that misery and vice would always reassert themselves: I want to say right but early, since the mechanism still runs and we have merely bought centuries with fossil energy and plant breeding. Paul Ehrlich's 1968 famine forecasts for the 1970s: flatly wrong, and I feel no temptation whatsoever to call them early, even though the mechanism is the same exponential-growth-against-finite-resources one. The Limits to Growth projections of 1972: right but early, like Malthus. Peak oil in the Hubbert tradition: wrong, and worse than wrong, because the predicted date keeps being moved forward as it passes, which strikes me as rescue rather than prediction. Y2K: right in a different way altogether, since the catastrophe did not happen precisely because it was foreseen and remediated. My tentative principle is that a failed prediction is right but early when the mechanism it invoked is still operating and only the timing was misjudged, and merely rescued when its proponents keep re-dating it as events pass it by; but this cannot be right, because the mechanism is still operating in every one of these cases, including Ehrlich's and Hubbert's, so the principle cannot explain why my verdicts on them differ from my verdict on Malthus. So please give me your best account of the distinction between right-but-early, falsified-and-rescued, and right-by-being-prevented, and whether my Malthus verdict is defensible at all or just the rescue instinct in respectable dress. Answer in exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) your principle, stated precisely enough to be applied by someone who disagrees with you; (2) which of my verdicts it overturns, and why that one rather than another; (3) the runner-up principle, and which verdicts it saves that the winner does not; (4) what your principle says about two cases I have not mentioned — a 1970s forecast of catastrophic cooling from industrial aerosols by the year 2000, and a 1990s prediction that India's growth would stall for demographic reasons; (5) the point at which your principle collapses into an unfalsifiable license to keep predicting the same thing indefinitely. Do not soften the fifth point to spare my Malthus verdict.
