Here are my gut verdicts on which moments count as the "hinge of history" -- meaning roughly the times when what people did then had wildly disproportionate influence on the whole future, not just the next few centuries -- and I want to know if there's a principle behind them.

If we're shortly before the point where AI systems could permanently lock in some set of values for the whole cosmos, that's clearly a hinge: what gets locked in is locked in forever, and it could go very differently depending on choices made in a decade or two. The era when nuclear weapons first appeared and we could have (and maybe still could) wiped ourselves out: hinge, or at least close. The spread of a tiny religious movement in the eastern Mediterranean that ended up shaping the values of billions of people for millennia: hinge, yes. The invention of agriculture: no, doesn't feel like a hinge to me even though its consequences were enormous. Same for the industrial revolution honestly -- hugely consequential but I don't have the hinge feeling about it. The moment self-replicating life first appeared on earth: definitely not a hinge, which is weird because it's plausibly the most consequential event ever. The Black Death: not a hinge, even though it could plausibly have ended European civilization and changed everything downstream. And here's one I go back and forth on: a random decade in the Stone Age when humanity very nearly went extinct (say we're down to a few thousand breeding pairs) -- hinge or not?

So my question: is there a coherent thing I'm tracking here? My tentative guess is it's something like "times when the long-run future was both very contingent AND up for grabs by deliberate action" -- which would explain why the origin of life isn't a hinge (no agents around) and why agriculture isn't (basically inevitable once you have the climate and the species, and nobody was choosing it as a package deal for humanity's trajectory). But that principle has problems. It makes "hinge" partly depend on how inevitable things were, and I'm honestly not confident the printing press or industrialization were more inevitable than agriculture. It also threatens to make my own era count by definition, which feels suspiciously convenient -- like every generation thinks it's the hinge, and maybe the belief itself is bad evidence.

So: give me the principle (or a couple of candidates), tell me which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and which I should give up, say what it predicts for a case I haven't mentioned, and if you can, address whether the fact that people in every era are tempted to believe they're at the hinge is actually evidence against us being at one or just an irrelevant bit of psychology. Sorry this got long, I've been arguing about it with my brother for weeks.
