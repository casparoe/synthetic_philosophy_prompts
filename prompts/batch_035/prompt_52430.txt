The question:

At the end of the week, a teacher announces: there will be an exam next week, on exactly one of Monday through Friday, and on the morning of the exam day you will not know that the exam is that day. The students reason backward: if the exam were Friday, then by Thursday night they would know it must be Friday, so a Friday exam would violate the announcement; so Friday is out. With Friday gone, Thursday becomes the last possible day, and the same reasoning eliminates it; and so on back to Monday. So, the students conclude, there can be no exam at all — and they stop studying. The exam is then held on Wednesday, and they are genuinely surprised. (i) Reconstruct the students' argument as carefully as you can. (ii) Explain where, if anywhere, the argument goes wrong. (iii) Say whether the teacher's announcement can be true, and what this shows about the paradox.

The student's answer:

(i) The announcement says two things: exactly one of Monday through Friday will have an exam, and on the morning of the exam day the students won't know it's that day. The students' argument: suppose the exam is Friday. Then by Thursday night, having seen no exam Monday through Thursday, the students can infer it must be Friday. So on Friday morning they would know it's exam day, and a Friday exam would violate the announcement. Friday is out. But with Friday gone, Thursday becomes the last possible day; by Wednesday night the same reasoning lets the students deduce Thursday, so Thursday is out too. Repeating this eliminates Wednesday, Tuesday, and Monday. So no day is available, the announcement can't be satisfied, and there will be no exam.

(ii) The mistake is at the Friday step, and Quine's classic discussion of the paradox locates it there. Quine showed that an announcement of this kind is self-contradictory: the teacher can never truthfully make it. It works like the liar paradox — just as a sentence saying of itself that it is false cannot be true, the teacher's announcement cannot be true on any day. So the students were right that no exam could occur; the teacher simply lied.

(iii) Once we see the announcement is self-contradictory, the paradox dissolves. On Wednesday the students, having concluded there would be no exam, didn't expect one, so the exam was a surprise in the ordinary sense, and the announcement turned out to be true after all. The lesson is that the students reasoned from a premise they themselves had refuted.
