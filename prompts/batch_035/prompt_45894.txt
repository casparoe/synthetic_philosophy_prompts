Every month I host a small rationalist meetup — I've been around the LessWrong-adjacent blogosphere since the Sequences were still being serialized, so the room skews toward people with strong opinions about forecasting, calibration, and when it's fair to call a theory dead. This month's talk was on long-range predictions, and afterward two of the regulars, Dara and Marcus, got into a disagreement I couldn't referee, about whether Malthus was "right but early."

Dara opened by saying that Malthus is the textbook case of a falsified theory: the 1798 Essay predicted that because population grows geometrically while food supply grows arithmetically, unchecked population growth would hold humanity at subsistence, and instead the two centuries that followed saw population increase roughly eightfold while average living standards exploded. A theory whose central prediction came out exactly backwards has been refuted, she said, and everything since has been a motivated attempt to un-refute it. Marcus replied that this conflates a mechanism with a timetable: Malthus's claim was that population presses against subsistence unless checked by moral restraint or relieved by innovation, and the specific rescuers of the interim — coal, the Haber-Bosch process, the Green Revolution — were contingent windfalls that the theory never asserted would never arrive, so the honest verdict is "sound mechanism, wrong timing," and a mechanism that merely needed more time hasn't been falsified, just delayed. Dara shot back that "right but early" is precisely the rescue move that makes doom theories unfalsifiable: by that standard the Population Bomb authors, whose predicted famines never materialized, were right but early, the Millerites were right but early, and no failed prediction with a date attached could ever count against anything. Marcus countered that Popper's model is simply too crude for structural long-run claims, where the timing depends on variables like the rate of innovation that the theory never claimed to pin down, and that the opposite bias is just as bad — extrapolating one decade of falling metal prices into "resources are effectively infinite," as in the Simon-Ehrlich wager, is the same category error with the sign flipped. Dara's last word was that rescue has to cost something: a theory that survives every missed date by redescribing itself as early has bought immunity too cheaply, and she wanted to know what Malthusianism has ever forbidden. Marcus answered that it forbids plenty — e.g., sustained exponential growth in per-capita welfare for a closed population with unrestricted fertility and fixed agricultural land — and that a prohibition without a date is still a real prohibition.

Both of them kept making moves that sounded right to me, which is why I couldn't referee. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each substantive claim in their back-and-forth, say whether it is right, wrong, or right-with-an-important-caveat, and justify the rating. Then address the underlying issue: is there a principled line between a legitimate "right but early" and a degenerating rescue of a failed prediction, what would either side have to concede to draw it, and where does the actual Malthus case fall relative to that line? Finally, state your overall conclusion in a single final line of the form "Verdict: ..." naming who is right about what.
