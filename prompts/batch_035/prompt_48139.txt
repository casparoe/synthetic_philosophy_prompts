Hey, so I'm a backend/systems engineer, zero philosophy training, everything I know about ethics I picked up from blog posts. A friend of mine who did a philosophy degree keeps bringing up this "utility monster" thing — I gather it's Nozick's objection to utilitarianism, and as I understand it the idea is: imagine a creature that gets vastly more welfare out of any given resource than anyone else does. A utilitarian who just sums everyone's welfare and maximizes the total then has to hand basically everything over to the monster and let everyone else live at subsistence, and that seems obviously wrong, so something's busted in the view. I think I deal with a version of this at work, and I want you to translate the philosophical debate into my vocabulary so I can find out whether I've understood it or am fooling myself.

Here's my world: we run a shared cluster, and a scheduler has to divide CPU, memory, network between a bunch of services. Different services get different value per unit of resource; the value gets normalized into a common unit (latency targets hit, revenue, whatever the SLO is converted into points), and the naive scheduler is exactly a utilitarian — pick the allocation that maximizes total points. And yes, we have literally had utility monsters: some badly designed batch job that wins huge from every extra core, so the sum-maximizing scheduler starves everything else until someone patches in a fairness constraint. The fixes we actually use are proportional fairness, max-min fairness, hard floors (every service gets a minimum allocation no matter how little it produces with it), and sometimes just capping how fast any one service can grow its share.

So, the request. Restate the utility monster debate in this vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Treat each person as a service with a utility curve over resources, the utilitarian as a sum-maximizing scheduler, the monster as a service whose curve is superlinear. Then translate the standard anti-utilitarian replys one by one — the "separateness of persons" complaint (that's Rawls, right?), the idea of minimum thresholds, the "everyone deserves a fair share" idea, and Nozick's own point — into scheduler terms: proportional fairness, floors, caps, etc. For each one, tell me what carries over, what you had to distort to make it fit, and what can't be expressed in my vocabulary at all.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Two questions. First: in my world there's an obvious answer to "which aggregation function is correct?" — whoever owns the system writes the spec. The fairness constraints are there because the owners, or the customers, would simply not accept a cluster that starves them, so the aggregation is ultimately justified by appeal to something outside the system, namely what the stakeholders will tolerate. Does the philosophical debate have anything like that? Is "don't be a utilitarian" just "the people wouldn't accept it", or is the philosophers' point something my spec-author story can't capture? Second: my gut says the monster case is just bad engineering — sum was the wrong aggregation, fine, pick a different one. But the philosophers seem to think the monster reveals something about *aggregating* at all, not just about the sum. Is that a real difference, or are they just not used to the idea that there's a designer? Be honest about which side of the translation is doing the work.
