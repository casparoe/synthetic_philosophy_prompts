I serve on my city council, and next month we vote on a combined affordability package: a municipal minimum wage phased up over three years to roughly two-thirds of the city's median wage, and a cap on rents for all units built before a fixed cutoff year, set at current rents adjusted for inflation. One faction argues the two measures are natural companions — both shield residents from "the market" — while the opposing faction argues that whatever one thinks of helping low-income residents, both measures are condemned by the same economic principle: interfering with prices in markets whose problems are better addressed at their source. Draft the written opinion I will circulate to the full council before the vote: it should recommend a concrete course of action (adopt both, reject both, or adopt a modified version — specify the modification and why it matters), with reasoning that engages the strongest version of each side's position rather than a strawman.

In particular, the memo should confront whether the case for or against a price floor in the labor market is genuinely parallel to the case for or against a price ceiling in the housing market, or whether the relevant differences break the symmetry — for instance, who actually bears the incidence of each intervention, differences in short-run versus long-run supply elasticity, the possibility of monopsony power among employers versus the market position of landlords, whether existing tenants versus would-be future tenants are helped or harmed differently than incumbent workers versus job-seekers, and the "second-best" point that a market already departing from the competitive ideal may not be made worse off by further intervention. The memo should also address the ethical structure of the disagreement: whether price controls are best understood as redistribution, as regulation of exploitation or price gouging, or as violations of a liberty interest in setting one's own prices.

Please write as a philosopher-economist addressing a thoughtful but non-specialist council, and think carefully before committing to a recommendation — begin by laying out the strongest considerations pulling in each direction. Structure the memo to clearly separate (i) points on which well-informed people across the spectrum largely agree, (ii) genuinely contested empirical and normative claims, and (iii) the value judgments on which your recommendation ultimately rests. Do not present contested views as consensus, and where the relevant empirical magnitudes (e.g., likely disemployment effects, effects on new construction) are unsettled, say so explicitly and explain how your recommendation is robust — or fragile — with respect to how those uncertainties resolve.
