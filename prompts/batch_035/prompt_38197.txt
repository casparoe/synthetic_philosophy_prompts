longtime lurker here (lesswrong, alignment forum, occasionally the EA forum, back in the day even the old sl4 list), and I've got a half-baked idea I'd like you to turn into an actual research proposal. no need for citations, I can dig those up myself later.

the idea: everyone in interpretability circles knows the "ontology identification" problem at this point -- roughly, that even if we fully reverse-engineer a trained network's internals, there's no guarantee the concepts it uses (whatever its "tree" or "deceptive" or "human-wants-me-to-do-X" detectors actually track) map cleanly onto the concepts WE care about. the ELK writeup gestures at this with the smart lab assistant story: the assistant knows more than it can report, and its internal ontology might not even contain the thing we want it to report on. the usual takes are either (a) we need to somehow train the ontology into the model, (b) we need to find the "natural" joints and hope they're close to ours, or (c) despair.

but there's this totally different tradition that had a go at the "concepts don't have clean definitions" problem like 70 years before any of this: Wittgenstein's family resemblance stuff, and the language games stuff too. games was the famous example -- no set of necessary and sufficient conditions for being a game, just overlapping criss-crossing similarities, and yet we coordinate on the word "game" just fine across a whole culture. and it's not just Wittgenstein, the prototype theory crowd in cog sci ran with basically this idea empirically.

so my question is: does the family-resemblance / prototype-theory picture of concepts give us anything *actionable* for ontology identification, or is it just a vibe? like, maybe the trained network's concepts are inevitably going to be family-resemblance concepts rather than definitional ones, and maybe that's actually fine or even good, because family-resemblance concepts can be *stretched* to cover new cases by tracing similarity chains -- meaning the "our ontology vs the model's ontology" gap might be bridgeable by something like translating along similarities rather than finding an exact dictionary. or maybe it's the opposite: maybe prototype concepts are exactly the kind of thing that drifts under distributional shift, so they're the WORST case for the reporter-style approaches.

here's what I want: a proposal for, let's say, a 3-4 month independent research project (I've got some spare time and possibly a microgrant) that takes this crossover seriously. it should state the question precisely enough that it could come out either way -- right now I honestly can't tell if the Wittgenstein angle is load-bearing or just decorative. it should explain the method: what would I actually DO, week by week or month by month, given that this is mostly a conceptual/theoretical project and not a "train models in a garage" one (though toy experiments are allowed if cheap). it should say what a good deliverable looks like -- like, is it a taxonomy of cases where family resemblance helps vs hurts? a sharpened argument that the whole analogy is confused? -- and crucially what would count as the project FAILING or being a waste of time, since I'd rather know that now.

one thing I'd like you to take a stance on in the proposal: the obvious objection is that Wittgenstein was describing how humans use words in a social practice with correction and training and shared stakes, and there's no reason any of that transfers to reading weights off a network. if that objection kills the idea, the proposal should say so and pivot to whatever survives. but don't just conclude "philosophy unrelated to ML, do not cross-post" -- figure out if there's a there there.

also feel free to tell me if my framing of the ontology identification problem itself is off or strawmanned, that's part of what I want the proposal to nail down. keep it to roughly one page, casual is fine but it should be something I could actually send to a funder with minor edits.
