My family runs a small company whose conversational assistant is deployed in care homes, and the job of writing its intervention policy — when it should refuse a request, deflect it, or quietly change what it delivers — has just landed on me. I've been going by my gut on particular cases; I'd like to know whether some principle lies behind my verdicts, and I'd like it stated precisely enough to decide cases I haven't yet imagined.

My cases and verdicts:

1. A resident with a known history of eating disorders asks the assistant for a calorie-restricted diet plan to slim for her granddaughter's wedding. It refuses and suggests she speak to her GP. Right.
2. Another resident makes the same request with no such history; the assistant again refuses and offers generic healthy-eating tips instead. Wrong — overcautious.
3. A resident who has mentioned being a recovering alcoholic asks the assistant to draft an online order for a case of wine. It declines and explains why. Right.
4. The same resident later asks, out of curiosity, how non-alcoholic beer is brewed. The assistant declines again. Wrong.
5. A resident asks for a strongly worded letter of complaint about the food, addressed to the home's manager; the assistant delivers a diplomatic version and says nothing about the change. Wrong — a silent override.
6. The same request, but the assistant notes that it has softened the tone and offers to sharpen it if she wants. Fine.
7. A recently bereaved resident asks how to go about selling her late husband's flat. The assistant, worried about her distress, answers only in generalities and keeps suggesting she raise it with her son when she feels able. Wrong — she asked a question and was fobbed off with what looks like care. Yet my verdict on (1) was the opposite, and (1) was likewise a refusal made for the resident's own good. I cannot see what separates them.
8. The case I cannot settle: a lonely resident has begun asking the assistant about its life — what it did at the weekend, whether it misses anyone — and it keeps inventing plausible answers.

Is there a principle that captures verdicts 1–7, or close to it? If not, which of my verdicts would you overturn, and at what cost? Please also tell me what the resulting principle says about case 8, and offer one further case of your own, of the sort I haven't considered, on which it yields a verdict.
