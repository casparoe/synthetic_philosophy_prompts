There's a post I keep seeing recced on rationalist-adjacent blogs arguing that imprecise probabilities are a dead end, and I mostly found it convincing but its been nagging at me. The gist of the post's summary of the view:

"The case for imprecise probabilities is that when you're genuinely ignorant, pretending to have a sharp credence is fake precision. So instead of saying p(doom) = 0.2 you say it's somewhere in [0.1, 0.4]. But notice what you've actually done: you've introduced a second-order probability, uncertainty about your own credence, and any coherent agent will just integrate over that and recover a single number. The set-of-probabilities framing is an accounting trick, not a new epistemology. And decision-theoretically it's worse: expected utility is undefined once your probabilities are a set, so the agent can't choose at all, and every proposed fix — like taking the worst case over the set — is just a sharp probability with a pessimism term bolted on. Ellsberg's urns show that humans pay a premium to avoid ambiguity, but we already knew humans are inconsistent; building a whole formalism around a bias isn't progress."

I'm pretty sympathetic — I came up on the "every question has an answer, it's fine to be uncertain about your uncertainty about your uncertainty, it's sharp all the way down" school — but I've also read enough griping about people strawmanning the imprecise crowd that I want a sanity check before I cite this post in an argument. Which parts of that summary would someone who actually works on imprecise probabilities sign off on, which parts conflate things they'd insist on keeping apart, and does the "it collapses back into a point estimate / it's just a bias" conclusion actually follow from whatever parts are true? Also, and I genuinely don't know the answer to this one: is "take the worst case over the set" a fair characterization of their decision rules, or do they have rules that do something less obviously collapse-y than that?
