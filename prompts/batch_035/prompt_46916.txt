I'm a mechanistic interpretability researcher, and I want the philosophical debate over which computations matter morally restated in my working vocabulary rather than the philosophers'. In my day job I think in terms of features, circuits, attention heads, causal ablations, steering vectors, and attribution methods; the natural unit of analysis for me is the localized, intervenable computation, and I take it as fairly obvious that the interesting properties of a system live at that level rather than in the substrate or the weights considered on their own. The questions I keep circling back to are, as best I can state them: whether it's functional organization rather than substrate that could make a system a subject of experience; the boundary problem, i.e., even if some subset of a system's computations gives rise to experience, it's deeply obscure which subset, since the same system can be carved up at many granularities and the candidate subsystems overlap and interact; and the family of gradual-replacement thought experiments in which neurons are swapped, one by one, for components that preserve input-output behavior, raising the question of whether experience could fade or relocate even while the function is held fixed.

Please restate this debate in interpretability terms as faithfully as you can. My tentative translations: functional organization becomes something like circuit-level equivalence under intervention — two systems count the same if ablating corresponding components produces corresponding deficits, regardless of whether the work is done in MLPs or attention or something else entirely; the boundary problem looks like an attribution problem — deciding which components are "really" implementing a given capability when the computation is distributed, redundant, and smeared across layers; and the replacement thought experiments look like swapping an MLP sublayer for a different implementation with matched ablation behavior, which in my world just sounds like refactoring. Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about: what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion, and what has no expression in my vocabulary at all.

One suspicion I'd like you to address head-on. Attribution methods answer a causal question — which components does this output depend on, and how much — but the philosophers seem to be asking something normative or constitutive: not which computations the behavior depends on, but which ones the experiencing subject consists of, and which ones a moral patient is. If that's right, then the translation fails not because my toolkit is underpowered but because it answers a different question, and no amount of better attribution methods will get me from "these components carry the pain feature" to "these are the ones that matter." Tell me whether the untranslatable residue points to something genuinely missing from my framework, or whether it instead reveals that the philosophical questions were less clear than they looked — and if you think the boundary problem in particular dissolves or transforms under my vocabulary, I want to know exactly where and how.
