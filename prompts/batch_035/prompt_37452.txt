Please argue that self-deception is possible by developing a "bureaucratic" picture of the mind: the person is not a single epistemic agent but a loose federation of subpersonal processes that handle information with different priorities and access rights. On this picture, the traditional paradox of self-deception — that one would have to simultaneously know p in order to hide it and not know p in order to be deceived by it — dissolves, since one process can possess what another is structurally denied, just as a records office can bury a file without the department acting on it ever reading it. Address two objections in detail. First, the individuation objection: if the mind is genuinely partitioned, what results is not self-deception but ordinary deception between distinct subagents, so the "self" of self-deception has been explained away rather than accounted for. Second, the differentiation objection: the bureaucratic picture seems unable to distinguish genuine self-deception from ordinary motivated error or self-serving bias, which nobody would call deception at all. In your response, clarify what role, if any, intention plays on your account, and conclude by explaining what marks off genuine self-deception from these neighboring phenomena.
