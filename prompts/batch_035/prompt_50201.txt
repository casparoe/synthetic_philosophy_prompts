At our department's weekly political theory workshop, two of my fellow PhD students — one in formal theory, one in political philosophy — spent the entire coffee break arguing about whether it's rational to vote, and the rest of us couldn't resolve it.

It started when Maya, the formal theorist, mentioned she wasn't sure she'd bother voting in the upcoming election. She said the case is basically airtight: in a large electorate, the probability that your vote decides the outcome is astronomically small — on the order of one in millions — so even a large benefit conditional on your side winning, multiplied by that probability, rounds to zero. Since voting has real costs (registration, queuing, informing yourself), the expected value is negative, and the rational person abstains. She added that the famous repair for this argument — tacking a "citizen's duty" term onto the calculus — is a fudge: with a free-floating term for whatever motivation you happen to have, the theory can accommodate any behavior whatsoever and thus explains nothing.

Jonah, the philosopher, said the argument smuggles in egoism. The calculus is normally written as if the only payoff that counts is the benefit to the voter herself, and of course that looks absurd. But if you take seriously the idea that the outcome of the election matters to millions of people, then the relevant benefit isn't your private gain but the total social gain, and even a probability of one in ten million times a huge total benefit is a non-trivial number. On that arithmetic, an impartially motivated voter could rationally vote even after subtracting the costs, and Maya's "airtight case" just amounts to refusing to do the multiplication across the whole population.

Maya said she'd take the deal, but the price is that the theory then licenses far more than voting. If tiny-probability, large-total-payoff gambles are worth pursuing, then actions with similar or better leverage — giving a small amount of money or an hour of canvassing to the better campaign — should dominate voting in the decision calculus, and a genuinely expected-value-maximizing citizen should be doing those things constantly. Since almost nobody, including the theorists who make this argument, behaves that way, she said the impartial-calculus defense isn't really an expected-value argument at all; it's a rationalization of a practice people were going to keep up anyway.

Jonah replied that Maya was treating every feature of voting as instrumental when at least one isn't. Voting, he said, is partly expressive — like cheering at a football match, where no one cheers in order to alter the score, and yet the cheering is perfectly sensible. Declaring your allegiance in a public ritual with your fellow citizens is something you can have reason to do regardless of causal efficacy.

Maya's closing shot was that this is just the duty term wearing casual clothes: an unmeasured, content-free benefit that makes any behavior "rational" by fiat, and unfalsifiable for exactly the same reason. Jonah said it was hardly content-free — we know quite a lot about when people vote and what they say it means to them — and that refusing to count expressive value unless it can be measured in dollars is a prejudice of the model, not a finding. Neither would budge, and the workshop convenor had to cut it off to start the next paper.

I found both of them persuasive at different moments and would genuinely like an adjudication rather than a verdict I could have predicted going in. Please go through the exchange move by move — including Maya's initial abstention argument, Jonah's impartial-voter response, Maya's "then do the dominated actions too" rejoinder, and the final exchange about expressive voting versus the duty term — and say who is right at each step and why. I'd also like to know what, if anything, both of them are missing about the paradox of voting as it's usually framed in the literature.
