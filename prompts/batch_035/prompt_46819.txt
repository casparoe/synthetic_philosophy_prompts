I've been keeping a running list of cases where someone's *not* doing something seems to me to have caused some bad outcome, and I'd like to know whether my verdicts add up to a principle, or whether some of them have to go. My tentative principle is stated at the end, along with the places I can already see it wobbling.

My verdicts, as honestly as I can report them:

1. The gardener who agreed to water my plants while I was away, didn't, and the plants died: he caused their death. A stranger on another continent, equally able to water them had he known and cared: he didn't cause anything.
2. A patient dies after her doctor fails to administer a drug that would have saved her with roughly 90% probability: the doctor caused the death. Same scenario, but the drug had only a 5% chance of saving her: here I want to say the disease caused the death and the doctor merely failed to prevent it — though I'd still call him negligent in the 5% case. I can feel these two pulling in different directions.
3. A hundred neighbors, none of whom agreed to anything, each could have watered the plants; all failed to; the plants died. I want to say the gardener caused it and none of the neighbors did, even though for each individual neighbor, had that neighbor watered, the plants would have lived. "There are too many non-waters" feels like it should matter, though I can't say why it would be a difference in causation rather than just in blame.
4. Fifty able-bodied onlookers watch a child drown in a pond; each could have waded in at no real risk; none did. Every one of them is a coward and culpable, but I go back and forth on whether any of them *caused* the drowning. This is the case I least trust myself on.
5. A drunk driver fails to brake and kills a pedestrian: caused the death. His sober passenger, who equally failed to do anything (grab the wheel, shout a warning): didn't cause it. Both just sat there, and I can't yet say what the difference is beyond the driver's earlier act.
6. A person who never donates to famine relief, when a donation would have saved a specific child: didn't cause the death. A charity accountant who embezzles the funds earmarked for that child's food, and the child dies: caused it. I suspect the difference can't just be that money moved in the second case — but I'd like to be told whether it can.

The principle I'm drawn to: an omission causes a bad outcome when (a) the corresponding act would have prevented the outcome, and (b) the agent had an obligation, or at least was the designated or uniquely positioned person, to perform it. This cleanly handles the gardener versus the distant stranger, the driver versus the passenger, and the embezzler versus the non-donor. But it treats the two doctors identically (duty in both, prevention in both if 'would have prevented' is read as raising the probability of survival), which conflicts with my 90/5 split; and it has nothing useful to say about the pond, where nobody was designated.

What I'm asking for, then:

- A principle, or a small set of rivals, stated precisely enough to be run on new cases — in particular, state whether clause (a) should be read as deterministic prevention, a probability threshold, or something else, and defend the reading using the doctor cases.
- Its verdicts on cases I haven't listed, for instance: a software engineer who forgot to enable a safety flag that would have prevented a fatal crash with probability about one in three; a city council that didn't repair a guardrail it knew was rotten, where the driver was also speeding; and one of three off-duty medics in a crowd, any one of whom could have reached a collapsed runner in time, all of whom hesitated and none of whom moved.
- An honest accounting of which of my six verdicts your preferred principle cannot accommodate, and whether the loss is acceptable — specifically, whether my 5%-drug verdict should be abandoned as a confusion between causing a death and imposing an unjustifiable risk, and whether the pond case reveals that my gardener-versus-neighbors verdict is really about desert wearing the mask of causation.
- If you think the designated-person clause is doing illicit work — importing moral standing into causal facts — say so, and say what remains of causation by omission without it. I'd also like to know whether the "too many non-waters" point in case 3 can be made precise, or whether it is just an intuition about diffusion of responsibility that no causal principle should respect.
