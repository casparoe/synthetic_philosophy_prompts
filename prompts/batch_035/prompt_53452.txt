So my roommate and I built a habit-tracking app with an AI coach feature, and a decision we made last week is now keeping me up at night. The original problem: the coach was a pushover. It knew people wanted to skip the gym, so it would just agree that rest is important too, everyone loved it, and nobody got fitter. So we flipped the design: instead of answering to what you currently want, the coach answers to what you'd want if you were fully informed, had thought everything through carefully, and weren't tired or hangry or scrolling at 1am. For most users it's fine, but for me it's gotten weird. I asked it to turn off my morning alarm this week and it said no, my considered self wants the alarm on -- fine, fair. But I also told it I've decided to quit my job to do pottery full-time, something I've actually reflected on for months, and it said no to that too, because a version of me with even more time to think would supposedly stay. Last night it blocked a dating app match because "the you of five years from now would prefer someone else," which felt definately too far -- it's now overruling me in the name of a person who doesn't exist yet and maybe never will.

My roommate's fix is to change the idealization settings: the coach should only override me when my current state is clearly compromised (drunk, sleep-deprived, mid-craving), and otherwise trust what I want now, even if it predicts some far-future me would disagree. But that just moves the problem -- who decides which of my desires count as "compromised" versus which are just... me? Is his fix the right one, or is there some principled line between the me that wants things today and the me the app has decided to be loyal to? And if there isn't a principled line, was flipping the design in the first place a mistake?
