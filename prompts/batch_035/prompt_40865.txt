I'm taking environmental ethics this semester and for my term paper I've gone pretty far down the lifeboat road, and now I want to know if there's any honest way out, or if I'm just defending a dead metaphor. Let me give you the actual position, because it's been narrowed a lot from the original.

We spent like two weeks taking apart Hardin's 1974 essay in class, and I'm not defending the full package. My view, after absorbing the professor's beatdowns, is roughly this: for cases of sustained transfer — ongoing food aid commitments, permanently open immigration — there's a real capacity constraint, and a community at or past that constraint is permitted, maybe required, to limit entry even when the consequence is people drowning or starving outside, because the alternative is swamping the boat. But I've bolted on two amendments I was basically forced into. First, capacity isn't fixed the way Hardin treated it — newcomers can expand capacity over time (more labor, more ideas, etc.), so my claim is only about the short run, before capacity has any chance to adjust. Second, I've restricted the view to sustained transfer and not one-off rescue: pulling a swimmer out of the water doesn't obligate you to feed her grandchildren, so emergency cases and ongoing obligations come apart, and I only bite the bullet on the ongoing ones.

So, what would count against this thing? I want a real map of where its exposed, not the standard lecture. A few directions I care about:

1. Empirical: what would the world have to look like for the short-run constraint claim to be false — like if capacity actually adjusts fast, or rich countries are nowhere near any constraint worth the name, does my view lose all bite or just some? And is "short run" doing enough work that the view is basically never triggered in practice?

2. The metaphor itself: beyond the obvious "nations aren't boats" stuff, where does the lifeboat framing smuggle in premises no real case has? I've been chewing on one myself — the swimmer is a sudden surprise in the frame, but famine and migration are predictable and ongoing, and we normally think rescue duties and aid duties have totally different structures. Does my whole position rest on blurring that, given that I explicitly split rescue off from aid?

3. Moral: is there a version where the view fails on its own terms — it claims to be about maximizing survivors and actually doesn't? That kind of internal objection is what I'd personally find most persuasive, way more than just "letting swimmers drown is monstrous."

Two meta requests. First, tell me honestly whether I've qualified this into unfalsifiability — the amendments were retreats, and at some point a view that backs up every time you poke it isn't a view anymore, it's a bunker. Is mine still a live position with real content, or a rump? And if it is a bunker, is that fatal for a view of this kind or just normal refinement? Second, please keep seperate what's basically settled among people who actually know this stuff (the Hardin literature, the famine-aid debates, commons problems) from what's genuinely contested between schools — I don't want my paper asserting "philosophers agree X" when X is one side's talking point. If you're not sure which side of that line something falls on, say so rather than picking.
