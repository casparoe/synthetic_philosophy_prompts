I sit on the ethics committee of a memory clinic, and a recurring kind of case has sent me back to Dennett's idea of the self as a center of narrative gravity: not an inner observer or a pearl of consciousness but an abstract posit, like the center of mass of an object, which we (and on his story the brain itself) construct because treating a person as a single narrating center makes behavior intelligible and predictable. He also insists that such posits are real rather than illusory in the dismissive sense — you can balance a wrench on its center of mass — which is part of why the view interests me rather than striking me as one more way of saying the self is nothing.

The case type: a patient signed an advance directive while mildly impaired but still articulate, instructing us to forgo artificial nutrition once she no longer recognizes her family; she has now reached that stage, is placid and affectionate, lights up at music and visits, and her daughter argues that the woman in front of us is not the author of the document and should not be governed by what amounts to a stranger's prior instructions. A colleague on my committee holds that the narrative-gravity view dissolves the whole dispute: if the self is an abstraction rather than a persisting organ, there is no deep fact about whether the present patient is or is not the directive's author, so the committee should set identity talk aside and weigh her current interests directly. That strikes me as proving too much — the same move would void any promise made to one's own future self whenever circumstances change enough.

Setting aside the question of which reading of Dennett is historically correct, describe the five most important implications the center-of-narrative-gravity conception of the self would have for how our committee should actually decide these cases — among them whether advance directives gain or lose authority under it, what happens to the daughter's appeal to the present-day patient if the self is an abstraction, whether the disanalogy between selves and centers of mass (which have precise mathematical definitions and no interests) breaks the analogy where it matters, and whether the view pushes us toward narrative continuity rather than biological continuity as the marker of who counts. Rank the five from most to least important for our actual policy decisions. Give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion — begin directly with item 1.
