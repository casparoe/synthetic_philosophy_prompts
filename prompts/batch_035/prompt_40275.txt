I run content policy for a mid-sized social platform, and the chunk of my job I need help with is ad review — specifically the escalations where users or regulators have flagged something as "manipulative." My team is three analysts plus me, none of us have philosophy training, we get maybe 15-20 of these a week, and we have to disposition each within a couple business days because advertisers get furious when their campaigns stay paused. Right now the unofficial rubric is basically vibes, and it's failing us in two directions: we let through stuff that later looks embarrassing (a fitness app buying audiences of teen girls who searched "thinspo"-adjacent terms), and we pull stuff that on reflection was fine (a charity ad with a genuinely true story about a specific kid, that just made people cry). Legal only sees the cases we propose to act on, so anything we mark "manipulative" goes to them and they want reasons, not intuitions.

Here's the conceptual material I'd want the procedure built from, such as I understand it. The most promising line I've come across in the literature is that the mark of manipulation isn't giving someone bad reasons — it's influencing them in a way that bypasses or exploits their reflective capacities, working through mechanisms that are insensitive to whether the person would endorse the influence if they saw it clearly. So a sad but accurate photo engages you, an engineered drip of fake "someone is viewing this listing right now" notifications exploits you. But my analysts immediately broke this when we tried it informally: almost all effective persuasion works partly through non-reflective channels (urgency, framing, emotional resonance), and if we call all of that manipulation we'd have to ban basically all advertising, which is not a decision I get to make. There's also the question of whether the vulnerability matters — exploiting a phobia seems different from exploiting the fact that humans like discounts, and I don't know if that difference can survive in a checklist. And separately, whether the claims involved are true seems like it should matter, but then we hit the weight-loss ad case where everything is literally true and it still feels off.

So what I want is a decision procedure my team can actually run in fifteen minutes per case: an ordered list of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a "not manipulation, release it" or an "act on it," and — this matters most — the points where it should stop and hand the call to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. It has to be runnable by someone with no philosophy background, so tell me which theoretical distinctions genuinely need to survive the simplification and which can be dropped. Then run it on three cases for me: (1) an e-commerce ad showing "only 3 left in stock" where the inventory claim is factually accurate but the counter resets whenever it hits zero, (2) the charity ad with the real story and photo of one specific child, and (3) the fitness app campaign targeted at users our ad system has inferred have body-image insecurity, with all claims in the ad true. On at least one of those I expect the procedure to give an answer my own team (or our product side, which makes money off targeting) won't like — that's fine, I want to see it happen, and I want you to be upfront about where the procedure will systematically misfire and what it flatly cannot settle, e.g. whether we treat some audiences as more protectable than others and whether that's a values question the checklist has no business deciding.
