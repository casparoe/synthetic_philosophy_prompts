I hold what I take to be the orthodox view of Wittgenstein's private language argument: the target is not privacy of subject matter — talk about one's own sensations is obviously fine — but the picture in which a word could get its meaning from a purely mental, private ostensive definition, the diary case in which a subject records a recurring sensation with a sign and then, relying on memory alone, later re-identifies the sensation and applies the sign again. On my view, the argument works: in that setup there is no distinction between the subject's sincere application of the sign and a correct application, and without that distinction there is no meaning, only ritual. The upshot, as I understand it, is that normativity for words is essentially communal — grounded in training, agreement in judgments, and the possibility of public criteria for mistake.

Tell me what would count against this position, and rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. I'm less interested in the reflex riposte that I surely know what I mean by my own pain than in the more serious exposure. Some candidates I want assessed, though you should add ones I've missed:

1. Empirical cases of incipient language without a community: deaf children raised without linguistic input who develop home sign systems with stable vocabulary and grammar, twins' idioglossias, or individuals in prolonged isolation who develop idiosyncratic notation that they later use successfully. Do these show that a single individual can bootstrap enough regularity to fix meaning, or does the orthodox reply — that such systems are only contingently private, could in principle be taught to others, and that their apparent stability borrows normativity from general facts about human responses — hold up? Where exactly does the contingent/logically private distinction do its work, and is it doing real work or insulating the thesis?

2. The memory objection run in reverse: if memory is too unreliable to sustain a private ostensive definition, it seems too unreliable to sustain the public learner's grasp of a rule between corrections either. Does the argument generalize into a full rule-following skepticism about all language (the skeptical reading, associated with Kripke, on which there is no fact of the matter about anyone's meaning anything)? If it does, is that a reductio of the argument, an acceptable conclusion, or evidence that the argument was never meant to be an argument at all but a therapy against a picture we needn't accept?

3. The solitary refiner: an individual who builds a language-like system entirely alone — inventing terms, recording definitions, correcting themselves against earlier entries — with no community at any stage. If we're inclined to say they mean something, what exactly did Wittgenstein's setup rule out, and was ruling it out legitimate or question-begging?

For each defeater, say what a committed defender of the orthodox reading would concede as genuinely counting against it versus dismiss as missing the point, and be honest about which dismissals are principled and which are ad hoc. Finally, give your assessment of whether the position, as I've stated it, has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if it has, whether that is a problem for a view of this kind or a natural feature of remarks that were never offered as a thesis. No reading lists or citations; I want the reasoning, not the literature.
