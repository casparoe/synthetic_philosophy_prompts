I'm taking an upper-level epistemology seminar, and we've just moved from Descartes' dreaming argument to G. E. Moore's 1939 paper "Proof of an External World." Everyone in the class can recite the famous bit at the end — Moore holds up his hands, gestures, and takes himself to have proven, validly and from premises known with certainty, that two human hands exist and therefore that an external world exists. But our instructor has told us that the real action is in the opening stretch of the paper, which she says is the part philosophers skip, and I have to admit I'm lost in it. Below is my best effort at summarizing, in my own words, the moves that precede the hand proof; I'd like you to help me work out what each of them is doing, and how they're supposed to connect to the proof that follows. (I don't have the text with me right now, so please treat the following as a paraphrase rather than a quotation — if I've gotten a detail wrong, correct it, but I'm fairly confident about the structure.)

Moore starts by complaining that although philosophers have talked for centuries about proving the existence of an "external world," he can't find anywhere a clear statement of what the phrase means or what exactly is supposed to be in doubt. He brings up Kant's remark — the one about it being a scandal that philosophers hadn't yet managed to prove the existence of things outside us, which Kant contrasted with the scandal of natural philosophy — and says that even here he isn't sure what Kant meant by the key expressions. He then introduces, on Kant's behalf, a pair of distinctions that are supposed to give the question determinate content. First, there is a difference between things that are, as he puts it, to be met with in space and things that are merely presented in space: when you press on your eyeball and see a double image, or when you see the afterimage of a light, something is presented in space, but you can't touch it, walk around it, or meet with it — so the afterimage is not, in his sense, a thing to be met with in space, and hence not part of the external world. Second, there is the class of things external to our experience in another sense: things that exist now but have never been observed, or that existed before anyone did. Moore seems to think that once we fix the first sense — the external world being, roughly, the things to be met with in space — he can prove such things exist; and in fact he ends up conceding that by this standard dreams and dream-objects are just as much a part of the external world as waking experiences, which frankly sounds like it gives the game away to the skeptic. He also flags, near the end, an odd limitation: his proof establishes that things to be met with in space existed at the moment of the lecture, but he says he cannot similarly prove that they existed five minutes earlier — that the past is a further question, requiring a different proof.

So here is what I'd like help with. First, what work is the met-with-in-space versus presented-in-space distinction really doing? Is Moore's thought that the skeptic's challenge only becomes well-posed once we exclude afterimages and sense-data, and if so, why can't the skeptic just refuse to accept that classification? Second, what am I to make of his remark that dreams are part of the external world — is that a clever deflation of the dreaming argument, a crude stipulation, or a concession that the proof no longer addresses the worry Descartes raised? Third, why does Moore insist that the question "is there an external world?" cannot even be asked, let alone answered, until we've defined our terms — and given that he spends so much of the paper on that preliminary task, is the hand proof better read as the punchline or as an illustration whose real point is the earlier semantic housekeeping? Finally, if you can, tell me how the paper's structure is supposed to fit together as a whole: what does the opening's fussing over Kant's expressions buy the conclusion, and would the proof be weaker without it? I'd appreciate an answer that takes both the letter and the spirit of the paper seriously, including where Moore himself seems unsure what he means.
