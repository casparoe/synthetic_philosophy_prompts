I help run a small community foundation in a mid-sized city — I came to this with no academic training, just years of organizing neighborhood fundraising drives, and over that time I've arrived, on my own, at a position roughly like this: our giving dollars should go wherever they do the most measurable good per dollar, which usually means evidence-backed global health charities rather than local projects, and a person whose talents could earn a high salary can do more good by giving a large share of it away than by working directly for a cause. Next month I've been invited to present this view to our regional association of community foundations — an audience of maybe forty people, mostly longtime local organizers, a few faith-based charity workers, a retired labor organizer, and two program officers from a social-justice fund. Their sympathies run toward place-based philanthropy, mutual aid, and "changing the system rather than treating symptoms," and several have already told me they find the spreadsheet approach cold and they find the "work in finance and donate the proceeds" idea frankly offensive.

My argument, in brief, is that (1) a dollar has the same moral weight wherever it lands, and a dollar spent on malaria prevention does vastly more good than a dollar spent on a local mural; (2) the discomfort with measurement is mostly a comfort with not having to compare at all; and (3) the point of the career advice isn't to sanctify finance but to make the finance people fund the things this room already cares about.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections this specific audience will actually raise — phrased the way a community organizer or a social-justice program officer would put them, not the way a philosophy professor would — ranked by how much damage each could do to my credibility with the room. For each: a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, what I should concede outright rather than fight, and where my reply is soft, so I don't lean on it too hard. I'm especially worried about the objection that we can only measure a narrow band of what matters, and therefore the measurable crowd out the important — and about whether my "a dollar is a dollar" premise just assumes away the claims of community and loyalty that everyone in that room lives by. Include the one question I'm hoping nobody asks, and tell me what to say if someone asks it anyway.
