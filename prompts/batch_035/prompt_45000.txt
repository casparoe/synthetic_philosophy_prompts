**The exam question (25 points: (a) 6, (b) 7, (c) 6, (d) 6).**

Our department chooses among three or more seminar time slots by plurality with a runoff between the top two candidates, and a proposal is on the table to switch to the Borda count. At the last faculty meeting, a colleague remarked that Arrow's impossibility theorem proves that no ranked-ballot method can be fair, so neither procedure is legitimate, and that if we really cared about legitimacy we would move to approval voting, which sidesteps Arrow entirely.

(a) State Arrow's impossibility theorem precisely, being careful about what kind of object it concerns.
(b) Explain whether, and in what sense, the theorem applies to the two departmental procedures under discussion.
(c) Explain what the independence of irrelevant alternatives condition amounts to for a social choice function as opposed to a social welfare function, and assess whether each of the two procedures satisfies it.
(d) Assess the colleague's remark: does the theorem show that no ranked method is fair, and does approval voting in fact sidestep it?

**The student's answer, reproduced verbatim.**

(a) Arrow's theorem is a result about social welfare functions: mappings that assign to each profile of individual weak orderings of the alternatives a social weak ordering. Assume at least three alternatives and at least two individuals. The conditions are: Unrestricted Domain (every profile of individual orderings is admissible); Pareto (if every individual strictly ranks x above y, the social ordering does too); Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (whether society ranks x above y depends only on the individuals' rankings of x against y); and Non-Dictatorship (no individual is such that the social ranking of every pair always coincides with that individual's ranking of the pair). The theorem: no social welfare function satisfies all four; any social welfare function satisfying Unrestricted Domain, Pareto, and IIA is a dictatorship. The restriction to at least three alternatives is essential, since with exactly two alternatives majority rule satisfies all four conditions.

(b) Strictly speaking the theorem applies to neither procedure, because both are social choice functions — they select a winner — while the theorem concerns social welfare functions, which output complete rankings. It bears on them only through an extension that turns each into a ranking. For Borda there is a natural extension: order the alternatives by point totals. This extension violates IIA. Suppose three voters, alternatives a, b, c, with Borda scores 2, 1, 0. Profile 1: Voter 1: a ≻ b ≻ c; Voter 2: b ≻ a ≻ c; Voter 3: c ≻ a ≻ b. Scores: a = 4, b = 3, c = 2, so society ranks a ≻ b ≻ c. Profile 2: Voter 3's ballot changes to c ≻ b ≻ a, all else fixed. Scores: a = 3, b = 4, c = 2, so society ranks b ≻ a ≻ c. The social ranking of a and b has reversed even though no voter changed their ranking of a against b — a violation of IIA. For plurality with runoff there is no comparably canonical extension to rankings, so it is better evaluated at the level of choice functions, which is part (c).

(c) For social choice functions the analogue of IIA says: if x is chosen at one profile and y is not, and every individual's ranking of x against y is the same at a second profile, then y should not be the choice at the second. Plurality with runoff violates this: whether a candidate reaches the runoff depends on first-place support for other candidates, so who wins can change even when no voter's view of the eventual winner against the actual winner has changed — a candidate can be eliminated in the first round who would have beaten the eventual winner head-to-head. Borda, taken as a choice function, violates the condition for the same reason it violates IIA at the ranking level, as the example in (b) shows.

(d) The colleague overstates the theorem twice. First, "no ranked-ballot method is fair": the theorem shows only that four specific conditions are jointly unsatisfiable, and each condition is individually contestable — IIA in particular has been disputed on the ground that the position of a third alternative in voters' orderings can legitimately bear on a comparison between two others. A method is not shown unfair by the theorem unless one accepts the axioms. Second, "approval voting sidesteps Arrow": approval voting is not a function of ordinal preference profiles at all, since two voters with the same ranking may draw their approval cutoffs differently, so the method operates on richer inputs than the theorem's framework allows for. It sidesteps the theorem by changing the question, not by satisfying conditions the theorem says are jointly unsatisfiable. The colleague is right, though, that strategy is the deeper worry for ranked methods: as Arrow himself later proved, every non-dictatorial choice function on three or more alternatives is manipulable, so switching to approval voting would not get us out of strategic voting anyway.
