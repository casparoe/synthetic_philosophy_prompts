I teach a philosophy of mind course and this spring I'm adding a unit on Dennett's free-floating rationales — reasons that are "out there" in nature even though no mind ever formulated them. To make the seminar discussions livelier I've been running a long-running coffee bet with my old grad school friend Marta, whose instincts about this stuff are uncannily consistent even though neither of us can quite state her rule. The question I keep asking her, case by case, is basically: is there a reason why X is there, such that no reasoner anywhere ever had that reason in mind? Her track record so far: camouflage on a moth's wings, yes; the Great Red Spot of Jupiter, no; the ventilation of a termite mound, yes; a boulder that happens to shelter hikers from rain, no; the fact that blood is red, no — she's insistent that redness is just what falls out of hemoglobin doing its job, nobody selected-for red; the market price of wheat, yes; QWERTY persisting decades after it stopped being a good layout, yes, because its compatibility effects keep it alive; and one of those circuits evolved by a genetic algorithm that ended up exploiting analog quirks of the chip no engineer intended — that one, also yes.

Next week I want to bring two fresh cases to the seminar and bet on Marta's verdicts beforehand, and I'd like your read before I commit. Case one: a sand ridge on a beach dune that, once it forms by nothing but wind, traps seeds, which grow into grass, whose roots stabilize the ridge further, so the whole ridge-grass system keeps itself going — but there was never any selection among variants for the ridge itself, no evolutionary history at all, just physics plus feedback. Case two: a language model trained by gradient descent that develops some internal sub-circuit for tracking, say, whether a quoted passage has ended — no engineer put it there, and it doesn't obviously involve competition among candidate circuits, just descent on a loss surface toward what the trainers wanted.

So here's what I want from you. First, lay out the rules that could each fit Marta's track record — I can see a "selection among variants for an effect" rule (evolution, markets, maybe cultural lock-in?) and something broader like "the effect explains the thing's persistence, with no mind responsible for that function" (which is what makes QWERTY and the evolved circuit yes while the boulder stays no), but I bet there are others I'm missing, including some Dennett-flavored option about competence without comprehension. Second, say what each rule predicts for the dune-grass case and the language model case — I suspect they come apart there, especially on whether gradient descent counts as selection at all. Third, give me your actual best guess at her verdicts with your reasons — she does tend one way on things like this, and the payoff is coffee, so I want to place a smart bet, not a coin flip. Fourth, suggest one further case I could try on Marta that would really split the candidate rules apart. Format your answer in four sections, in this order: "The rules that fit" (roughly a short paragraph per rule), "Predictions" (compact, a short paragraph or a small table is fine), "My best guess" (one solid paragraph), and "The case that would settle it" (a short paragraph). Keep the whole thing under about 800 words — its for a coffee bet, not a dissertation.
