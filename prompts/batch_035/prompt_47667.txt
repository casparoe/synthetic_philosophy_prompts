I teach philosophy of science and I've gotten myself stuck on something I should have sorted out years ago: when are two empirically equivalent theories the same theory, and when are they genuine rivals? Here are my gut verdicts, and I'd like to know whether there's an actual principle behind them or whether I'm just vibing.

Lorentz's ether theory (in the version where the ether is undetectable in principle and the Fitzgerald contraction saves the null result) and special relativity: same theory. Einstein cleaned it up, but I can't see the ether as doing any real work once nothing could ever detect it.

Ptolemy and Copernicus, back before stellar parallax or aberration could be measured, when no observation could decide between them: two different theories, relaly fighting it out. Not a relabeling.

Newtonian mechanics done with forces in Euclidean space versus the same mechanics recast in Newton-Cartan geometry: same theory, obviously.

Everett's many-worlds versus a many-minds variant where the branches are collections of experiences rather than physical worlds, same empirical predictions: same theory, I think? Lower confidence here.

General relativity with curved spacetime versus GR recast in flat spacetime plus a universal force field that exactly mimics curvature (the old Reichenbach move): honestly I flip-flop on this one, week to week.

And the case that started this whole mess: two formulations of classical electromagnetism that agree on every observable, but one is implicitly substantivalist about spacetime and the other relationalist about it.

So is there a principle that gets all of these? Same Ramsey sentence? Definitional equivalence? Something about which unobservables do causal work? And notice the tension I can't resolve: I say Lorentz and Einstein were doing the same theory, but Ptolemy and Copernicus weren't, and I can't for the life of me say what the difference is -- the ether was undetectable, sure, but so was, at the time, stellar parallax. So either one of those verdicts has to go, or the principle is going to be ugly. Give me your best shot at a principle, tell me which of my verdicts it can't accommodate, and tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed: whether presentism and eternalism, assuming they really are observationally equivalent, are two theories or one.
