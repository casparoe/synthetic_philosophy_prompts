I've been reading a popular history of the Enlightenment, and its chapter on Hume summarizes his argument about miracle reports as follows. Hume defines a miracle as a violation of a law of nature, where a law of nature is a regularity established by completely uniform past experience. Since our experience of these laws is uniform, while our experience of testimony turning out to be false is common, testimony on behalf of a miracle is always opposed by stronger evidence, and so no amount of testimony can ever make a miracle probable. Hume then offers a maxim: we should accept a miracle report only if the falsity of the testimony would itself be more miraculous — meaning more surprising or astonishing — than the event reported; and since no testimony can meet that bar, believing a miracle on the strength of testimony is never rational. The book adds that later critics exposed the whole argument as circular, since Hume had to assume the very uniformity of nature that the alleged miracle is supposed to interrupt, and it concludes that the argument has been dead for a long time, leaving us free to weigh extraordinary reports case by case and sometimes to accept them. I have the feeling the chapter is unfair to Hume in places, though I can't put my finger on where, and I'm also unsure whether its final conclusion follows even if the circularity point were correct. Please go claim by claim and tell me which parts a careful Hume scholar would accept, which are oversimplifications, and which are genuine misrepresentations; then say whether the chapter's conclusion — that the argument is refuted and case-by-case acceptance of miracle reports is now licensed — actually follows from what survives the corrections.
