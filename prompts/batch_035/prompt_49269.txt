I recently accepted a seat on the appeals panel of a large, user-edited history encyclopedia, and I've discovered that a substantial share of our docket concerns historical atrocity denial. Our written policy is unhelpfully thin — it prohibits "denial and gross distortion of well-documented atrocities" — so we decide case by case, and our accumulated rulings are the only guidance we have. Before the new case below comes to a vote, I want to understand what our own precedents actually commit us to.

Our rulings so far, in chronological order:

1. Removed: a post asserting that the gas chambers at Auschwitz were postwar reconstructions built for propaganda purposes.
2. Removed: a formally written essay arguing that the deaths of Ottoman Armenians in 1915 constituted a two-sided civil war with comparable casualties on each side rather than a genocide.
3. Restored: a contribution by an amateur historian disputing whether a particular auxiliary police battalion was present at a specific massacre, based on archival records concerning that unit's movements.
4. Removed: a video asserting that the figure of roughly 300,000 dead at Nanjing was a political fabrication and that the true toll was a few hundred at most.
5. Restored: an article surveying historians' casualty estimates for a nineteenth-century famine, presenting the full range of published figures without endorsing any point in the range.
6. Removed: a post conceding that the Holocaust occurred and was a grave crime, but arguing on the basis of Red Cross death records that the true toll was about a tenth of the accepted figure.

The new case is our hardest yet. It is a long, heavily footnoted essay whose author accepts that the Holocaust occurred, that extermination camps and gas chambers existed, and that the killings were intentional genocide. However, he argues from crematory capacity and transport records that the total death toll was around three million rather than five to six million, and he adds that the Auschwitz memorial's own postwar revision of its toll from about four million to about 1.1 million shows that even the official figures respond to politics rather than evidence. His tone throughout is somber; he explicitly condemns the killings; and his only accusation of bad faith is directed at the museum administration's supposedly inflated numbers, not at any wider group.

I'd like your help thinking this through rather than a simple verdict. First, what should we decide if we want to remain consistent with our own precedent record, and which of the six earlier cases is the closest analog? Second, spell out at least two distinct rules that each fit all six existing rulings equally well — for instance, a rule about which core facts must be conceded versus a rule about material minimization of scale, or whatever other candidate rules you find in the record — and say what each predicts for the new essay, since I suspect they diverge on it. Third, if our precedents genuinely underdetermine the decision, state plainly what principle we would be adopting with each possible outcome, so that we vote with our eyes open. Finally, describe the further case — one that could plausibly reach our docket — that would most cleanly separate the candidate rules from one another, since I'd like to bring it up at the meeting.
