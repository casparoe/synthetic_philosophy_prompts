Every year my reading group picks one popular book on collective decision-making to work through, and this year's choice has a chapter on Amartya Sen's liberal paradox. I'm the one presenting it, so I'd like to know how much of the chapter's summary to trust before I stand up and repeat it. The chapter puts the result roughly as follows:

"Sen proved in 1970 that the idea of individual liberty, once stated precisely, contradicts itself. His condition of liberalism says that each person is a kind of local dictator over his own private affairs: whatever I prefer about my own reading, dress or worship, the social ranking must follow my preference. Sen then showed that this condition, joined with the Pareto rule — society must rank one state above another whenever everyone prefers it — yields a contradiction for any society of two or more people. In effect it is Arrow's famous impossibility theorem in miniature: the local-dictator clause is a scaled-down dictatorship condition, so either somebody ends up with dictatorial powers or the social ranking goes round in a circle. The celebrated illustration involves a prudish man and a lewd man and a single copy of Lady Chatterley's Lover: each is given the veto over whether he himself reads it, but since each also cares about whether the other reads it, the two vetoes and the Pareto rule push the social ranking round a cycle, leaving the group with no coherent choice at all. Sen's own moral was that rights simply cannot be fed into a social welfare function alongside preferences: the consistent course, followed by real legal systems, is to protect rights as side constraints that are never traded off against anything. And since the example works only because the two men are busybodies with opinions about each other's reading — preferences no reasonable person has — the paradox is best regarded as a curiosity about meddlesome types rather than a serious problem for liberal economics."

Something about the chapter's framing bothers me — it reads as though it's selling me a tidy resolution — but I don't know enough social choice theory to say where, or whether, it goes wrong.
