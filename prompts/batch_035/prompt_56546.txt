I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, but I've ended up caring a lot about the question "what is life?" because of arguments at work about whether self-replicating software could ever count as alive, and I've discovered my own answers are a mess. I've written down my gut verdicts on a set of cases and I want you to act as a philosopher of biology, think carefully and thoroughly, and figure out what principle, if any, captures them. Please start by restating each verdict and flagging any you find ambiguous before trying to reconcile them, then work toward one or a small number of candidate principles, stated precisely enough that they give verdicts on cases I haven't listed. Tell me what those verdicts are. And be honest about which of my judgments your principle has to overturn — don't contort it to save all of them.

The cases and my verdicts:

1. A flu virus: not alive, not even borderline.
2. A prion: not alive, and further from alive than a virus.
3. A bacterial spore that's been dormant in amber for millions of years and can still be revived: alive the whole time, even with every process paused.
4. A mule: alive, obviously, despite being sterile — so I can't think reproduction by the individual is required, but I'm unwilling to give up reproduction entirely; maybe it's the lineage that matters, and I'd like you to test that thought.
5. A HeLa cell culture: alive, even though the cells are cancerous, effectively immortal, and no longer part of anything resembling the organism they came from.
6. A minimal bacterial cell built in a lab with a genome designed by software and synthesized from scratch: alive on first contact, origin irrelevant to me.
7. A mammalian red blood cell: alive, I think — it metabolizes glucose — even though it has no nucleus and can't make proteins or reproduce. But I notice I'd say a red blood cell in a blood bag in a refrigerator is still alive, and I'm not sure that verdict and verdict 3 can both come from the same place as verdict 1.
8. A self-replicating computer virus, the boring real-world kind: not alive.
9. A glider in Conway's Game of Life: not alive, and I don't feel any temptation to say yes.
10. Here's my problem case. Suppose a piece of software in a simulated environment maintains itself against random bit-flips, harvests simulated energy, builds copies of itself with heritable variation, and over time evolves defenses and predators in that environment — a genuinely open-ended evolutionary system. My principles say I should call this alive; my gut says no. I'm professionally committed to the idea that substrate shouldn't matter — if I met a carbon-chauvinist who said a silicon-based alien wasn't alive, I'd mock them — yet every time I actually picture the software case I say it isn't life. I can't tell whether verdict 10 is a real belief about the world or just me refusing to extend a word, and I'd like your read on that specifically.

The pairs I most want reconciled: the seed-like cases versus the virus (both are inert packages of instructions waiting for the right conditions, and I say one is alive and the other isn't — the best I can do is mutter that the seed carries its own machinery and the virus hijacks someone else's, but I don't know if that's a principle or a rationalization), and verdicts 8–10 against my own substrate-neutrality commitments.

A request about honesty: if you think the whole project of finding one principle is misguided — say because "life" is a cluster concept with a family-resemblance structure, or because my verdicts are tracking two different things that happen to be coextensive in familiar cases — say that plainly rather than forcing a unification I'll regret. Likewise, if you think one of my verdicts is just confusion rather than a considered position, overrule it and tell me why. I'm not looking for validation; I'm looking to find out what I actually think, or what I'd think if I thought it through.
