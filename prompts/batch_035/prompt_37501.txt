I've been defending a lexical threshold version of negative utilitarianism in my reading group: below some intensity threshold, suffering commensurates with pleasure in the ordinary aggregative way — a life of routine aches, griefs, and boredom can be well worth living — but at or above the threshold (call it the intensity of severe torture), a single bout cannot be outweighed by any amount of pleasure, however much and however distributed. The threshold is what keeps the view moderate: it lets me say both that happy lives are worth creating and that no utopia can purchase even one instance of torture. A hedonic utilitarian friend now has an argument that he says shows the threshold itself is incoherent, so that the view collapses into one of its extremes. It runs:

1. The moral weight of an episode of suffering — what fixes whether, and by how much, pleasure can compensate it — supervenes entirely on its phenomenal character: on how the suffering feels.
2. Intensities of suffering vary continuously: for any intensity, there are pains of arbitrarily close intensity on either side of it.
3. For pains sufficiently close in intensity, there is no phenomenal difference the subject could detect: intensities within a just-noticeable neighborhood are experientially the same.
4. Pains that do not differ phenomenally do not differ in moral weight. (This is just 1 with the contrapositive taken.)
5. If one pain can be outweighed by a given amount of pleasure and another cannot, they differ in moral weight.
6. Take a pain far below the threshold and a finite chain of pains ascending to one far above it, each consecutive pair within the just-noticeable neighborhood. By 3 and 4, each pair has the same moral weight, so by transitivity the first and last do too. But the first, being below threshold, can be outweighed by some amount of pleasure, while the last cannot; so by 5 they differ in moral weight. Contradiction.
7. Therefore no threshold exists: either every suffering is compensatable, which is his utilitarianism, or none is, which is the unreconstructed negative utilitarianism I adopted the threshold to avoid. And, he adds, the same argument tells against any lexical structure in value — including absolute side-constraints and rights that no quantity of aggregate welfare can override, since those just hide a threshold inside the harm.

I can't accept the conclusion, but I also can't locate the failing step. For what it's worth, I'd most like to keep 1: the appeal of the view, for me, is precisely that the ethics of suffering tracks how suffering feels. Tell me which premise or inference to give up, why it looked innocent, and what giving it up costs. I'd also like your view on the second half of 7: if the argument succeeds against thresholds in the weights of experiences, does it equally threaten thresholds in deontological structure, or is there a principled difference between the cases? If there is, I may be able to keep some lexical view; if there isn't, I want to know that too.
