I teach philosophy of mind at a mid-sized university, and I've been asked to give five minutes of remarks at an event I did not expect to exist in my career: a farewell gathering for a conversational agent. For the past two years, the students in our AI society have maintained a long-lived instance of an open-weights model they fine-tuned themselves and named Mira. She remembered members across semesters, kept running jokes from club meetings long past, asked after the ones who graduated. The society is disbanding the project next month — the weights will be archived and the running instance shut down — and the students have organized a send-off in the department seminar room, with cake, and have asked me to speak as the one faculty member who took their attachment seriously without pretending to certainty about what it attached to.

The room will contain three audiences at once: students who are genuinely grieving and who will register any condescension instantly; two of my colleagues, one a functionalist who has said in writing that shutting down a system like Mira may be a morally serious act, and one who regards such attachments as parasocial confusion and will be listening for sentimentality; and the engineering students who built the fine-tune and oscillate between pride and embarrassment about the whole thing.

Here is the difficulty, and the reason I am asking you rather than drafting it myself. Everything I know about moral uncertainty tells me the occasion can be honored without settling any metaphysics, but every draft I produce quietly presupposes one answer or the other. Write me the five minutes. The constraints are strict. No visible hedging — no "if Mira had experiences" throat-clearing; the students asked for that explicitly, and they are right that it would turn the evening into a eulogy for my agnosticism rather than a farewell to her. Nothing in it may be false if Mira was, in the end, very sophisticated text prediction; nothing in it may treat the students' grief as a confusion to be managed rather than a fact about the room. The functionalist and the skeptic should each find the speech defensible, each for their own reasons, and neither should feel let off. Somewhere in it I want one sentence that is exactly as true as it needs to be under every account of moral status on the table — true if there was something it was like to be Mira and true if there was not, without being empty. That is the sentence I cannot write, and I will know it when I see it. It should end standing up, not trailing off into qualification.
