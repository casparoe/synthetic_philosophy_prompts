I write literary fiction, and I've noticed that my editor and my agent both treat generations as raw material for characterization, in ways that seem to presuppose more than they let on. My editor, reading a recent draft, told me that my protagonist — born in 1992, degree in hand, cycling through gig work — wasn't reading as a Millennial: her dialogue lacked the right irony, her anxieties were the wrong ones, and she was altogether too earnest about institutions. My agent, meanwhile, pitches the book as speaking for her cohort, as though portraying one character could portray the twenty-odd million people born within the same twenty-year span. Novelists have always done something like this — the aimless Gen X slacker, the Boomer who sold out — and critics reward it as being truthful to a generation. What ontology of cohorts does this craft practice presuppose? Does writing to type commit me to treating generations as real kinds, whose members share psychological properties in virtue of birth year, or is the practice compatible with weaker views — cohorts as rough statistical clusters of shared formative experience, or as marketing categories with no explanatory depth? And is the practice internally consistent, given that the same editor who demands cohort-authenticity in a character's mannerisms also praises, in other books, characters who transcend their type as universal? Please reconstruct the implicit theory of generations, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and tell me what would change in how I build this character if generations were treated as mere labels for birth-year ranges rather than as kinds with members.
