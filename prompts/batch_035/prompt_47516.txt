I'm teaching a week-long intensive on definitions of life to incoming astrobiology graduate students — a mix of planetary scientists, microbiologists, and two philosophers — and I want to open with a diagnostic rather than a lecture. Please write a "which definition of life do you actually hold?" quiz: eight to ten questions, each presenting a concrete case with two or three answer options, followed by a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto named positions.

The cases must include, in some form: a virus; a bacterium desiccated into a fully dormant spore that has done nothing metabolic for ten thousand years and could be revived; a mule and a sterile worker ant, presented together; digital organisms — self-replicating, mutating, evolving programs in a simulated world; a laboratory protocell that maintains itself metabolically but copies itself sloppily, with heredity too weak for cumulative evolution; a candle flame or growing crystal; and a line of cultured cells kept alive and dividing decades after the donor's death.

The positions that must appear as leaves in the scoring key are: the NASA-style working definition combining self-sustaining chemistry with Darwinian evolution; purely evolutionary definitions on which life is whatever undergoes natural selection; autopoiesis and other self-maintenance definitions; thermodynamic or dissipative-structure definitions; and the deflationary view that "life" is a family-resemblance cluster with no essence, admitting genuinely borderline cases. If you think the logical space contains another distinguishable leaf, add it, but only if you can name a proponent or a coherent one-sentence characterization.

Constraints on the questions. First, no question may name a definition or ask whether life is "essentially" metabolism, heredity, and so on — students must be sorted by what they say about cases, not by which label they find attractive, and the quiz must be answerable by someone who has never heard of any of the positions. Second, design at least three questions so that evolutionary and self-maintenance definitions give opposite answers — the dormant spore, the digital organisms, and the sloppy protocell are natural sites for this, but sharpen them so the forced choice is clean rather than ambivalent. Third, include at least one question where a student can answer "borderline — it's a matter of degree," so that repeated borderline answers can carry someone to the family-resemblance leaf; make sure the scoring key actually uses this path. Fourth, in the scoring key, explicitly flag any stable combination of answers that none of the standard positions accommodates, and say in one sentence why nobody occupies it — because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it is simply unexplored. Finally, note any pair of positions your instrument cannot reliably separate and say which question you would add to split them, if you had room.

Keep the whole deliverable — questions and scoring key together — under 850 words, and aim for answer options short enough that the quiz could be administered on one printed page.
