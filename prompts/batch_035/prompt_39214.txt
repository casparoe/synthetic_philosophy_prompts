So I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums since roughly 2012, and this weekend the absent-minded driver problem came up at my board game night and absolutely wrecked the vibe. The setup, so we're all on the same page: a driver is heading home late at night. There are two identical highway exits along the way. The driver is absent-minded — they understand the whole setup perfectly, but they can't recall how many intersections they've already passed, they know they can't recall it, and at each intersection they're in exactly the same internal state. Payoffs: take the first exit and it's a miserable route home, worth 0. Take the second exit and it's a great route, worth 4. Blow past both and you loop home the long way, worth 1.

Here's their exchange as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Maya went first: "At any intersection it's 50/50 whether I'm at the first one or the second. If I exit now, half the time that's worth 0 and half the time 4, so 2 on average. If I continue now but exit at the next intersection: half the time I'm at the first one, keep going, reach the second, and exit for 4; half the time I'm at the second, keep going, and take the long way for 1. That's 2.5. So continuing wins."

(2) Dev: "Your 'exit at the next one' plan is a fiction. When you arrive at that next intersection you'll be in the exact same mental state, running the exact same reasoning, and you'll conclude 'continue' all over again. Absent-mindedness means any deterministic rule you follow at one intersection you follow at both. Your real options are always exit (payoff 0), never exit (payoff 1), or randomizing. If you exit with probability p at each intersection, expected payoff is 4p(1-p) + (1-p)², which maxes out at p = 1/3, giving 4/3. Decide ex ante to be that kind of driver and stop computing from inside the car."

(3) Maya: "Then my per-intersection numbers can't both be right. If 50/50 is my credence, exiting at an intersection is worth 2, which beats your 4/3 — but if I actually follow 'always exit' I collect 0 for sure. So either the 50/50 is wrong or your policy math is wrong. Also, your coin-flip policy means that somtimes I'm sitting at the SECOND intersection, where exiting is a guaranteed 4 versus a 1, and I'm supposed to flip a biased coin about it?"

(4) Dev: "The 2 is an illusion because your action at this intersection is perfectly correlated with your action at the other one — it's one decision wearing two hats, not two decisions. Per-action expected values that ignore that are exactly the mistake naive causal reasoning makes in Newcomb. And yes, you flip at the second intersection, because ex ante that's the price of the policy being worth anything. Sucks to be absent-minded."

(5) Maya: "Here's what actually bothers me. How likely I am to be at the first intersection depends on my policy. If I exit with probability 1/3 at each intersection, the expected number of intersections I reach is 1 + 2/3 = 5/3, so conditional on finding myself at an intersection, I'm at the first one 3/5 of the time, not 1/2. If I always exit, I'm at the first intersection with probability 1 — which is exactly why 'always exit' nets 0 even though 'exit is worth 2 given 50/50.' The 50/50 just isn't a policy-independent fact. So your 4/3 and my 2.5 are both conditioning on a credence that maybe no one should have, and I half suspect this whole fight is really about which credence to condition on."

Then they started arguing about whether the driver at the second intersection even counts as "the same agent" as the driver at the first one, and that's where I gave up and came here.
