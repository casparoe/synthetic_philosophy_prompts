My best friend from undergrad works at an astrobiology research center, and one of her side gigs is sitting on the little internal commitee that maintains their unofficial "is it life?" rulings — the ones people lean on when arguing about how to design life-detection experiments for future missions. She says the official working definition they cite is the NASA-flavored one, "Life is a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution," but she insists nobody there actually applies it consistently, herself included. We were a few beers in last weekend and she walked me through her own personal scorecard, and I've been turning it over ever since.

Her rulings so far: E. coli, alive, obviously. Influenza virus, not alive. A mule, alive. A prion, not alive. Chlamydia — the obligate intracellular bacterium that can't reproduce outside a host cell and even has to import its ATP — alive. Crystals that grow and fragment in supersaturated solution, not alive. Digital organisms in an evolutionary simulation (self-replicating programs that mutate and compete for CPU time in silico), not alive. And a worker honeybee, which is sterile, alive.

Next time we meet she'll have two new cases, and I want to walk in already knowing what she'll say. Case one: a collaborator's lab has an RNA system where RNA enzymes stitch together supplied fragments to build more of themselves, copying errors creep in, and there's genuinly natural selection between the variants in the tube — but there's no membrane, no metabolism beyond the ligations themselves, and the fragments are just fed in from outside. Case two: a protocell with a fatty-acid membrane that it repairs and rebuilds, with internal chemistry that maintains itself far from equilibrium — but its genome is a completely static, error-corrected template, so it cannot evolve at all, ever, even in principle. Predict her verdicts on both, tell me which rule she's implicitly running given her track record, what other rules would fit that same track record just as well and where they'd come apart on these two cases, and what further case I should spring on her that would best tell those rules apart. I'm less interested in the biology than in reverse-engineering her rulebook — and honestly, in figuring out whether she has one.
