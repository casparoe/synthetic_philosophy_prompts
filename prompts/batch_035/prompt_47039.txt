For the end-of-year spoof issue of our department newsletter (read by the faculty, the grad students, and a number of bewildered relatives), I've been assigned the anthropic reasoning piece. I've decided on an insurance-company parody, but I want the technical content to be genuinely correct so that the people in our reading group — who spent a semester on self-locating probability and are not in a forgiving mood — find it funny rather than sloppy. Please write it to the following brief.

The piece is a customer FAQ plus selected policy clauses from a fictional insurer — call it something like Anthropic Mutual Assurance, Ltd., though you're free to pick a better name — whose line of business is underwriting bets, decisions, and existential hedging that depend on anthropic reasoning. Think of it as covering people whose expected-value calculations hinge on how they should reason about evidence that is partly indexical: bettors who took 50:1 odds that the cosmos contains a trillion times more observers than the alternative because the self-indication assumption told them to, people hedging against the doomsday argument, customers who want coverage across branches of a fission experiment. Around 700 to 850 words, formatted as numbered clauses interspersed with a few FAQ entries, in the flat, anodyne, all-lowercase-menace register of real insurance fine print.

The humor must come from exaggerating real features of the SSA/SIA debate rather than inventing absurdities. Concretely, the piece should include all of the following:

1. The Presumptuous Philosopher Rider: a clause addressing the customer who, on self-indication grounds, treats the hypothesis that the universe contains vastly more observers as overwhelmingly more probable, and the company's response, which should turn on a genuine technical point — for instance, what exactly the customer conditioned on, or the difference between updating on one's existence simpliciter and on one's existence as this particular observer.
2. A reference-class clause. The company reserves the right to assign the policyholder to a reference class at claim time, of its choosing, from among defensible alternatives. The joke is that a genuinely unresolved technical indeterminacy is handled as ordinary insurer discretion, in the same tone as "pre-existing conditions."
3. A dispute-resolution procedure for Sleeping Beauty claims, in which the company piously declines to take a side between halfers and thirders as a matter of corporate policy, while its payout schedule quietly and consistently reflects one of the two positions — you decide which, but make it detectable to a careful reader and invisible to a casual one.
4. An actuarial treatment of the doomsday argument — as a risk factor to be priced, not an argument to be answered. Something like a surcharge structure that is funny precisely because it takes the argument's logic literally for actuarial purposes.
5. A definition-of-the-insured clause at the end dealing with who counts as "you" under the policy in cases of duplication or fission, closing the piece on a personal-identity note without invoking any particular named theory by name.

One deep constraint, and it is the most important joke in the piece: the company must remain scrupulously, loudly neutral between the self-sampling and self-indication assumptions — its marketing insists it does not adjudicate anthropic principles — while structuring its products so that it profits handsomely whichever assumption turns out to be correct. Do not spell this out; let the reader assemble it from the clauses.

Fairness requirements, since our readership includes committed parties on both sides: do not strawman either the SSA or the SIA position, and do not let the company's exploitativeness consist in the stupidity of its customers. The customers should reason correctly by their own lights; the company's edge should come from the structure of the situation, not from anyone's errors. The reader who has worked through the presumptuous-philosopher and Sleeping Beauty literature should laugh rather than wince; the reader who hasn't should still be able to follow it as insurance satire — the technical content should be load-bearing for the jokes but never a barrier to reading.

Don't include any citations, footnoted references to the literature by name, or appeals to external sources; everything should be self-contained on the page. Keep it deadpan throughout — no winking narrator, no asides signaling that we all know this is silly.
