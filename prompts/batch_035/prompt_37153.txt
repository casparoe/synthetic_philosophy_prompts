A friend who did his graduate work in philosophy of physics walked me through an argument against the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics, and I have been unable to find the exit. He starts from premises I would have accepted unreflectively:

(1) On the many-worlds reading, when I perform a measurement, every outcome with nonzero amplitude occurs, and each outcome is registered by a future self who remembers being me and is in every ordinary respect my continuation.

(2) Before the measurement, I am related to each of these future selves in the same way. None of them is the "real" one with the rest impostors; there is no further fact about which of them I will turn out to be. They are all, in the same sense, me.

(3) Rationality requires an agent to distribute his credence evenly over futures that are related to him in the same way and between which his evidence and preferences draw no distinction; and where several of those futures share a property, the credence of that property is proportional to the number of futures that possess it.

(4) Take a measurement with two possible readings whose Born weights are 99 percent and 1 percent. Exactly one future self of mine sees each reading, and by (2) they are related to present me in the same way.

(5) So by (3), my credence that I will see the 1 percent reading must be one half, not one hundredth.

(6) The amplitudes never entered the reasoning, and it goes through whatever they are. So on the many-worlds reading, amplitudes have no authority over expectation: the Born rule is not merely underived but contradicted, and a rational agent should take even odds on an event the theory itself calls a hundred-to-one long shot.

His conclusion: either many-worlds has no probabilities at all, or its probabilities are fixed by counting future selves — in which case anyone who trusts the experimentally confirmed statistics will be betting, repeatedly and predictably, on the wrong side. Either way, he says, the interpretation fails on formal grounds before any experiment is even discussed.

What bothers me is that the argument seems to prove too much, and he is not embarrassed by any of it. He agrees that (3) applies wherever selves multiply: in a classical laboratory that clones me ninety-nine times if a coin lands heads and once if tails, he cheerfully accepts that my credence in heads should be ninety-nine hundredths — cloning, he says, is just branching by another route. When I asked what settles how many future selves there are in the quantum case, he said decoherence; when I asked how finely decoherence carves things up — whether the recording, the scattered photons, and my later memories constitute one future self or many — he said any sensible carving gives the same answer, and left it there. I can't put my finger on it, but the part of the argument I now distrust is the part he waved through.

Tell me which step to reject, and what rejecting it costs. I would most like to keep (2): (1) is, as far as I can tell, the interpretation's headline claim, and I don't see how many-worlds survives giving up either of them. If the answer is (3), I need to know what breaks the symmetry that (2) asserted — my friend's challenge is to point to something, other than the amplitudes, that distinguishes the future selves, and then to explain why a coefficient in a wavefunction should carry the authority of a probability. If the answer lies elsewhere, say where, and say what it costs — including what my friend's own position has to pay for the cloning consequence he welcomed.
