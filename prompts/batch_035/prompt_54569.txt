Something's been bugging me about how psychiatry decides what counts as a mental disorder, and I want to know what theory of disease the whole setup is quietly assuming.

For a diagnosis to count in the DSM, the symptoms generally have to cause the person distress or mess up their functioning somehow. You can have odd beliefs or hear voices, but if it doesn't bother you or impair you, most of the time it's not classified as a disorder. Meanwhile over in the rest of medicine nobody runs that check at all. Nobody waits for hypertension or an early tumor to bother the patient before calling it a disease; we treat plenty of things the patient never felt and would never have noticed on their own. So one part of medicine seems to be saying "disease is whatever biological part isn't doing its job, and the patient's opinion about it is irrelevant," and another part seems to be saying "no disorder without some kind of negative value for the person." Same medical system, different rules.

And the history makes it weirder. Homosexuality got dropped from the DSM back in the 70s after argument and protest, with nothing about people's underlying make-up having changed — which sure looks like the disorder status was tracking a social judgment rather than a biological fact. But then deafness is still treated as something to fix, with cochlear implants covered by insurance, while parts of the Deaf community insist it's a culture and not a pathology. And psychiatry is happy to medicalize severe shyness when it impairs you while leaving ordinary shyness alone, even though most people would guess the same mechanism is problably underlying both, just at different intensities — the line gets drawn by how much trouble it causes, not by what the mechanism is.

So here's what I want worked out. What view of health and disease does the psychiatric distress/impairment rule actually presuppose? Is it the view that disease judgments are fundamentally value judgments — nothing is a disorder unless it's bad for someone — or the view that a disorder is a failed biological function plus harm, or can the distress requirement be justified some third way, like as an epistemic gate for who comes in seeking treatment, or as a way of respecting autonomy about which mental states to change? And does the physical-medicine half genuinely contradict the psychiatric half, or could one underlying theory license both practices, with the difference coming from somewhere else — say, that we can specify what a heart is for much more cleanly than what a mind is for? Finally, if we concluded that the distress rule really does presuppose that disease is partly a value judgment, what would have to change: in the DSM, in what insurance covers, or in how we draw the line between treating and enhancing?

As you go, please say how confident you are in each major claim, and single out the one step in the reconstruction you're least sure about. Then end with a machine-readable final line of the form \answer{...} containing exactly one of: normativist, naturalist, hybrid, underdetermined — whichever best captures what the practice as a whole actually presupposes.
