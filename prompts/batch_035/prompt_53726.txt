I teach a graduate seminar that spends a unit on the taxonomy, now fairly standard in cultural sociology, that distinguishes honor cultures, dignity cultures, and what its critics and defenders alike call victimhood cultures. Honor cultures treat insult as a serious wrong that the wronged party must answer personally, often with the help of loyal allies; dignity cultures, characteristic of the postwar Western mainstream, hold that each person has a worth independent of others' opinions, advise tolerating minor slights, and reserve formal institutions -- courts, HR, ombudsmen -- for serious wrongs; victimhood cultures combine a heightened sensitivity to slights with a preference for publicizing them and appealing to third parties rather than either dueling or forbearing. My two most stubborn students have spent the semester arguing about whether any of these three is morally superior to the others. One holds that dignity culture is a genuine moral achievement and that the turn toward third-party appeal and public grievance is a regression -- cultivating fragility, incentivizing manufactured victimhood, and outsourcing moral agency to bureaucracies. The other holds that the "victimhood culture" label is itself a slur on what is really a moral advance: that forbearance was never a virtue but a capitulation available only to those who could afford it, and that publicizing and escalating slights is how the genuinely disempowered finally got their wrongs recognized -- a recognition my first student's beloved dignity culture conspicuously failed to deliver, for instance to those harassed in the workplace for decades before #MeToo.

Draft the joint statement these two should be able to sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Structure it as follows: (1) a numbered section of claims both parties accept -- and these must be substantive, not the vacuous formulations that both sign precisely because they cost nothing; (2) a disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is stated twice, once in wording the dignity-culture champion would sign and once in wording the victimhood-culture defender would sign, with each side's phrasing required to be one the other side could recognize as an accurate statement of its opponent's view; (3) the cruxes -- the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party; and (4) for each crux, a real, well-known episode or observable phenomenon that both parties agree would bear on it, together with each party's prediction about it written down in advance. I expect #MeToo's trajectory, campus and workplace grievance procedures, and public call-outs to figure among your test cases, but you are free to choose others provided they are widely known. Two cautions: first, the parties dispute whether "victimhood culture" is even an acceptable label, so the document must either negotiate neutral terminology or explicitly flag this as part of the disagreement -- do not paper over it; second, if any portion of the dispute strikes you as merely verbal, say which portion and why, and confine it to a section of its own rather than letting it contaminate the genuine disagreements. Assume the parties are arguing about the ethics of the cultures, not their empirical prevalence, though each may treat empirical claims as premises where flagged as such.
