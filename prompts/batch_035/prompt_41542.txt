My buddy Sam hit me with this at a barbecue and i've been going in circles since. He wants to prove that "all-powerful" is a contradiction in terms, and not just for God:

(1) Being all-powerful means being able to do anything that's logically possible to do.
(2) It's logically possible to make a stone that its own maker can't lift -- a guy with a concrete truck and a free week could do it tomorrow.
(3) So an all-powerful being is able to make a stone it can't lift.
(4) If it goes ahead and makes the stone, then afterwards there's a possible thing it can't do (lift the stone), so it stops being all-powerful.
(5) If it can't make the stone, then there was a possible thing it couldn't do all along.
(6) Either way, no all-powerful being exists.
(7) And Sam says the same trap catches every unlimited ability: a locksmith who can open any lock vs. a lock no key can open, a solvent that dissolves anything (so what's the bottle made of?), a chess engine that beats anyone.
(8) So "unlimited ability" is like "the biggest number" -- grammar pretending to be an idea.

Here's my problem. I don't even believe in God, so (6) doesn't sting. But (8) does. When we say my chess engine beats everyone, we're saying something true and understandable, not talking nonsense with a hidden cap on it. Sam says we're just speaking loosely and every real ability is secretly limited, and honestly that feels like it proves too much.

Where I keep getting stuck: my first move was to say the stone in step 3 is like a square circle, an incoherent thing, so failing to make it costs nothing. But Sam points at step 2 and says the stone is clearly makeable -- weak little humans can make one -- so how can the very same stone be contradictory? And I've noticed something I can't quite nail down: in step 2 the words "a stone its maker can't lift" describe a stone whose maker is me with my concrete truck, but in step 3 the same words have to describe a stone whose maker is the unlimited being. Sam's comeback is that the description doesn't mention who the maker is, so it can't change meaning when the maker changes, and I cant prove him wrong. My other idea was dropping (1) and saying all-powerful includes doing the logically impossible too, but Sam says then logic is just one more thing the being can switch off, which seems like a worse fire. If I have to keep one step I'd keep (2), the concrete truck, because it's the only part of the argument that's ever actually happened. So where exactly is the trick, and what does each way out cost me?
