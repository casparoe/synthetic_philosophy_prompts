My cousin hit me with this one and I can't find the seam. (1) Every emotion is directed at something you believe exists — you can't pity someone unless you take there to actually be someone there to pity. (2) Nobody reading Anna Karenina believes she exists; they know it's a novel. (3) So readers don't really have emotions toward her — whatever they have, it isn't pity. (4) Yet readers cry, and that feeling has to be about something, and the only candidates they believe in are the sentences on the page (maybe themselves). (5) So what moves readers is, literally, arrangements of words: people cry at ink. (6) And notice the same move works everywhere — by (1) I can't pity anyone not in the room with me, can't be scared of anything that isn't about to hurt me, so the only things I can ever feel anything about are things physically present to me. Which is nuts, since im still pretty broken up about my grandmother, and she's been gone ten years and wasn't made of sentences either. Every step still looks fine to me individually, so tell me which one is the bad one, and what I have to swallow to reject it. For what it's worth I'd rather keep (2) — saying readers are basically deluded about there being an Anna seems like the worst way out.
