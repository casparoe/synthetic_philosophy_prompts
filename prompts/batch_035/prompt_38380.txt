I teach rational choice, and every year we hit Kavka's toxin puzzle and I end the seminar hand-waving, so let me just put my verdicts on a batch of cases on the table and you tell me what principle, if any, is behind them.

The toxin case itself: a millionaire offers me a million tonight if at midnight I intend to drink a toxin tommorow that will make me ill for a day but do no lasting harm. My verdict: it's rational to try to form the intention, and it's not irrational of me to sit there at five to midnight attempting it, but I don't think I can form it by sheer decision — any intention I did form would evaporate the moment the money cleared, and I more or less know that in advance.

Ulysses and the sirens: rational, easy, no puzzle. He binds himself from outside, wax and ropes, and the binding does the work. Fine.

Nuclear deterrence: I think it can be rational to genuinely intend massive retaliation after an attack, even though once the attack has happened retaliation achieves nothing except more dead people. This one I'm quite confident about, and it's presumably in tension with my toxin verdict — there I say you can't hold on to an intention you won't want to execute, here I say you can and had better.

The colleague who tells her PhD students she will fail anyone caught plagiarising, even though actually failing them would be an ordeal she dreads and would much rather avoid once the moment came: rational, and I notice her arrangement works exactly the way deterrence does — it only functions if she's really willing, not if she's bluffing and everyone can tell.

A friend who pays a £200 deposit forfeited if he smokes: rational, but it feels like a different species from the deterrence case — he's changing tomorrow's incentives rather than holding a costly intention against them. Still counts, though.

A graduate student who publicly announces a thesis chapter deadline to create social pressure, knowing full well the deadline is unrealistic and that the announcement will just make him miserable: irrational, or at least irrational in a way the smoker case isn't.

And the one I keep going back and forth on: someone with a degenerative illness arranges that a device will end her life on a fixed date unless she presses a weekly button to cancel. While she's well, she intends never to press it; she knows that as the disease progresses her future self will be frightened and will press. Is her present intention rational, and — the part I really can't settle — is it an intention she can actually sustain, or does it collapse into the toxin pattern exactly when it matters most?

So: is there one principle here about when it's rational to intend what you won't want to execute, and when sheer decision can produce such an intention at all? If yes, state it precisely, tell me which case motivates each clause, say what it predicts on cases I haven't listed (I'd like at least one new one, with the verdict derived rather than asserted), and be honest about which of my verdicts it has to overturn. If no single principle covers the lot, give me the smallest set that does and say where you'd redraw the lines. I'd rather lose a verdict than carry two principles that agree everywhere except where it counts.
