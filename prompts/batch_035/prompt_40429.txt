In a philosophy of mathematics course I'm taking, the instructor keeps reaching for the same analogy to motivate nominalist fictionalism: mathematical discourse is supposed to be like literary fiction. On this picture, saying that there are infinitely many primes is like saying that Sherlock Holmes solves crimes in London — true in an important and perfectly respectable sense, but true *within the story*, not true in the sense that commits you to anything existing. My housemate, who absorbed this with enthusiasm, is now using the analogy to argue a specific point against our professor's platonism: since nobody thinks we must posit a population of fictional Victorian detectives in order to explain why it is useful or enjoyable to speak as if Holmes existed, we equally have no need to posit abstract objects — numbers, sets, functions — to explain the success of mathematics, including its applications in physics and engineering. On his view, the platonist is just someone who has mistaken a magnificent, indispensable fiction for an inventory of what there is, and we can keep doing all the arithmetic and Fourier analysis we like while declining the ontological bill.

I'm suspicious that the analogy is doing more work than it can carry, but I can't quite pin down where. Please audit it properly. First, give me the explicit correspondence table: in the comparison between mathematics and fiction, what is supposed to play the role of the author, the canon or text, the reader, and truth-in-the-story as opposed to plain truth — and, crucially, what is the analogue of the utility we get from fiction, given that mathematics is allegedly indispensable to our best scientific theories in a way that stories about detectives are not? Second, lay out where the mapping breaks: fictions are authored, dated, and revisable, while mathematical truths seem to be discovered, necessary, and not up for retconning; Doyle can contradict himself without catastrophe, while an inconsistent axiom system trivializes everything provable in it; fictional claims are ingested under an explicit pretense, while mathematicians and physicists seem to assert their claims flat out. Third, tell me which of these disanalogies actually matter for my housemate's conclusion and which are merely colorful. Does the claim that we can use mathematics without believing in numbers survive once the differences between the two halves of the analogy are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the conclusion the whole time? And if the fiction analogy fails, is there a better one available to the nominalist — the rules of a game, a measurement convention, a useful myth — that does the job my housemate needs done?
