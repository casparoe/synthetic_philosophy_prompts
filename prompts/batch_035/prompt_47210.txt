I'm putting together a philosophy-and-film session for undergraduates on the meaning of life, and I've settled on Groundhog Day — but my own reading keeps splitting in two, and I'd like help thinking it through properly before I walk into the classroom.

On the first reading, the film is an almost too-convenient dramatization of the absurdist position. Phil Connors is condemned to repeat an identical day, with full memory and no lasting consequence — the Sisyphus condition with better production values. His trajectory through amusement, hedonism, despair, and the famous suicide montage, in which he kills himself over and over only to wake to the same clock radio, tracks Camus's claim in The Myth of Sisyphus that the real philosophical question is whether one can live with the absurd rather than escape it. And the scene I most want to teach: the elderly homeless man whom Phil, by that point nearly omnipotent within the day, simply cannot save — the one event in the loop that resists him. That looks like exactly where Camus and Nagel locate the absurd, in the collision between our demand that things matter and the world's flat refusal, here given a face and a death certificate.

On the second reading, though, the film is an anti-absurdist parable smuggled in absurdist clothing. The loop is exitable, conditional on Phil's moral self-improvement and — let's be honest — on winning Rita, which imports precisely the teleology Camus refuses: Sisyphus's boulder has no summit, but Phil's repetition turns out to have had an exit all along, and the film rewards him with it. And the content of Phil's transformation looks like contemporary meaning-in-life philosophy rather than defiance: he builds objective goods (piano, ice sculpture, French poetry, genuine service to the townspeople), something like Susan Wolf's fit between subjective attraction and objective worth, rather than learning to love his hill.

So: give me a reading that takes both the film and the philosophy seriously. Where does Groundhog Day genuinely dramatize what the absurdist arguments claim, and where does it quietly distort them — is Phil's early despair really the absurd, or just consequence-free hedonism curdling into boredom? Is the ending a cop-out from the absurdist standpoint, or is there a defensible reading on which the escape isn't the point? And does the film add anything to the debate between absurdist and meaning-in-life views that argument alone doesn't? Please flag how confident you are in each interpretive claim, and single out the one you're least sure of — especially where the reading depends on a contestable interpretation of a particular scene.
