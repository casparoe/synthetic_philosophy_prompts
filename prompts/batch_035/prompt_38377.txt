Write a satirical op-ed of about 700 words, in the voice of a confident mid-sized-city newspaper columnist — invent a name and a beat, say "life and civic affairs" — who has recently discovered Gödel's incompleteness theorems and now deploys them as an all-purpose trump card in every dispute, from the school-board budget to his brother-in-law's claims about the Paleo diet. It should read as his actual column, title included.

The humor has to come from getting the actual mathematics right while the applications go spectacularly wrong. He must never misstate the theorems: he should correctly distinguish the first (any consistent, recursively axiomatizable theory strong enough for arithmetic leaves true-but-unprovable sentences in the language) from the second (such a theory, if consistent, cannot prove its own consistency), and he should recite the conditions of applicability — perhaps more precisely than his editors are comfortable with — before applying them to things that are not formal systems at all, such as his homeowners' association bylaws, his marriage, and the municipal sewer budget. He should run through at least three of the standard misuses: everything-is-relative relativism, the Penrose–Lucas-style claim that human minds transcend any machine, and the idea that science as an enterprise has been shown incomplete by a man with a notebook in 1931 — plus at least one application so mundane it circles back to being almost profound.

His rhetorical armor should itself be an abuse of the second theorem: when readers write in to point out that the theorems concern only formal axiom systems, he replies serenely that no critique of his column can be proved from within his column. End with a short editor's note or appended letter from a local mathematician whose correction is technically impeccable — she should mention, accurately, that many perfectly good theories escape incompleteness entirely (Presburger arithmetic and elementary geometry on Tarski's axioms are complete and decidable), and that Gödel was a Platonist who thought mathematical truth outruns provability but was no relativist about anything else — followed by the columnist's two-sentence rebuttal, which misses her point in a way that is itself characteristic of the pattern being satirized.

Constraints: keep it deadpan throughout. The satire should be fair — exaggerate the genuine, recognizable habits of Gödel-citation (the theorem stated correctly, then applied to whatever the citer already believed) rather than inventing errors no one actually makes. Do not put verbatim quotations in Gödel's mouth; if the columnist paraphrases him, mark it in-fiction as a paraphrase from memory, since that is exactly the sort of thing the columnist would do. And write it so that by the end a reader has felt the real question the column accidentally raises — what would it actually take to legitimately export a formal result to informal domains, and whether any such export has ever been pulled off — even though the columnist himself never notices he has raised it.
