I'm a systems programmer and a longtime reader of the rationalist blogosphere, and I've gotten into a running argument with a colleague about what programming language standards actually are, which I've come to suspect is secretly a philosophy question. C++ is defined by a written standard maintained by a volunteer committee under ISO, and JavaScript works much the same way. When GCC, Clang, and MSVC disagree about whether some corner-case program is valid C++, the resolution path is always the same: a bug report, then a defect report against the standard, then an edit to the document. The compilers are treated like faulty measuring instruments, and the text like the quantity being measured — nobody ever says the standard is simply indeterminate on the point and legislates an answer; they write more words until the disagreement goes away. Meanwhile Python, as my colleague gleefully points out, is the opposite: CPython is the de facto reference implementation, and "the spec is wrong, fix the spec" is a routine, respected resolution there. So two flourishing engineering communities, doing the same kind of work, have institutionalized opposite answers to the question of what fixes what a language's rules mean: an authoritative text on the one hand, the behavior of the community's shared implementation on the other.

There's a third strand that makes me doubt the text-first picture is even sincerely held where it officially exists. Every language community I know of, including the spec-first ones, has a strong norm against breaking working code: a program that has compiled and run on millions of machines for twenty years acquires something like a right to keep working, even if it turns out to have relied on behavior the standard never promised — or behavior some compiler happened to allow that the standard never did. Committee members will spend enormous effort redesigning a feature precisely so that the accidentally-depended-on behavior is preserved. That looks like an admission that the community's accumulated practice, not the document, is the real arbiter of what the rule was all along. I gather this is Wittgenstein's territory — Kripke's Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language, with the plus/quus example, which I know mostly through blog posts and encyclopedia entries rather than the primary texts. In my corner of the internet, rule-following comes up almost exclusively in arguments about whether an AI that passes every test has thereby learned the rule, but standards committees seem to me the cleanest real-world laboratory of the issue: a finite text, drafted by people who could not have anticipated the corner cases, purportedly governing infinitely many future programs, with an explicit dispute-resolution procedure attached.

So here is what I'd like an answer to. What view of rules and of linguistic meaning does the standards-committee apparatus — bug reports, defect reports, and "clarifying" edits — presuppose? Does the practice require that there be a fact of the matter about what the standard says, sitting there to be discovered, or could a community-agreement view (something like assertibility conditions fixed by the community's pattern of assent) equally well license it? Relatedly: does the escalating hyper-precision of standards drafting, the notorious "standardese," presuppose that a rule can be made fully determinate by sufficiently careful formulation, and is that presupposition compatible with the rule-following paradox's claim that no formulation interprets itself? And where does the norm against breaking working code fit — is it evidence that even the spec-first communities don't really believe their own picture, or can a consistent story be told on which the text fixes meaning and the norm merely reflects prudence about migration costs? Throughout, I'd like you to distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and to identify the points at which the practice is genuinely underdetermined between rival views. Please answer in American English.
