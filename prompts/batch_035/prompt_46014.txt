After thirty-five years as a research pharmacologist, I have spent my retirement reading metaethics, and I have assembled the following set of commitments. Before I write them up more formally, I would like an audit of whether they hang together:

1. I accept the is-ought gap in its strongest form: no set of purely descriptive premises, however complete, entails a normative conclusion without at least one normative premise.

2. I am nonetheless a naturalist about moral facts: there are moral truths, and they are truths about the natural world — not supernatural, not sui generis in some spooky sense.

3. I find Moore's open question argument persuasive: for any natural property you propose, it remains a genuinely open question whether what has that property is good, so "good" cannot be defined in natural terms.

4. I believe science can, in principle, settle substantive moral questions. Once we determine empirically what human welfare consists in, facts about welfare can settle what we ought to do — the way facts about deficiency diseases settled the question of whether sailors should carry citrus.

5. I also hold that two agents who agree on all the facts, and reason flawlessly, could still rationally diverge in their final ends, with no further fact that could adjudicate between them.

Which of these are in genuine tension, which only appear to be, and what is the cheapest consistent revision? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally damaging, and tell me which single change would restore the most coherence at the least cost. I genuinely do not know where the trouble lies, so do not pull your punches.
