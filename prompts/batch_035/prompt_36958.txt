My seminar has spent most of a semester on population ethics, and the question of whether a larger population is, other things equal, better has turned out to be where all momentum dies. The total view says the sum of wellbeing is what matters, so adding people with lives worth living makes the world better, which drives straight toward Parfit's repugnant conclusion: that a vast population of lives barely worth living could be better than a smaller one of excellent lives. The average view avoids that but implies the monstrous result that adding happy people can make the world worse if they're below the current average, and that a world of one blissful being could beat a world of billions who are almost as happy. Every repair attempt — critical-level views, variable-value views, lexical perfectionism, person-affecting restrictions that only count people who could have existed otherwise — gets hit by its own nightmare case: the mere addition problem, the sadistic conclusion, the non-identity problem, and the family of impossibility results showing that no view can satisfy a handful of intuitions we all claim to hold. Everyone in the room accepts the formal setup and everyone can recite the counterexamples, yet nobody has budged since week three.

My working hypothesis is that the stall isn't really about ethics at all, but about the admissibility of the framework: one camp treats interpersonal aggregation of wellbeing across possible people as obviously coherent and then quibbles over the aggregation function, while the other camp suspects the whole apparatus of comparing worlds with different people in them is a category mistake dressed up as arithmetic — in which case the two camps are answering different questions under one name, and no theorem within the framework can settle it. But I'm not confident, because the anti-aggregation camp keeps arguing inside the framework anyway, which suggests they don't really believe their own deflation. There's also a third possibility I can't rule out: that we're dealing with bedrock intuitions — about whether creating a happy person benefits anyone, about the symmetry or asymmetry of creating happy versus miserable lives — that no argument reaches in either direction.

So I'd like a diagnosis rather than a verdict on which population is better. Is this a substantive moral disagreement, a verbal one about what "better than" means when applied to world-pairs, a hidden metaphysical dispute about the normative status of merely possible people, or a cluster of questions that got fused together historically? Please support the diagnosis with what the positions actually commit themselves to when pushed, not just labels. And given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a new impossibility theorem, a decision-theoretic reformulation, a conceptual distinction, a change in the question — or an argument that the debate should be expected to run forever, and why.
