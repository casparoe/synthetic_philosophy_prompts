Is there an established term for the following configuration, and if there is not, I would like you to coin one and define its edges with some care — a bare label will not satisfy me.

I have spent years around public life, and I keep noticing a pattern in how societies treat leaders who have done something plainly wrong — authorised mistreatment of a detainee, deceived a legislature, betrayed a confidential source — in circumstances where refusing would, as far as anyone can tell, have produced a worse outcome. The familiar debate about such cases concerns the act itself: whether it was justified, excused, or neither. What interests me is something that happens afterwards, and that everyone seems to expect without ever articulating. The leader is not permitted to treat the matter as simply closed. To be forgiven, or even vindicated, they must visibly carry the act: they must say, and appear to mean, that it was wrong even though they would do it again; they must decline to celebrate the outcome it produced; they must accept the stain on themselves rather than spread it around among subordinates or circumstances. Two instances, to make it concrete. A wartime prime minister who deceived parliament about a planned operation, and whose deception is widely credited with saving lives, is nonetheless expected in his memoirs to confess to nightmares rather than to pride — the confession is read as confirmation of his decency, its absence as confirmation of his ruthlessness. And a police commander who used intelligence obtained through a betrayal is judged fit for office partly on whether he can be observed to be bothered by it; what he may not do is be at peace.

What strikes me is that the visible suffering appears to function as payment. Nobody is made better off by the leader's misery — the deceived are not compensated by the deceiver's insomnia, and everyone knows this — and yet the misery seems to balance some ledger. There is also, curiously, a correct dose, which the public administers with some precision: too little remorse and the leader is a monster; too much and they are self-indulgent, milking the episode for sympathy; and remorse that is suspected of being performed for an audience seems to count against the agent as though the performance partly cancelled the payment.

I take it this lies somewhere in the neighbourhood of dirty hands, but as I understand it, that phrase names the problem of the act — how it can be right, or permissible, to do wrong — and not the aftermath practice I am describing, which seems to be a social mechanism layered on top of the act, with norms of its own about timing, dosage, and sincerity. It also does not sound quite like the idea, which I have come across but cannot place, that some wrongs leave a remainder which no good outcome cancels: that idea is about the structure of the wrong, whereas what I am after concerns what the agent owes in the way of visible carriage of that remainder, and what the public does with the display. Nor is it straightforwardly atonement, since no repair is possible or even attempted in most of these cases.

So: does this configuration — the requirement of visible, appropriately dosed, plausibly sincere suffering as the price of a dirty-handed act's social acceptance — have a name? If it does, please set out how the term is used and how the phenomenon it picks out differs from the nearest neighbours, including the ones I have mentioned and any I have missed. If it does not, coin a term, give a precise definition, and then work through the boundary cases: remorse that is displayed before an audience and so risks being instrumental; remorse that continues long after anyone thinks it owed; remorse expressed by an agent who turned out to have been wrong about the consequences altogether, so that the act was not dirty but merely botched; and whether the mechanism can apply to institutions — which cannot feel anything, but do seem to apologise a great deal. I would like to come away knowing exactly what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where it stops.
