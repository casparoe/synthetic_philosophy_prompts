The bill goes to the highest bidder, but the second-highest bidder must also pay the amount of their final bid and gets nothing in return — so unlike a normal auction, losing costs you money. In classroom demonstrations, bidders routinely push their combined bids past a dollar. (a) Explain why, from inside the auction, each individual raise can seem rational to the bidder at the moment they make it, and identify where the genuine mistake occurs. (b) How does this game relate to escalation of commitment as studied by organizational psychologists, and how does the sunk cost fallacy fit in? And the student's answer, also verbatim:

"The dollar auction traps people through sunk costs. Say I've bid 95 cents and someone bids a dollar: if I stop now, I lose 95 cents for sure; if I bid $1.05 instead, I might win the dollar and lose only 5 cents. So from inside the game, every raise is actually rational — the bidder is choosing between a certain loss and a slightly bigger bet on winning, and taking the bet is the right call. The only genuinely irrational move is the very first bid; once you're in, each raise makes sense from where you stand, which is why bidding sails past a dollar even though both players can see it happening. Shubik invented the game in the 1950s to prove that real people are irrational, and classroom experiments bear this out: the winner usually ends up roughly even, and the auctioneer only pockets a few cents. As for part (b), the sunk cost fallacy and escalation of commitment are basically two names for the same phenomenon — Staw just gave it the psychology label. His experiments showed it's driven entirely by self-justification, i.e. people not wanting to admit their earlier decision was wrong."
