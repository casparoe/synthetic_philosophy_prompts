Quick context: I run a small cog neuroscience lab (two PhD students, one postdoc, an MEG setup, decent compute) and I've got maybe five years of runway to bet on one or two lines of work in the predictive processing / free energy principle space. The field feels weird to me right now -- there's the empirical Bayesian-brain stuff (predictive coding, precision-weighting, prediction-error signals), and then there's the very abstract Friston-style stuff where everything from organisms to cells ends up "minimizing free energy," and I genuinely can't tell how much the abstract machinery constrains the concrete models versus being a redescription of them. So I want help building an agenda, not a survey, and not a reading list.

Which open questions in this space would you priortize for a lab like mine, and in what order? Candidates I can see, roughly: (1) whether the FEP makes any predictions that distinguish it from the much weaker claim that the brain is roughly Bayesian -- or whether the famous equivalence chain (free energy upper-bounds surprisal, minimizing surprise keeps you in characteristic states, so persisting systems look like they minimize free energy) only holds in regimes that never bind empirically; (2) whether the Markov blanket formalism actually does any work in these models or is just decoration; (3) precision-weighting and the clinical applications, like psychosis models where priors are supposedly overweighted relative to sensory evidence; (4) whether predictive coding as a proposed microcircuit actually maps onto cortical anatomy, which keeps getting asserted more than demonstrated; (5) something I'm not listing. Rank them and defend the ranking -- I want to know which of these is upstream of which, which a small lab with MEG can realistically attack vs. which are really math/philosophy problems wearing neuroscience clothes, and which you'd deprioritize because even a clean answer wouldn't change anything downstream. Also: what evidence arriving over the next couple of years would move things up or down your list -- e.g. if someone finally nailed an unambiguous decoding of prediction-error signals, or if the "all organisms self-evidence" reading keeps spreading into areas like social cognition where I can't see any empirical grip at all.

One more thing I keep chewing on: a lot of the appeal of FEP to grad students seems aesthetic -- it unifies perception, action, attention, and maybe life itself under a single variational quantity. If unification is a virtue here, is it the kind of virtue that can guide a lab's actual experiments, or only a virtue in the seminar room? Fold that into the prioritization wherever it fits.
