I am a novelist drafting a book set in 1988–89, centered on a mid-level Soviet-area analyst at a Western think tank. My draft keeps failing in a specific way, and I would appreciate expert advice on it. When my characters fail to anticipate what my readers know is coming—the collapse of the Eastern Bloc—my beta readers say those characters come across as stupid, as though I were dumbing them down for dramatic effect. Yet whenever I let the characters sense the approaching upheaval, the story reads as if history were a road with signposts, and my most historically literate readers complain that I have made the outcome feel inevitable in a way it did not feel to anyone living through it. I am also unsure whether this is even the kind of flaw a novel can have: a novel is not a work of history, and readers do arrive already knowing the ending.

Please act as a philosopher of history who also thinks seriously about narrative craft. Before giving your advice, take a few sentences to brainstorm about the underlying structure of the problem: what exactly the error is, whom it belongs to (my characters, my narrator, my readers, or me), and whether the remedies historians are typically urged to adopt carry over to fiction at all. Then advise me concretely: what principles should govern how I distribute foresight and blindness among my characters, and is there some way to treat the reader's knowledge of the ending as a resource for the novel rather than as a bias to be corrected for? Please think carefully and reason step by step, and write your answer in American English.
