I've come around to a view my friends consider eccentric: that generations — Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z, and so on — are real kinds, not arbitrary twenty-year bins invented for marketing decks. My line of thought is that people born into a given cohort pass through the same historical events at the same developmental windows, and those events causally shape them in convergent ways; so cohort membership is a real property that supports induction, and 'Millennial' is closer to a natural-kind term than a stereotype. In fact, I want to say something stronger: generations may be like biological species in the sense philosophers of biology press — not classes defined by a common essence, but individualized historical lineages held together by causal structure — and perhaps even 'interactive' in the sense that belonging to the kind changes how institutions treat you and how you behave, which feeds back into making the kind real rather than merely projected. My friends, one of whom studies demography, keep insisting it's all just age effects and life-cycle effects dressed up, and that once you control for those, the 'generation' itself does no work.

I'd like someone to tell me what would actually count against my view. Not the lazy 'people are individuals, stop stereotyping' — the serious defeaters. For instance, demographers apparently have techniques for separating age effects, period effects, and cohort effects; what should my view predict those analyses find, and what findings would gut it? What if the clustering is real but too weak, patchy, and edge-blurred to constitute a kind at all — would that force a retreat to 'there are cohort effects but no generations,' and is that retreat a capitulation or just the sensible version of the view? Could the species-analogy backfire, e.g., if species lack anything like the reproductive isolation that makes lineages discrete, whereas cohort boundaries are fuzzy by construction? And how much mileage can the interactivity point really carry if the label 'Gen Z' influences people only insofar as they've already been sorted into it by institutions?

Please lay out the defeaters ranked from fatal through merely awkward, say for each whether a committed defender of my view should concede it or dismiss it, and be candid about whether the view can be protected by qualifications ('real but socially constructed,' 'real but only probabilistically projectible') without becoming trivially true. I'm also curious what the view predicts that its rivals don't — what observation or empirical result would distinguish it from the position that generations are just convenient bookkeeping.
