Two colleagues of mine got into it at coffee hour last week and I've been turning the argument over ever since. The topic was Max Black's old thought experiment — the universe containing nothing but two perfectly identical iron spheres, one mile apart, in an otherwise empty, perfectly symmetric space. Black used it against the identity of indiscernibles, the principle that things sharing all their properties must be one thing.

Marcus's line was straightforward: in that universe the two spheres share every qualitative property — same mass, same shape, same microstructure, and by the symmetry even the same relational profile, since each is one mile from an iron sphere in a universe with nothing else in it. Yet there are two of them, not one. So the principle is false, and with it the Leibnizian idea that qualitative facts settle numerical identity.

Dana wasn't having it. Her first move was location: whatever else you say, the spheres differ in where they are — one is here, the other is there — and location is a property, so the spheres don't share all their properties and the example never gets off the ground. Marcus said that was exactly the point Black anticipated: location in that universe can only be fixed relationally ("one mile from the other sphere"), and by symmetry each sphere has exactly the same relational description, so "here" and "there" don't distinguish them unless you've already counted them as two. Dana said that proves too much — by that reasoning you couldn't distinguish the two ends of a line segment either, but obviously you can.

Round two: Dana switched tactics. She said the example is self-undermining, because to state the setup you have to stipulate that there are two spheres — you smuggle numerical distinctness in at the beginning, then act surprised to find it at the end. The example presupposes primitive identity; it doesn't refute the thesis that identity is grounded in qualitative facts. Marcus fired back that this conflates how we describe a world with how the world is: if the qualitative facts really were fixed and symmetric, the two-ness would be real but ungrounded — and an ungrounded difference is still a difference, so the grounding thesis fails even if stating the case requires identity-talk.

Round three is where it got heated. Dana offered a fallback: fine, suppose the strong principle fails, but perhaps the spheres are discernible in a way nobody at the table had considered — for instance, each sphere bears the relation "one mile from a sphere that is not itself" to the other sphere, and neither bears it to itself, so there is after all a respect in which they differ, and the principle survives in weakened form. Marcus rolled his eyes at that: a relation defined in terms of non-identity can't be used to establish discernibility, he said, any more than you can prove two things differ by pointing out that they're not identical. That, he said, is just circular.

They finally agreed to let me referee, and I've been putting it off because I honestly can't decide who's right where. Can you adjudicate move by move — who wins each exchange as it actually played out, whether Dana's self-undermining charge has any force, and whether her "not itself" fallback is circular or sneaky-but-clever? And is there anything both of them are missing that someone who works on this literature would want to add?
