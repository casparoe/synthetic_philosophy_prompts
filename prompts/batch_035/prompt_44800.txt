I am a retired population ecologist — thirty-two years of banding passerines and estimating nest survival, followed by a decade of invertebrate work during which I once reared, by rough count, four thousand mosquito larvae in a single tub of water. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy, and the literature on wild animal welfare has unsettled me more than anything I read during my working life, because it takes the numbers I spent a career generating — the fraction of fledglings that never see their first winter, the larval mortality curves I once tabulated without a second thought — and asks whether, on balance, those lives were good for the animals living them.

Two old colleagues of mine have been arguing about this for years. One is a conservation biologist of the ecosystem-integrity persuasion, who holds that habitat destruction is a wrong against ecological wholes — communities, processes, the thing itself — regardless of what it does to the welfare ledger. The other has drifted, partly through me, toward a hard-headed welfare aggregationism: she suspects that for the vast majority of wild animals, mostly small-bodied and r-selected, life is a coin weighted toward suffering, and she is therefore not even certain that draining a wetland, ceteris paribus, makes the world worse for the animals concerned — fewer mayfly lives not worth living, fewer tadpoles eaten alive from within. The conservationist finds this view monstrous; the welfarist finds his response sentimental. I have persuaded them to attempt an adversarial collaboration, and I have volunteered to draft the joint statement, since I am the only party who can write about both sides without my hands shaking.

I would like you to draft that document. It should contain, in this order:

1. A numbered section of claims both parties can sign. These should be substantive, not vacuous — I will not accept "wild animals can suffer" as a finding — and they should draw on what both an ecologist and a welfarist actually know: that most wild animals die before reproducing, that habitat destruction typically reduces total animal abundance while often shifting communities toward short-lived, high-fecundity species, that our grasp of the valence of experience in insects and other small-bodied taxa is weak, and so forth.

2. A table of disagreements, each phrased twice if necessary — once so the welfarist would accept the wording of her own position, once so the conservationist would accept his. I specifically want their disagreement about whether habitat destruction reduces aggregate wild animal suffering stated precisely: is it about the sign of average lifetime welfare, about how to aggregate across animals of very different cognitive complexity, or about what destruction actually does to population structure? If part of their dispute turns out to be verbal — for instance, if they are using "harm" to track different things when applied to animals that would not otherwise have existed — please say which part and why.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling any one of them would move at least one of them. I suspect one crux is whether naturalness or ecological integrity has value independent of welfare, but I do not want that to be the only one; there is also the matter of how much to trust our ecological predictions about the downstream effects of any intervention or loss, on which my career made me considerably less confident than most.

4. For each crux, a concrete study, observation, or argument that both parties would agree bears on it, with each party's prediction written down in advance — what result the welfarist expects and what result the conservationist expects, so that the prediction itself could later be scored. Where no such test exists, say so honestly rather than inventing a fake common ground.

Please write it as the document itself, with a short preamble I could read aloud to them before they begin. I am not asking for a verdict on who is right; I have watched them argue too long to believe a verdict from a stranger would help. I am asking for the version of their disagreement that is exact enough that, twenty years from now, one of them might concede to it.
