So I'm a shift supervisor at a regional package hub, never went to college, and last winter we had this huge blowup about overnight shifts — whether to spread the miserable Sunday shifts around evenly or pile them on the two guys who said they'd take them for extra pay. Ever since I've been chewing on a general question about fairness that I've never seen anyone spell out, and I wrote up my idea as a short essay. I'd like you to act as a philosophy professor who's spent a career thinking about fairness and about when it's okay for numbers to matter in moral decisions. Please brainstorm a bit before writing anything, and be genuinely critical — my coworker Deb says my whole thing is just utilitarianism with extra steps, and I want to know if she's right. Here's the essay:

"Rules vs. adding things up. When people argue about what's fair, they usually do one of two things. Either they add up everyone's gains and losses like a balance sheet and pick the plan with the best total, or they dig in on rights: nobody can be made to suffer for others, period, no matter how many benefit. I think both are wrong, and there's a third way. My idea: every rule faces a rival rule, and you judge complaints one person at a time. A complaint against a rule is reasonable only if the complainer does worse under this rule than under the rival, AND what they lose is bigger than what the people on the other side would each lose if the rival rule won instead. Numbers enter one person at a time, through these comparisons, and never by adding seperate people's burdens into one pile. A rule is fair when no rival can beat it this way — when every alternative would create a bigger single loss for someone than this rule creates for anyone.

Applied to our shift fight: the rival to 'spread the Sunday shifts evenly' is 'give them to the two volunteers for extra pay.' The volunteers' complaint against spreading is that they lose pay, but what they lose is smaller than what any one of the rest of us would lose under the volunteer rule — a whole Sunday every month, wrecked sleep, missed family stuff. So spreading wins, and it wins without ever adding all our little burdens together against the two of them.

But here's where I get stuck. Imagine a rule where one person suffers something really bad so that a million people each avoid something tiny. Under my test, his complaint is reasonable — his loss is bigger than any single loss on their side — so the rule is unfair, even though the total balance sheet screams that it's worth it. Part of me thinks that's exactly right, that's the whole point of my approach. But flip it around: a million people each avoid something almost as bad as what one person would suffer. My test still says the one person wins, because each of the million only has a slightly smaller complaint. And that feels wrong to me — at some point, alot of almost-as-bad complaints should add up. But I can't see how to let them add up without opening the door to the balance sheet, which I built this whole thing to avoid."

Please give me detailed comments, and organize them like this: first, plain-language comments on whether each part of the argument actually works — especially whether my comparison test is circular, since I'm defining a "reasonable" complaint by comparing losses, but I never say where the measuring stick for comparing losses comes from or whether you can even compare them across different people. Second, tell me if my test gives strange results on concrete cases — please run it on two or three invented scenarios and include at least one where the answer would upset me, because I suspect the "million almost-as-bad complaints" case is exactly that. Third, tell me whether this is actually a third way or whether Deb is right that it collapses into one of the two standard approaches. And last, any suggestions on making it clearer, since I want to send it to our regional HR person who liked an earlier draft. Please be honest about what you're unsure about instead of papering over it, and write everything in American English.
