A friend of mine who's doing a PhD in behavioral economics has this habit of posing little choice questions when we hang out, and last month she got me with two of them that I havent been able to stop thinking about. First she asked: would you rather have $2,000 for sure, or a gamble that gives you $2,000 with 89% probability, $12,000 with 10%, and nothing with 1%? I took the sure two thousand — I'm between jobs right now and that's basicly two months of rent, and the 1% chance of walking away with nothing when the 2k was right there in front of me felt like gambling away something that was already mine. Two weeks later, different conversation, she asked: would you rather have a gamble that pays $2,000 with 11% probability and nothing otherwise, or one that pays $12,000 with 10% and nothing otherwise? I took the second one without much agonizing — neither one felt like "my" money this time, so I went for the bigger prize (12k would clear my credit card debt and then some) and the odds barely seemed different anyway.

Then she told me she'd asked the pair on purpose, and that my two answers contradict each other. Her explanation was something like: whatever your personal attitude to risk, there's a basic principle that if two options share a part that's identical in both, you can strip that part off and your ranking of what's left shouldn't flip — and if you strip the shared part from both of my choices, the leftovers are literally the same comparison in both rounds, so it's incoherent to pick the safe option the first time and the risky one the second. She says any way of valuing gambles that a reasonable person would endorse makes my two answers jointly irrational. Is she right? The rounds genuinely felt different to me — in the first one the two grand was basically in my hand, in the second it was all a long shot — but she says how a question feels doesn't count, only the choices, and my choices contradict. So: was I being irrational? And if I was, which answer should I have changed — or is she wrong that there's only one consistent way to value these things, and if she's wrong, what's the actual story on which both picks make sense? She's offered to run a version of this with real money at her place next week, and I want to know how I should answer before I do.

One more thing: when you reply, can you keep separate what's just arithmetic that nobody disputes from what people who actually study this stuff disagree about? From how she talked, I got the impression that whether my kind of answering counts as irrational is itself debated among people who know the field, and I'd rather see clearly where the settled part ends than get a confident ruling that sounds more settled than it is.
