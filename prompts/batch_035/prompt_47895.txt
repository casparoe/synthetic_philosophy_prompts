I'm on the methods committee of a mid-sized psychology journal and we're deadlocked over a proposal to change what counts as evidence in what we publish. One faction wants us to go Bayesian: no more p-values, authors report Bayes factors or credible intervals, and — this is the part that got heated — authors would be allowed to keep collecting data until the Bayes factor crosses some threshold (say 10:1) and then submit. Their argument is that unlike p-values, Bayes factors aren't invalidated by that kind of peeking, so it's actually the honest thing to allow. The other faction says this is just the replication crisis with new math, and wants to keep null hypothesis testing but with alpha at .005 plus mandatory preregistration, including preregistered stopping rules. One member keeps bringing up something called the likelihood principle as if it settles it, nobody's sure it does. We have to send a recommendation to the editor-in-chief by Friday. Draft the committee memo: recommend one concrete policy (a hybrid is allowed if you actually say what's in it), and treat the losing side's best point fairly — especially whether "Bayes factors don't care about stopping rules" is really as clean as it sounds once you remember what it assumes about the model being right. I want something the losing faction could respond to, not a victory lap.
