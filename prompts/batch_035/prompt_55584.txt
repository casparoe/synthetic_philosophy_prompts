I'm a senior in a high school philosophy elective, and we just finished a unit on William James and W. K. Clifford — whether it's ever okay to believe something without sufficient evidence, or whether believing on thin evidence is always a kind of wrongdoing. I run our class discussion forum, and for the end of the unit I want to post a diagnostic quiz titled "Which Ethics of Belief Do You Actually Hold?" The goal is to sort my classmates by how they react to concrete cases, not by which philosopher's name they like, so no question may just ask something like "Do you side with Clifford or James?" You are a professional epistemologist who specializes in the ethics of belief; before writing the quiz, think carefully about which axes genuinely divide the positions — whether we can choose our beliefs at all, what counts as "sufficient" evidence, whether practical consequences can legitimately tip the balance, and whether the harms of belief to others change what's permitted — and brainstorm cases that pull those axes apart before you commit to the final questions.

The quiz should have eight to ten questions, each presenting a concrete scenario with two or three answer options, followed by a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions. The cases must include at least these: a mountaineer stranded on a ledge who can only make a dangerous jump if she genuinely believes she can; a person whose close friend is accused of a serious crime with strong evidence against him, and whose support of the friend might depend on believing in his innocence; someone who sits down at a set time and tries to simply decide, by an act of will, to believe it will rain tomorrow; a student deciding whether to believe she will pass a hard exam when her past record is genuinely mixed; a grieving person whose religious faith, held without decisive evidence, is the only thing keeping her functioning; and someone who joins a community whose comforting shared beliefs are unsupported and, if false, will gradually make him worse at judging the world. Please make some answer options state the reason for a verdict rather than just the verdict itself, so the quiz can separate classmates who reach the same conclusion for theoretically different reasons — I've noticed people can agree the mountaineer may believe while disagreeing completely about why.

The scoring key should map patterns onto at least these positions: strict Cliffordian evidentialism; James's will-to-believe restricted to genuine options; the "ought implies can" objection that since we cannot choose beliefs, the whole ethics of belief misfires; the indirect-control view that we are responsible for belief-shaping practices like evidence gathering and association, not for the beliefs themselves; and a permissive view on which evidence sometimes underdetermines belief without pragmatic considerations counting as reasons. Please also note any two positions the quiz cannot cleanly separate, and identify any region of the logical space that no position occupies — I want to discuss with my class whether nobody holds those combinations because they're incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.
