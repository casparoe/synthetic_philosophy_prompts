For a graduate seminar on rational choice I'm preparing a unit on what to do when expected value is undefined or otherwise ill-behaved — the St. Petersburg game, the Pasadena game, and their cousins — and I want to open it with the specifications question before we ever discuss the proposed fixes. Your job is to write the specification.

First, state the problem compactly so the document is self-contained: in the Pasadena game, a fair coin is flipped until it first lands heads on toss n, whereupon you receive (−1)^(n+1) · 2^n / n dollars, so that payoffs alternate in sign and grow without bound while their probabilities shrink in step; the expected-value series comes out as the alternating harmonic series, which converges only conditionally, so its "value" depends on the order in which the states are summed, and by the rearrangement theorem can be made to equal any real number. Note the contrast with St. Petersburg, whose series diverges, and with the textbook case, where the series converges absolutely and order is irrelevant.

Then write the desiderata: the requirements any adequate theory of choice under indeterminate expected value must meet, each with the motivating case or argument behind it. At a minimum, adjudicate whether each of the following belongs on the list as a genuine requirement or is a prejudice of one camp: (i) extension agreement — the theory must reproduce textbook expected value whenever that value is finite and well-defined; (ii) dominance — the theory must never rank a strictly dominated option at or above its dominator; (iii) order-invariance — the value of an option must not depend on how its states are enumerated, since enumeration is an artifact of description; (iv) verdict delivery — the theory must return a determinate preference between a Pasadena ticket and a sure thing, or else supply a principled account of when verdict-freeness is permissible rather than a bug; (v) compositionality — the theory must say something non-arbitrary about composites, such as playing Pasadena twice, or a 50/50 mixture of a Pasadena ticket with its sign-flipped mirror, where many people's intuitions are that the mixture has a well-defined value even though the components do not. For each requirement, be explicit about what breaks if it is dropped — a concrete pair of options, or an agent who gets exploited — rather than gesturing at "unacceptable consequences."

Two further tasks. First, flag any subset of the desiderata you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable, and say which and why; if you believe no such conflict exists, argue for that instead, since a proof of joint satisfiability would itself be a result. Second, add one desideratum of your own invention — something the lists in the literature tend to omit — and defend it; then identify which single requirement on your final list excludes the most popular families of fixes (utility truncations or caps, aggregation functions that sum absolute values or truncate tails, and verdicts that fall back on dominance or risk-avoidance principles alone), with one sentence per family on which requirement it violates.

Keep the emphasis on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring contenders. Hard limit: 1,200 words, inclusive of the problem statement.
