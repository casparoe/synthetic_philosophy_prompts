I teach an upper-level cognitive science seminar on concepts and analogy-making, and I am writing the take-home final. Please write one exam question worth 25 marks, designed for about 60 minutes of work, on conceptual slippage in microdomains, in the tradition of Hofstadter's Copycat project — but the question must be fully self-contained, answerable by a student who has never heard of Copycat. All the material the student needs should be inside the question, as follows.

The question should introduce a microdomain: strings of letters drawn from the alphabet {a, b, c}, such as aabbcc. The domain's operative concepts are letters, sameness groups (maximal runs of one letter, e.g. the groups aa, bb, cc in aabbcc), group length, first/last position, and left/right neighbors; the permitted transformations are duplicating or deleting material. The question should then pose two analogy problems of the form "source string → transformed source string; target string → ?":

1. abc → abcc; cba → ?
2. abc → abcc; aabbcc → ?

Both should be shown to the student. In problem 1 the letter-level and group-level readings of the rule (duplicate the rightmost letter versus duplicate the rightmost group) happen to coincide, so it functions as a warm-up. In problem 2 they diverge: the rigid answer aabbccc duplicates the rightmost letter, while the answer aabbcccc requires the solver to map the source's rightmost letter to the target's rightmost sameness group, letting the concept of letter slip to the concept of group.

The question should ask the student to do three things. (i) Give answers to both problems, explain why problem 1 is easy and problem 2 is not, and defend a preferred answer to problem 2 — noting that the shallow answer is not strictly wrong, so the defense must appeal to something like the depth of the correspondence rather than to correctness. (ii) Reconstruct the case for fluid concepts: explain why a solver with a fixed vocabulary of predicates and fixed object-to-object correspondences struggles here, since nothing in the source string fixes whether its letters should map to the target's letters or to its groups, and say what it is supposed to add for concepts to be context-sensitive pressures that trade roles rather than fixed predicates. The student should also present the strongest incrementalist rebuttal — that one can simply add predicate remapping as one more operator in a heuristic search over correspondences — and the fluidist reply to it. (iii) Evaluate the objection that success in such microdomains is an artifact of the domain having been reverse-engineered from the program's strengths, and say what evidence from outside the microdomain would bear on the dispute between fluid and compositional accounts of concepts.

The difficulty must come from the crux, not from trivia: a student who merely names Copycat's architecture or recites its terminology should not pass. Alongside the question, provide a model answer of about 600 words and a marking scheme with marks allocated per part summing to 25, which separates an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves each must contain — for instance, which moves concerning underdetermination, depth, or the reverse-engineering objection distinguish the top band.
