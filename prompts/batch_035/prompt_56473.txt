I teach political philosophy, and I also sit on the restitution commitee of our university museum. We have a case coming up at our Michaelmas meeting that I'd like a draft opinion for. We hold eleven Benin-court objects — brass plaques and one carved ivory — that entered the collection in 1961, documented as purchased from a London dealer who had bought them in the 1950s from a private collector whose grandfather served on the 1897 expedition. Nothing in the file says looted outright, but it's hard to beleive the chain of custody doesn't start at the sacking of Benin City. Two trustees want full and unconditional return now. Our director wants return only as a long-term loan back to us, on the grounds that once you concede title, the Parthenon Marbles logic is unstoppable and every object in the building becomes a liability. And there's a wrinkle about the recipient: the claim came from the Nigerian federal commission, but these were court pieces, and the argument runs that the proper claimant is the Benin royal court — a line of custody violently interrupted but never lawfully extinguished.

Please draft the written opinion I'll circulate before the meeting. It needs a concrete recommendation — full return, conditional return, or retain-and-consult — with a time frame, and it should engage the director's precedent worry seriously rather than just waving it off. The philosophical core I want you to actually take a stance on: is restitution here a matter of corrective justice owed to a continuing community that can still be wronged by what happened in 1897, or is it just a title dispute about whether tainted provenance ever cures with time and clean hands? Answer in British English, and skip any bibliography — I want the reasoning, not a reading list.
