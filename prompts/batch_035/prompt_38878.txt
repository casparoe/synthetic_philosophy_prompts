something ive always wondered about my own workplace. I'm an economist at a central bank, and we have what looks like a straight up methodological contradiction baked into the org chart. The monetary policy side forecasts with models built on a representative agent — one infinitely-lived household that plans perfectly over its whole future — and nobody blinks at that, it's basically the Friedman move from The Methodology of Positive Economics: assumptions can be as unrealistic as you like if the predictions come out, the famous "as if" line. But two floors down, the financial stability people treat unrealistic assumptions as disqualifying. If you hand them a model with a single representative agent they'll bounce it, because (they say) a crisis is exactly the thing a representative agent is structurally unable to see coming — you need impatient vs patient households, leveraged banks, actual heterogeneity, or the model can't even represent the mechanism that blows up. Same institution, same hiring pool, but one division's baseline norm is "assumptions don't matter, predictions do" and the other's is "assumptions matter enormously, prediction isn't even the main point." What does each practice actually presuppose about what a model is *for* — prediction, explanation of mechanisms, stress-testing counterfactuals? Is the pair of presuppositions reconcilable, or is one division just confused about its own methodology (my own guess is the DSGE folks are implicitly leaning on the F-twist even in contexts where nobody checks the predictions, but I'd like to be told if that's unfair)? Don't give me a neutral survey of the options — take a definite position on what the two divisions presuppose, say whether the combination is coherent or one of them needs to change, and tell me concretely what the weaker division's day-to-day practice would have to look like if it were brought in line with its own implicit view.
