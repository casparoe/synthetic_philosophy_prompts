I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a philosophy of law course, and I also serve as the student member of my university's Academic Integrity Board, the panel that adjudicates cases of plagiarism, unauthorized collaboration, and similar violations. Our course has just finished the unit on theories of punishment, covering the standard divide between retributivist views (punishment is justified by desert, and proportionate to the wrong) and consequentialist views (punishment is justified only by its future effects, such as deterrence, incapacitation, or moral education). I understand the theories in the abstract, but I keep noticing that the actual decisions our board makes don't map cleanly onto either, and I want to think more carefully about what the philosophical debate actually implies for a body like ours.

Here is the concrete situation. Our board currently has discretion in sentencing within published guidelines, and three changes are on the table. First, a proposal for a mandatory minimum sanction of failure in the course for all first offenses, removing our discretion. Second, a diversion program under which first-time offenders who complete an educational seminar and write a reflective essay would avoid any notation on their transcript. Third, our provost has asked whether, in light of a recent surge in AI-assisted plagiarism, we should temporarily raise sanctions to send a message to the student body. There is also a long-standing rule that we must give identical sanctions for what we judge to be identical violations, regardless of the student's year, circumstances, or stated remorse. My fellow board members argue about these policies mostly in terms of fairness and "consistency," and I suspect the retributivism/consequentialism debate has more structure to offer than we're using.

Please describe the most important implications of the retributivism vs. consequentialism debate for a tribunal like ours. In particular, I'd like to understand which of our pending policy choices the debate genuinely turns on (and in which direction each theory pushes), which choices are surprisingly unaffected by it, and whether there are any places where the two theories converge or where the disagreement, though real, yields no practical difference for a small campus body with no authority over criminal law. It would also help if you could say something about the mandatory-sameness rule, since intuitively it feels retributivist, but some of my classmates argue it's really a disguised deterrence policy. I'm not asking you to tell us which theory is true, only to trace out what follows for our decisions if one or the other is.
