I'm a science and ideas correspondent, and for the past year my beat has been AI risk and the people who think in centuries. At a conference in the spring, a researcher I've come to think of as my most persistent source walked me through an argument for what he called the singleton hypothesis — the view, associated with Nick Bostrom, that a single decision-making agency could at some point acquire decisive strategic advantage and thereafter preclude all challenges, internal and external. I have spent two months trying to find the weak step, because the argument seems to me to prove not just its conclusion but a great deal else that I'm confident is false. Here it is as he gave it, with numbered steps:

(1) In any competitive domain, if the returns to scale in a capability grow faster than the costs of coordinating a larger unit, then larger units of agency will systematically displace smaller ones. (2) In human affairs, the returns to scale in the relevant capabilities — computation, capital, data, and the capacity to project coercion — have outpaced coordination costs at least since industrialization, and machine intelligence accelerates this by substituting for human judgment throughout the chain of command. (3) So there will come a point at which some single agency, whether a state, a coalition, or an AI system, can achieve decisive strategic advantage: the ability to defeat any coalition of rivals. (4) An advantage of that kind is self-reinforcing, because the winner can use it to extend its lead, and the standard corrective mechanisms — countervailing coalitions, internal checks, exit options — all presuppose that no one has it. (5) Therefore a singleton is the default trajectory of this century, and the only open questions are which agency gets there first and how benign it turns out to be.

Here is what bothers me. Substitute "metabolic capacity" for capability and "genetic relatedness" for coordination costs, and premises (1) and (2) appear to predict that natural selection should have consolidated life into a single planetary organism; instead we have ecosystems, arms races, and parasites. Substitute capital and firm size, and they appear to predict a single global firm by now; we got monopolies that were either broken up or quietly eroded. Rome, the medieval Church, and the British Empire each held something close to decisive advantage over their rivals and every one fragmented.

So tell me, as if for my own understanding before I write the column: which step do I reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd like to keep (2), which strikes me as the empirically defensible part and, frankly, my beat. I could live with giving up (4). And I want to know whether the flaw, once you name it, infects the singleton literature my source keeps citing, or only his streamlined version of it — because I have roughly 900 words on Thursday, and I would rather not hand my readers a deterministic story I can't myself defend.
