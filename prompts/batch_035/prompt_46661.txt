I'm a graduate student in moral philosophy, and for the past two years two members of my reading group have been locked in a standing argument about whether the relation "better than" is transitive. One of them — call her the structuralist — holds that transitivity is built into the very idea of a comparative: if A is better than B and B is better than C, then it is a conceptual truth that A is better than C, and apparent counterexamples must be quietly reinterpreting some other relation (choiceworthiness, better-for-someone, better-relative-to-a-particular-standpoint). The other, a Temkin-style intransitivist, holds that the spectrum arguments refute this outright: one can construct a long chain of pairwise comparisons, each between items differing only minutely — a series of lives, or of painful experiences trading duration against intensity — such that each item is judged better than its successor, yet the chain runs from an option that is plainly better to one that is plainly worse; and he insists this is not an artifact of shifting standpoints but a discovery about betterness itself. As an experiment in good faith, they have agreed to try to produce the closing document of a proper adversarial collaboration, and they have asked me to commission a first draft they can then fight over line by line.

Acting as a professional philosopher with expertise in formal axiology and the logic of comparatives, write that draft. It should contain, in order: a numbered section of agreed claims — and these must be substantive, not vacuous pleasantries (candidates worth considering: what exactly the spectrum cases show about the reliability of isolated pairwise judgments; what decision theory actually requires, as opposed to what it is merely standardly assumed to require; whether the relation the cases test is betterness itself or an admixture of it with choiceworthiness; whether transitivity holds unrestrictedly or only within a fixed evaluative standpoint) — followed by a disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is stated in wording that the side holding that view would accept as fair to it; then the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party; and for each crux, a concrete case, thought experiment, or argument that both parties would accept as genuinely bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. For example, if a crux is whether making a single evaluative standpoint explicit and holding it fixed throughout the chain dissolves the appearance of intransitivity, the structuralist should predict the intransitistic judgment evaporates under that regimentation while the intransitivist predicts a residue survives — and the draft should say what observations would count as the residue. If another crux concerns whether "better than" is a context-indexed expression, say what would settle that semantic question and what each side expects the answer to be.

Before writing the final document, do a brief brainstorm: generate candidate formulations for the agreed claims and the disagreement statements, test each one for slant (would the other side refuse to sign this?) and for vacuity (is this something only one side ever seriously doubted?), and assemble the document only after that filtering. Two boundary questions should be settled early and explicitly: whether Chang-style parity cases, where two items are comparable though neither is better and they are not equally good, belong in scope or should be quarantined as a distinct phenomenon; and whether "equally as good as" inherits any of the troubles afflicting "better than." Where you conclude that a portion of their residual disagreement is verbal — a dispute about how to use the words rather than about the facts — say which portion and why; and where you think one side has quietly conceded more than it should in the agreed-claims section, flag it rather than papering over it. Please do not include citations, a bibliography, or references to specific works by title; describe the positions and arguments in your own words, since the draft has to stand on its content alone.
