My roommate has an argument that the whole manipulation/persuasion distinction is a scam, and I can't decide if it's devestating or cheating. (1) On the non-moralized definitions, manipulation is influence that works by bypassing the target's capacities for critical reflection. (2) But those capacities are never fully in charge: framing, anchoring, fluency, mood — some non-reflective processing is always in the causal chain whenever one person moves another's mind. (3) So every successful influence works at least partly by bypassing reflection. (4) A default rule that enrolls you as an organ donor unless you opt out works through inertia, so it bypasses reflection, so it's manipulation. (5) By the same logic, a speech that persuades through emotion, a teacher whose charisma carries the content, and a lawyer who wins on sheer confidence are all manipulation too. (6) And at the limit, giving reasons in a warm voice rather than a flat one manipulates, since warmth changes uptake through non-reflective channels. (7) A concept that covers saying the same argument nicely is broken — that's the paradigm of legitimate persuasion. (8) So (1) is false, and manipulation is really a moralized concept: influence the accuser happens to dislike. She takes this to mean the charge of manipulation never explains anything, it just decorates a verdict. Something feels like it slips between (3) and (5) — "works partly by bypassing" vs "works by bypassing" — but when I try to nail it down I wobble, because maybe in the charisma case the non-reflective stuff really is doing the work. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? For what it's worth I'd rather keep some version of (1), since if she's right that manipulation is just dressed-up disapproval, I want to know exactly what I'd be giving up.
