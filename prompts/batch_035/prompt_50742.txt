So I teach ethics, and every year I hit the wall of consequentialist cluelessness and change the subject, because when I actually consult my gut verdicts on cases, they're a mess. I want to know what principle, if any, captures them. Here they are. Tipping the waiter an extra fiver: where that money goes downstream is unknowable, and I don't think the act is bad, nor that he's obligated to trace its effects — fine, full stop. Buying a t-shirt I know was sewn in a sweatshop: structurally the same kind of purchase with the same kind of unknowable ripples, but I hold it against the buyer, and not just mildly. My friend taking a lucrative job at a tobacco company on the theory that he'll donate most of the salary and thereby save lives: I refuse to credit the speculative distant benefits at all. But my other friend declining to agonize over which cereal to buy, given that its supply chain has effects she can't possibly trace: I think that's fine, she isn't required to run the numbers. Lying on my CV about being fired: not made okay by observing that the truth might have caused more harm somewhere down the line — the act is wrong on its face, and my cluelessness about ripples doesn't touch that one bit. And voting in an enormous election where my ballot has a vanishing chance of being decisive: I think I ought to vote, and the tiny probability carries real weight — which seems to flatly contradict my refusal to let vanishing probabilities into the tip and cereal cases.

So: is there a single principle behind all this, or do I have to pick between a baroque one that saves every verdict and a simpler one that overturns some? I do have a tentative principle of my own, something like: unknowable ripple effects count neither for nor against an act, except when they run through an institution or practice you're choosing to sustain — but I can't tell whether it even survives my own cases, because the tip and the shirt run through pretty similar institutions (a restaurant's tipping norms, a brand's supply chain), yet I only penalize one of them, so either the principle mispredicts one of my verdicts or my verdicts are just inconsistent. Please test it against the cases, repair it if it's repairable, and tell me what the repaired version predicts for the case I keep flip-flopping on: whether to have a child, given that a child's descendants will have effects I'm maximally clueless about and which, for all I know, dwarf everything else I'll ever do. And be honest with me about which of my verdicts the best available principle simply can't accomodate — I'd rather overturn a verdict than pretend it fits.
