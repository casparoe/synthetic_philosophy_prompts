I write novels; it has been my trade for twenty years, and the only technical vocabulary I command with real fluency is the vocabulary of that trade: point of view, psychic distance, free indirect style, the distinction between what a character does and what a novelist does to a character, backstory, revision, the implied reader. A philosopher friend has lately been walking me through P. F. Strawson's essay Freedom and Resentment, and I want to see how much of it I can carry across into my own terms. Please perform the translation for me, as faithfully as you can, and then give me an honest residue report. I am fluent in the craft vocabulary, a beginner in the philosophy, and I would rather be told that something does not translate than be handed a flattering analogy.

Restate, in the vocabulary of fiction-writing, the following elements of Strawson's position, one by one: (a) the pessimist's demand that genuine responsibility require a freedom somehow exempt from ordinary causation; (b) the optimist's appeal to the practical benefits of sanction; (c) Strawson's dissolution of the dispute through the contrast between the participant stance, with its reactive attitudes — resentment, gratitude, forgiveness, hurt — and the objective attitude, in which one regards a person as a creature to be managed, treated, trained, or healed rather than as someone whose attitude toward one matters; (d) his distinction between excuses, which concede the deed but dispute the ill will, and exemptions, which remove the person from the sphere of interpersonal demand altogether; and (e) his observation that the objective attitude can be sustained only intermittently, since it is at bottom incompatible with ordinary involvement with those around us.

Here are the mappings I would like you to try, though you should correct them wherever they mislead:

— the participant stance as close psychic distance: the narration sliding from a detached report of her thoughts into the bare interiority of her own diction, the reader lodged inside the character's will;
— the objective attitude as the clinical register: the character rendered as a case note, a mechanism, something the narrative handles rather than someone it addresses;
— reactive attitudes as what I manage on the page when I want the reader to resent a betrayal or feel gratitude for a loyalty — the reader's genuine anger at a character who is, in the strict sense, my puppet;
— excuses as retroactive revision of backstory: the withheld information that changes how an already-read scene is to be taken, without changing the event itself;
— exemptions as the point at which a character stops being written as an agent and starts being written as a device or a symptom — the child, the fanatic, the affliction.

Then the residue report, which is what I actually want from you: which of these translations carry cleanly, which carry only with distortion, and which cannot be made at all. Three questions in particular. First, Strawson's claim that we cannot in general opt out of the participant stance looks, from where I sit, plainly false: readers opt out whenever the book closes, and I as an author can hold the objective attitude toward a character for four hundred pages. Does this show that his claim concerns something else — life rather than text — or does it show that my vocabulary is missing the distinction between characters and persons? Second, my trade has no way to express that a person's determination is hidden and unrevisable, since in fiction the determination is visible and editable: I can rewrite the villain's childhood next week. Does the revisability of fictional causation infect every mapping above, or only some? Third, psychic distance is a scale, whereas Strawson's participant/objective contrast looks like a binary; is that a grain my vocabulary sees and his flattens, or am I smuggling in the assumption that all involvement is a matter of narrative positioning? Close by taking a side rather than reporting, soothingly, that both vocabularies are somehow richer than the other: I want to know whether the residue points to something the philosophical debate lacks, or to something my craft vocabulary cannot say.
