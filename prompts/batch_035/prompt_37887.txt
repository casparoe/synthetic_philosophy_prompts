I'm a novelist contributing to an anthology of philosophical fiction, and I'd like a short story — roughly 900 to 1,400 words — dramatizing Sartre's concept of bad faith. I should say at the outset what I don't want, because the standard treatment has calcified: the waiter who performs his waiterness, the woman on a date who leaves her hand where it is because she declines to notice it, the gambler who speaks of himself in the third person. A story that merely transposes these is illustration, not fiction, and I could write that myself.

Instead, I want the piece built around what Sartre calls the spirit of seriousness — the character treating their values, duties, and identity as external objects, with the same standing as furniture, such that their choices present themselves to them as obedience rather than invention. Not the freedom-denial of "I couldn't help it" (the crude version), but its quieter cousin: a person who has located the source of their obligations outside themselves and therefore experiences their life as the competent discharge of what is already written. The character should be, in ordinary terms, good at what they do and decent to the people around them; I have no interest in the lazy bureaucrat-as-villain cliché, and an obviously dreadful character would let the reader off the hook.

The constraint that matters most: the character must never be shown doubting, agonizing, or inwardly divided. Bad faith, done in earnest, is seamless from the inside. The self-deception must be legible to the reader only through what the character fails to notice, never through anything the character confides. This means the story needs one small, concrete moment in which the reader can see the refusal happen — some specific thing the character's attention slides off — even though the character never registers it. Choosing that moment is, I suspect, most of the work, and I'd like you to treat it that way: invent the situation and its details rather than sketching them; argue for your choice of point of view (close third person makes the seamlessness easier, but first person might be more unsettling); and end without the character learning anything. This should be a tragedy of clarity for the reader alone.

Two further requests. First, the prose itself should carry the spirit of seriousness — the narration as earnest as the character, without a wink — so that the reader, not the narrator, performs the diagnosis. Second, after the story, tell me briefly what you deliberately left out and why, and where you were most worried the piece would slip, on one side into retelling the waiter, on the other into cheap authorial irony. I'd rather have your honest account of the dangers than a tidy defense.
