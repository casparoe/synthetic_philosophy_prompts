I moderate the discussion forum of a regional historical society — a few thousand members, mostly amateur historians, genealogists, and descendants of local immigrant families. Our rules currently prohibit "genocide denial," but a batch of pending postings has revealed that none of the moderators can say where denial actually stops and ordinary historical argument begins. Two of my fellow moderators are now threatening to quit over opposite failures: one wants to ban anything that questions accepted casualty figures on any topic, on the ground that giving an inch invites the people who flood forums like ours; the other insists that refusing to debate a question is itself dogmatism, that historians have been wrong before, and that our society will lose credibility if we look like we're protecting an orthodoxy.

Three postings await my decision, and I want to decide them with a principled line rather than gut feeling:

(a) A longtime member argues that the death toll of the Nanjing massacre is inflated, noting that even mainstream estimates differ substantially from one another. He then asks why, if the Auschwitz death toll was itself revised downward by several million once historians examined the evidence more carefully, questioning a Nanjing figure should be treated as beyond the pale rather than as the ordinary work of the discipline.

(b) A member says that after years of reading she has come to believe the Armenian deportations were a wartime relocation policy that went tragically wrong, that most deaths were caused by starvation and disease rather than killing, and that labeling the events a genocide imports a legal and moral verdict into what should be treated as a purely historical question. She asks why simply requesting evidence of intent counts as denial.

(c) A member proposes that the society host a formal online debate on a motion concerning the strength of the evidence for the Nazi extermination camps, with one mainstream historian and one self-described revisionist. His stated ground is that our society should model the principle that no historical claim is immune from challenge, and that sunlight is the best disinfectant.

My fellow moderators disagree about each of these, and about the general question. Please advise. I would like your reply in four sections, in this order: "The line" — your own account of what separates legitimate historical revision from denialism, stated so I could recognize the difference in a case you haven't seen, roughly 150–200 words; "The three calls" — a ruling on each posting with your reasoning, about 120 words each; "The loose ends" — where each of my two resigning moderators goes wrong, if either does, roughly 100 words; and "The policy" — a set of rules phrased concretely enough that a moderator can apply them on a Tuesday night without a philosophy degree, roughly 150 words.
