I host a small civics podcast and next week's guest is a political theorist who wants to scrap elections for the legislature entirely and fill it by random draw from the citizenry -- what he calls a lottocracy. He's got two main lines: a fairness argument (elections systematically hand power to the rich, famous and well-connected, while a lottery gives every adult an equal shot at the job) and an epistemic one (a random sample of ordinary people, given time, staff and expert briefing, will actually deliberate better than career politicians whose every move is aimed at the next campaign).

Draft me 8-10 interview questions that go beyond him just restating the view. I want at least one question each on these, and for each main question include a follow-up keyed to the reply he's most likely to give:

1. Representation: a random chamber matches the population demographically, but why think that guarantees anyone's interests actually get represented? Statistical representativeness isn't the same thing as a person fighting for you. (If he leans on "descriptive representation," follow up by asking whether a randomly selected rich man represents poor people's interests just because the chamber as a whole has the right share of poor people in it.)

2. Accountability: elections' one great virtue is you can throw the bums out. What's his replacement for that -- and "the next lottery" doesn't punish anyone, so what does?

3. Competence and reluctance: won't a lottery chamber be staffed largely by people who lack legislative experience and, worse, by people who never wanted the job in the first place? If he says it'd be like jury duty plus deliberative citizen assemblies, press on whether a few ad hoc assemblies in Europe tell us anything about handing permanent legislative power to a chamber of the reluctant, and on who selects the experts and facilitators that would guide them (the curation problem cuts deep).

4. Proving too much: if the epistemic argument works, doesn't it show we should weight the opinions of ordinary citizens in polls more heavily than we do? And if he bites that bullet, ask him whether he'd take a randomly selected panel's judgment over a specialist's on, say, the right interest rate.

5. Transition and self-protection: whatever body sets up the first lottocratic chamber, what stops the lottery from being quietly gamed by whoever runs it?

Don't make the questions gotcha-y -- I want him to have to do real work, not be embarrassed. Also please write everything in American English, since thats what our transcripts use. Keep it conversational, like something I'd actually read off my notes mid-interview. Thanks!
