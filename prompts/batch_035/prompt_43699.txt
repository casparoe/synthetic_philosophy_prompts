Random question that's been living in my head rent free. I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background, but I got curious about Confucian role ethics after a coworker recommended a book on it, and I keep wanting to run real situations through it to see if it actually computes.

Here's the case: the Boeing 737 MAX. As I understand it (correct me if I'm wrong), the MCAS flight control software relied on a single angle-of-attack sensor, engineers inside Boeing had raised concerns about it well before the Lion Air crash, some stuff got flagged and watered down along the way, and after the second crash it came out that various people knew about problems but nobody escalated hard enough to stop the planes from flying. Classic org failure, and as an engineer it haunts me because the usual reading is "individual engineers should've blown the whistle, obey conscience not the org chart."

Now the Confucian part. My (amateur) understanding of role ethics is that it says morality isn't about applying universal rules impartially, it's about the specific obligations you have from your roles -- engineer to employer, employee to manager, member of a company that stands or falls together. And there's that Analects bit where some governor brags about a son who turned in his own father for stealing a sheep, and Confucius basically says no, real uprightness is the father covering for the son and the son covering for the father. That sounds almost like a defense of the culture at Boeing where people protected the company instead of going public! But I've also read that Confucianism has this idea of remonstrance, that a subordinate's loyalty actually *requires* them to push back hard on a superior who's going wrong, not just quietly comply.

So: analyze the MCAS saga through Confucian role ethics, and take it seriously as a theory, not as a strawman for "Asian collectivism." Who were the role players (individual engineer, engineering manager, test pilot, FAA, the flying public -- do passengers even count as a "relation" here?), what would the role-obligations actually say each of them should have done, and does remonstrance save the theory from endorsing mutual concealment here or not? Then flip it around: where does role ethics genuinely illuminate the case, where is it silent or misleading, and does the MAX disaster expose something the theory never anticipated -- like what happens when your "relation" is with a corporation rather than an actual person who can feel shame when you remonstrate with them? I'd rather have an honest "the theory doesn't have an answer for that" than everything forced to fit.
