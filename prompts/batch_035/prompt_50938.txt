I'm an undergraduate taking a political philosophy course in which we've spent the last three weeks on liberty: first Berlin's distinction between negative and positive liberty, then Pettit's republican alternative, freedom as non-domination. In this week's discussion section, two classmates — I'll call them D and M — got into a dispute about whether non-domination is genuinely a third concept of freedom or just negative liberty done more carefully, and our TA cut it off before it resolved. I'll reconstruct the exchange as faithfully as I can.

(1) D opened with the standard example: a slave whose master happens to be kind and never interferes in anything the slave does. On a simple negative conception, D said, that slave counts as fully free — no one is interfering with him — which is absurd; so non-interference can't be what freedom is, and the slave's unfreedom must consist instead in the master's domination. (2) M replied that the example only feels forceful because the master could interfere at any moment, so the slave is not actually secure against interference, and a more careful negative theory can simply build that in: what matters is not merely actual interference but exposure to it — expected interference, the probability of it, the vulnerability. No third concept is needed. (3) D said the master's power is not well captured as a probability at all: suppose the master is scrupulous, or the law effectively restrains him, so that interference is genuinely unlikely — the slave is still dominated, because the master retains the standing entitlement, could in principle revoke the slave's protections, and the slave must watch himself and mind the master regardless; the wrong sits in the relationship itself, not in the risk. (4) M answered that once D talks that way, non-domination has stopped being a concept of *liberty* and become a hybrid of liberty and justice — a claim about how power may legitimately be arranged — so it can't compete with negative and positive liberty as a third item on the same list. And, M added, notice where the view overgenerates: on D's account, a worker in an at-will job whose boss could fire her at any time is dominated in the same sense as the slave, which either trivializes the slave case or shows that non-domination is tracking something other than how free a person is. (5) D's last word was that the overgeneration may be a feature rather than a bug — the at-will worker really is dependent on her boss's goodwill in something like the way the slave is, with the difference one of degree — and that M's repair in (2) had anyway conceded the important point, since being exposed to interference is not being non-interfered with: a dispositional, probability-sensitive negative liberty is a different theory from the simple one the slave example was aimed at, so the example did its work after all.

They were still going when the room emptied out. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move — who is right at each of the five steps, and what, if anything, are both of them missing? One further request: where it matters, please distinguish what is more or less settled among people who actually work on this debate from what is genuinely contested — for instance, I don't want to come away thinking the hybridity objection is a knockdown if it is really just one live position among several, and I'd like to know whether the same is true of the claim that non-domination and a modalized negative liberty ultimately come to the same thing.
