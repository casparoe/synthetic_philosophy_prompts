I'm in a philosophy of action seminar this semester, and last term I took behavioral economics, and the two have collided in my head in a way I can't undo. My study group has been fighting about this argument for two weeks and I need an outside opinion.

(1) Akrasia is acting freely against your own better judgment: you judge, all things considered, that you should do A, and you do B instead. (We got roughly this from Davidson in class — the akratic person does one thing while believing it would be better, all things considered, to do something else.)

(2) Behavioral economics gives a complete alternative story about the same cases. With hyperbolic discounting, my preferences just reverse with distance: on Sunday I genuinely prefer "write the essay Monday" to "waste Monday on my phone," but Monday morning the discount curve puts the immediate phone-pleasure on top, and I genuinely prefer the phone. No ghost of "weakness" is needed — just one curve evaluated at two different times.

(3) Both of those are my preferences, at the times I hold them. Nobody says someone who prefers salad at lunch and pizza at dinner has failed to stick to their own will. Preferring different things at different times isn't a defect, it's just being a creature whose preferences vary.

(4) If every supposed case of acting against your better judgment is really just preference varying over time — which (2) plus (3) seems to hand us — then there's no room left for akrasia as a distinct thing. What we call weakness of will is just the current self losing to a preference curve and then a later self moralizing about it after the fact.

(5) And this is the uncomfortable part: if that's right, "better judgment" can't be privileged. The Sunday preference isn't my real will with Monday's preference being an intruder — the curve is all one structure, and picking out one point on it as the "real me" is arbitrary.

(6) So nobody ever acts against their own better judgment; they just act, and one segment of their preference curve files a complaint later.

Here's my problem. I sort of love this conclusion (it would get my procrastination off the hook), but it seems to prove way too much. My roommate has been trying to quit vaping and describes standing outside the shop mid-panic telling herself this is stupid while buying anyway. She doesn't seem to have a mere preference reversal — she seems to be judging, at the very moment of buying, that she shouldn't buy. And it feels like the argument would dissolve every kind of irrationality, not just akrasia. But I can't see which step is the bad one becuase (3) seems airtight, (2) is literally textbook, and the definition in (1) is what the whole seminar is organized around, so giving that up feels expensive.

Where does it go wrong, if it does? Name the step to reject and what rejecting it costs. If more than one step is broken, tell me both. I'd honestly be fine keeping (5) if that helps — the thought that the Sunday self just arbitrarily appoints itself the real one has always appealed to me — but I want to know the price. And if you think the argument actually works, say so, but then explain how my roommate's case isn't akratic, because that's the part I can't swallow.
