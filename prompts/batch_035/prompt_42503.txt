My friend and I argued about this until like 2am and I still can't put my finger on where he goes wrong, if he even does. We were talking about Dennett's four kinds of minds — the hierachy where you go from Darwinian creatures (hardwired, the bad designs just die off), to Skinnerian (trial and error in real time, the rat has to actually get shocked to learn), to Popperian (you rehearse the options in your head first, so the bad hypothesis dies in your place instead of you), to Gregorian (you import mind-tools, culture, other people's recipes, so part of the testing happens outside your skull).

His argument, as clean as I can make it:

1. The only difference between a Skinnerian creature and a Popperian one is where the generate-and-test loop runs — out in the world vs. inside the head.
2. Running that same loop in an inner simulator isn't a different kind of process, just a faster and cheaper instance of it — and anyway the simulator itself was built by Skinnerian learning sitting on Darwinian design.
3. If a difference in speed and cost doesn't make a difference in kind, then Popperian creatures are just Skinnerians with a good inner loop, not a new kind of mind.
4. Same move one level up: Gregorian creatures are the same thing with prosthetics. Other people's brains and cultural tools are just extra places to run the loop, so that level isn't a new kind either.
5. So everything above the Darwinian level is a gradient of efficiency, not a taxonomy of kinds, and the Popperian/Gregorian levels are bookkeeping.

Then the part that really bugged me: he said, fine, push it down a level too — natural selection is itself just extremely slow blind trial and error, so even the Darwinian level is the same process at a different clock speed. Which means there's really just one kind of thing, generate-and-test, and everything from a sieve sorting gravel to a chess engine to me sits on a single continuum. He says that's just Dennett being a good deflationist and my friend is being consistent. I say it proves too much and the whole point of the hierarchy got lost somewhere. It feels like there's a real difference between a rat that has to touch the electrified fence and a rat that flinches at the thought of the fence, and I don't believe that difference is just speed. But every time I try to say why, my answer collapses too, because whatever imagination adds, its still a feedback process on variations — decoupled ones, sure, but variations getting selected.

So which step should I be rejecting? I'd like to keep the idea that the Popperian level is a genuine discontinuity and not just a Skinnerian with a faster clock, but I want to know what rejecting his step actually costs, because I suspect he's at least half right about one of the levels and I can't tell which half.
