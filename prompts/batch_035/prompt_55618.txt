I'm a science-and-ideas journalist, and a while back I did a piece on the history of ideas about aging, so I finally got around to actually reading Cicero's Cato Maior de Senectute -- the dialogue where old Cato, in his eighties, answers four complaints about old age. A friend of mine who hasn't read it (but has read a lot of pop philosophy) gave me this take on it, which I mostly buy but want checked:

"Cicero's Cato is basically running a Stoic argument: virtue alone is sufficient for happiness, so since old age takes nothing away from virtue, it can't make life worse. He proves the point by example rather than argument, listing famous Romans who stayed productive into extreme old age and concluding that anyone miserable in old age has only their own character to blame. The four objections -- that old age blocks active public life, weakens the body, kills physical pleasures, and brings death near -- are each dismissed with the same move: what the complaint attributes to age is really attributable to character. Even the fear of death is handled this way; Cato says the soul is immortal and will meet everyone it has loved, and that's presented as the only answer a person of character could give."

I'm pretty sure that's right in outline (the four complaints are definitely those, and Cato does lean on examples), but a couple of things smell off to me. For one, I remember the pleasure passage being a lot more Epicurus-flavored than "virtue suffices" -- something about the joys of living well, of a good conscience, and even of eating with an appetite, which doesn't sound like austerity. Also I vaguely recall Cato telling stories about Sophocles composing tragedy in extreme old age while his sons tried to have him declared incompetent, and about him himself learning Greek literature late in life "as one of the pleasures of old age" -- which sits oddly with a model where pleasure is irrelevant. And I'm not sure it's true that every complaint gets the "it's character, not age" treatment: the body section seems to concede that bodily strength declines and to pivot to "keep exercising within your limits," which is advice, not a denial. On death, I think Cato actually offers a second argument for people who doubt the soul's immortality -- something about death being either dreamless sleep or a good of a kind, borrowed from Greek philosophy, and I don't remember which of the two he says he's personally committed to.

So, three questions. First, is my friend's summary a fair reading, or does it flatten Cicero into a Stoic he isn't? (I've seen people say the Cato Maior is really a popularization of Stoic ethics, and other people say it's closer to the Old Academy and that Cicero's own sympathy there is with Plato, not the Stoa -- no idea which is right.) Second, for each of the four complaints, tell me whether Cato's actual move is (a) deny the premise, (b) concede the fact but reframe it as a good, or (c) concede the fact and just tell you how to manage it -- because that's the distinction my friend's summary erases, and I suspect the four complaints don't all get the same treatment. Third, the summary says the death-argument mix is "virtue plus personal immortality optimism," which I think undersells it: lay out what the text actually gives, including the disjunctive fallback, and say whether the religious part is doing load-bearing work or is ornamental.

Don't bother with citations or section numbers, I can look those up -- I want the substance. And please don't source either of us to a specific translation; just describe what the text says. If you genuinely can't tell whether something in my rendering is right (say, whether Cato claims to know the soul is immortal or only to believe it), say so plainly rather than picking one -- I'd rather know what's uncertain than get a confident guess dressed up as an answer. Also flag anywhere my friend's summary, my "smells off" list, or the premise of one of my three questions itself contains an error -- I'm a journalist, my byline is attached to this stuff, so I need the audit to be unsentimental. I've got until Friday to turn the piece around, so do go through it carefully rather than fast -- and if it helps, start by just listing which claims in the summary are uncontroversially true, which are contestable, and which are wrong, then build out from there.
