I write novels, and my current one has forced me to spend an unseemly amount of time deciding how badly to hurt people on the page, so I have started keeping a list of my own verdicts about when pain is bad — and I have come to suspect they do not all hang together. The torture of a resistance courier: bad beyond qualification, and nothing the scene could add or subtract would change that. The labor pains of a character who wants the child and welcomes the birth: I cannot make myself write those as a harm, and when I try, the prose turns against me and reads as triumph. The phantom limb pain of a veteran whose arm is long gone, so that the pain protects nothing and reports nothing: still bad, and the pointlessness of it seems to make it worse rather than better. A character modeled on the clinical phenomenon of pain asymbolia — a woman who feels the dentist's drill as pain, names it pain, points to where it is, and is simply not bothered by it: when I wrote her, the pain would not register as a harm no matter what I did with the sentences. And two cases that trouble me more than the rest. First, the dread my coward feels over a root canal that is then cancelled: that dread reads as a bad thing in its own right, though no pain ever occurs. Second, a character who consents, in an intimate scene, to pain she has explicitly asked for: again it does not read as bad, though I take the sensation itself to be much the same one that was unqualifiedly bad in the torture cellar. My tentative principle is that pain is bad when, and only when, the person in it minds it — which fits the asymbolic, the mother, and the lover, and can perhaps be stretched to the phantom limb, but which I can see failing in three places: the masochist minds her pain in some sense yet is not harmed; a dog presumably cannot mind its pain in any reflective sense yet I cannot write an injured dog as anything but harmed; and there is a case I have not dared to write, a prisoner so conditioned by his captors that he genuinely no longer minds what they do to him, whom I still want to say is being harmed, badly. So my question, as one who has to make these calls for a living but has no training in the philosophy: is there a principle here — whether badness as minding, badness as some relation between the sensation and the whole person it belongs to, or badness located in the felt quality of the sensation itself — that captures my verdicts? If so, state it precisely enough that I could apply it at the desk, tell me what it implies for the pains I have not listed (I would particularly like to know about pain in dreams, and about grief that is described as painful when nothing bodily is wrong), and if there is no such principle, tell me plainly which of my verdicts I should be made to surrender and why those rather than the others.
