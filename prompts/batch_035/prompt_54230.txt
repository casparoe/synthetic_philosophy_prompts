I'm a sixth-form student working on an extended project qualification about whether historians can escape the standards of their own time, and I've been collecting my gut verdicts on cases to see what lies behind them. My tentative principle is that judging a historical figure by a standard is fair only if that standard was 'in the air' at the time — that contemporaries were already arguing about it. I can already see the principle wobbling, but I'm not prepared to abandon it without a fight, so I'd like you to test it against my cases and repair or replace it as needed.

Here are my verdicts. It is fair to condemn the American slaveholders of the 1850s, since abolitionists were loud in their own day. It is fair to call the forced residential schooling of Indigenous children wrong, even though that policy was mainstream and widely praised at the time. It is unfair to call Aristotle foolish for believing that insects arise spontaneously from mud, since nothing then available could have settled the matter. It is unfair to mock Newton for spending decades on alchemy. It is fair to call a witch-trial judge cruel, even though nearly everyone then believed in witchcraft; yet I hesitate to call the frightened villagers who denounced their neighbours cruel, and I'm not sure why. The case I keep flipping on is the nineteenth-century craniologist who measured skulls in order to rank the races: was he a moral monster, or merely a man of the science of his day?

Please reply in British English, in the following order. First, a section called 'My verdicts, restated' (roughly 120 words), laying out cleanly what I judged and of whom. Second, 'The candidate principle' (roughly 250 words), stating the best principle you can construct, tying each clause to the case that motivates it. Third, 'Where it strains your verdicts' (roughly 200 words), stating plainly which of my judgements the principle cannot accommodate, including whether anything survives of my 'in the air' test. Fourth, 'The craniologist' (roughly 100 words), on what the principle predicts for the case I keep flipping on. Fifth, 'What I should give up' (roughly 80 words), giving your honest recommendation about which of my verdicts has to go.
