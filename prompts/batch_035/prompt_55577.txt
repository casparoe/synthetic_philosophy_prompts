I facilitate a small ethics discussion group at my neighborhood community center — about a dozen adults of mixed ages, no philosophy background, one two-hour monthly session. Next month's topic is one I've been chewing on privately for years: whether it's automatically right to save the greater number of people when you can't save everyone. Years ago I came across an essay by a philosopher named Taurek arguing that the numbers don't count in themselves — that when you can rescue either one stranger or five strangers, it isn't obvious you must save the five, and that something like flipping a coin between the groups is a respectable option. I've never been able to articulate why this doesn't strike me as monstrous, and I'd like the group to at least feel the pull of it before we argue about it, not just dismiss it.

Please design the session for me. I want an actual activity — staged choices, role cards, small real stakes like candy bars rather than a discussion — arranged so that participants first act on the "save the five" reflex, then work their own way, step by step, through a sequence of smaller forced choices, and only notice at the end where their own answers have landed them. They should build the reasoning themselves; I don't want to lecture, and I'd rather the name of the view never come up until the debrief. That debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the parts of the argument: what each individual person stands to lose, the pairwise comparisons the view rests on, and where fairness or a coin flip enters. I also want you to tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and which feature of the position the activity simply cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply myself.

Take the role of an experienced designer of interactive philosophy sessions for non-academic audiences. Think carefully and brainstorm a couple of possible framings before committing to one, then give me a complete, runnable plan: materials, roles, rules, a minute-by-minute timeline, and the debrief. Assume I have no one to help me run it.
