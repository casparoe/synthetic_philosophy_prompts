In the historiography course I'm taking this term, the lecturer introduced source criticism almost entirely through a courtroom frame: the historian is a detective, the sources are witnesses, and the central craft is cross-examination. A biased chronicler is a hostile witness; a forged charter is perjury; checking a claim against a second, independent source is corroboration; and the maxim from evidence law that a single witness is no witness was invoked to explain why isolated attestation is treated so cautiously. The analogy was not decorative — it was carrying real conclusions. It licensed the claim that we can know the distant past about as well as a jury knows guilt, and it explained the standard division of labor between Herodotus, the engaging but flawed witness, and Thucydides, the first historian to insist on autopsy and inquiry and so the first properly to cross-examine. It also left a skeptical aftertaste: if every source is a biased witness, and none of them can ever be recalled to the stand, perhaps the whole edifice is a conviction built on tainted testimony.

Please audit the analogy properly rather than just nodding at it. Give me the correspondence table — historian, detective, source, witness, cross-examination, corroboration, provenance as chain of custody, forgery as perjury — and then the disanalogies, sorted by which ones actually matter for the conclusions the analogy was licensing. Some candidates I want you to take seriously: sources are often not testimony addressed to anyone at all (pollen layers, coin hoards, tree rings, ridge-and-furrow field patterns, the accumulated copying errors in a manuscript tradition), so the witness picture doesn't even get a purchase on much of what historians use; bias in a courtroom discredits a witness, whereas a source's open hostility can be among its most evidentially valuable features; there is no live adversary who can be pressed or caught in contradiction, only texts that sit inert; and the detective aims at singular guilt while the historian is often after patterns no culprit intended. I'd also like you to weigh the fact that courtroom proof is designed around a threshold decision with stakes, which historical knowledge arguably is not.

Then take a definite position, and give your reasons rather than leaving me a menu. Does the forensic picture, patched where it needs patching, still support the lecturer's upbeat conclusion that the past is knowable in roughly the way verdicts are — and the skeptical conclusion that it is all tainted testimony — or do the disanalogies gut one, or both? If the analogy fails in its load-bearing parts, say what better analogy captures what historians are actually doing with traces, and defend that replacement against the obvious objections to it; and say explicitly which parts of ordinary source-critical practice — provenance checking, corroboration, suspicion of the self-interested source — survive the switch and which quietly do not.
