I record a small ethics podcast aimed at advanced undergraduates, and next week's guest is a philosopher who has spent her career defending the moral equivalence thesis about deception: deliberately getting someone to believe something false by merely misleading them — by exploiting what a hearer will infer from a true sentence, a gesture, or a pointed silence — is, morally speaking, no better and no worse than lying outright. In her telling, the widespread intuition that the bald-faced lie is worse is a cognitive artifact: we punish what is explicit and avowable more than what is deniable, but explicitness itself carries no moral weight. She is articulate and quick with stock deflections, so I don't want questions she can answer with a rehearsed line about intentions and outcomes being identical; I want questions that force her to take a stand at the points where her position is least comfortable.

Please prepare the question set for the taping — eight to ten questions in total — organized as follows. Section one, "Scene-setting" (two questions, two sentences each), should establish her view and get her to commit, on the record, to its strongest form, including the claim about the asymmetry being an artifact. Section two, "The pressure points" (five questions, the heart of the draft), must include at least one question on each of the following: (a) the paired vignette, where a lie and a half-truth produce the same false belief in the same person at the same moment — say, hiding a frightened friend from a pursuer and telling him either that she left the country, or instead saying that nobody has come through this door, which is true but engineered to be understood as meaning she isn't in the house — and whether anything of genuine moral significance distinguishes the two performances; (b) the sub-sentential escape: if lying, on her favored account, requires asserting something one believes to be false, what does she say about misleading by means that involve no assertion at all — a shake of the head, a misleading silence, letting someone overhear a staged conversation — and does the equivalence thesis survive if the category of the lie turns out to be this fragile; (c) detectability and accountability: the misled hearer has no sentence to deny having uttered and no record to confront, so is misleading not the more insidious practice, and shouldn't her view predict that we blame liars more harshly only because lying is easier to prove; (d) the reform implication: if the asymmetry really is an artifact, should we stop teaching children that there is a morally safer route through a true sentence, and what does she say to parents who insist that a generalized habit of merely misleading corrodes trust just as thoroughly as lying does; and (e) what she would count as evidence against her own view — for instance, whether a robust experimental finding that people judge the two practices differently even when they fully understand the mechanism would shake her, or whether she would dismiss it as just more artifact. For each question, add the reply she is most likely to give, briefly stated, and a follow-up keyed to that specific reply rather than a mere restatement of the original question.

Section three, "The closer" (one question plus a one-sentence back-up), should be the single question she will find hardest to deflect. My candidate is asking her to apply the view to herself: whether she has ever, in her own life, chosen the plain lie over engineering a misleading truth, and what that in-the-moment choice reveals about the asymmetry she denies on paper. Keep the whole draft under a thousand words; I would rather she spend her airtime working through the follow-ups than rebuilding the theory from first principles.
