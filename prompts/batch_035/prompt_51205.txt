I've been reading about the Treaty of Versailles, and one thing about it keeps nagging at me. The famous war guilt clause put the blame for the First World War on Germany as a country and turned that blame into a bill: the reparations were still being paid off almost a century later, by taxpayers who were not even born when the war started. That seemed fine as far as lawyers were concerned — the debt attaches to the state itself and survives changes of government, even though everyone who made the decisions in 1914 was long dead. And the treaty even called for putting the Kaiser himself on trial, though that never happened, so both levels seem to be in play at once. What view of responsibility does this practice presuppose? Is the nation being treated as a continuing agent that can act and be blamed across generations? Or are later citizens treated like heirs who inherit an estate along with its debts? Or is it something else — a claim that the successors received benefits from the wrongdoing, or simply that someone has to pay and the state is the only debtor who can actually be collected from? Could several of these theories all license the same practice, or does the practice really commit to one of them? And is it consistent at all, given that in ordinary life we punish only people whose personal guilt we can show? Finally, if we took seriously the idea that only individuals can be responsible for starting a war, what would have to change in how we think about reparations and official apologies — would they become mere gifts or diplomatic gestures rather than obligations?
