I'm a PhD student in political theory (dissertation on sortition, if that matters) and I've somehow ended up on my city's participatory budgeting council — specifically the "innovation subgroup," which meets monthly and mostly argues about parking. Anyway. A councilman's aide with a rationalist blog has proposed a two-year futarchy pilot: for the next budget cycle, instead of the usual committee deliberation over three competing infrastructure packages, we'd run conditional prediction markets on a set of headline outcome metrics (transit ridership, ER admissions from traffic accidents, business openings on the the affected corridors, that kind of thing) and adopt whichever package the markets say delivers the best results on them. The values part would be set by the assembly in advance — we vote on the metric basket, then traders bet on which package best achieves it.

Half the subgroup is thrilled, half thinks it's tech-industry cosplay. I've been asked to draft the memo with our actual recommendation before the full assembly votes on greenlighting the pilot next month, and I want it to be fair rather than a hit job either way. The stuff I want it to actually grapple with:

- The markets would be small — a few hundred thousand euros total, traded mostly by locals and whoever the aide can recruit — so thin-market and manipulation worries seem serious. But I've come across the counterargument that small stakes don't necessarily mean bad prices, and I don't fully understand when that counterargument works and when it doesn't. The memo shouldn't just assert one side.
- The aide's twist, which I haven't seen discussed anywhere: he wants a veto band. If the market's preferred package only beats the runner-up by less than some margin (he suggested 5% on the composite index), the decision falls back to a supermajority vote of the assembly. He claims this neutralizes the manipulation problem. My instinct is that it doesn't do what he thinks — maybe something about how the veto band changes what traders are actually betting on? — but I can't articulate it cleanly and I'd want the memo to either make the point properly or concede his.
- The reflexivity issue: the market forecasts outcomes conditional on each policy being adopted, but we're adopting the policy based on the market, and I keep going in circles on whether that's a real circularity or just sounds like one.
- Whether the vote-on-values, bet-on-beliefs division of labor even applies here, given the metric basket has to be a stand-in for welfare and everyone knows it's lossy. Some subgroup members say a lossy proxy is fine because it's still better than deliberation-as-usual; others say once the proxy is known, participants will game the metrics directly (build the things that get counted, not the things that count), and I want the memo to say something non-obvious about which worry dominates at our scale.

Write the memo: a concrete recommendation (greenlight, reject, or greenlight with specific modifications — and if modifications, name them), reasons the aide could actually respond to rather than feel dismissed by, and a serious treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side, whatever side that ends up being. Keep it under ~1200 words. Audience is elected members and citizen delegates, roughly half of whom think prediction markets are just gambling and a few of whom think they're magic — so no jargon left unexplained, and please don't open with a paragraph about how markets aggregate information, everyone's heard that one.
