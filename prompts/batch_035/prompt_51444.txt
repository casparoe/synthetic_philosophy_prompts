I am drafting a short essay (roughly 1,200 words when finished) for a university philosophy society's newsletter, on the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism. The draft below contains the full argument, though not the polished prose. Before I rework it, I would appreciate detailed comments, described after the draft.

DRAFT

Title: Who Bears the Offset? Separateness of Persons, Compensation, and the Aggregation of Risk

The familiar objection to utilitarianism, with roots in Rawls and Nozick, is that it sums welfare across persons and thereby permits losses to some to be offset by larger gains to others, treating persons as if they were so many containers of utility. I want to suggest a reformulation of what the objection really targets, and then show that the reformulation has surprising consequences.

Start with a fact about trade-offs within a single life. They are commonplace and, generally, unobjectionable: I accept a loss now for a larger gain later, and I accept a small risk of a bad outcome for a good chance of a better one. What makes these acceptable, I propose, is that there is a single subject who both pays and is paid — one bearer who receives the compensation. Call this the bearer condition. The separateness of persons objection, so reformulated, is not an objection to offsetting as such, but to offsetting without a bearer: when A's loss funds B's gain, no one receives the compensation.

The reformulation matters because it threatens far more than utilitarianism's treatment of distributive extremes. Consider any policy that imposes a small risk of serious loss on each of many in order to confer diffuse benefits: road networks, mass vaccination, infrastructure. Here there are two ways to compute the offset. Ex ante, each person faces a risk r of loss L against a benefit b; if b exceeds r·L, each person is, within her own life, better off, and the bearer condition appears to be satisfied. Ex post, however, someone actually bears the uncompensated loss. So the separateness objection, on my reformulation, must choose between the ex ante and ex post perspectives, and each choice carries a cost.

Take the ex ante route. It satisfies the bearer condition, but then it also licenses Taurek-style lotteries: killing one of five via a fair lottery, to save the other four, gives each person a one-fifth chance of death offset by a four-fifths chance of rescue. If we find the lottery impermissible, ex ante offsetting is not sufficient for permissibility. Take the ex post route. It condemns essentially all ordinary risk imposition, which looks like a reductio of the objection itself — unless one is prepared to embrace a very demanding, rights-based view on which nearly all collective risk imposition is wrong.

I conclude, tentatively, that the separateness of persons is best understood as an unstable hybrid: it needs the ex ante perspective to avoid absurd consequences and the ex post perspective to retain its bite against utilitarianism. The interesting question is whether a principled single perspective can be defended. My tentative suggestion is that the action lies in a distinction between risks that are strictly symmetric across persons and risks structured so that the eventual losers come from a predictable subset — perhaps only the latter involve offsetting without a bearer, in the sense that matters.

REQUESTED COMMENTS

1. Accuracy. Does my statement of the standard objection, and of the moves I attribute to the debate (Taurek on numbers and lotteries, the ex ante/ex post contrast), contain mischaracterizations? I have deliberately avoided attributing specific formulations to specific authors, but if a formulation I present as standard is in fact idiosyncratic, I want to know.

2. The bearer condition. Is the compensation-based reformulation a faithful rendering of the objection, or does it distort it? One worry I have is that the objection might really be about fairness or comparative claims between persons rather than about the existence of a bearer of compensation, in which case my reformulation changes the subject. Please assess this and suggest how I might respond, if a response is available.

3. The dilemma. Is the forced choice between the ex ante and ex post perspectives genuinely forced, or is there an obvious escape route I have missed — for example, distinguishing what it is permissible to impose on someone from what one is required to compensate them for afterwards, or allowing the perspectives to apply at different levels of deliberation?

4. The closing suggestion. Is the symmetric-versus-predictable-subset distinction promising, or is it an ad hoc device introduced only at the end to avoid the reductio? If the latter, would I do better to accept the hybrid verdict and drop the suggestion?

5. Reader's report. Please also give me two or three concrete objections that a careful reader sympathetic to utilitarianism would raise against the essay as it stands — ones the draft does not itself acknowledge — so that I can either answer them or concede them explicitly in the revision.

Please be as specific as possible, and quote or paraphrase the relevant passages of the draft when commenting on them.
