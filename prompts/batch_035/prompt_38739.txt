A guy from my philosophy discussion group pitched me on Peter Singer's drowning child argument the other day, and I can't tell how much of his pitch to trust. This is roughly what he said: "The argument starts with a case. You pass a shallow pond, a small child is drowning, and wading in will ruin your expensive shoes. Obviously you have to save the child — shoes be damned. Children dying of preventable disease far away are exactly the same case, so distance makes no moral difference, and so does the fact that millions of other people could also have helped. From this he derives the rule that you must keep giving until giving more would cost you something morally as important as a child's life — so basicaly you give your income away until you're about as badly off as the people you're helping. And since almost nobody does this, the conclusion is that whenever we spend money on ourselves we are, in effect, letting children drown — we're just as bad as someone who walked past the pond. That's also why he says we should feel exactly the same guilt in both situations. Mind you, none of this works unless you're a utilitarian — if you're not, the whole argument collapses."

I'll admit the pitch sounded coherent. But something about the last line smelled off — I have a vague memory that Singer deliberately built the argument so that non-utilitarians could accept it too, though I couldn't say why when put on the spot. And I honestly don't know enough to spot where else the pitch might be cutting corners, if it is at all.

Where it's distorted or wrong, tell me what the fair version would say instead. And then the part I most want: does the "we're just as bad as the pond-walker" conclusion survive once the pitch is corrected, or was it riding on the distortions?
