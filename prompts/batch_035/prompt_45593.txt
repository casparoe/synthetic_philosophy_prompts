Background first: I'm a backend engineer and team lead at a mid-size company, roughly 40 product teams plus a few platform teams. No philosophy background, obviously. Since forever, each team here has picked its own stack: languages, databases, CI setup, on-call rotation, you name it. It's messy, but teams ship fast and they know their own systems. Our new CTO now wants to pull a bunch of these decisions up a level — one approved stack list, one CI pipeline everyone has to use, a company-wide on-call policy, mandatory security tooling, the works. His pitch at the all-hands was basically consistency, and, his words more or less, that it's the only setup you can actually audit. A group of team leads pushed back saying decisions belong with the people closest to the work, and his response was, fine, tell me *which* decisions and *why* — right now you're just telling me you don't want to give up control. And the annoying part is I think he's half right: our pushback is vibes.

Years ago I idly read something about how some countries split decisions between the national level and regional/local governments, and the idea was roughly that the higher level should only grab a decision if the lower level genuinely can't handle it as well — but that there was an actual test behind it, not just "local is nice." I can't remember where I read it or what its called, so:

1. Is there a real, worked-out version of this idea, and what's it properly called? I gather the US, Germany, Switzerland, and the EU all do versions of this — do they share some set of questions you can run a decision through, or does each just wing it?

2. Take our actual disputed list — programming language per team, database choice, the shared CI pipeline, the on-call policy, security review requirements, and whether teams can pick their own observability tooling — and run each one through whatever the principled test is. For each one, tell me which level should own it, and whether the answer is "one level owns it fully" or something more like "floor set centrally, discretion above it." I'd definately like to know which of the six the CTO should win.

3. Where does this idea go wrong? Known ways it gets gamed or produces the wrong answer. And be honest about whether it even fits a company: a CTO can just rewrite the rules next quarter, whereas states, I assume, can't get overruled like that. Does that break the whole framing, or is it still worth using? For what it's worth, the single-sign-on rollout that got forced on everyone last year turned out fine, so I'm not dogmatic about either side — I just want the leads' next doc to be more principled than "trust us."

If I've garbled the thing I half remember, say so. Please don't point me at books or papers, I want the actual reasoning in plain terms. And write in American English — a coworker already complained that my docs mix spellings.
