I spent most of my working life in marine geophysics, and I happened to be a graduate student during the years when continental drift, in its new plate-tectonic dress, went from something polite people ignored to the organizing framework of the whole discipline. I remember the sequence well: the magnetic stripe anomalies over the ridges, the deep-sea drilling cores, and then, rather suddenly, everyone I knew describing the same coastlines and the same seismograms with an entirely new vocabulary. Now that I am retired I have taken up philosophy of science, and my reading group has reached Kuhn.

My younger colleagues in the group, none of whom lived through it, tell me that what happened to my generation was a gestalt switch, on the model of the duck-rabbit figure: after the switch, one does not acquire new reasons so much as see the very same data differently — subduction zones where one previously saw geosynclines — and one cannot see both ways at once. From this they draw a strong conclusion: that the acceptance of plate tectonics was not a rational, evidence-driven process in the way we veterans like to think, that the two communities were incommensurable in the sense of having no common ground for argument, and that the eventual consensus was a matter of generational turnover and persuasion rather than justification. One of them has even suggested that my confident recollection of being compelled by the magnetic anomalies is a post-hoc rationalization of something closer to a conversion experience.

I would like a careful audit of the gestalt analogy before I concede any of this. Please set out explicitly what is meant to correspond to what: the ambiguous figure, the two readings of it, the instant of reversal, the unlearnability of seeing it both ways at once, the perceiver who switches. Then tell me where the mapping fails. It seems to me that the magnetic stripes were not ambiguous in the way a duck-rabbit is — they did not flip back and forth under inspection; that some of my colleagues remained bilingual readers of both frameworks for years, which the duck-rabbit picture seems to forbid; that the change took a decade and tracked new instruments and expeditions rather than occurring in an instant; and that a good deal of argument between the camps was carried in a shared vocabulary of station data, error bars, and fieldwork. Which of these disanalogies matter for the conclusion my reading group wants, and which are merely loose fit? Does the claim of full incommensurability survive once the analogy is repaired or abandoned, or does it depend essentially on the analogy's failures? And if the gestalt picture is the wrong model, what would you put in its place — something from perception, from language, or, better still, from the history of a science like mine?
