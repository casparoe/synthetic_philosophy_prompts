I'm a high school senior doing an independent reading project on computability theory, and I've become stuck on what is supposed to be a foundational point rather than a technical one. As I understand it, Turing's analysis from the 1930s connects an informal notion — a function being calculable by a finite procedure, a human working mechanically with pencil and paper, unbounded in time but limited in working memory per step — to the formally defined notion of Turing-machine computability. Nobody has ever exhibited a procedure that seems effective in the intuitive sense but outruns the machines, and the famous independent formalisms (Turing machines, Church's lambda calculus, general recursive functions) all turn out to compute exactly the same class of functions. Yet when I try to find out what *kind* of claim this equivalence is, the literature splits into camps that seem to talk past each other. One camp says it is essentially a definition: once you fully grasp "effective calculability," you see that it just means Turing-machine computability, so the thesis cannot be false, and there is nothing left to discover — the way "bachelor" means unmarried man. A second camp insists it is a deep but unprovable mathematical truth, in the same epistemic boat as other classic results that can't be derived from anything more basic, with the convergence of the independent formalisms serving as evidence that we've hit on a natural, non-stipulative boundary. A third camp treats it as an empirical hypothesis — a claim about what any device could in principle be made to do, refutable by future physics if, say, some physical process could be harnessed to decide membership in a non-recursive set. And a fourth camp says it is a conceptual analysis of a somewhat vague practice-based notion, judged by fruitfulness and precision rather than strict truth or falsity.

My working hypothesis is that this is not one disagreement but several stacked on top of each other: the empirical camp is really talking about a different, physical thesis (a claim about machines in our universe, which would allow idealizations like infinite precision or unbounded parallelism that the original human-calculator version explicitly forbids), while the definitional and deep-truth camps are genuinely disagreeing about the status of the original claim — and that *that* disagreement might be the same stale one philosophers have about analysis generally. But I'm not confident, because the definitional camp seems to have an answer to the convergence argument (the formalisms coincide because they all capture the one concept we were reaching for) and the deep-truth camp seems to have an answer to the stipulation charge (if it were mere stipulation, the choice would have been arbitrary, but Turing's restrictions on what a human clerk does in one step feel forced, not chosen). Maybe neither camp can lose, which makes me suspect the question is somehow ill-posed — but I can't see exactly where the equivocation, if there is one, enters.

Could you take on the role of a philosopher of mathematics with a serious background in computability theory, and think carefully before committing to a verdict? Would a formal characterization of "effective calculability" that provably diverges from Turing computability settle it, or would each camp simply absorb that result in its own way? And if nothing could settle it, say so plainly and explain why the debate keeps going anyway.
