I'm on the committee for our PhD program's political philosophy comprehensive exam, and it's my turn to write the utilitarianism question. I want to build it around Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, but I'm completely sick of reading answers that just recite the theorem and conclude "therefore utilitarianism is the rational choice." Anyone who memorized that should fail this question.

Here's the crux I want it to turn on. As far as I can tell the theorem has two soft spots, and I want candidates who only know the headline result to bleed marks on both. First, the comparability issue. The theorem is normally glossed as: if each individual is an expected-utility maximizer, and the social planner is too, and some Pareto condition holds, then the planner's utility function is a weighted sum of individual utilities. But each person's utilities are only unique up to positive affine transformations taken separately, so "the weighted sum" isn't even well defined until somebody picks a scaling across persons, and the theorem itself is silent on how. Whether Harsanyi derives interpersonal comparability or just quietly assumes it somewhere is, to me, the real question. Second, ex ante vs ex post. The Pareto premise is stated over lotteries, and that's where the Diamond-style objection bites — the weighted-sum planner ends up indifferent between flipping a coin to decide who gets a benefit and just handing the benefit to whoever was already lucky. Something like that; a good candidate should be able to reconstruct the case from scratch rather than just name-drop it.

So write me one essay question, answerable in about 90 minutes, that can't be passed by reciting the result. Use a "candidate X claims..." framing — for example, X claims that the theorem plus impartiality (equal weights) delivers full-blown utilitarianism with interpersonal comparability thrown in as a free bonus, so egalitarian redistribution has no rational basis and is just sentiment. The question should force assessment of X on three fronts: (1) what the theorem strictly establishes versus what the interpretation layer adds, (2) where the scaling/comparability assumption actually enters — my own view is it sneaks in via the equal-weights/anonymity step, but I'd accept a well-argued alternative, e.g. that it enters through the planner's EU representation itself, so don't hard-code my view into the marking scheme as the only right answer, and (3) whether the ex ante framing lets a consistent egalitarian reject one of the premises rather than fight the conclusion.

Then give me a model answer of roughly 650 words and a marking scheme out of 20 built on five must-hit points, with marks for each and a one-line note on the mistake each point is designed to catch (the way candidates typically go wrong — conflating the two theorm versions, treating "weighted sum" as meaningful without a scaling, that kind of thing). If the impartial-observer variant comes up, keep it to one short paragraph; I don't want this turning into a survey of Harsanyi's whole career.
