I am a retired physicist — statistical mechanics and condensed matter theory were my bread and butter for close to four decades — and in retirement I have been working through the history of Islamic philosophy, which has left me genuinely puzzled about the debate on causation between al-Ghazālī and the Aristotelian tradition he attacked in The Incoherence of the Philosophers. My understanding, which may itself need correcting, is that al-Ghazālī denied that fire has any power to burn cotton: the fire's contact with the cotton is merely concomitant with the burning, and God creates the burning directly, each time, with the regularity we observe reflecting only His habitual way of acting, not any necessity in nature itself. Averroes later pushed back in The Incoherence of the Incoherence, defending a natural necessity in things. I would like you to restate this whole dispute in the vocabulary I actually used at work: states of a system, equations of motion, initial and boundary conditions, symmetries and conserved quantities, effective field theories and the coarse-graining behind them, perturbations and their propagation. So, for instance, the Avicennian view al-Ghazālī attacks seems to me to say that the differential equation together with the state compels the future state, while al-Ghazālī seems to say the equation merely describes a regularity in what God does, with nothing in it that forces anything — the Hamiltonian doesn't "do" anything any more than the fire does.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher with genuine expertise in both medieval Islamic philosophy and the philosophy of physics, and begin by laying out the mapping explicitly before evaluating it. Restate each side's position as faithfully as you can in my terms — including the parts about God's habit being revisable, which I gather is how al-Ghazālī accommodates miracles — and then give me the residue report I care most about. Which of their claims translate cleanly, which survive only with distortion (and say what the distortion is), and which cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all? I notice, for instance, that the fundamental equations I spent my life with are time-reversal symmetric in many cases, contain no causal arrow, and nowhere encode a "must" — on my reading, the causal glue both parties were arguing about was never in my formalism in the first place, which makes me suspect al-Ghazālī is closer to how physicists actually talk about laws than his Aristotelian opponents were. Is that a fair thing to say, or have I smuggled in something Humean that al-Ghazālī would not have accepted? Finally, tell me what the residue shows: is it a defect of my vocabulary — something it cannot say that the kalām debate articulated — or a sign that the dispute was less substantive than it looked from a distance? Please be honest about the fudges rather than quietly resolving the ambiguities in one direction, and think through the failure cases before deciding where they point.
