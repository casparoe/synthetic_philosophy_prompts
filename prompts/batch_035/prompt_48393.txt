A friend of mine (philosophy of language masters, now works on model evals) has been on my case about LLMs for months. Her view is that an LLM isn't an agent or a believer of anything — it's a simulator, and the "helpful assistant" you chat with is just one character among many that the underlying model can be made to play. To demonstrate this she ran the same base model with two system prompts, one giving a cautious scientist and one giving a paranoid radio-host, asked both the same question about vaccine safety, and got confident, flatly contradictory answers. Then she leaned back and said: "See? Neither of them is the model talking. There is no model that talks."

Fine as far as it goes, but then she pushed it into an argument, and I can't find where it breaks. (1) At the mechanical level, everything the model does is next-token prediction: it's a function from a string to a distribution over continuations, and that's all it is. (2) The voice that continues the text is therefore a simulated character, set up by the context — scientist, radio host, assistant, whoever. (3) What a character says is said in character, and things said in character are not asserted by the simulator: an actor who says the butler did it, on stage, asserts nothing. (4) So nothing an LLM outputs is ever asserted by the LLM. (5) And if nothing it outputs is asserted by it, then it can never be wrong (wrongness needs an assertion), its outputs can't be testimony, and you can no more learn from them than you learn from a novel. She thinks (5) follows straight out of (4), and honestly the inference looked fine to me too, until I noticed what it proves. Last month one of these things found a genuine race condition in my dataloader — on her view that wasn't the model telling me something, and I didn't learn anything from it, which seems just false. And a while back one invented a whole citation for me, author names and venue and year, the works, and a labmate lost an afternoon chasing it — on her view that wasn't a falsehood either, just a character saying things, and nothing that was even false happened.

So: where does this go wrong? I'd like you to actually commit — name the single step you'd reject, explain why that step looked okay on the way in, and tell me what rejecting it costs. If you drop (3), you owe me a story about what marks the difference between a stage and a chat window, since my friend will ask why the fresh-context assistant answering a plain question is "in character" at all. If you drop (5), you owe me a story about who, if anyone, does the asserting in a chat, and what the fake-citation episode was — a mistake by whom, exactly? And if you think her two-persona demo doesn't actually license (2), that only showing outputs are context-sensitive, say so, but then tell me what (2) would need to be true for the argument to run. Don't give me a tour of the options and let me pick — take a side, even if your not fully sure, and tell me what you're least confident about. For my part, I'd rather keep (1), which seems like plain training mechanics, and I'm not giving up that I learned about my dataloader; so my suspicion is the rot is somewhere in (3)–(5), but I've stared at it for a week and can't tell whether the problem is the actor analogy or some slide inside (5) itself.
