Second-year PhD student in political science here (comparative politics by training, but next semester I have to TA a section on democratic theory, so I'm trying to work out what I actually believe about whether groups can be competent at truth-tracking). I know there's an old math result, Condorcet something, saying that if every voter is a bit better than a coin flip and their mistakes are independent, then a big enough majority is almost certainly right. Fine. But when I go through concrete cases, my gut does NOT follow the naive version of that, and I want to know what principle, if any, is actually behind my verdicts. So here are my verdicts, and I'd like you to find the principle that captures them — or tell me which ones have to go.

1. A 12-person criminal jury where each juror is maybe 55% likely to reach the correct verdict, and their errors are genuinely independent of each other. I'd trust the majority verdict over any single juror, and I'd trust it more as we add jurors. Fine so far, that's just the theorem.

2. Five specialists in exactly the relevant subfield, each right maybe 70% of the time, versus 500 randomly selected citizens each right 55% of the time. Plugging in the numbers the 500-citizen majority should come out ahead of the five specialists. My gut still says listen to the specialists. And the twist: if I could point at the single most competent person in the 500 and had to defer to ONE agent, I'd defer to the 70% specialist rather than to the 500-strong majority, even though the majority is "better" on paper.

3. 300 economists all trained in the same department, using the same models, asked whether some policy will raise GDP. My gut says the large number barely helps — they'll all be wrong together. But I ALSO think a panel of 200 epidemiologists is more trustworthy than a panel of 5 of them, even though epidemiologists share training, textbooks, and methods too. I honestly can't say what the difference is, if there is one.

4. If the average voter is actually worse than a coin flip (say 48%), adding voters should make the majority LESS accurate. I accept this in the abstract but refuse to apply it anywhere — I don't believe any real electorate is below 50% on the questions that matter, or maybe I just don't like where it leads. Not sure which.

5. Weighted voting. If we knew who the competent people were, we should weight their votes more. But if we knew that, why have a vote at all — just ask them. So my gut says both "weight by competence" and "skip the vote entirely", which can't both be a rule.

So: is there one principle that gets all five, or close? My half-formed guess is something like "defer to the majority only when you can't identify who's better informed and have no reason to expect correlated errors" — but I don't see how that survives case 3, and the elitist smell of case 2 bothers me. Whatever principle you land on, please test it against my five, tell me plainly which of my verdicts it has to throw out and why, and then — the part I actually care about for teaching — tell me what it predicts on cases I haven't listed. Like: does it tell me to trust a referendum more or less than a national election on the same question? A prediction market versus a committee of experts? Those are exactly the questions my students are going to ask me and right now all I have is vibes.
