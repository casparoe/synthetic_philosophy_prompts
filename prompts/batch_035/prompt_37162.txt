I've spent two decades writing literary fiction, much of it from inside the heads of believers — a Pentecostal mother, a lapsed Jesuit, a Hasidic boy losing his Hebrew — and I've come to Wittgenstein late, through the 1938 lectures on religious belief and the later remarks on forms of life and hinge certainty. My persistent impression is that he is describing my actual job: that when he says a believer's confidence in a Last Judgment differs in kind, not degree, from confidence about a train timetable, he's drawing the same distinction I draw every day between a proposition a character asserts and the way a voice behaves. But I want this tested, not flattered.

Restate Wittgenstein's position on religious belief in the vocabulary of craft, as faithfully as you can, and then give me the residue report. The vocabulary I mean: the distinction between what a character says and what their voice is; free indirect discourse as a mode of inhabitation rather than report; show versus tell; genre as an implicit contract between writer and reader; backstory that structures a novel without ever being stated; a character's deepest commitments as revealed by what they do with their hands under pressure; the difference between a character who is wrong and a character who is inconsistently written; the implied reader; the notion that a line of dialogue only has force inside a form.

Translate at least these four claims, one by one:

1. That the believer's utterance is not an empirical hypothesis on a par with a weather forecast — try it as the difference between a stated proposition and characterization, where the belief lives in the how of the rendering rather than the what.
2. Forms of life — the thought that religious utterances mean only against a whole practice — as something like genre: a line that lands in one form and is dead air in another.
3. Hinge certainty — the unquestioned stance a character cannot give grounds for without the narrative world collapsing under them.
4. His observation that the believer and the scoffer seem not to contradict one another — that their disagreement slides past itself. What is that in my terms? Characters at cross purposes? A dialogue scene in which neither party is addressing the other's utterance? Or is it something dialogue, as a craft object, cannot represent at all?

Then the residue report, which is what I actually care about. Which of Wittgenstein's claims translate cleanly? Which had to be bent to fit, and in which direction — did the craft vocabulary distort him, or did you quietly sand down the craft concept to make it fit? And which of his claims has no expression in my vocabulary whatsoever?

Two further questions. First, my working suspicion is that Wittgenstein is best read as saying religious belief is characterization rather than belief-state: that the believer is like a character whose faith is constituted by how the voice behaves, not by a proposition held. Is that a genuine translation, or have I smuggled in my own framework's founding assumption — that everything inner is ultimately a matter of how it is rendered? If it is smuggling, name what the craft vocabulary cannot say and say whether the failure lies with the vocabulary or with Wittgenstein's original, which may be less clear than it looks. Second, there is a standing dispute over whether Wittgenstein is offering a theory of religious belief or refusing to theorize and only dissolving our confusions about it. Does that dispute translate into something I know — the difference between a poetics and a technique, between a statement about what fiction is and a set of working moves? If it does, say which side of the dispute my craft analogy naturally favors, and whether that favoring is an argument or an artifact of the translation.
