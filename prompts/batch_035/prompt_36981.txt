Three of us have been slowly reading Gödel, Escher, Bach together, mostly as an excuse to argue, and last weekend we hit the MU puzzle and it started a fight that never got settled.

The setup, in case it's been a while: you start with the string MI and can apply four rules as often as you like — (1) if a string ends in I you can tack a U onto the end; (2) if a string looks like Mx you can double everything after the M, giving Mxx; (3) any III occurring in a string can be replaced with a U; (4) any UU can be deleted. Hofstadter asks whether you can produce MU. We churned out strings for an hour and never got there, and then it went like this.

Dmitri said the puzzle is a lesson in limits. From inside the rules, no amount of cleverness will ever get you to MU, and that's the same humility Gödel teaches — some things just aren't derivable, and the system itself can't even tell you which ones.

Fay said that's exactly backwards. The puzzle is rigged so that if you step outside the system and look at the strings as numbers, it collapses. She said the number of I's in any reachable string is always odd — MI starts with one I, and rules like doubling preserve oddness — so MU, with no I's, is impossible. And crucially you can SEE that it's impossible from outside, which is the whole point of the chapter. She called this jumping out of the system and said that ability, not derivation, is what intelligence actually is.

Dmitri pounced: MI doubled gives MII, which has two I's, even. Her outside view died ten seconds after being stated. But he went further and said a corrected version wouldn't rescue her either, because the outside view only worked at all because Hofstadter chose rules with a number-shaped hole in them — with uglier rules there'd be no tidy invariant, and she'd be stuck inside with the rest of us. And his stronger claim: for the systems Gödel actually cares about, ones strong enough to do arithmetic, there's no final outside — every jump lands you in a new system with its own blind spots. Calling jumping "intelligence" is selling a ladder with no top rung.

Fay didn't fold. Fine, the oddness thing was a slip; the real invariant is something about threes, though she couldn't reconstruct it on the spot. And even granting that every jump lands you in another system, that spoils nothing — the value was never reaching some system-free vantage point, it's that problems which looked eternal from the old place dissolve from the new one. Which is literally what just happened to MU, she said, however you end up counting the I's.

I couldn't referee this and it's been bugging me sense Saturday. Can you go through their exchange and say who's right at each step? Specifically: what's the actual invariant, and does it really settle the MU question (I got lost trying to work out whether MU counts as having zero I's and whether zero even counts as divisible by three)? Was Fay's move wrong in a way that matters or just a wrong guess at the right kind of fact? Was Dmitri right that the outside view only exists because the rules were chosen to have one, and does that support his no-final-outside claim or is he sliding between two different points? And on the big question — is the chapter a lesson in limits or a lesson in escaping them — who's closer?
