I'm a novelist, and I keep a notes app full of moments where my own adjectives misbehaved on the page, and I want to know if there's one principle behind all of them or whether I'm just muddled. Fair warning, this might be the weediest thing I've ever asked anyone.

Here's what's in the notes so far:

1. I typed "a tall man" for a hero I'd decided was six foot even, and it bugged me all afternoon till I made him six-one. Six feet felt like not quite tall; six-one felt safe. And yet if you asked me where the line actually is — six-oh-and-a-half? — I have no idea, and I also feel like there IS one, somewhere, that my ear knows about and I can't state. I think that's why I changed it. My ear knew.

2. A woman character, five-eight. I could not write "tall" and I could not write "not tall" — both came out sounding like small lies — so I wrote around it: "she stood level with most of the men in the room." And here's the thing: for her, I'm confident it's not that I don't KNOW which one is true. It's that neither is. At five-eight, "tall" for a woman just isn't either one.

3. The book I'm drafting follows a woman from 29 to 63 across the chapters. No single chapter is where she stopped being young — each chapter she's only a few months older than in the last, so I never had to say it. But I'm sure that somewhere in the manuscript there's a last page on which "young" is still true of her, even though that page looks exactly like the page after it. Said out loud this sounds insane. I still beleive it.

4. Same man as in (1), five-eleven, walks into a jockey's bar and I called him "the tall one" — fine. Six chapters later, at a wedding full of basketball players, I called him "not tall" — also fine. I don't think either was a mistake, and I don't think they contradict each other, though I can't say why.

5. My editor wanted "balding" instead of "bald" for a character with maybe 40% coverage up top. I kept "bald" — "balding" felt like a cowardly hedge. But in his own POV chapters I let him think "I'm not bald" and no reader has ever flagged it. From outside, bald; from inside, not bald. I'd defend both.

And the one I keep going back and forth on: she's sixty-eight. "An old woman" read cruel in one draft; "not old" read like a lie in the next; the current draft says "no longer young," which is true but is a dodge, and I know it.

So: is there one principle that fits the whole notebook? My own attempt so far is something like "there's a fact of the matter, but it's a fact about the context — the jockey's bar and the basketball wedding are different worlds with different cutoffs, and inside each world the line is sharp even if nobody can see it." Does that survive entry 2, where I'm sure there's no fact at all? I honestly can't tell whether entries 1 and 2 are disagreeing with each other or just talking about different things, and I suspect at least one of them has to go. Tell me which, what the repaired principle says about the sixty-eight-year-old, and what it predicts for a case I haven't thought of — for instance, whether "she's either old or she's not old" is something I'm even allowed to believe about her. I'd rather lose an entry than keep all five and get mush.
