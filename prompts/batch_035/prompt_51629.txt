I'm a second-year PhD student in moral philosophy, and I've committed my next three years to the debate over consequentialist cluelessness — roughly, the problem that agents acting on consequentialist reasoning cannot know enough about the long-run effects of their actions for expected-value calculations to be trustworthy. James Lenman's original objection, together with Hilary Greaves's distinction between simple and complex cluelessness, frames the literature for me: in the simple cases, we have no idea which act does more good; in the complex cases, we have well-grounded probabilities over each act's consequences, and they come out in favor of the act that intuitively seems wrong. My department wants a narrow, tractable dissertation question rather than a survey of the debate, and I have to settle on a direction by the end of term.

I'm torn between three candidate framings, which is where I'd like your judgment. (1) A sharpness question: the standard arguments silently assume precise numerical credences over long-run outcomes; if we instead model agents with imprecise credences, simple cluelessness arguably dissolves into suspension of judgment while complex cluelessness gets sharper, and it is an open technical matter whether that trade actually holds. (2) A gateway question: whether cluelessness is symmetrical across all actions, or broken by effects that pass through irreversible choke-points — extinction, permanent value lock-in, one-time institutional choices — so that a small class of actions has long-run consequences that are more predictable than their diffuse short-run ripples; the thought is that this would rescue a restricted consequentialism even if the broad version fails. (3) A self-trust question: whether an agent should give extra normative weight to her own all-things-considered estimates, in a way that lets her act on them despite cluelessness about the considerations she has not considered — a second-order move I suspect the literature has barely touched.

Please rank these three for someone with three years, a decent formal background but no empirical training, and the goal of producing work that moral philosophers outside the cluelessness niche would actually care about. Defend the ordering: what makes a dissertation question worth three years in the first place, how the three candidates trade off on tractability, importance, and dependence on contested assumptions, and which of the three you would tell me to drop entirely. Finally, be explicit about the fragile points in your own ranking: which argument, consideration, or development — say, a new formal result, or one of my framings turning out to have been worked through already — would most move you to a different ordering, and where you think your own reasoning here is most likely to be wrong.
