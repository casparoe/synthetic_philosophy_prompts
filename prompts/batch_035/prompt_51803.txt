I am putting together a diagnostic instrument for my advanced seminar in social and political philosophy, and I would like you to draft it. The instrument is a quiz, to be administered in the first session, titled tentatively "Which theory of exploitation do you actually hold — and is alienation doing any independent work for you?" The pedagogical point is to sort my students by their verdicts on cases, not by which labels they find congenial, so no question may name any theorist, school, or position; every option must be a substantive answer to the case presented.

The quiz should have eight to ten questions, each a short concrete scenario followed by two or three answer options. The cases must be drawn from the following situations, on which the major theories of exploitation genuinely come apart, and you should design the options so that different answer patterns point toward different underlying commitments:

(a) A self-employed artisan with no employees who sells at world-market prices to far richer buyers who reap a large surplus from the exchange.
(b) A sweatshop paying wages well above local alternatives, where the workers chose the job over worse options, in a context of severely unjust global background conditions.
(c) A drowning person's rescuer who extracts an enormous fee, and a lender who extends a loan on harsh terms to someone facing bankruptcy.
(d) A highly paid professional who would be even better off under an equal division of society's productive assets, contrasted with a destitute worker who would be only marginally better off under such a division — the paradoxical verdicts on who is exploited.
(e) A worker-owned cooperative whose members vote on everything but must compete in a market that forces them to intensify their own labor and lay off a subgroup.
(f) An inherited-wealth heir living comfortably off returns on capital without working, and, separately, unpaid domestic labor within a household.

The scoring key should map answer patterns onto at least these positions: the labor-theoretic view on which exploitation consists in the extraction of surplus labor from direct producers; the view on which exploitation is fundamentally a property-relations or unequal-exchange phenomenon rooted in unequal ownership of productive assets; the view on which exploitation is a forced or involuntary transfer regardless of the labor theory of value; the view on which the wrongness lies in the distributive background rather than the transaction itself; and the mutual-advantage or voluntariness view on which consensual exchanges in the absence of force and fraud cannot be exploitative at all.

The second axis concerns alienation. Several of the questions should be phrased so that answers reveal whether the student treats alienation as reducible to exploitation or as capable of coming apart from it — include at least one case of a worker who loves the work, understands the enterprise fully, and is paid nearly the entire value she adds, and at least one case of grinding, meaningless but autonomous smallholding labor performed without any wage relation at all. The key should sort students among the view that alienation is estrangement from one's essential productive nature, the self-realization view on which alienation is a failure of meaningful engagement in work, the reductionist view on which alienation is merely a symptom of exploitation, and the position that exploitation without alienation and alienation without exploitation are both possible.

After the key, add two short sections. First, an honest note on which pairs of positions the quiz cannot cleanly separate — where two theories predict the same answers across all the cases, and what further case would be needed to split them. Second, a note on the unoccupied cells: which combinations of verdicts across the exploitation and alienation axes nobody in the literature seems to hold, and your view on whether each such combination is unattractive, incoherent, or merely unexplored.

Constraints: the audience is advanced undergraduates with no prior exposure to the literature, so every case should be describable in a few sentences of ordinary economic life, with no technical vocabulary in the questions themselves. Do not include citations, bibliographies, or attributions of specific arguments to specific authors; the key may use standard position labels, but the questions may not. The finished quiz should fit on two sides of a handout.
