So I'm in a philosophy of math course this semester and last week we did Cantor, Hilbert's hotel, finitism, etc. A guy in my study group, Marco, has been pushing this argument on our group chat for days and I can't land a clean hit on it:

(1) If an actually infinite collection could exist, then a hotel with a countably infinite number of rooms, all occupied, could exist.
(2) In that hotel, the manager can move every guest from room n to room n+1, and room 1 is then free for a new guest, with every guest still having a room and every room still having a guest.
(3) Before the shift the hotel is full, and being full means having no room for a new guest.
(4) The shift adds no rooms and removes no guests, so the number of rooms and guests is exactly what it was before.
(5) So the very same hotel, with the very same rooms and guests, both has and does not have room for a new guest.
(6) Nothing can both have and not have room for a new guest.
(7) So no actual infinite can exist — not in hotels, not in the points on a line, not in the set of natural numbers, and not in an infinite past, so the universe had a beginning too (he likes that last bit a lot).

Here's what bugs me. The move from (3) to (5) obviously leans on something like "if every room is occupied, you can't take in another guest," and I know that's exactly the intuition Cantor taught us to drop, since the evens and the naturals are the same size via n ↔ 2n. But when I say that, Marco says fine, then the natural numbers are contradictory too, and he seems happy to bite that bullet, which feels like the argument proving way too much. And I can't figure out whether (7) about hotels/past events and (7) about the naturals are even the same claim — like maybe he's right about infinite concrete collections and dead wrong about the mathematical ones, but I don't know if I'm allowed to split it that way without just being a hypocrite.

We're each writing a short reply paper for Friday and I want mine to be an actual diagnosis, not vibes. Which premise or step would you reject, what's the equivocation or slide if there is one, and what does each way out cost? And please tell me whether the "same size" talk in (4) is doing something sneaky, because I half suspect it is. Also I'd rather keep the set-theoretic infinite than save (7) about the infinite past if I have to choose, so factor that in.
