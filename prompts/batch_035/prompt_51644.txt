Exam question: "Explain the hole argument as presented by Earman and Norton. What is manifold substantivalism, what role does general covariance play in generating the problem, and what options are available to the substantivalist in responding to it?"

Student's answer: "The hole argument is an argument against manifold substantivalism, the view that the points of the spacetime manifold exist in their own right, independently of any fields defined on them. Earman and Norton ask us to consider a region of spacetime — the 'hole' — and a smooth transformation that is the identity outside the hole but nontrivial inside it. The transformation leaves the metric field completely untouched and merely relabels the points: inside the hole, the point that previously carried one value of the metric now carries a different one. Since the theory is generally covariant, both assignments are solutions of the field equations, and since general covariance guarantees that all diffeomorphically related solutions are physically distinct, the theory is committed to an absolute indeterminism: the field equations cannot determine which metric value sits at which manifold point. The upshot is that any generally covariant theory of gravitation is indeterministic, which is absurd, so general covariance must be abandoned as a physical principle. This is essentially the same argument Leibniz gave against Newton in his correspondence with Clarke, where Leibniz introduced what he called Leibniz equivalence to argue that diffeomorphic worlds are one and the same world. Earman and Norton simply updated it for general relativity. The substantivalist therefore faces a dilemma: either give up the independent reality of manifold points and become a relationist, or accept the indeterminism. There is no third option, since any attempt to hold on to the points while identifying diffeomorphic models collapses into relationism anyway."
