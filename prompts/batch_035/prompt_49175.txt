I've just finished a graduate seminar on philosophy of mind, and I've come out of it holding a position I'd describe as uncompromising about the causal exclusion argument. I think Jaegwon Kim was basically right: given the causal closure of the physical and the implausibility of systematic causal overdetermination, when a physical realizer is causally sufficient for some behavior, an irreducible mental property that is distinct from that realizer is excluded from doing genuine causal work — what looks like mental causation is either a case of coincidental overdetermination (which cannot be the general pattern) or a convenient way of tracking physical causes. My own twist, and the part I most want pressure on, is that I take the argument to generalize far beyond mentality. If exclusion bites anywhere, it bites everywhere: on gene-level explanations in evolutionary biology, on macroeconomic explanations of recessions, on the "beliefs" of a chess engine or the internal states of a trained network. In each case I suspect we have either covert identity or reduction, or mere bookkeeping — so that the autonomy of the special sciences becomes a matter of practical bookkeeping rather than metaphysical fact. My seminar friends call this the "greedy exclusion" view, and they hate it; they keep insisting that it proves too much and that this is a reductio, whereas I think proving too much is the point.

So here's what I want from you: a map of what would actually count against this view. Consider the standard escape routes — proportionality constraints on which causal properties matter, interventionist accounts of causal relevance, the charge that the exclusion argument presupposes a production-style conception of causation that more deflationary accounts simply reject, and the line of thought that necessarily co-occurring but distinct properties can still differ in causal relevance. For each, tell me whether it merely trims the view or genuinely wounds it, and distinguish the objections a sympathetic defender would concede as a real cost from those they would dismiss as confused. I'm also unsure where the orthodoxy actually stands: which premises are effectively settled among specialists (causal closure itself? the overdetermination premise?) and which are genuinely contested (whether exclusion applies to functional properties generally, whether realization collapses into identity, whether Kim's own fallback of "causal inheritance" saves mental causation or just relabels the problem)? Please be scrupulous about that distinction rather than presenting one resolution of the exclusion debate as if it were consensus. Finally, rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and give me an honest assessment of whether my position has been insulated by qualifications — for instance, by quietly sliding from "exclusion bites everywhere" to "everywhere, modulo pragmatic convenience" — in a way that leaves it answerable to nothing. If you think the generalization to biology, economics, and computing is precisely where the view is most exposed, say so, and describe the kind of case that would show it.
