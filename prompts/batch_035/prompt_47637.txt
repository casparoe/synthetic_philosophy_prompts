I'm a philosophy PhD student working at the intersection of philosophy of mind and ethics, applying for a one-year dissertation research fellowship, and I need a two-page proposal (roughly 1000–1200 words). Please act as a senior philosopher who has served on fellowship committees — the kind of mentor who pushes back on vague framing before anything gets written. Start with a short brainstorming section: lay out three or four candidate ways to sharpen my half-formed idea into a researchable question, say which you find most tractable and why, and only then produce the proposal itself.

The topic I want to work on: whether sentience comes in degrees, and whether moral status should track those degrees, across the full range from insects down to thermostats and other simple artificial systems.

My half-formed worry is that the standard debate quietly rests on two assumptions, and that if either is wrong, the debate is asking a malformed question.

First, the debate assumes sentience is a scalar — that beings can be arranged along a single dimension, a kind of great chain of sentience, such that a bee is simply more or less sentient than an octopus or a fish. But the underlying capacities seem multidimensional: nociception, affective valence, the integration of information over time, the possession of a self-model, and so on. It's not obvious these compress into one ordering, and if they don't, then the question "does moral status come in degrees?" inherits a presupposition there may be no fact about.

Second, the debate assumes moral status is one thing — a single standing that either is or isn't graded. But maybe it decomposes. Perhaps the intensity with which a creature's suffering counts in our deliberations scales smoothly with the vividness of its experience, while its eligibility for certain kinds of harms — death, confinement, the frustration of preferences about its own future — has a threshold structure tied to whether it has future-directed interests at all. If so, the gradation question has different answers for different components of status, and asking about "moral status" wholesale conflates them. This would matter in practice: it could mean, for instance, that a being low on the gradient is owed real concern about pain relief but no concern at all about being painlessly killed, and that no single welfare framework fits every point on the spectrum.

The proposal should state the research question precisely; explain why the existing positions (the straightforward graded view, threshold views, and the view that moral patienthood is simply binary) leave it open; describe what I would actually do, month by month; say what would count as a result and what would count as the project failing — in particular, whether the proposed decomposition could collapse back into a single undifferentiated notion, and what I'd do if it did; and give an honest assessment of whether a year is enough for this or whether it needs to be narrowed. The fellowship committee includes non-philosophers — a cognitive scientist and a lawyer who works on animal welfare regulation — so the proposal should make the stakes concrete: what difference would the answer actually make for how we treat animals at different points on the gradient, or for how we reason about increasingly sophisticated AI systems?

A few constraints: don't include a bibliography or a literature review organized by citation — refer to positions and arguments by their content, and I'll add the references myself. And please don't end on a hedged, everything-depends note: the proposal should commit to the framing you judge most promising and defend that choice.
