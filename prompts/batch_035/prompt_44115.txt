My closest friend from graduate school and I argued until two in the morning about the difference principle, and she closed with a line of reasoning I can neither refute nor accept. She conceded the entire Rawlsian apparatus up to the standard justification of incentives, and then used that very justification to indict the principle itself. Reconstructed, her argument was this:

(1) A society's basic arrangement is just only if it can be justified to each citizen from a standpoint that abstracts from that citizen's particular position, talents, and advantages; otherwise what passes for justification is merely an armistice among bargaining powers. I accept this — I take it to be the point of the veil of ignorance — and she granted it for the sake of the argument.

(2) The difference principle is meant to make (1) operational for income and wealth: inequalities are permissible only when they work to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged.

(3) In its familiar application, the principle certifies substantial market inequality because the more talented will supply their skill and effort at full intensity only if reward is attached to it, and under that incentive scheme even the least advantaged are better off — better housed, better schooled, better doctored — than under any feasible arrangement with flatter rewards. An inequality is just when it is needed, given what the talented would in fact do without it.

(4) In certifying these incentive inequalities, the principle treats the self-interested demands of the talented as fixed background: it assesses the rules given the people, and never assesses the people. She insisted this is not a distortion of the familiar application but its exact logic, and I could not see how to deny it: the alternative would be asking the talented to demand less, and a principle addressed to the basic structure alone has no place for such a request.

(5) Nothing in that logic singles out the self-interest of the talented. What allows a set of demands to count as fixed background rather than as an injustice can only be how costly it would be to change — how entrenched the demands are — and never their content; for if content mattered, the principle would have to assess the people after all, and (4) would be surrendered.

(6) So run the same reasoning on any entrenched selfishness. If a majority's intolerance of a minority is so settled that legislating equality provokes a backlash leaving the minority worse off than a tolerated-discrimination regime would, then that regime is just: it maximizes the least advantaged's prospects over the feasible alternatives, with the majority's disposition as fixed background, exactly as with the talented. A family law shaped around what men will withhold unless they are privileged is just on the same footing.

(7) Therefore the difference principle, as standardly applied to incentives, launders entrenched selfishness into feasibility constraints and then certifies the result as just. Her conclusion: the principle must reach past the basic structure into the motives and ethos of citizens, or be abandoned.

I cannot accept this. She rests content on the first horn, having justice assess the ethos, but I argued all night that the principles of justice address the basic structure and leave persons' preferences and commitments outside their remit — and I find the applications in (6) intolerable besides. The argument seems to prove too much: it would make justice endorse whatever selfishness is settled deeply enough, leaving the principle with no critical grip anywhere. Yet each step looked fine as she stated it, and I cannot name the false one. Tell me which step to reject, explain why it looked fine when she said it, and price the alternatives honestly. If I must choose, I would rather keep the principle's confinement to the basic structure than keep its familiar use in defending actual market inequalities — but I cannot tell whether that preference is principled or merely comfortable, and that is what I want you to settle.
