I am midway through drafting a novel whose conceit I intend to be rather more than a gimmick. It is narrated in the first person by a character who, over the course of the book, gradually discovers that she is a character — that every observation she records is being composed by a hand she cannot see. My plan is to have her begin commenting on the very sentences that describe her commenting on them, so that the narration folds back on itself in tighter and tighter coils: sentences about sentences about sentences, with her "I" sitting, as it were, at the center of the spiral. I have even built a small formalism for myself, numbering the levels of recursion, so that I can raise or lower the depth of the loop chapter by chapter as her self-knowledge deepens.

I read Gödel, Escher, Bach some years ago, and more recently Hofstadter's follow-up, I Am a Strange Loop, and I took from them the following: whenever a symbol system acquires the capacity to represent itself, a self — an "I" — necessarily comes into being as an emergent pattern in that loop. If that is right, then self-reference is not merely a literary decoration but a mechanism, and my question is a practical one about thresholds. How many levels of self-reference must the loop contain before the narrator counts as a genuine self? One level seems clearly too few; my current draft manages perhaps three, and I could easily engineer five or six by having her annotate her annotations. Is there a principled point at which the recursion becomes "strange" enough to constitute her, in something the way a strip of paper only becomes one-sided after the full twist? I would rather calibrate this precisely than leave it to impression, since the entire architecture of the novel depends on her being more than a puppet that comments on its own strings — and, more urgently, since I have begun to worry that if I go too deep, I will not know how to bring her out again.
