So I had this four-hour argument with my friend Priya last weekend, and I need to actually do something with it or it's going to eat me alive. Background: I sit on the "emerging research review" panel at a national lab -- basically a low-level committee that looks at experimental proposals (mostly condensed matter, some quantum computing stuff, occasionally wilder things) and flags ethics/safety issues before they go up to the real oversight bodies. It's mostly paperwork about lasers and cryogens. We get maybe 40 proposals a quarter and I have like 20 minutes per proposal, and the panel is five physicists and me, none of whom have read a page of philosophy since undergrad.

The argument: Priya has gotten deep into panpsychism -- the view that consciousness or experience is a fundamental feature of matter, so even elementary particles have some tiny glimmer of it. She thinks this is at least as plausible as the alternatives (and honestly the alternatives ARE pretty mysterious when you press on them -- the hard problem and all that). Her claim was that if there's even a modest chance she's right, then basically every physical process we run in the lab -- particle collisions, quantum annealing runs, whatever -- involves some amount of experience, and possibly suffering, and nobody at any lab on earth so much as writes a footnote about it. She also made an expected-value point that bugged me: if the "amount of experience" scales with anything like complexity or integration, even astronomically diluted suffering times an astronomically large number of systems could add up to something non-trivial, sort of like how the St. Petersburg thing makes an infinite expected value out of tiny probabilities, though maybe that analogy is bad, she'd yell at me for it.

My side of the argument: it's unfalsifiable, the combination problem (how do micro-experiences add up to a rabbit, let alone a person?) has no answer, and if you take it seriously as a constraint on action you can never do anything -- every process is tainted, so the view gives no guidance at all. Also the moral status of an electron, if it has one, is so unlike the moral status of a mouse that I don't even know what "counting" it would mean. Her comeback: that's a decision under moral uncertainty problem, and decision theory under moral uncertainty doesn't say "do nothing," it says "weight the possibilities," and we already do exactly this with animals where we're unsure about their inner lives. Then we were going in circles and ordered more food.

Anyway. I can't put "maybe the electrons are sad" on a review form for a collider experiment, that's how you get a reputation. But I also can't fully shake the feeling that "we have no idea and refuse to think about it" is not a defensible institutional position either. So: write me a decision procedure my panel could actually run. Requirements:

- Something I can apply in 15-20 minutes per proposal with no philosophy background, written so I can put it in the panel's internal guidelines without embarrassment.
- It has to be honest about the fact that the underlying metaphysics is contested and probably unverifiable, and still produce an action -- the whole point is it can't just output "more research needed" for everything, that's the current situation dressed up.
- It should distinguish, somehow, between cases where a panpsychist consideration would actually change what we do versus cases where it washes out (e.g., does it matter whether a proposal creates novel physical configurations vs. just runs a process that happens in nature constantly anyway? Is a D-wave anneal different from a volcanic hot spring, morally? I genuinely don't know, that's the kind of question it needs to handle).
- It needs to say where the procedure stops with "no action, log the concern" and where it escalates to an actual human judgment call, and it should mark the steps that are basically values dressed up as facts.
- Then run it on three real-ish cases from our docket: (1) a routine proposal for a 48-hour ion trap quantum simulation, (2) a proposal to run ultra-intense laser pulses through diamond anvils to probe warm dense matter, and (3) a collaboration wanting to use our facility for analog gravity experiments involving acoustic black holes. Pick whichever one you think best shows the procedure giving an answer I won't like, and actually give me that answer.

And yes I realize I'm asking you to operationalize something Priya couldn't even argue coherently for one evening. That's kind of the point -- if it can't survive being turned into a checklist for tired physicists, it was never going to affect anything anyway.
