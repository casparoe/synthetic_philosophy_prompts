Having spent a career in radiochemistry, I have lately been reading philosophy of probability, and I would value an audit of a summary I have encountered. A popular book on chance and determinism, whose title I omit since I am working from notes, characterises Lewis's Principal Principle as follows:

"Lewis's Principal Principle says that once you know the objective chance of an event, your degree of belief that it will occur should be set equal to that chance. So if the half-life of radium-226 tells you there is a 50 per cent chance a given atom decays within 1,600 years, you should be precisely 50 per cent confident that it does. The principle is really a definition of chance: the chance of an event just is the number a fully rational agent ought to assign to it, and on this basis Lewis showed that objective chances exist and can be reduced to long-run frequencies. A fatal flaw was found later, however, the undermining problem: because a probabilistic theory itself holds only with some chance, the principle yields an outright contradiction, and Lewis conceded that it had to be abandoned."

Most of this chimes with what I think I understand, but the closing claim surprised me, as I had gathered that the principle is still taken seriously. Please go through the summary claim by claim and tell me which parts a philosopher working on objective chance would accept, which are harmless simplifications, and which are genuine misrepresentations. I should particularly like your judgement on four points: whether the principle is a definition of chance or instead a normative constraint relating chance to credence; whether it applies unconditionally once the chance is known, or only relative to admissible evidence; whether Lewis identified chance with long-run frequencies; and whether the undermining problem refuted the principle outright or led to a refinement that preserved its spirit. Please answer in British English.
