so I'm in a philosophy of biology course writing a term paper section on whether causal-role accounts of biological function can make any sense of malfunction at all, and my professor keeps saying the interesting cases are never the textbook ones. I keep trying to build the case I want and every version I write cheats somewhere. So I want it built to order.

Here's the spec. One scenario, a single organism described in one paragraph, in which the selected-effect (etiological) account says an organ or behavior is malfunctioning while the causal-role account says it isn't — and where the organ is in perfect physical condition and is actively doing something at the moment described. The hard part: none of the standard moves are allowed. Not a vestigial organ (no degraded whale pelvis stuff), not a spandrel or some novel useful side effect selection hasn't noticed yet, and not an intact-but-idle organ that just never gets the occasion to fire (that seems like no-opportunity rather than malfunction, on either account — tell me if I'm wrong there). Also, the organ must genuinely have lost its selected capacity — present members of the species just can't do the ancestral thing anymore — without being damaged, and that loss has to be recent, on the order of a few hundred generations. I suspect the recency matters for whether the etiological account still assigns the function in the first place, so please tell me if that's right and what happens at the boundary.

Then walk me through the design. What the etiological account says, and why the malfunction verdict falls out of its own standards instead of being stipulated by me. What the causal-role account says, being explicit about which systemic capacity is being analyzed when it calls the organ fine — this is where I want to be careful, since I suspect the verdict is capacity-relative and a critic will say I rigged the choice of capacity to get the answer I wanted. Which feature of the case is doing the work. What happens to each verdict if you remove that feature (for instance, make the loss ancient instead of recent). And finally how a causal-role defender would attack the case — ideally without just conceding that all malfunction talk belongs to the etiological side. Constraints on the biology: realistic only, no genetic engineering, no futuristic tech, just ordinary evolution. The scenario can be a made-up species if that helps, but it should be biologically plausible, the kind of thing an evolutionary biologist would nod at rather than roll their eyes at.
