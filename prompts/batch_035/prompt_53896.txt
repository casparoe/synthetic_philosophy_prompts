So this argument has been rattling around in my head since my cousin's graduation party in June. My uncle Ray (retired chemist, big on documentaries) and my friend Dana, who's a psychiatric nurse, got into it after dinner. Ray's opening move: our moral feelings -- the gut sense that hurting kids is wrong, that backstabbing a friend is bad -- are just things evolution installed in us because they helped our ancestors' genes. Guilt kept cooperators in line, disgust kept us away from disease and parasites, loyalty protected kin. So, he said, these feelings evolved to spread genes, not to track truth, the same way our craving for sugar and fat exists to keep us fed but is now actively misinforming us about what's healthy. Morality is basically the sweet tooth of social feelings: useful to the genes that built it, but it doesn't point at anything real. Dana pushed back that this proves way too much: her eyes, her ears, and the brain Ray used to construct the debunking argument all evolved too, and she doesn't see him concluding that his own reasoning is unreliable. If evolutionary origin debunks a belief, then science, math, and the debunking itself all go down the drain together.

Ray said no, there's a difference: with perception, seeing things as they actually are is precisely what gets you to survive -- misjudging the cliff is how you fall off it -- so evolution rewards truth-tracking there. But with moral feelings, natural selection only needed them to make us cooperate; whether they matched any independent moral facts was irrelevant, because fitness doesn't care about moral truth even if there is such a thing. Dana's reply was that he's being too quick about the cooperation/truth gap: maybe flourishing and surviving well really are what goodness is about, in which case evolution pointed us in roughly the right direction after all. Ray said fine, but then "good" just reduces to whatever spreads genes, so a genocide that made a group more fit would be morally good on her account, and since obviously it isn't, the account is broken -- so she has to pick: either evolution gave us a bad moral compass, or the compass was never pointing at anything. Dana came back with what I thought was her best point: even if our moral gut feelings are unreliable, that shows we should distrust intuitions, not that there's no moral truth -- we don't conclude from the sugar craving that nutrition isn't a real subject, we correct it with science. Ray answered that moral reasoning has nowhere to stand, because its premises all come from those same evolved intuitions: it's the debunked instrument trying to recalibrate itself, like trusting the crooked ruler to check the ruler. Dana's last word was that if he takes that seriously he's sawing off the branch he's sitting on, since his whole argument was built with the same intuitions-plus-reasoning he just declared untrustworthy. They sort of shrugged and dropped it, but I keep going back and forth and honestly can't referee it myself.

Can you go through their exchange move by move and tell me who's right about what? Like, which of Ray's moves actually work and which overreach, and same for Dana -- I'd especially like to know whether her "this debunks your reasoning too" point is a real problem or just a rhetorical dodge. Also, and this matters to me: where you give a verdict, please be clear about which parts of this are basically settled among people who study this stuff and which parts are genuinely up in the air -- I don't want to walk away thinking some contested view is the established answer. If the honest answer is that nobody knows how the strongest version of this plays out, say that rather than picking a winner by default.
