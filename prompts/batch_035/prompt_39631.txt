I'm taking an intro ethics course, and we just finished the section of Kant's Groundwork on the formula of universal law. The way our instructor glossed the test: act only on maxims you can at the same time will everyone to act on, where the operative failure mode is that universalizing the maxim destroys the possibility of achieving the end the maxim states — as with false promising, where if everyone made promises they intended to break whenever convenient, nobody would believe promises, and so the liar's own end of getting the loan could not be achieved. Fine so far. But that night at dinner I tried applying the same mechanism to my own career plans and walked myself into an argument I now can't get out of: (1) An action is impermissible only if its maxim cannot be willed as a universal law. (2) A maxim cannot be willed as a universal law when universal adoption of it would make it impossible to achieve the end stated in the maxim — this is the mechanism that does the work in the false promising case. (3) My maxim in going into dermatology is roughly: I'll train for this specialized occupation in order to earn a living. (4) If literally everyone adopted that maxim, everyone would be a dermatologist, no one would grow food or build clinics or treat anyone outside dermatology, and no one could earn a living by it — the maxim's own end is defeated by its own universalization, exactly as in (2). (5) So becoming a dermatologist is impermissible, and by parity of reasoning so is any specialized occupation, which means Kant's test condemns the division of labor outright. That conclusion seems obviously absurd, and yet each step looked like something my instructor endorsed.

I can see five possible escapes and can't tell which one to take: perhaps the maxim in (3) is misdescribed, and my real maxim smuggles in a clause like 'in circumstances where enough other people are doing other jobs,' which has to be carried along into the universalization; perhaps the test is supposed to hold fixed the background of what everyone else is doing and vary only my conformity to the maxim; perhaps (2) is too crude and the genuine test concerns making an exception of oneself rather than ends being defeated; perhaps the false promising case doesn't actually work the way (2) says it does, since a world of universal liars still contains gradations of trust; or perhaps I'm simply wrong that the conclusion is absurd and Kant should bite the bullet. I'd rather keep (2) than anything else, since it's the only part of the lecture I understood well enough to reuse, but I want to know what each choice costs. Assess my argument in exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, one point per escape route, stating for each whether taking it dissolves the argument, what it costs, and whether it also undermines or preserves the false promising case — no text before the first point or after the last.
