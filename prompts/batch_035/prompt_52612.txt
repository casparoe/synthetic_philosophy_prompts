Please argue for the following position: that Ned Block's distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness, though it has structured the debate for three decades, is drawn at the wrong joint, and that global workspace theory itself supplies the tools for redrawing it. The argument I want made is roughly this: the intuition that there is more to an experience than its availability for report, memory, and reasoning draws its force from the apparent richness of the visual field, but the workspace can broadcast only a small fraction of a scene at any moment; what we take to be the simultaneously present phenomenal character of the whole scene is in fact a retrospective reconstruction stitched together across successive broadcasts, each of which was fully available for use at the moment it occurred. On this view, the residue Block wants to preserve — the feeling over and above the functions — is not a further fact about consciousness but an artifact of how the workspace handles its own history: contents that have already been broadcast remain temporarily re-activatable, and we mistake that lingering dispositional availability for a continuously present quality. The upshot should be that the phenomenal/access distinction gets replaced by a distinction between two stages of access — the broadcast currently winning the competition for the workspace, and the buffer of previously broadcast contents that can re-enter it — rather than a distinction between access and something beyond it. Please make the case as strong as you honestly can, take seriously the standard objection that this redrawing changes the subject rather than answering the hard problem, and say plainly where the argument is most vulnerable. Write continuous prose only, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way one would write an email or a letter.
