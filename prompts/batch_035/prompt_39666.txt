I've been testing my reactions to violations of the independence axiom (and its sure-thing relatives) in decision theory, and I can't find the pattern in my own verdicts, so I want a principle that captures them. The classic Allais choice — a sure million versus a gamble giving a 10% shot at five million, an 89% shot at the million, and a 1% shot at nothing, with preferences flipping when the million outcome is removed from both options — seems to me clearly permissible: a defensible preference for certainty that I would not call irrational. But when I imagine the same structure scaled down to a certain $10 versus a gamble on $10 to $15 with a small chance of getting nothing, the preference for the sure thing strikes me as irrational, or at least as evidence of some malfunction rather than a value. A person whose independence violations leave them open to being money-pumped through a sequence of trades, each of which they endorse when offered, is, I think, irrational. Yet someone who refuses to bet on a draw from an urn of unknown composition even at objectively favorable odds, thereby violating sure-thing reasoning, seems rational to me — and so does someone who declines a bet at fair odds because their accepting it would be evidence that the coin is biased against them, which also breaks dominance-style reasoning. Preference reversal, choosing gamble A over gamble B while demanding a higher selling price for B, I count as flatly irrational. The obvious candidate principle — a violation is irrational just when it makes one exploitable — doesn't obviously handle the scaled-down case (no one is exploiting the $10 agent) or the evidential one (that agent is exploitable by a bookie who knows the coin is loaded, yet I think he is fine). So: is there one principle that delivers all of my verdicts, stated precisely, with the case motivating each clause? What does it say about a further case I'm unsure about — a person whose Allais-type preference vanishes when the same options are described in terms of total wealth rather than gains from a reference point: is the original preference thereby shown irrational, or merely shown to rest on a permissible sensitivity to framing? And please be honest about which of my verdicts the principle cannot save. End your reply with a single line of the form "Principle: ..." stating your best principle in one sentence.
