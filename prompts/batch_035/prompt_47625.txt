I'm preparing a position statement for an AI-welfare reading group on whether reinforcement learning agents can suffer, and I want a consistency audit before I circulate it. Here are my commitments:

(1) Functionalism about suffering: what matters is causal organization, not substrate, so silicon systems are in principle eligible for moral patienthood.
(2) The best mechanistic account of valence in humans is the reward-prediction-error signal carried by dopamine neurons, so the structural analog of valence in an RL agent is its temporal-difference error signal.
(3) I take the wanting-versus-liking dissociation from incentive-salience research seriously: the systems that drive pursuit and learning can come apart from those that constitute felt pleasure or distress, so a signal that drives learning and behavior is not thereby a valenced experience.
(4) A precautionary stance: where there is genuine uncertainty about sentience and the population at stake is or could be enormous, expected-value reasoning favors extending some moral concern even at fairly low credence.
(5) My considered verdict that current model-free deep RL agents trained by TD methods to play Atari games or the like are not moral patients, and that welfare discourse about them is premature.
(6) A gradient claim: a persistent, model-based agent with recurrent internal state, homeostatic objectives, and months of continuous training is a far stronger candidate for patienthood than any episodic model-free agent.

I don't know where the trouble is, if anywhere. Which of these are genuinely in tension, which only look to be, what is the cheapest fix for each real one, and what does each fix cost? Can the package as it stands actually deliver the gradient I claim in (6), or does that verdict float free of my stated commitments? Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating all of them as equally bad, and do not recommend revisions I haven't earned.

- "apparent_tensions": an array of objects, each with "commitments" and "why_it_dissolves"
