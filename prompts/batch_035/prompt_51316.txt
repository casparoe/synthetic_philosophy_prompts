I work as a policy analyst on the content-integrity team of a mid-sized online platform, and I am tasked with drafting the protocol our moderators will use when they encounter posts advancing conspiracy claims. The volume is high — several hundred escalations a week, each needing a disposition within 24 hours — and the stakes are asymmetric: an erroneous "harmful conspiracy theory" label suppresses legitimate criticism of institutions, while an erroneous pass amplifies claims that can cause real-world harm. Our moderators are trained on content policy but have no background in epistemology, so the protocol must be usable by someone reading it for the first time in the middle of a shift.

What I do not want is the familiar editorial reflex of treating "conspiracy theory" as a synonym for "false." The philosophical literature has convinced me that this collapses several distinct things: a conspiracy as an event (corporations and governments demonstrably do conspire, sometimes), a conspiracy theory as an explanatory hypothesis, and the sociological phenomenon of self-sealing communities that treat all counterevidence as confirmation. I want the protocol built on the distinctions that actually do epistemic work — among them, the scale-to-evidence ratio (a theory positing a handful of self-interested actors with a motive requires less independent corroboration than one positing thousands of tightly disciplined participants across decades), the self-sealing test (does the theory explain away its own counterevidence more fluently than it explains anything else?), and the provenance test (is the poster reasoning from public evidence, however badly, or from a framework in which official denial itself is evidence of guilt?).

Please write the protocol as an ordered decision procedure: the tests to run, in sequence; what counts as a pass or fail on each; where the procedure stops with a definite disposition (label, downrank, escalate to our harm review, or leave alone); and — this is essential — where it explicitly hands the decision to a human judgment call because the remaining question is one of values, institutional trust, or downstream harm rather than anything the epistemology can settle. Be honest about the asymmetry I mentioned: the procedure should tell moderators when they do not know enough to say a claim is false, even when it smells like one.

Then run the protocol on three worked cases: (a) a post alleging that a specific pharmaceutical company suppressed internal data about a side effect of one of its drugs, citing a real settlement and court records but extrapolating to a cover-up the records do not show; (b) a post claiming a recent outbreak originated in a research facility, arguing from publicly reported safety lapses, where official bodies are genuinely uncertain and the post does not treat their uncertainty as proof of guilt; and (c) a post claiming that a global network of elites fabricates all elections, in which the moderation team's preferred outcome is an unambiguous "false information" label but where I suspect the honest epistemic assessment will not support it in that form. For case (c) in particular, tell me plainly what the protocol yields, what it refuses to yield, and where the authority to remove or label must come from instead. Finally, include a short section on the ways this protocol predictably misfires — the kinds of true-but-marginal claims it will slow down, and the kinds of false-but-official-sounding claims it will wave through — so that I can put those limitations in the documentation I submit with it.
