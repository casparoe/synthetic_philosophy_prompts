You are to act as a philosopher of the social sciences with a working knowledge of Karl Popper's critique of historicism. Please think carefully before committing to verdicts, and begin with some private brainstorming about the possible readings before you organize them.

At a recent dinner, a friend of mine who has been devouring big-picture histories -- rise-and-fall-of-empires stuff, a fat book on Weimar, another on late Rome -- shut down my skepticism with the old line that history doesn't repeat itself, but it rhymes. For him this wasn't a throwaway quip: he was using it to defend a real predictive claim, namely that studying how earlier democracies and empires came apart gives us genuine purchase on what the next two decades of Western politics will look like, and that the people who fail to see it coming will fail because they didn't do their history homework. I've since started reading Popper's attack on historicism, and I have the uneasy sense that his argument and my friend's slogan are aimed at each other, but I can't tell exactly where they connect or whether either one lands. So here is what I want: treat "history doesn't repeat itself, but it rhymes" as a sentence doing philosophical work, and lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing. Off the top of my head there seem to be at least these candidates, but don't feel bound by my list: a near-truism that any two sufficiently complex human situations will share some structural features if you squint; a claim that constant features of human nature make similar situations reliably produce similar outcomes; a claim that there are recurring institutional or economic cycles that operate with some regularity; a claim about prophecy, i.e. that identifying the rhyme lets you forecast the next verse; and no doubt others I haven't thought of. For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude, substantive but defensible, or straightforwardly false, and -- crucially -- whether it would actually support the use my friend put it to, namely prediction. Distinguish carefully between a rhyme being a law, a trend one can extrapolate, and a mere post hoc resemblance: which of those does my friend need, and which, if any, does the slogan give him?

Then bring in Popper. His argument, as I understand it, has at least two prongs: that the growth of human knowledge is itself unpredictable, and history is largely the history of knowledge, so the future course of history is unpredictable; and that even genuine long-run trends in history are not laws, so extrapolating them is illegitimate. Say which of your readings, if any, survive each prong, and whether there is a reading strong enough for prediction but weak enough to escape Popper. I'd like the readings laid out as a numbered menu I can bring back to my friend, with a verdict at the end on which reading people who repeat the line at dinner tables probably have in mind. Please be explicit about where your reasoning is most likely to fail: which verdict in your menu rests on the most contestable premise, and what argument or piece of evidence would flip that verdict. I'd rather have honest uncertainty flagged than false confidence, since I'm going to be defending whatever you write at the next dinner.
