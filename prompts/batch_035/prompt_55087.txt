My roommates got into it last night over a sentence in a news article, and I want a referee who actually knows the philosophy.

The article was about Mars missions and said the first person to walk on Mars is probably alive today. Dana (philosophy major, so, grain of salt) immediately goes: that's just the King of France sentence. There is no first Mars walker, so by Russell's analysis it comes out false — the sentence asserts there's exactly one such person, there isn't, so it's false, full stop.

Priya said that's crazy. She'd happily bet fifty bucks the sentence is true — it's a prediction, predictions have truth values, that's the whole point of makeing them. Nobody would bet on "the present King of France is bald." So the two cases can't possibly be treated the same way.

Dana backed up a bit: okay, Russell's actual analysis doesn't need the person to exist now. The paraphrase is more like "exactly one person will first walk on Mars, and whoever that is, is alive today." Descriptions aren't names — that was Russell's whole move — so there's no reference problem, just quantifiers. The Mars sentence is a disguised general claim about the future, and it's true or false depending on how the future goes.

Priya wasn't having it, and she had two complaints. First, if Dana's paraphrase does all the work, then the interesting question is just whether statements about the future are true or false right now, which is a completely separate old fight that has nothing to do with descriptions — Dana is borrowing credit from a different debate. Second, Dana's paraphrase sneaks in "exactly one person will first walk on Mars." If we never go at all — program cancelled, budgets evaporate — was the original sentence false all along, same as the King of France one? That feels wrong: "the person who cures cancer will be famous," said in 1970, doesn't seem to have been false, it just never got decided.

Dana doubled down: no, it was false, exactly like the King of France case, and Priya's intuition is just wishful thinking. Priya said that if Dana's right, every failed aspiration retroactively made the people who voiced it wrong about the present, which is bananas.

They've been sniping at each other since breakfast. Can you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right about what, and where each of them is overreaching? I'm especially curious whether the Russellian paraphrase really handles future-referring descriptions as cleanly as Dana claims, whether Priya's "different problem, you're borrowing credit" point is fair or a dodge, and what, if anything, both of them are missing about how the word "probably" fits into all this.
