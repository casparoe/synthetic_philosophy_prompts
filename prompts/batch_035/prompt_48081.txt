I'm preparing a position statement for an interdisciplinary reading group on cultural evolution — the group is a mix of anthropologists, psychologists, and a couple of biologists, and each of us has to circulate a short statement of our commitments before the term starts so the others can poke at it. Mine is drafted, but the more I reread it, the less I trust that it's internally coherent, and I'd like an audit before I send it out. My background is in anthropology, with a strong amateur interest in evolutionary biology, which probably explains why my commitments pull in more than one direction.

Here is the set. (1) I take it as settled that culture evolves in a genuinely Darwinian sense: populations of variants, inheritance with some fidelity, and differential retention driven by selection-like processes — this is a real population-level process, not a metaphor, and anyone who rolls their eyes at "cultural evolution" as loose talk is wrong. (2) I think the meme, in roughly the sense Dawkins introduced in The Selfish Gene, is the right unit of analysis: cultural items come as discrete, recombinable tokens, and it is their fidelity of copying — through imitation, teaching, and now mass media — that explains why songs, recipes, rituals, and proverbs remain stable across generations. (3) At the same time, whenever I look at actual transmission up close — ethnographies of oral storytelling, how children's games propagate playground to playground, the transmission-chain experiments in the psychology literature — I see transformation rather than copying: every retelling is partly a reconstruction, what survives is what human cognition can easily rebuild, and so the stability of culture lives in the biases of human heads rather than in properties of the transmitted items themselves; on this view copying fidelity is low, and mostly an illusion outside of writing and modern recording media. (4) I hold that the currency of cultural selection is attention and memorability, not usefulness: whether a variant spreads is largely independent of whether it benefits its bearers, and a good deal of what achieves epidemiological success is junk or worse. (5) And yet I am strongly moved by the observation that the big cultural packages of history — moralizing religions, ritual complexes, agricultural taboos — are mostly there because they did work for the communities that carried them, even at real cost to individuals; I have no patience for the fashionable line that religion is a kind of mind-parasite spreading against our interests, and I regard wholesale maladaptation stories as lazy. (6) Finally, methodologically, I want the field to lean hard on phylogenetic reconstruction — building family trees of artifacts, folktales, and decorative motifs on the model of historical linguistics — because I think that is cultural evolution's best shot at being a genuine historical science rather than a drawer of adaptive just-so stories.

What I'd like from you: go through these six commitments, tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly, which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection, what the cheapest fix would be for each real tension and what that fix would cost me elsewhere, and, at the end, which single revision would clean up the most. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them all as equally damning, and be specific about which commitment has to give in each case — a diagnosis that ends with "it's complicated" is of no use to me. For what it's worth, each of these feels individually obvious to me when I'm in the right mood, which is exactly why I no longer trust the package as a whole; I honestly cannot see where the instability is concentrated, and I'd rather find out from you than from the reading group.
