Longtime reader here — been around since the Overcoming Bias days, read the CEV doc when it was fresh, so this stuff is basically load-bearing for how I think about alignment. I've ended up with a take on coherent extrapolated volition that I don't see many people defending, and I want it stress-tested, because I've held it long enough that I no longer trust my own evaluation of it.

The usual CEV story (Yudkowsky's 2004 version): a Friendly AI shouldn't just obey our current stated preferences — the wish if we knew more, thought faster, were more the people we wished we were, had grown up farther together — it should satisfy the extrapolated, idealized version instead. Most of the fight around it is about whether extrapolation converges to one thing or fragments into a bunch of attractors, and about whose volition is in the pool. That fight is not where I live.

My position is this: the CEV step is defensible as a *robustness improvement*, not as a correctness claim. The gap it exploits is that raw stated preferences are distorted in documented, systematic ways — framing effects, mood at the moment of elicitation, whoever had the mic last. Even a crude idealization (say, averaging over emotional states, or discounting a preference you'd disavow a week later) filters out a lot of that variance. And for a system with lock-in potential — something steering for a very long time — stability of the target matters more than getting the target exactly right, because a noisy target compounds while a biased-but-stable one at least doesn't drift. So the claim isn't "extrapolation recovers our true values." The claim is "almost any monotone move toward coherence beats deferring to the raw snapshot, even when the extrapolation is quite wrong." I'd rather be governed by a mediocre extrapolation of me than by me-on-a-bad-day, permanently.

So what I'm asking: what would count against *that* claim specifically — not against CEV in general? I'm not looking for a bibliography of people who dislike CEV, and I'm definitly not looking for agreement. Sort the defeaters by how much they should move me, from ones I'd have to concede on the spot to ones I could reasonably shrug off. Some pressure points I can already half-see, use them or ignore them if they're dumb:

- Evidence that raw elicited preferences are already pretty stable across time and framing, so the variance gap I'm relying on is smaller than I think and the whole improvement is marginal.
- A case where crude idealization is systematically worse than the raw snapshot — e.g., if moral urgency happens to correlate with emotional intensity, then mood-averaging might smooth out exactly the signal that matters most. Occasional bad cases I can absorb; a systematic bias in the wrong direction I can't.
- The argument that "better than raw deference" answers the wrong question once the comparison class is "whether to lock in anything at all," so my robustness framing is answering a question nobody asked.

Also give me an honest read on whether I've qualified this into unfalsifiability. "Better than the alternative, whatever happens" is a suspiciously cheap thing to believe, and "beats the snapshot" could quietly become a standard that moves whenever it's challenged. If you think the position rests on a confusion — say, that I'm treating robustness as doing work that only accuracy can do, or assuming without argument that idealization errors are unbiased — say that plainly up front rather than politely working within my framing. If the question itself is confused, I want to hear that first, not last.
