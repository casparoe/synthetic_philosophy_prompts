I'm a software engineer with no philosophy or formal statistics background beyond what leaks into the job, so forgive me if this is a basic question. At my company, big architecture decisions are settled by majority vote of the senior engineers — most recently, whether to break our monolith into event-driven microservices or keep a synchronous core. During that debate a colleague invoked Condorcet's jury theorem: if each voter independently has better than even odds of landing on the correct answer, then the probability that a majority vote gets it right climbs toward certainty as you add voters. I worked through the binomial math myself and it does check out, so I accept the theorem as stated.

Here's what I can't reconcile. By that logic, our nine-person committee should be right nearly all the time, and if we opened the vote to all hundred-odd engineers in the org, we'd be right essentially always — even on questions where, honestly, I'd guess the average engineer is barely better than a coin flip. But that doesn't match reality: decisions get revisited six months later, sometimes outright reversed, and adding more people to a design review seems to make it slower without making it better. So: does the theorem apply to a situation like ours at all, or only to cases like actual juries where there's a definite fact of the matter that exists independently of the vote? If it doesn't apply, which assumption are we violating, and is there a repaired version we could use — for instance, weighting votes by each engineer's track record on past decisions that later proved good or bad? And if the theorem really does work the way I described, why do companies pay expensive principal architects instead of just polling the whole engineering org?

I suspect I've gone wrong somewhere in the setup rather than in the arithmetic, so please be direct about where. Since I'll be bringing this back to the committee, I'd also like you to say how confident you are in each of your main claims and to single out the one point you'd be least willing to defend.
