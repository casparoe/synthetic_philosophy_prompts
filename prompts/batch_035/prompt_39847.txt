In my political-theory reading group we have spent the term on historical materialism, and one picture keeps doing argumentative work that nobody audits: society as a building. The metaphor traces back to Marx himself, whose preface to A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy describes the economic structure of society as the foundation on which legal and political forms arise, and people in my group slide effortlessly from that architectural image to whatever is true of buildings being settled for societies.

My friend leans on it hardest. He argues that you cannot rebuild the upper floors while leaving the foundation in place, since the old floors are standing on it; so nothing counts as genuine social change until the property relations themselves are transformed. Reformist victories, on his telling, are like redecorating rooms in a building whose foundation is untouched, and he extends the point to things like union-recognition law, workplace codetermination, and welfare-state expansion: all of it, in his view, is cosmetic. When someone objected that in an actual building the load runs both ways — the upper stories bear down on the foundation, and a sagging frame can be the reason a foundation gets repaired at all — he retreated to the standard line, one Engels himself took in his later letters: the economy is determining only in the last instance, and the superstructure acts back upon the base. But he has not withdrawn any conclusions; he now talks as though this reciprocal action were a minor side door in an otherwise one-way building.

Please audit the analogy. I would like a correspondence table — what plays the role of the foundation, the floors, the load, the architect, the demolition — followed by the places the mapping breaks, with the disanalogies sorted into those that matter for his reform-is-futile conclusion and those that do not. In particular, I want to know what the building picture quietly imports: real buildings have designers and blueprints, do not maintain or reproduce themselves, stand or collapse as wholes rather than being replaced piecewise, and have their foundations chosen to suit the intended structure rather than the floors arising from whatever foundation happens to lie under them. I would also like you to say what determination would have to mean for the metaphor to do its work — causal sufficiency, functional explanation, selection pressure on which superstructures survive a given base — and whether any of those readings, rather than the architectural image, is what actually carries his conclusion. Finally, tell me whether reform-is-futile survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether there is a better analogy for the relation historical materialists wanted to capture; I have seen organism, skeleton, and riverbed-with-water proposed, so please say what each would change about the verdict.
