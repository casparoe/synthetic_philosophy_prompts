In Germany, the state tax offices collect a levy on behalf of the recognized religious communities: registered members of, say, the Catholic or Protestant church pay a surcharge of roughly eight to nine percent of their income tax, and the revenue is handed over by the civil administration, not by the churches themselves. Two features of the arrangement are worth dwelling on. First, liability follows registered membership: only members of a collecting community pay, and they fund their own confession rather than religion in general. Second, membership ends by a purely civil act — a declaration of exit before a state office, which the state then communicates to the church. The state, not the church, is the authority that determines when you have ceased to belong, and the churches must accept the result for tax purposes. Note the tension this creates: Catholic doctrine holds that baptism confers a membership that cannot be undone, and treats the formal declaration of exit as itself a religiously significant act — yet the civil effect of that same declaration is simply to stop the withholding.

What theory of religious belonging and of church–state relations does this practice presuppose? Some questions I would like a reconstruction to address: Does the system presuppose that religious affiliation is a revocable association membership, on the model of a club or a gym subscription, chosen and cancellable at will — and is that presupposition consistent with the churches' own self-understanding of membership as conferred and indelible? Is the state's power to define the moment of exit compatible with the deference the same system shows elsewhere, or does it quietly assert that the state's category of "member" is the authoritative one and the church's a shadow category? What conception of state neutrality is being taken for granted: is acting as the churches' collection agency while withholding from taxpayers who never joined a form of evenhanded cooperation with organized religion, or a substantive endorsement that a stricter separationist would reject? Is the underlying thought that citizens should fund only the religion they belong to — in which case the practice presupposes that religious support is like a private consumption choice, on the model of a voluntary association's dues, rather than a public good that society funds in general (as happens where an established church is financed out of the general budget, with nonmembers contributing through ordinary taxation)? And finally: does the arrangement presuppose one coherent view, or is it philosophically underdetermined, so that several different theories of religious belonging would equally license it? Please tell me which parts of the practice are strict presuppositions, which are merely compatible with it, and what in the system would have to change if the rival, ascriptive understanding of membership were taken seriously instead — say, if the state refused to recognize exits, or recognized them only at the church's own initiative. I would also like to know, briefly, whether the practice's implicit theory sits closer to the cooperative model some other European states use or to the stricter separation of the French and American variety, and whether that placement follows from the practice itself or is an overinterpretation of it.
