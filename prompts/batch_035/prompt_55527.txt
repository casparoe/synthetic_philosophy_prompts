My partner and I have been going through IVF, and somewhere along the way I started writing down my gut verdicts on cases about creating people, to see if there's a principle behind them. Here's the list as it stands.

1. Deliberately conceiving a child you know will have a short, painful life (you carry a horrible genetic condition, you know it, and you refuse any screening): clearly wrong.
2. Not conceiving a child who would have had a wonderful life: not wrong, not even slightly. A couple who just don't feel like having kids aren't failing anyone, and I don't buy that a possible person somewhere is being cheated.
3. But a woman who conceives while drinking heavily, knowing the child will be born with serious impairments but a life very much worth living — the child, grown up, is glad to exist: I blame her, even though I can't say who she wronged, since the child wouldn't have existed otherwise and is happy she exists.
4. IVF, two embryos available, one likely to have a disability, one not: I think choosing the non-disabled one is fine, even obviously right.
5. Yet if a deaf couple say they'd prefer a deaf child, so they can share the language and the culture, and pick the embryo with the deafness gene, I feel that's wrong — and I can't see how it differs from 4 except in which way the preference runs. This pair bothers me more than any of the others.
6. A government that could cheaply make all future children healthier (folic acid in flour, say) and doesn't: I think that's wrong, even though every resulting child still has a life well worth living.

So 1 and 2 together give me the asymmetry — creating suffering is wrong, not creating happiness isn't — which I'm fairly confident about. But 3 and 6 seem to say there's a duty to make the people you create better off, and 4 and 5 pull in opposite directions. My tentative principle was something like: you can only wrong people who exist or definitely will exist, and only by making their lives worse than they otherwise would have been. But under that, 3 and 6 look fine (the counterfactual child is no worse off than the alternative — there was no alternative), which I can't swallow.

Is there one principle that gets all six, or close? If two candidates, say what each costs me. And please state the winner precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case, then tell me what it says about the one I keep going back and forth on: a couple who could have a child now, or wait a year and have a different child in better circumstances — is waiting better for anyone, given the child born now would also have a life worth living? Also be honest about which of my verdicts has to go — I'd rather lose one than carry round a principle with a clause bolted on for every case.
