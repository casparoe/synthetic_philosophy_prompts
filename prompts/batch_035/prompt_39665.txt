"John Taurek argued that the number of lives at stake should not by itself determine whom a rescuer saves. (a) Reconstruct his reasoning for the claim that we are not required to save the greater number just because they are greater in number. (b) Explain his coin flip proposal and why he thinks it fitting. (c) Present one objection to his view and sketch how he might respond."

And here's the student's answer, copied as is:

"Taurek asks us to imagine we can rescue either one stranger or five strangers, but not both, and that we have no special ties to anyone involved. Most people say save the five, because five deaths are worse than one death. Taurek's move is to ask: worse for whom? Each of the five suffers a loss if they die, and the one suffers a loss, but no individual suffers a greater loss because there happen to be others suffering alongside them. So from the rescuer's standpoint there is no way of adding up the five losses and comparing the total to the single loss; the comparisons that make sense are pairwise, one person's loss against another's, and those come out even. This is why Taurek's view lines up with the old utilitarian idea that everybody is to count for one and nobody for more than one — each person's loss must be kept seperate from everyone else's.

Since the numbers give the rescuer no reason to prefer the five, Taurek proposes flipping a coin: heads, save the five; tails, save the one. The point is to give each of the six individuals an equal one-in-six chance of being rescued, so that nobody's claim is subordinated to anyone else's.

Objection: Taurek's view lets lots more people die whenever rescuers happen upon groups, and that seems terrible. Reply: what looks terrible depends on the aggregate perspective Taurek is rejecting; from the standpoint of any actual individual involved, nothing worse happens to them when others die alongside them than would have happened anyway, and a rescuer owes each person equal concern, which the coin flip expresses."
