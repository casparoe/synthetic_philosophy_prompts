I've been reading about the Valladolid debate and I want an analysis of it through Aristotle's theory of natural slavery, run in both directions. Facts so we're on the same page: in 1550 the Spanish crown convened a junta of theologians at Valladolid to hear Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda and Bartolomé de las Casas argue over whether the conquest of the Indies was just. Sepúlveda leaned on Politics Book I -- some people are natural slaves, lacking the deliberative capacity to govern themselves, and being ruled by a superior is actually for their own good -- plus a just-war layer: the indigenous practiced human sacrifice, so war against them was legitimate and the resulting subjection benefited them. Las Casas argued the indigenous had cities, laws, agriculture, religion, and were fully rational, so the conquest was robbery dressed up. No clean verdict was ever handed down. Here's what I want:

First, a careful mapping -- what natural slavery actually claims as a theory (the descriptive claim about a human type, the teleology, the benefit clause), what each debater was doing with it, and which features of the debate it genuinely illuminated versus distorted. Second, where the theory breaks on this episode. The one I keep circling is what you might call the identification problem: even if you grant that natural slaves exist, Aristotle gives no procedure for sorting them from everyone else that can't be gamed by whoever holds the whip -- Sepúlveda basically read conquest outcomes backwards as evidence of natural inferiority, which is circular. And the benefit clause looks empirically falsifiable and falsified. Third, the parts the theory is just silent on -- don't force it to speak where it doesn't, e.g. on race, which is an anachronistic overlay that neither Aristotle nor really Sepúlveda had in its later form. Fourth, the awkward stuff on Las Casas's side: he at one point accepted importing enslaved Africans to spare the indigenous and later repudiated it -- does that concession show that anyone who accepts the natural-slavery framework at all ends up applying it somewhere, i.e. the problem is structural rather than a matter of picking the wrong candidate population? And finally, what the episode shows about the theory itself: is natural slavery refuted here, or merely misapplied, and can a hierarchy claim whose truth can never be distinguished from its abuse be harmless even in principle? Also worth noting the empirical asymmetry that Sepúlveda never went to the Americas and Las Casas had spent decades there -- say what that does to the arguement if anything.
