I teach an upper-level seminar in philosophy of cognitive science (fifteen students, mostly philosophy majors who have taken intro philosophy of mind and a semester of cognitive science), and I'm writing the take-home final. I want one essay question on the language of thought hypothesis that cannot be answered by reciting the standard summary — "Fodor says thoughts are sentences in mentalese, and compositionality explains how finite brains think infinitely many thoughts." My idea is to build the question around the argument from systematicity, but framed so that students have to locate where the argument's real work happens. Concretely, the question should center on the gap between two claims: (i) a thinker's cognitive capacities are systematic — someone who can think that a loves b can think that b loves a, and this is not an accident about individuals but holds across thinkers; and (ii) thoughts themselves must be built out of constituents that get recombined across thoughts. Students slide from (i) to (ii) as though it were obvious, and I want the question to force them to say exactly what licenses the slide, whether the slide is supposed to be a conceptual truth, an inference to the best explanation, or an empirical prediction, and what a system would have to be like for (i) to hold while (ii) fails. A good answer should also have to explain why the argument is supposed to run through systematicity rather than productivity alone — i.e., why unboundedness of thought is, by Fodor's lights, a weaker datum than the lawful interconnection of abilities — and it should engage the connectionist alternative at a level beyond "connectionists disagree but Fodor replies," for instance by asking whether a network whose capacities happen to be systematically connected has explained systematicity or merely exhibited it, and whether the classicist's demand for a "principled" explanation is a substantive constraint or a rigged one.

Please write the question itself (students get a week and write about 1,500 words; you can phrase it as a single essay prompt with sub-questions), a model answer of about 700 words that would genuinely earn top marks, and a marking scheme out of 20 points: five or six must-hit points, the marks each is worth, and a one-line note on each saying how students typically lose those marks — e.g., conflating systematicity with productivity, treating "the brain implements a language of thought" and "the mind thinks in English" as the same claim, or answering the connectionist objection by name-dropping. I'd like the model answer to take an actual position rather than survey, and the marking scheme should make clear which moves separate an A answer from a B answer. No bibliography needed; students will be citing from the seminar readings themselves, so just use positions and arguments rather than citation details.
