I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training beyond an audiobook or two, and somewhere down a rabbit hole I landed on the debate about how to talk about what could have happened to *me* specifically. The naive picture, as I understand it, is transworld identity: there is one person, me, and in other possible situations that very same person exists and does things differently. The alternative, David Lewis's counterpart theory, says there is no single me straddling situations — instead, in each other situation there's somebody *resembling* me in the right ways, and when I say I could have become a manager instead of an engineer, I'm really talking about that counterpart. As a data modeler this is oddly intuitive to me: you would never design a system where one customer record literally lives in two databases at once; you'd store separate records plus a mapping between them, and transworld identity looks to me like a distributed-systems identity bug. But I suspect my engineering intuition is smuggling in assumptions rather than settling anything.

Here's my actual request: I've come to find counterpart theory pretty compelling, and I want to know what would count against it. Not just the famous complaint that it's somehow unfair to Humphrey — that on this view, when Humphrey says he could have won the election, he's reporting somebody *else's* victory, and no one else's win is his near-win — but the full map of exposure. Which arguments or consequences should genuinely worry a counterpart theorist, which cases does the theory arguably misdescribe (I've seen hints that iterated modality — what I could have done given what had already happened differently — is a stress point), and which objections do counterpart theorists themselves concede have force versus dismiss as terminological? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. I'd also like to know whether the theory is even at risk in principle, or whether it can be qualified endlessly — if a defender can always adjust which resemblance relation counts, is the view insulated to the point of unfalsifiability, and is that a fatal flaw for a metaphysical view or just how this corner of metaphysics works?

One more thing I'm uncertain about, and I'd like you to address it head on: does counterpart theory actually *predict* anything different from transworld identity, or is it a notational re-description where the two views agree on every substantive modal claim and merely use the word "could" differently? If it's the latter, tell me what a live dispute would even look like — what argument, case, or result could make one side give up. And separately, I've assumed counterpart theory comes bundled with Lewis's heavier machinery about concrete parallel worlds; does it, or can you accept counterparts while thinking of possible situations in some lighter way? I'd rather know if I've signed up for more than I intended.
