I've been asked to give a short talk on the procreation asymmetry — the view that creating a happy person isn't a good thing in the way making an existing person happy is, but creating a miserable one is bad — and I keep coming back to Children of Men. The film's premise seems tailor-made to test it: a world where no one has been born for 18 years, and the youngest human's death is treated as a global tragedy. If you take the asymmetry seriously, the *absence* of new happy lives shouldn't itself be a tragedy, just the suffering of those already here — yet the film plays universal childlessness as the end of the world, and the discovery of Kee's pregnancy as humanity's salvation. So my question is: does the film refute the asymmetry, or does it only work because the childless world is also a world of war, fascism, and despair, so you can't separate the loss of future people from the misery of current ones (a confound a philosopher would want cleaned up)? Read the film through the asymmetry: which scenes would you point to on either side, does the film ever suggest the birth matters only instrumentally (as a symbol of hope that steadies existing people) versus as a good in itself, and where does it just refuse to be an ethics thought experiment at all? I want a reading that's fair to both the film and the philosophy, including admitting where the reading strains.
