I'm writing an essay that uses Kafka's The Metamorphosis as a stress test for Tolstoy's theory of art, and I'd like a reading that takes both the novella and the theory seriously, rather than using one as a stick to beat the other.

Here is the theory I'm working with, paraphrased from Tolstoy's What Is Art? (1897). For Tolstoy, art is not the making of beautiful objects but the transmission of a feeling the artist has experienced into an audience, so that the audience is infected by it; the better the art, the stronger and more reliable the infection. He attaches three conditions to this. The feeling must have individuality — it must be this particular artist's own feeling, distinct from the ordinary run of feelings. It must have clarity — the audience must receive just the feeling the artist meant to transmit, without distortion or confusion. And it must have sincerity — the artist must actually have felt the feeling he transmits, since an audience detects feigned emotion the way it detects a fake smile. On top of these Tolstoy imposes a social criterion: the best art transmits feelings that unite human beings rather than divide them, and it must be comprehensible not just to a trained elite but to everyone, with the uneducated laborer's comprehension as the standard. Obscurity, he held, is a symptom of decay: art that requires special cultivation to be felt has ceased to transmit anything, and has become a game of allusions played among connoisseurs — the imitation of earlier art rather than art itself.

The Metamorphosis looks, at first blush, like a case built to refute him. Nothing in it is explained. Gregor Samsa's transformation is an unexplained given, never rationalized; the story's meaning has been disputed for a century without settlement; and it is the very model of the literary, allusive, connoisseurs' artwork that Tolstoy despised. And yet the feelings it transmits are unmistakable to any reader: the panic of trying and failing to produce human words when Gregor answers through the door; the father's bombardment with apples, one of which lodges in Gregor's back and rots there; the sister Grete's violin playing, which draws Gregor out of hiding in the one scene where he still responds to beauty, and which ends with her declaring that the family must get rid of it; the closing tram ride into the country, where the family discovers that Grete has bloomed. A reader with no literary training at all is infected by shame, revulsion, pity, and exhausted relief, with total reliability, while the story's propositional content remains a puzzle.

So my question is where Tolstoy's test actually cuts. Does the novella pass or fail, and in either case which of his conditions does the work? In particular: is his clarity condition ambiguous between clarity of feeling and clarity of meaning, and does the story exploit the gap? Is the class criterion satisfied by a work that has ended up on every school syllabus in the world, or does accessibility in feeling not undo its origin in an art addressed to a literary elite? And do the feelings it transmits — mostly revulsion and the shame of being a burden — count under Tolstoy's rule as uniting feelings, or are they the divisions of family life the theory never classified?

I'd like the reading to work through specific scenes rather than generalities, to say plainly where the novella resists the Tolstoyan verdict and where the theory has to bend, and to end with what the work shows about the theory that the theory's own argument could not: I suspect the lesson is that sincerity and infectiousness can be vivid while interpretation stays open, and that this collapses Tolstoy's tacit identification of comprehensibility-in-feeling with comprehensibility-in-meaning. Please base the reading on the story and the theory as I've stated them, and don't speculate about Tolstoy's actual opinions of Kafka or lean on biographical details about either man.
