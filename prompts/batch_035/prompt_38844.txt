I'm a backend engineer at a mid-sized company, and last quarter my team shipped an internal tool that ingests claims from our product documentation and scores each one as true, false, or unverifiable. It works well enough until a claim is about the scoring of the claim itself — someone submitted "the sentence in this row will be scored false" — and the service then re-evaluates its own output in an endless loop until the on-call engineer gets paged at 3 a.m. While debugging it, I fell down a rabbit hole and learned that philosophers have been filing essentially the same ticket for over two thousand years: the Liar paradox. What surprised me more is that there are several competing "fixes" on offer, and they look a lot like different engineering strategies. One school says the input should simply be rejected as not well-formed — self-reference gets banned, and the language gets split into levels so that no sentence can talk about the truth of its own level. Another says some sentences are neither true nor false because they never bottom out in any non-semantic fact, so you introduce a third status. A third says you should just keep re-evaluating forever and treat never converging as the correct behavior. And a small but loud minority says the input is genuinely both true and false, so the system is working as designed and there was never a bug at all. A colleague of mine who did a philosophy degree before switching to software tells me these map onto real positions — Tarski-style language hierarchies, Kripke-style groundedness, revision theories, and dialetheism — and that each has serious defenders.

I'd like you to write a piece of satire about this situation. Specifically, write a parody of a production incident postmortem, in the format our industry uses after outages (Summary, Impact, Timeline, Root Cause Analysis, Lessons Learned, Action Items), for a fictional company that operates a truth-evaluation-as-a-service API and has just suffered a prolonged outage caused by a customer submitting the Liar sentence through the public endpoint. Aim for roughly 700-800 words, structured under the standard postmortem headings, and keep the tone deadpan throughout — the piece should read as a plausible corporate document, not a wink at the reader.

The humor needs to come from faithfully acting out the real philosophical positions rather than inventing problems. The Root Cause Analysis should blame the customer for self-reference and propose banning it, with the rollout of the level-separation scheme described in the Action Items. The three-valued "neither true nor false" status should get deployed mid-incident and promptly cause a second incident when another customer submits a sentence claiming it will be assigned none of the three values. One engineer on the timeline should keep insisting the correct fix is to iterate the evaluation indefinitely, and the postmortem should note that this was declined for reasons of cost rather than correctness. And somewhere in the document there should be a dissenting voice — perhaps in a quoted comment appended to the review — arguing that since the sentence is both true and false, the system was returning the right answer the whole time and the "outage" was a billing event, not a technical one. The final Action Items should quietly concede that the problem was contained rather than solved, in the way real postmortems do. A reader who knows the philosophy should recognize each position and feel it was portrayed fairly; a reader who doesn't should still come away thinking they've read a remarkably candid incident report.
