I'm a PhD student in history and philosophy of science, working on the levels of selection, and I've built an argument I now distrust because it seems to prove far too much. I want a diagnosis: which step to reject, and what the rejection costs.

Call it the always-genes argument:

(1) A level L is a unit of selection in an episode just in case (i) there is variation among entities at L, (ii) offspring at L resemble parents at L (heritability at L), and (iii) this variation causes differences in reproductive success among entities at L, thereby changing the population's composition. This is essentially Lewontin's trio of conditions generalized to arbitrary levels.

(2) In any episode of natural selection at all, alleles vary; alleles are copied to offspring with high fidelity; and alleles differ in replication rates, with the change in allele frequencies simply being the evolutionary change. So the gene satisfies the definition whenever selection occurs.

(3) In any episode, the causal story can also be told at the genic level: allele A spread because its phenotypic effects, in that environment, disposed its bearers to outreproduce the bearers of a, and thereby disposed A to out-replicate a.

(4) If a level satisfies the definition in every episode, then selection is really at that level, and talk of selection at other levels is a bookkeeping shorthand.

(5) So in every episode selection is really at the genic level, and organism- and group-level selection is derivative.

Here is what I mean by proving too much. Run the same schema with organism substituted for gene: organisms vary; offspring organisms resemble parents; and in every episode, differences in organismic traits cause differences in organismic reproduction, changing composition. By (4), selection is really at the organism level in every episode. Run it again with group, wherever there is population structure and assortment: groups vary, daughter groups resemble founder groups, and group-level differences cause differential proliferation of groups. So wherever groups are structured at all, selection is really at the group level. A schema that certifies every level as the real one thereby certifies none, and the question of what the unit of selection is would have only trivial answers. But the question is plainly not trivial: whether, say, a honeybee colony or a shifting-balance deme is a unit bears on which causal explanations of adaptation are available, not merely on how the change is recorded. So the argument fails somewhere, and I can't see where.

A few constraints on the diagnosis. Tell me which numbered step to reject, or which inference between steps, and explain why that step looked fine to me when I wrote it. Then price the rejection: what does giving it up force me to say about paradigm cases on each side — genic stories of mimicry or industrial melanism at one end, Wright's shifting balance or caste ratios in social insects at the other? If instead you think the right move is to accept (5) outright, as the genic selectionists did in their exchange with Sober, then say what that view has to pay, for instance how it handles alleles whose fitness is context-dependent, where the honest genic description seems to require splitting one allele into several context-indexed alleles. If more than one step is at fault, say which bears primary responsibility. I'd most like to keep (1), since it is close to the working definition in the field, but I want to know what keeping it costs me before I dig in.

Finally, please state plainly how confident you are in each part of your diagnosis, and single out the one point you are least sure of. I would rather have a candid ranking with one flagged uncertainty than a confident-sounding exposition that hides where the going gets speculative.
