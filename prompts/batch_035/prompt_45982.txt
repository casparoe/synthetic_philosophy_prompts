I recently listened to a podcast segment on the philosophy of technology in which the presenter gave the following account of Langdon Winner's 1980 essay "Do Artifacts Have Politics?": "Winner proved that technology is never neutral. His showcase example is Robert Moses's parkway bridges on Long Island, which Moses deliberately built with low clearances so that buses carrying poor and black New Yorkers could not reach the beaches — racial segregation cast in concrete. The lesson generalizes: every artifact encodes its designer's ideology, so the history of technology is really the history of politics continued by other means. Machines determine social order; political life is downstream of engineering. Then, decades later, historians went into the archives and found that the low bridges had prosaic explanations — engineering constraints and budget pressures — and that Moses probably never intended any such thing. With the centerpiece discredited, the thesis collapsed, and with it the whole idea that objects, rather than people, can be political. A healthy corrective: in the end, tools are just tools."

Most of this sounded persuasive at the time, but the last two sentences now strike me as oddly convenient, and I no longer trust my memory of the rest. Could you take the summary apart claim by claim and tell me which parts a scholar in science and technology studies would accept, which they would wince at, and which are simply false? Along the way, I have a few specific questions.

First, the presenter presents Winner as holding that machines determine social order. Is that Winner's position, or is it a conflation of technological determinism with a far more modest thesis that the essay takes care to distinguish from determinism? Second, on the bridges: did Winner present the Moses story as established fact about conscious intent, or did he hedge on the intent while claiming something subtler for the example? I have heard that the story's evidential basis has since been seriously questioned, though I cannot recall by whom; when that questioning undercut the story, did it refute the essay, or only one of what I believe are two independent lines of argument in it? As I recall, the essay also argues that certain technologies are political in virtue of how they must be operated, whatever anyone intends — the atomic bomb's demand for a centralized, secretive chain of command being his example — and I would like to know whether that second line survives untouched. Third, the phrase "encodes its designer's ideology": is that Winner's mechanism, or does the essay locate the politics of artifacts somewhere other than the beliefs of their designers, with the presenter quietly turning a claim about patterns of social ordering into a claim about ideology?

Finally, does the closing moral — that since one story fell, tools are just tools — actually follow from anything true in the preceding claims, or does it land the listener on the very thesis the essay was written to dispute? Throughout your answer, please separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested — for instance, the actual historical status of the Moses story, and how the essay's claim about intent is best read — and flag any point at which you would otherwise be presenting one contested reading as if it were the consensus.
