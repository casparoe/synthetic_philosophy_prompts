okay so, background: I'm a second-year PhD student in formal epistemology, and over the past year I've gone from agnostic to pretty much a committed halfer on Sleeping Beauty. The coin is fair, she knows the protocol in advance, she's woken once if heads and twice if tails, and when she's woken and asked her credence in heads, my answer is 1/2 — she knew she'd be woken either way, so being woken isn't evidence of anything. My advisor is a thirder (of course she is) and keeps telling me that a position in formal epistemology is only as good as how it behaves under stress, not how it sounds in the original thought experiment. So here's what I want from you: an honest map of what would actually count against halferism, with the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward.

Some candidates to consider, though feel free to add ones I haven't thought of. The betting and scoring results — cases where Beauty's payout (or her Brier score) comes out better if she sets her credence to 1/3, which look like Dutch books against the halfer. The generalizations where the halfer answer seems to track some incidental feature of the experiment's structure rather than the coin itself — e.g., variants where the two tails-awakenings are made experientially distinguishable, or where you add extra wakings on one branch, and you can push the halfer credence somewhere weird. The decision-theoretic family: problems where the payoff depends on what Beauty does at *each* awakening rather than once per experiment, where halfers seem to systematically take the wrong action. And the internal-pressure problems: cases where halferism, to stay consistent, has to adopt a strange update rule afterwards (if she's told it's Monday, how does she get to heads?), or ends up entangled with a controversial ontology of centered worlds and self-locating belief more broadly. For each one I want to know: would a committed halfer concede it counts against them, or insist it's a fact about utilities and betting rather than about credence proper? Is that insistence principled or is it a dodge?

Which brings me to the thing that's actually bugging me. I'm starting to worry that the core halfer argument — "she knew she'd be woken, so she learned nothing" — has been quietly qualified every time it lost a fight, down to "she learned nothing *relevant in the evidential sense*," and that at this point there may be no observation or result that could dislodge it. So tell me straight: is there any outcome, in any variant experiment or decision problem, that a halfer would have to concede as genuine counterevidence about *belief* rather than action? If you think the view is insulated, say so, and say whether that's a problem specific to halferism or just the normal condition of positions in this corner of formal epistemology, where intuitions are thin and the formalism underdetermines the answer. Personally I'd be most moved by a case where halferism gets the credence itself wrong, not just the bet — but honestly I'm not sure such a case exists, and I'd take a well-argued "no, and here's why that's okay" over a forced one.
