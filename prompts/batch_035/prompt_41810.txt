My flatmate Amara and I got into a long argument on Friday about something that happened on our street, and neither of us could land it, so I'd like you to referee the exchange move by move. The background: a woman on our street, Daphne, promised to feed a neighbour's cat while the neighbour was away for a week. Daphne forgot entirely; the cat went unfed for four days before another neighbour noticed, broke in, and got it to a vet in bad shape. When the story came up, Amara flatly denied that Daphne caused the cat's condition: causes are events, she said, and doing nothing is not an event -- Daphne simply failed to cause feeding, which is very different from causing starving. I pushed back that this can't be right, since if Daphne had fed the cat, it would have been fine, and that counterfactual dependence is exactly what makes us point at her; in ordinary life we say things like "her forgetting caused it" without hesitation.

Amara then came back with what I found genuinely troubling: by my counterfactual test, hundreds of people caused the cat's suffering -- every neighbour who could have checked in, the vet on the next street, even the cat's owner, and none of them forgot, they just didn't do it. Amara said if we count all of them as causes, the concept has been stretched to uselessness, and if we don't, the counterfactual test can't be what's doing the work. I answered that what singles out Daphne is that she had taken on the job: her omission is special because of the promise, and an account of causation by omission should build in those normative facts -- who was supposed to act, whose inaction counts as a deviation from how things should have gone. Amara replied that this gives the game away entirely: now whether Daphne counts as a cause depends on facts about promises and obligations, not about what physically happened, which seems wrong for something we'd thought of as a causal matter; and she added that it also means we've quietly collapsed causation into blame, whereas intuitively we want to be able to ask separately whether Daphne caused the harm and how much blame she deserves, since a stranger who owed nothing and skipped a casual promise would differ causally, on my view, only in the ethics, not in the physics.

My last move was to say that maybe Amara is right that "caused" here can't be all-or-nothing -- maybe Daphne is the *most* salient of the many non-feeders because her deviation was from a normality baseline as well as a promise, and salience comes in degrees. Amara said that if causation is now a matter of salience relative to a baseline, then there is no fact of the matter about omissions and causes at all, just facts about what we happen to notice, and she'd rather say omissions simply aren't causes than accept that. Please adjudicate our exchange move by move: for each step, say who's right, who's mistaken, and why, and then give an overall verdict on what, if anything, both of us were missing about whether (and in what sense) Daphne caused the cat's condition.
