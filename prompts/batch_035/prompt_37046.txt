I've just spent an entire evening arguing with a close friend, a psychiatrist who is broadly a rationalist type, about heritability, and I came away realizing I could barely teach the concept even though I believe I understand it. The immediate trigger was his claim that twin studies showing high heritability of IQ settle the question of how much room schools have to raise achievement; I think that's a category error, but I fumbled the explanation badly enough that I want to be sure I could do it properly next time. So here is my request.

I've been asked to run a fifty-minute session for a mixed audience — some social science graduate students, some journalists, a couple of policy staffers — on what heritability does and does not mean. Assume you are a quantitative geneticist who also teaches philosophy of science and has spent years watching public discussions of this topic go wrong. Do not write me the explanation itself; design the plan for delivering it.

Specifically, I want you to commit to definite choices, with reasons, rather than laying out options for me to sort through. Decide:

1. The order of ideas. What has to be established before what, and why? In particular, decide for me whether the population-relative, environment-dependent definition of heritability has to come before or after the classic misconceptions are raised, and defend your sequencing choice.

2. The carrying example. Pick one concrete worked example that has to carry the whole session — it should let me show heritability varying with environment, and heritability being high while environmental interventions still matter enormously. Tell me what the example must contain and what it must avoid, and tell me which commonly used examples (twin studies, height, IQ itself) I should refuse to lean on at which points, and why.

3. The misconception map. List the misconceptions this audience will predictably bring — I can tell you now that "heritable means fixed or unchangeable" and "heritability tells you how much genes determine a given individual's trait" will both appear within minutes — and, for each, tell me exactly where in the sequence to head it off and what formulation to use. Include the subtler ones: the inference from within-group heritability to between-group differences, and the assumption that a single heritability number is a stable property of the trait rather than of a population in an environment.

4. The boundary of the session. What should I openly refuse to cover in fifty minutes, and say so out loud.

5. One check — a single question I can pose at the end, with the answer(s) that would show the idea has landed versus the answer(s) that show it hasn't, even when the answer sounds right.

Think through the dependency structure carefully before committing: the temptation will be to lead with the definition, and I suspect that's wrong for this audience, but I want your considered judgment, not a hedge. Take a definite position on each point.
