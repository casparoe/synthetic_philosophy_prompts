For the last session of my graduate seminar on social learning, I want to hand out a poem alongside the standard Banerjee / Bikhchandani–Hirshleifer–Welch reading on information cascades — the models where people choose in sequence, each observing only their predecessors' actions plus a private signal, and where it takes only a small run of identical early choices for every later agent to rationally ignore their own information. The poem should be a pantoum, because the form is almost comically well suited: in a pantoum every line returns a second time in a later stanza, displaced and recontextualized, so that each line functions both as a response to what came before and as evidence for what comes after — exactly the double life an action leads in a cascade. The recycled lines can do the theoretical work: what one stanza presents as someone's private conviction, the next stanza inherits as hearsay, third-hand and confident.

Here is the setup I'd like the poem to enact, though you should feel free to improve the details: a row of people, one after another, deciding between two indistinguishable options — two restaurants on the same block, two unlabeled doors, a new app and the old one it replaced. The first chooser follows her own signal; the second and third weigh theirs against hers; from the fourth or fifth onward the history is overwhelming and the private signals go unconsulted. I'd like someone who knows the literature to be able to point at the exact line where the original signal makes its final appearance — the line after which it never returns, though its consequences run through everything that follows. And I'd like the closing stanza, where by convention the poem's first and last lines come back together, to register the strange epistemic situation at the end of a cascade: the unanimous action now looks like an overwhelming accumulation of independent evidence, when in fact it is one signal endlessly relayed. If you can gesture at that without stating it outright, that would be ideal.

Two constraints on tone. First, please don't mock the herders — the whole point of these models is that cascading is rational given what each agent can observe, so the poem should have no fools in it, only people reasoning well from bad positions. Second, the poem shouldn't name its own machinery: no use of the words cascade, herd, signal, or information anywhere in it. The form and the scene should carry the theory on their own. Six or seven stanzas should be about right — long enough for the chain to get going, short enough that the returning lines stay legible.
