I serve on incident review boards for autonomous-vehicle deployments, and our reports keep having to say things like "the failure of the automatic emergency braking system to trigger caused the collision" or, more awkwardly, "the system's decision not to brake was correct, so the collision was caused by the pedestrian's behavior" — and I've come to realize we have no considered view about what makes an absence a cause. Before I look at any particular theory, I want the specification. Set out the desiderata a theory of causation by omission must meet to be adequate for this application: the cases it has to get right (including ones where two redundant safety systems both fail to act, so neither failure is individually necessary for the harm, and ones where a system correctly refrains from intervening and must not come out as a cause); the distinctions it cannot collapse (between an omission and the mere non-occurrence of an event that was never in the offing, between what a system failed to do and what it was in no position to do, between an absence that made a difference and background conditions that were simply in place throughout); and the constraints imposed on it by practice (in law, omissions ground liability only where there was a duty to act, whereas our systems bear no duties — so say whether that asymmetry belongs in the theory of causation or should be kept out of it and handled elsewhere). For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it, mark which are non-negotiable and which look instead like prejudices of one camp (for instance, the demand that absences be events, or that omission-causation be derivative from causation by whatever event did occur instead), and flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — that is the part I most want to see. Then, briefly, hold a simple counterfactual-dependence account, a norm-restricted account that ties omissions to breached expectations, and a quasi-event account that identifies each omission with the actual event that happened in its place up against the list. Keep the weight on the criteria; the scorecard should be short.
