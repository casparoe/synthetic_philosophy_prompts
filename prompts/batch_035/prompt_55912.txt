I am a novelist, and I have written myself into a corner that turns out to be philosophical rather than dramaturgical. My current book is set in Marseille in 1943, and its central character is a woman who hides refugee children while her confessor — a learned, austere man I have modeled deliberately on Augustine of Hippo, after spending a season with his treatises On Lying and Against Lying — maintains that no lie is ever permissible, not one told to save a life. I want the confessor's absolutism to be a serious position, not a costume: the reader should feel its pull as strongly as the children's danger, which means I myself need to understand what the argument actually turns on. Before I decide how the story resolves — whether she lies and then cannot confess it, or finds some third way through the policeman's questions — I want the underlying specification made explicit. So write me the desiderata: the requirements any adequate theory of the morality of lying must meet — the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it must refuse to collapse, the settled judgments it must either honor or explain away — with the consideration behind each requirement, and a flag on any pair of them you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied.

Let me give you the material the theory has to organize, so we are not trading intuitions in a vacuum. Augustine defines lying by what he calls the double heart, not by the words: on his account a man may say a true thing and yet lie, if he thinks it to be false and utters it for true, while another may say a false thing and yet not lie, if he believes it to be so as he says. He sets jokes aside as never having been counted lies — the joker's tone itself signals that no deceit is intended — and then works through a ledger of lies sorted by the harm they do and the good they seek, at the bottom of which even the lie that saves a life is condemned. So the desiderata should at minimum handle: the murderer at the door asking directly where the fugitive hides; the answer that is strictly true but crafted to mislead; the joke and the ironic remark; the actor and the novelist (my own trade — I invent falsehoods professionally and intend readers to inhabit them); the placebo and the comforting half-truth told to a dying patient; the false statement made to someone who will plainly use the truth to commit an atrocity; and the difference between telling a lie, deceiving by other means, allowing a deception to stand, and simply declining to disclose. I also want to know which distinctions are genuine requirements and which are prejudices of one camp — for instance, whether the theory must grade the wrongness of lies by their consequences or must treat them as uniform, and whether the obligation to truth is owed in full even to those hunting children, or whether that is precisely what the Augustinian cannot concede without losing everything.

Then, briefly, score Augustine himself against the list: where his definition is surprisingly strong, where his treatment of the lifesaving case strains the most defensible requirements, and which single requirement on your list does the most work in either vindicating or excluding him. The purpose to keep in view is the novelist's one: I need to know whether my confessor can be written as coherent, or whether coherence itself is what the story must deny him. Do not cite sources or point me to further reading — I want the reasoning self-contained, since I will be working from it at my desk with no library at hand.
