I'm taking an evening philosophy course called The Examined Self, and for my final paper I picked Death of a Salesman and self-deception. Here's the problem I keep running into. Philosophers say self-deception looks paradoxical: to fool yourself you'd have to already know the truth in order to hide it from yourself, which seems to require believing and disbelieving the same thing at once. Sartre's way out is that the self-deceiver knows the truth the whole time (that's what bad faith is), Davidson's is to split the mind into parts so one part can pull the wool over the other's eyes, and then there's the deflationary camp, people like Alfred Mele, who say there's no paradox because self-deception is just motivationally biased evidence-gathering -- selective attention, never one mind holding p and not-p simultaneously. The more I stare at Willy Loman, the more he seems like exactly the test case these theories were made for: he insists Biff is on the verge of big things, that he himself is still vital in New England, that being well liked is the whole game -- while also, apparently, hiding a rubber hose in the basement and planning a suicide he never quite admits to planning. But whenever I try to say which theory he actually fits, my draft goes mushy and I just end up summarizing the play.

So what I want is a reading of the play through that debate. Does Miller's Willy match Sartre (he knows, and is frantically managing the knowledge), Davidson (two quasi-agents with different evidence about the same life), or the deflationary view (no contradiction because he only ever attends to what suits him)? Which scenes do the most work for each reading -- I'm thinking of the restaurant scene where Biff tries to tell him the truth about the math exam, the conversations with Ben, and the seeds obsession near the end -- and is there any scene that resists all three accounts? And how do you read the ending: Willy walking into a death he frames as a final gift to Biff, complete with the imagined grand funeral -- is that self-deception succeeding, failing, or curdling into something else, like deliberately using a fantasy he half-knows is a fantasy?
