I'm applying for a nine-month dissertation fellowship. My half-formed idea concerns the analogy between Ulysses' contract and constitutional precommitment: political theorists routinely justify entrenched rights and hard-to-amend constitutional provisions by analogy to Ulysses ordering himself bound to the mast before the Sirens. But the analogy hides a structural difference I want to press. Ulysses outsources enforcement: his crew, ears plugged with wax, do not share the preference he anticipates in himself, so his precommitment works through an external enforcer with stable interests. A constitution has no such enforcer — the institutions that interpret and uphold it are staffed and sustained by the very successors whose preferences it is supposed to constrain. Constitutional precommitment must therefore be self-enforcing in a way Ulyssean precommitment is not, and I suspect that once this is taken seriously, the justification of entrenchment either fails outright or quietly transforms into something else: perhaps a coordination device, or the reproduction of a settled second-order preference across generations rather than its imposition on them.
