I teach an intro philosophy of science course, and next term I'm doing a unit on Ian Hacking's stuff about interactive kinds and looping effects — the idea that when the human sciences classify people (as mentally ill, as gay, as autistic, as obese, whatever), the classified people come to know the classification, act under it, and change, which changes the classification in turn, so the human sciences are always studying a moving target in a way particle physics isn't. Instead of assigning yet another paper I want to pair it with fiction, and I keep landing on Ken Kesey's One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest (I'll have them read the novel, but most will only know the 1975 film, so treat either as fair game).

Read the book/film through Hacking's looping effects. The ward is obviously a classification machine — Acutes vs Chronics, the staff meetings where patients get their diagnoses adjusted, group therapy where the men are made to narrate themselves in clinical vocabulary until Harding believes his homosexuality is a pathology and Billy Bibbit's stutter tracks whatever his mother would think. Chief Bromden might be the purest case in all of fiction: classified as deaf and mute, he performs the classification for years, so the chart stays accurate. And McMurphey seems to arrive as the anti-interactive object — the one man who refuses to become what the file says he is. Except by the end he's lobotomized, which is the institution making a classification literally, surgically true, which feels like it changes the moral of the story. Here's what I want you to be honest about: does the novel actually depict looping effects, or just the cruder thing — power imposing labels top-down? Hacking's loops run through knowledge and self-understanding; Kesey's ward mostly runs on force, sedatives, and electroshock. Where does the book track Hacking, where does it not, and is there a scene where the two come apart in an interesting way rather than just a confusing one?

Give me this structure: 1) "The loop in the ward" — the scenes where classification genuinely reshapes the classified from the inside (~400 words); 2) "Where Kesey isn't Hacking" — where the mechanism is coercion rather than looping, stated plainly, no hedging (~300 words); 3) "Scenes to teach" — three or four specific scenes, each with what it shows and what it doesn't (~250 words); 4) "What the novel adds" — one honest paragraph on what fiction gives my students that Hacking's essays don't (~150 words). No need to quote the text, scene descriptions are fine.
