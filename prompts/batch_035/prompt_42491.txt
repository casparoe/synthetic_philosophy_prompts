I took a philosophy of science course last year in which we spent a week on anthropic reasoning, and I've been trying to keep reading around it since. I recently got hold of a popular book on probability and its future of humanity, and the chapter I'm on summarizes the doomsday argument in a way that mostly tracks what I remember from the course, but with a couple of moves that felt too fast. Since the chapter itself is presenting both the argument and its supposed demise in about four pages, I'd like an audit of the summary before I internalize it.

The chapter's summary goes roughly as follows. The doomsday argument, devised by the physicist Brandon Carter and popularized by the philosopher John Leslie, starts from your birth rank: you are, very roughly, the hundred-billionth human ever born. By the Copernican principle that we should expect to find ourselves in an unremarkable position, not near the very beginning or the very end, there is only a one-in-twenty chance that you are among the first 5% of all humans who will ever live. It follows that there is a 95% probability that the total number of humans will be no more than twenty times the roughly 100 billion who have lived so far, which, given plausible population figures, means there is a 95% chance humanity goes extinct within about ten thousand years. The chapter stresses that this conclusion follows from Bayes' theorem alone and needs no assumptions about wars, plagues, asteroids, or anything else about the world. It then says that the physicist Richard Gott famously derived the same result by applying the Copernican principle to time itself, showing that the future duration of anything should be comparable to its past duration, so humanity is unlikely to last much longer than it already has. Finally, it reports two objections as settling the matter: first, that conditioning on the trivial fact that you exist at all — which is more likely in worlds containing more observers — exactly cancels the doomsday shift, which is why most researchers now regard the argument as refuted; and second, that every one of the tens of billions of humans before us could have run the same argument, and nearly all of them were wrong about the end being imminent, so the method simply doesn't work.

Please go through this summary claim by claim and tell me what a philosopher working on anthropic reasoning would accept as stated, what is a conflation or an overstatement of a more modest point, and what is just wrong. A few things I'd especially like sorted out: whether the 95%-confidence bound on total humans follows from the sampling assumption alone or whether it silently needs a prior over possible total population sizes, and if so what happens to the ten-thousand-year extinction figure; whether Gott's argument is really the same result as Carter and Leslie's or a distinct argument that happens to reach a similar-sounding conclusion, and whether the chapter's "comparable to its past duration" phrasing captures it; and whether the two closing objections refute anything, only one version of the argument, or just one way of setting it up — and, in the case of the past-humans objection, whether it's even true that nearly all past reasoners running the argument would have been wrong, given what the argument would actually have told them. I also noticed a tension I can't resolve myself: the chapter says the conclusion follows from Bayes' theorem alone, with no assumptions about the world, yet also says a single further assumption about conditioning on your existence cancels everything out. Those two claims can't both be right about an argument with the same content, so one of them must be misdescribing the argument's structure, and I'd like to know which. I don't need citations or a literature review — just a careful accounting of the summary against what the argument actually is.
