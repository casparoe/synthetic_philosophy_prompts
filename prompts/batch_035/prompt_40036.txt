I have worked as a ward nurse in acute medicine for the better part of twenty years, and I have no background in philosophy. Some months ago a friend described to me an old dialogue about an ant colony called Aunt Hillary, in which an anteater argues that the individual ants are as dim as can be, while the colony itself is genuinely intelligent — that it holds beliefs, makes decisions, and can even hold a conversation, with the anteater's depredations tolerated rather than catastrophic, the way a mind loses neurons without losing thoughts. I have been turning this over on my own ever since, because it reminds me of something I know intimately but never had words for.

Here is the thing I actually know. On a busy ward, no single nurse holds the whole picture of a patient. Each of us knows fragments: the obs from one round, the comment passed at handover, the worry a physiotherapist mentioned in the corridor. Individual acts of noticing and passing on are as small and mechanical as any ant's behaviour. And yet the ward, over the course of a week, tracks a deteriorating patient in a way that often outruns any one of us — the escalating frequency of observations, the notes accumulating weight, the registrar eventually summoned — even when no individual could have said at the outset that the patient was going to become unwell. I have come to suspect that the ward, and not any of us, is doing the knowing.

What I would like you to do is translate the anteater's position into my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can and without flattering my suspicion by smuggling it in as if it were already established. Take the claims one by one: that intelligence and belief belong to the colony rather than the ants; that the colony's signals and symbols have no counterparts at the level of individual ants; that damaging or removing individual ants does not much injure the colony-level mind; and that the colony-level mind could in principle be a proper object of conversation, friendship, or harm. For each, tell me what it becomes when expressed in terms of handovers, observation rounds, escalation criteria, care plans, and shift patterns — what maps cleanly, what has to be bent to fit, and what cannot be said in ward vocabulary at all. Then give me the residue report: is there something in the ant-colony position that my vocabulary genuinely cannot express, and if so, does that point to something my way of seeing the ward leaves out, or to a looseness in the original claim? I should also like you to state plainly whether, on your best judgement, the translation ends up supporting the view that the ward itself is a knower, or whether the analogy breaks at the point where it matters.

Please write your answer in British English, and in plain terms throughout — I have no tolerance for jargon I would not hear at a bedside.
