My grandmother's trust, bless her, says her savings go to "whichever of my grandchildren are truly in financial need," and if none qualify it's split equally among all six of us. She died in September and somehow I got named executor, so now this is my actual job. Five of the six cousins are easy — a surgeon, a guy with a paid-off house, one cousin who's been unemployed a year and is behind on rent, etc. The hard case is Maya: two years ago she sold her condo and poured everything into a business that just went under. She's got roughly $40k left, about $60k in debt, a job that covers her expenses but barely, and she's 44. I genuinely do not know if she's "truly in financial need."

At Thanksgiving my uncle Dave (retired accountant, exactly the personality you'd expect) and my cousin Lena (social worker, argues for fun) went at this for two hours and I just took notes, because someone has to decide this by spring. Here's roughly how it went:

(1) Dave said look, there's a fact of the matter here. Maya's situation is what it is, and either she's in need or she isn't. The fact that it's hard for US to find out where the line falls doesn't mean there's no line — our ignorance doesn't make reality fuzzy.

(2) Lena said that's insane. If Maya had one dollar less she'd still be in need, and if she had a million more she clearly wouldn't be, but there is no specific dollar amount where it flips from needy to not. So it's not that we're ignorant of the cutoff — there IS no cutoff. Grandma's instruction just doesn't settle cases like Maya, full stop.

(3) Dave said fine, but then answer this: the trust was written eight years ago. Either it entitles Maya to money today or it doesn't — are you really saying there's no fact about where grandma's money is supposed to go, right now, before anyone decides? The money doesn't hover in some metaphysical escrow waiting for us.

(4) Lena said yes, basically — the will is genuinely indeterminate about Maya, and that's exactly why someone (me, apparently) has to do what the document itself couldn't: make it determinate. When I decide and it's final, then it becomes true that she counted as in need, or didn't. Before that, asking "but is she REALLY in need?" is like asking whether a specific 6'0" guy is really tall — nothing to be ignorant ABOUT.

(5) Dave said the tall guy example just proves his point — the guy is a determinate height, and "tall" either applies or doesn't; you're confusing the word being messy with the world being messy. And notice, he said, you're the one who has to throw out basic logic to get your view: either Maya is in need or she isn't is just the law of excluded middle, and if your theory tells you that sentence isn't true, your theory's in trouble, not the sentence.

(6) Lena said the sentence is only "obviously true" because you're picturing clear cases — a bankrupt cousin and a surgeon — but the whole debate is about the middle. On a borderline case, "she's in need" is no more true than false, and that's not a breakdown, it's how vague words have always worked. Dave replied that "no more true than false" is a sentence that should embarass an adult, and around we went.

I have to make this call by spring and honestly I could go either way. Can you go through their exchange step by step and tell me who's right about what — not just who wins overall, but which specific moves hold up and which don't? And if it's not too much to ask, tell me what you'd actually do in my seat, since one of these views seems to make my decision create the facts and the other seems to make me a very bad detective.
