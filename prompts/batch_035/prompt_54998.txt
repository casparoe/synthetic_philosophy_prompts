I teach a continuing-ed evening class — mostly retirees, a couple of engineers, no math beyond high school — and in two weeks I've got one 90-minute session on whether relativity kills the open future. The idea is the Rietdijk–Putnam thing: observers moving relative to each other slice spacetime into "now" at different angles, so an event that's still future for one observer is already settled for another, and since neither is wronger than the other, apparently everything is fixed. I don't want you to explain the argument to me; I want a plan for how I'd explain it to them. Specifically: what has to come first — I assume relativity of simultaneity, but should the simultaneity stuff come before or only after I've gotten them worried about the block picture, and why does the order matter? Then the carrying example: everyone uses the one with two people walking past each other on Earth, one of whose "now" contains events in Andromeda that are decades in the future for the other. I half like it but worry it makes the class think something spooky is being beamed from the galaxy. Decide whether to keep it (and say what work it's actually doing, what it has and lacks as an example), or replace it with something closer to home that doesn't invite sci-fi talk. Then the misconceptions. The ones I'm bracing for: relativity means everything is relative so nothing is real; the big one, that a fixed future means a determined future, i.e. that the galaxy "already decided" — I know the fixed-vs-caused distinction has to go somewhere in the sequence, tell me exactly where, because if it lands too late half the room will have already concluded that relativity proves fatalism; and people quietly hanging onto a cosmic now that physics just hasn't found yet. For each one, tell me the point in the session where it should be headed off, not just that it exists. Also one question I can ask at the end that would show they actually got the argument rather than just nodded along — something where the wrong answer is revealing, ideally. And tell me what to openly punt on and admit I'm punting on: I'm assuming general relativity and Gödel are out, but flag anything else that will bite me if I try to squeeze it in. There's also a guy who read a pop-physics book and is convinced time is an illusion, which sounds like agreement but I suspect isn't — worth a sentence on whether to hand him the mic at all.
