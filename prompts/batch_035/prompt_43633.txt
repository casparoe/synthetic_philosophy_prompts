Our history of science seminar is doing Kepler this term, and I've been assigned his astrology stuff, which is way more interesting than I expected. I need to predict what he'd say about some cases that came up after he'd ruled on everything else. Here's my problem: his track record seems consistent with a couple of different underlying rules, and I can't tell which.

First, the verdicts I've gathered from what he actually wrote. He's clear that the zodiac signs themselves have no power -- a birth in Aries means nothing as such, the signs are just where the sun happened to be. Same for the twelve houses, which he treats as pure convention and pretty silly. But the aspects he keeps: conjunction, opposition, trine, square, sextile, the works, because rays from two planets hitting the earth at a certain angle to each other can genuinely do something -- geometry is the language nature speaks. On the fixed stars he basically says their influence is negligible, something about the angles being too tiny / the stars too far. And on the big picture he defends a stripped-down astrology against the skeptics: there's a real kernel, it's just buried under centuries of garbage. He even thinks the Saturn-Jupiter conjunction of 1603 (with Mars piling in) really did matter for world affairs. But when the new star appeared in 1604 he mocked the astrologers who confidently read specific meanings off it, while still allowing it might be an omen somehow.

So here are the new cases I have to rule on, as Kepler would:

1. Galileo has just announced four little moons going around Jupiter. Do those have any astrological influence? Does Jupiter now get new powers, or do the moons add basically nothing? I gather Kepler wrote something to Galileo around then, so I want to know whether my prediction matches what he actually hinted at, but mainly I want the prediction derived from his rule.

2. Sunspots. If the sun itself is the biggest influencer around, does a blemish showing up on its face change anything, or is it the kind of thing his rule can't even see?

3. A comet that travels across the whole sky without ever making a major aspect with a planet. Does it portend anything, by his lights, or does it slip through the whole system?

The reason I'm stuck: rule A says only the aspects between the classical bodies, taken at the earth, do anything, so new objects near a planet and blemishes on the sun are basically noise and a non-aspecting comet is inert. Rule B says astrology should be reformed on physical grounds wherever there's a real body doing something, so new moons and sunspots are at least candidates for real influence and we should look for effects, and rule B would probably let a comet matter even without aspects (people always thought comets were ominous, and it's a dramatic physical event). Both rules fit the old verdicts as far as I can tell -- they both reject signs, houses, and confident sign-reading, and both keep the aspects. What do you think: what's his actual rule, what would he say on each of the three, and which of the three (or some further case you can think up) would best separate rule A from rule B? I'd also take a rule C if neither of mine is really his. Less interested in the right answers than in the reasoning that gets there. Sorry for typos, wrote this in a hurry before seminar.
