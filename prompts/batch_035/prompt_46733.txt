Next semester I am teaching a seminar unit on the two classical pragmatist accounts of truth, and I want an essay-style comparison that goes past the standard textbook contrast — Peirce as the truth of the long run, James as cash-value — because my students can recite that caricature already. Please compare Peirce's account, on which the truth is the final opinion that inquiry, properly conducted, would eventually converge on, with James's, on which a belief's being true consists in its working: its leading us profitably through experience, its verifiability, its being made true by events rather than merely discovered by them.

The comparison should be organized around distinctions I care about, not around the labels:

1. The tense of truth. Peirce's truth exists, if at all, at the limit of inquiry — at no actual date. James's truth has a present tense: a belief can be true now, in the interim, in virtue of how it currently pays, and later be made false by experience. Is either theory making truth hostage to the future in a way the other does not, and does that hostage-taking produce a genuine difference in what each theory counts as true today?

2. Whose verification counts. Peirce's convergers are a community of inquiry regulated by a method; James repeatedly allows that what works for a particular person, in his life, can be true for him. Does this reduce to a difference in the unit of verification (community versus individual), or is there a deeper disagreement about whether truth is a property of beliefs at all versus a relation between a belief and a course of experience?

3. A battery of cases, which the answer must actually adjudicate rather than mention in passing:
   - The squirrel case James made famous: one man walks round a tree, a squirrel on the trunk keeps facing him, and the two disputants cannot agree whether the man goes round the squirrel; James resolves it by asking which practical difference each reading would make. Does Peirce's account license the same resolution, or would he insist the question has a determinate answer that inquiry would settle regardless of practical consequences?
   - A belief that works for an entire lifetime and is never overturned within it, but which unlimited inquiry would eventually refute — say, a person who dies holding a falsely comforting belief about how a dead relative felt about them. Is it true? Give each theory's verdict, and if they agree, say whether they agree for the same reason.
   - A truth about the distant past for which all evidence has been destroyed, such that properly conducted inquiry, starting from what remains, would converge on the wrong answer. Is the truth lost on Peirce's account? And is there anything in James's account that even addresses this case, or does it simply fall outside a theory built around forward-looking verification?
   - Religious faith that is not checkable by any possible experience but transforms a life. James's verdict is familiar; the question I want pressed is what Peirce's account says about it, and whether that exposes a real limitation or just a difference in ambition.

4. One meta-level question the essay must take a position on: is James's account best read as a theory of truth at all, or as a theory of when a belief is warranted or of what asserting it does, with the truth itself left unanalyzed? And correspondingly, is Peirce's account a theory of truth or a theory of the real — of the object of the final opinion?

End with a verdict: which of the two is closer to offering a complete theory of truth, and — this is the part my students will find hardest — flag at least one distinction on which the two positions cannot actually be separated, so far as you can tell, saying whether that is because the views really converge there or because the textual record is too tangled.

Please lead with the bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating of this brief. At the end, state how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the single point in the comparison you are least sure of, explaining what would make you revise it. Do not treat the caricatures as the views: where the historical Peirce or the historical James is in tension with the simplified version, work with the historical position, and say so when you do.
