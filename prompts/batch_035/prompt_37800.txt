I am a retired condensed-matter physicist who, since leaving the lab, has been working systematically through political philosophy — Rawls, Berlin, Pettit's republicanism, a good deal of Bostrom and the AI-risk literature. Next month I have been invited to present to a political-philosophy workshop at the university where I held my chair. The audience: two Rawlsians concerned with public reason and legitimacy, a republican in the Pettit tradition for whom domination is the central political evil, a committed value pluralist, a game theorist who works on deterrence and arms-race modeling, and one Aristotelian who thinks technology debates are always secretly debates about the good life. None of them works on AI. My thesis is that if transformative AI systems arrive, we should prefer — and perhaps deliberately steer toward — a singleton, in Bostrom's sense of a single decision-making agency at the highest level with no competitive peers, over a multipolar world of many competing systems. My argument is not Bostrom's. I frame it in terms of attractor dynamics: among rapidly self-improving competitors, the returns to preemptive action and the fragility of mutual deterrence under swift capability change make sustained multipolar equilibrium a very narrow, unmaintained basin, so that the long-run attractors are essentially two — ruin, or consolidation. Consolidation, once achieved by an agency with roughly right values, is stable against erosion in a way no balance of powers is, since it eliminates the race dynamics that erode safety margins in the first place. I add, more speculatively, an observationally grounded premise from the Fermi paradox: the silence of the sky is weak evidence that expansionist civilizations either perish or coordinate, because a multipolar expanding civilization would fragment and diversify its visible signatures across the galaxy, whereas we see nothing at all.

My instinct is that this audience will hear a physics-flavored defense of world government, and I want to be ready for what they will actually say rather than for the objections I would get from the AI-safety crowd. Please prepare my Q&A sheet: the eight or so objections I should expect from this particular room, ordered by how much damage each would do, each phrased the way a Rawlsian, a republican, a pluralist, a game theorist, or an Aristotelian would actually put it, with a reply I can deliver in two minutes and a candid note on where the reply is soft. I particularly want you to address whether my attractor language is doing any real argumentative work or is merely a euphemism for "it would be convenient if one actor won" — I suspect the game theorist will press exactly this, and I do not want to bluff. Tell me what I should concede up front, what I must hold at all costs, and what the question is that I am hoping nobody asks. I have a suspicion it is the one where the legitimacy objection arrives disguised as a technical question about the word "we" in my thesis — who, exactly, is doing the steering toward this singleton, and by what right — but I would rather know now whether that suspicion is correct.
