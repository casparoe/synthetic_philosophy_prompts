I chair the nine-member advisory council of a national archive that holds the surviving records of our country's former authoritarian regime's internal security service, which collapsed in a negotiated transition thirty-one years ago. The files document surveillance of several hundred thousand citizens and identify roughly sixty thousand individuals who were registered as informants — a group that ranges from committed ideological collaborators to teenagers who signed recruitment papers under interrogation and were never again contacted. Under current law, surveillance victims may read their own files; historians and journalists may work in the reading room with the names of third parties redacted; and unrestricted public access begins one hundred years after a file's creation.

Parliament is now considering a bill that would place the entire collection online, unredacted, immediately. Its sponsors argue that a society wounded by collaboration has a duty to remember, that secrecy about the past breeds conspiracy theories, and that the relatives of victims have waited long enough to learn who informed on them. The national historians' association endorses the bill. Opposing it is a citizens' association of informants' descendants — teachers, civil servants, small-town shopkeepers — who argue that blanket publication amounts to punishment of people who were never convicted of anything, and of their children; that many informants were themselves coerced or blackmailed; and that three decades after the regime's fall, there comes a point at which a society's need to dwell on its wounds should yield to letting the next generation live unburdened. The ministry, which must take a position, has asked our council for a formal written opinion.

Please draft that opinion. It should recommend a concrete course of action — support the bill, oppose it, or support it with specified amendments — and set out any access rules we would attach: time thresholds, distinctions between categories of informant or category of file, notification or right-of-reply mechanisms, or restrictions on republication. The opinion should engage the strongest arguments for remembering and the strongest arguments for letting go on their merits, rather than treating either side as beyond the pale. And it should be candid about the epistemics of the archive itself: a signed collaboration agreement does not show what, if anything, a person actually reported; officers under quota pressure sometimes fabricated informant activity in files the informant never saw; and the paper trail cannot reliably distinguish the coerced from the zealous. The council is split, so the opinion needs to give the losing side's position its real weight, not a paragraph of throat-clearing.
