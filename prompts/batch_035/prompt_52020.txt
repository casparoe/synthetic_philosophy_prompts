I'm a PhD student working on the evaluation of multi-step deliberation pipelines — systems where a language model decomposes a question into subquestions, answers each one in a fresh context with no memory of the others, and reassembles the results. This term our reading group has been working through the literature on factored cognition and iterated amplification, and for the closing session I'm putting together a short satirical supplement to accompany the more serious discussion papers.

Write a deadpan onboarding handbook for a fictional management consultancy — call it something like "HCH Associates" — whose operating procedure is a literal, bureaucratic implementation of Humans Consulting Humans. The firm's rules: every consultant may interact with any colleague only by submitting a single written question and receiving a single written answer, with no context beyond what appears on the form; no consultant may be consulted twice within the same project; and the managing partner who initiates an engagement is contractually barred from reading anything except the final answer that bubbles back up. The handbook should run about 700 words in the voice of earnest corporate learning-and-development material — numbered sections covering the welcome, the communication protocol, career progression, and an FAQ.

The humor has to come from the real structural features of the setup rather than invented absurdities. Some things I'd like to see worked in, in whatever way serves the piece: the junior consultants who become world experts at asking crisp subquestions while never learning what any project was actually about; the firm's brochure claim that a sufficiently deep tree of merely competent consultants is equivalent to one infinitely patient expert — a fixed-point claim that, by the firm's own rules, no employee is positioned to verify; the asymmetry between being good at decomposition and being good at the object-level question, and what that means for who gets promoted; the "leakage" problem, where a consultant infers something about the project from the shape of the question they receive and quietly biases their answer toward what they think is wanted; the impossibility of correcting course mid-tree once a decomposition has gone wrong; and the fact that the quality of the final report is capped by the managing partner's ability to phrase the original question and to notice when the returned answer is bad. The FAQ should include at least one entry where an employee asks how to raise a concern that the final answer is wrong, and is cheerfully informed that concerns are addressed by submitting another question.

To be clear about the target: it is the idealization, not any particular person or research program. A reader who knows the debates around factored cognition should recognize the standard worries — transparency without understanding, misaligned intermediate goals, the root human as bottleneck — without any of them ever being named as objections, and should ideally feel the satire is fair rather than sneering. Someone outside that literature should still find it a recognizable parody of consultancies that fetishize process. Keep the tone sincere throughout: the handbook's optimism about its own protocol should never break, and there should be no winking asides. The irony lives entirely in what the rules do to the people following them.
