I'm a novelist and my friend who's gone very deep into the AI-discourse corner of the internet has an argument she wont stop running on me. It seems to prove too much and I can't find where it bends.

(1) You're entitled to attribute beliefs and desires to a system when doing so lets you predict its behavior better than you could by treating it as a mere mechanism — she says this is basically the intentional stance idea, that predictive payoff is what licenses the attribution.
(2) I predict my characters perfectly that way. I know exactly what Nora will do when the telegram arrives, because I know what she wants and what she believes about her sister, and I have never once been wrong about her.
(3) My track record with Nora beats any psychologist's track record with any living patient.
(4) So if (1) is the test, Nora has beliefs and desires, full stop.
(5) And the engineers who predict their chatbot by asking what it wants are in my position with Nora, not the psychologist's with the patient: they built it, they know its whole history, and whenever it surprises them they go back and adjust until it doesn't.
(6) So the test in (1) licenses attributing real beliefs to anything whose behavior is just an extension of the attributor's own decisions.
(7) But Nora doesn't believe anything — she's invented, and if there is any fact about what she believes it's only the fact about what I would write next.
(8) Therefore (1) fails as a test, and my friend concludes that nobody can use predictive success to argue that chatbots really believe anything — the argument that was supposed to elevate the machines tears down everyone else on the way past, and she says she's fine with that. I'm not.

Which step do I reject? If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (7) than keep (1), but I want to know what I'm actually paying — especially whether rejecting whatever step saves me from Nora also takes away the ground we stand on when we attribute beliefs to each other, or to the chatbots for that matter.
