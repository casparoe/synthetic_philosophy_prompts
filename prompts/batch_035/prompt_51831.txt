I'm writing a term paper for a philosophy of perception seminar on cognitive penetration — the question of whether beliefs, expectations, or emotions can literally alter what things perceptually look, sound, or taste like, as opposed to merely changing how quickly we notice them or what we conclude afterwards. My friend Maya, who is auditing the seminar, has developed a surprisingly stable set of verdicts about which empirical cases count as genuine penetration and which don't, and I've been keeping a running list as she reacts to each one. Here is her track record so far. When untrained wine drinkers are told a wine is expensive and report that it genuinely tastes better — not that they judge it to be better — she rules: penetration. When trained sommeliers discriminate flavours the rest of us can't detect, she rules: no penetration, that's perceptual learning. When hearing a label like "banana" just before a briefly flashed display makes people slightly faster at detecting a banana in visual noise, with no change in what the banana looks like, she still rules: penetration. When exhausted hikers literally see a hill as steeper than it is, she rules: not penetration, because fatigue is a bodily state rather than a cognitive one. When people with snake phobias detect snake images that are suppressed from conscious awareness faster than controls do, she rules: penetration, even though only detection speed is affected. When an expert radiologist sees a hairline fracture where a layperson sees only a grey smudge, she rules: perceptual learning again, not penetration. When a beep that accompanies a single flash makes people report two flashes, she rules: no, that's crossmodal integration, not cognition touching perception. And when being told to look for red things makes red objects seem to pop out of a cluttered scene, she rules: no, that's attention doing its ordinary work.

The new case, which came up in our last meeting and which she hasn't ruled on yet, is the so-called "Russian blues" effect: native Russian speakers, whose language has distinct basic color terms for light blue and dark blue, discriminate shades across that lexical boundary faster than English speakers do, and the advantage seems tied to having the labels available at the moment of the task rather than merely to years of using them. I genuinely can't predict Maya's verdict. On one way of running her rulings, penetration requires a top-down effect whose content is arbitrary — it depends on a belief, label, or expectation that could have been different without the world being different — which the linguistic case satisfies, while perceptual learning fails because it is fine-tuned to real environmental structure. On another way of running them, what matters is that the effect is fast and online, one shot from cognition to perceptual processing, whereas anything built up slowly by training, even linguistic training, is the system recalibrating itself rather than being penetrated. And she has at least one more rule in play, since her bodily-state and crossmodal exclusions suggest she also cares about the source of the effect being genuinely cognitive.

Please structure your answer with the following sections, in this order and at roughly these lengths: "Maya's verdict" (~100 words: your prediction, stated plainly), "The candidate rules" (~250 words: at least two rules that each fit her entire track record but diverge on the Russian blues case), "Best guess and why" (~150 words: which rule you think she is really running and what tips you off), and "The dividing case" (~100 words: the single further case you would put to Maya that would most cleanly separate the candidate rules, with a word on which rule says what about it). Feel free to note where her track record is loose enough that you're hedging — she has never explicitly defined penetration for me, so you're reconstructing her commitments, not reciting them.
