So I write literary fiction, mostly mid-sized novels, one every three or four years because I'm slow, and the slowness is almost all revision. I will rewrite a single chapter fifteen times and honestly can't tell you the fifteenth draft is better than the seventh. I've come to realize I'm a maximizer about this — I keep going because there might always be a better version of the paragraph out there, and I treat "I can't think of anything more to fix" as failure rather than as finishing.

I've been reading a bit about satisficing and bounded rationality, Herbert Simon's stuff — the idea that with limited time and limited minds, the rational move is to set a threshold for "good enough" and stop when you clear it, rather than search for the best possible option. And it strikes me this might be exactly the cure for my problem, but I don't actually know how to operationalize it for fiction. "Good enough" is doing a ton of work there. Good enough by what standard? Compared to what? My own taste changes week to week, which is how I end up "fixing" a chapter back to something close to what it was two drafts ago.

What I want is a procedure I can actually run at the end of each drafting day, in maybe twenty minutes, that tells me when a chapter is done and I move on to the next one. It should be grounded in what satisficing and bounded rationality actually say — aspiration levels, the cost of continued search, that kind of thing — but translated into novelist terms, not psychology-paper terms. I want it to tell me: what questions to ask myself in what order, what counts as a "yes this chapter is done" answer to each, when the procedure should flat-out tell me to move on even if I don't feel done, and where it has to hand the decision back to me because "is this chapter good" is a judgment call no checklist can settle.

Then please run it on three cases from my actual life: (1) a middle chapter that does its plot job but that I find boring, (2) my opening chapter, which my agent says is the weakest thing in an otherwise finished manuscript, and (3) a scene I've already rewritten eight times where I keep flip-flopping between two versions and can't tell them apart anymore.

One thing I want to be honest about: run the procedure faithfully on case 3 even if the answer is one I won't like — I suspect the right answer is that the two versions are equivalent and my continued searching is pure cost, and if that's what the procedure says, say so. And tell me where the procedure will misfire or fail me — I assume there are cases where satisficing a chapter is exactly wrong (maybe beginnings, maybe endings?), and I'd rather know the failure modes up front than trust the checklist blindly. Also worth noting my real constraint: I'm 52, I publish maybe three more books in my life if I'm lucky, so every extra year spent polishing chapter four has an actual price. I don't think the pure satisficing framing quite captures that, but maybe I'm wrong — you tell me.
