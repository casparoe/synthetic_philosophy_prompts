I got into an argument at a friend's birthday dinner a couple weeks back that's still bugging me. Her dad, who reads a lot of popular history, kept invoking "for want of a nail the kingdom was lost" — the nursery rhyme where the shoe is lost for want of the nail, the horse for want of the shoe, and so on up through the rider, the battle, and the kingdom. He was using it against her, actually, since she'd said something about the causes of WWI being structural, deep economic and alliance stuff. His point was that history is basically chaos all the way down, that no grand explanation of anything survives contact with the nail, and that people who talk about "underlying causes" are basically doing astrology with footnotes.

I'm pretty sure that one proverb is doing way too much work in his argument, but I can't quite separate out all the different things it could be claiming. Is it saying history is genuinely indeterministic? Or just that outcomes are super sensitive to initial conditions even in a fully deterministic world (the butterfly affect thing)? Or maybe it's only an epistemic point — we can't know in advance WHICH small events will matter, even if in principle the big causes dominate? Or is it a claim about explanation itself, that any causal chain is arbitrary because you can always keep tracing back to smaller and smaller stuff? I'd love a proper menu of the distinct precise theses packed into that proverb — which are trivially true, which are substantive and defensible, which are false or at least hotly contested — and for each one, whether it actually gets her dad what he wanted. Because here's what's nagging me: if the "keep tracing back" reading proves too much, doesn't the argument that undoes structural explanations of WWI also undo the nail? The nail only matters because of the whole geopolitical setup that made that battle matter; you could equally blame the blacksmith, the horse's owner, or the whole international system.

Bonus question, since I'll probably see him at Thanksgiving: does the proverb secretly assume a single unbroken chain of custody from nail to kingdom — one pivotal battle where one rider's horse mattered — and is that overdetermination-ish assumption its real weak point? I have a vague sense that in most actual historical cases the kingdom was falling one way or another and the nail is just a good story, but I'd like that suspicion either sharpened or debunked properly.
