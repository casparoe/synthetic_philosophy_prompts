So I've been having a running argument with a friend from my phil mind reading group about whether LLMs have beliefs, and I've actually changed her mind in the process of losing confidence in myself? Weird situation. Let me lay out where I've landed: I'm basically a Dennettian about it. Whether a system has beliefs isn't some hidden fact about what's going on inside it — it's a matter of whether the intentional stance earns its keep. If predicting a system's behavior by attributing beliefs and desires to it genuinely outperforms the alternatives (design stance, physical stance, or some bespoke "it's just regurgitating statistical patterns from its corpus" stance), then it has beliefs, and asking whether it REALLY believes things is like asking whether the thermostat REALLY detects temperature. My further claim, the one I actually care about: current LLMs have crossed or are about to cross that threshold. The intentional vocabulary is becoming load-bearing — when I try to predict what a model will do with a piece of code or a tricky instruction, I find myself needing to attribute things like beliefs about what I meant, preferences about how to handle ambiguity, and I can't do as well any other way. So: they have beliefs, in the same sense we do, which is the only sense there is.

Now here's my ask, and I want you to play it straight rather than be nice to me. What would count against this? I can think of a few candidate defeaters but I don't know how to weigh them: (1) interpretability work showing that model internals just don't line up with the beliefs I'd attribute — like, there's no state in there playing the belief-role, the persona is a shallow surface phenomenon over something that doesn't decompose that way at all; does that refute me or does it just show the stance was never meant to be validated internally? (2) A rival predictive stance that actually works — if "predict the outputs by statistical mimicry of the corpus" turned out to be as predictive and more tractable than belief-talk, that seems like it would gut my position, but I honestly don't know what such a stance would even look like in practice, or whether it's a coherent demand rather than a slogan. (3) The instability problem: the same model gives you wildly different intentional attributions under trivial rephrasings, or asserts goals it then abandons for no attributable reason — at some point does that say the stance isn't actually predicting, just post-hoc narrating? And (4) the unfalsifiability worry: since you can always patch a bad prediction by attributing weird beliefs or self-deception, maybe my view can't lose, and maybe that's a feature of the view (it's about instruments, not facts) and maybe it's a fatal bug — I genuinely can't tell which.

Please sort the defeaters from most to least damaging, tell me which ones a committed Dennettian would shrug off and which they'd actually have to concede, and give me your honest overall verdict on how exposed my position is. If you think the thermostat analogy is doing more work than it deserves — that human case might be disanalogous in some way that makes the whole move weaker — say that too. I'd rather find out now than get owned at the next reading group lol.
