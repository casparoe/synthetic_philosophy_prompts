So I've been hanging around LessWrong and adjacent spaces since roughly the Scott-Alexander-jacobtinkers era, and one thing that gets repeated like scripture is Aumann's agreeing-to-disagree result: two agents with common priors who have common knowledge of each other's posteriors can't disagree. It's usually cited as either "so epistemic rationalists should converge" or, more honestly, "the assumptions are doing all the work, especially the common prior one." The second reading is the one I care about.

I'm running a session at my local rationality meetup — about 14 people, mix of software engineers and a couple of philosophy grad students, 75 minutes, a room with tables we can rearrange and a whiteboard. Most of them have heard of Aumann's theorem the way people have heard of Gödel's theorem — the vibe, not the content. I want an activity where they feel, in their bones, that the common prior assumption is not a technicality. The naive move is to just have people state credences and update on each other's numbers; that kind of works but it's too bloodless, and it doesn't isolate the prior as the thing that's breaking.

Here's the design constraint I actually want: the game should set up two groups that get the *same evidence* — literally identical information revealed to both sides, publicly, so common knowledge of the evidence is not in question — and yet they should end up with persistently different posteriors, and the disagreement should be traceable, in the debrief, to something the participants brought into the room rather than anything that happened in the room. The tricky part is manufacturing the differing priors honestly: I don't want to just tell people "you believe X now." Maybe seed them beforehand with different framing exercises, or different reference classes they're forced to use, or have them first bet on a warm-up question where group identity forms around an answer and then carry that into the main game. I'm open to whatever mechanism you think actually produces a real prior difference and not just stubbornness, and part of what I want from you is a principled story for why your mechanism counts as a prior difference rather than an information difference — that distinction is the whole point of the session.

Also include: rules, how to structure the 75 minutes, what the payoff scheme should be (prediction-market-style play money is fine, they're competitive nerds, that's enough), a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto pieces of the formal setup — the prior, the posteriors, common knowledge, and where exactly the theorem stops applying — and an honest section on what the game can't reproduce (real Aumann stuff is about iterated announcement and convergence, which takes rounds we probably won't have, and the infinite regress of common knowledge is basically impossible to stage). And tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it — I'm guessing at least a few participants will try to game the mechanism instead of updating, or will converge immediately because the room is full of people who all read the same blogs, which would be its own kind of funny.

One more thing: in the debrief I want a segment connecting it back to real life — when is treating "we disagree" as evidence of irrationality on someone's part a mistake because the common prior assumption just doesn't hold between two random humans, and when is that a cop-out? The meetup crowd loves yelling "Aumann!" at each other as a cudgel, and I'd like the session to make the cudgel slightly harder to swing. If the debrief can give them a rule of thumb for telling prior-differences apart from information-differences in an actual argument, that's the session paying for itself.
