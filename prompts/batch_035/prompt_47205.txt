I sit on the board of a regional food bank, where I handle the finances as a volunteer; by trade I run a small construction supply business, so I'm the person the others expect to have opinions about numbers. Four years ago we began building our own distribution center — we've been renting warehouse space at $18,000 a month, and the board agreed the rent was money down the drain. The project was budgeted at $2.4 million, has so far cost $3.1 million, and last week the contractor told us that finishing will take roughly another $700,000, plus contingency. This is the fifth time the completion estimate has grown. A local foundation has offered $300,000 toward completion, but only if the board commits by the end of the month. Our reserves could cover the rest, though it would leave us thin. Walking away now would mean selling a half-finished concrete shell purpose-built for walk-in freezers — so any likely buyer will know it — and going back to renting.

The board is split, and I've noticed something about our arguments that I can't quite put into the minutes. Every time this comes up, we decide the same question: is the next chunk of money worth it, given what we've already spent? Each increment has looked small next to what was already sunk, and I recently read about a fundraising game — I believe it's called the dollar auction — in which people end up paying more than a dollar for a dollar because quitting feels like losing the whole amount already bid, and I suspect our board has been playing that game for four years without noticing. But our operations manager, who has been with the food bank for twenty years, makes a counterargument I can't dismiss as mere bias: we deal with the same funders, contractors, and county officials year after year, and a board that abandons half-built projects is a board people will remember. On her view, saying we've come too far to stop is not a fallacy but a statement about our reputation, and honoring sunk commitments may be more like keeping a promise than like bad arithmetic. I honestly don't know where the psychology ends and the real costs begin.

Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before Thursday's board meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — finish, walk away, or something in between (some of us have floated commissioning an independent cost estimate, though it would cost around $25,000 and take three weeks, which collides with the foundation's deadline) — with a time frame the board can act on. It should engage the operations manager's position seriously rather than waving it off as sunk-cost bias, and it should say plainly how the board is to distinguish, in this particular case, the reasoning that is keeping us in the auction from the reasoning that is genuinely sound.
