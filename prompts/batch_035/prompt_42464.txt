You are a professional philosopher of mind who works on the emotions. I am a PhD student guest-editing a special journal issue on what emotions are, and its centerpiece is an adversarial collaboration between two theorists who have argued for two decades without converting each other: one a neo-Stoic judgment theorist, holding that emotions are a species of evaluative judgment (fear being roughly the judgment that one is in danger, grief the judgment that one has suffered a serious loss); the other a Jamesian feeling theorist, holding that an emotion simply is the experience of one's own bodily perturbation (fear being the felt racing heart and tight chest, grief the felt sinking and heaviness). I have to write the introduction, so please draft the joint statement they should have produced after months of good-faith collaboration. Think carefully and brainstorm privately before drafting, then give me: a numbered section of claims both would sign, including about the phenomena any theory must accommodate; a disagreements table stating their residual disagreements precisely, with each row worded so that its own side would accept it; the cruxes — sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them; and, for each crux, a concrete case or everyday phenomenon both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Use only conceptual analysis and ordinary phenomena, not citations to the empirical literature. Make sure the flashpoints include at least these: recalcant emotions, like the arachnophobe who sincerely judges the spider harmless yet remains terrified; whether infants and dogs can genuinely fear things while lacking the concepts evaluative judgments seem to require; free-floating moods such as objectless anxiety, where there is no obvious judgment and no obvious intentional object; and epistemic feelings like the feeling of knowing and déjà vu, which have bodily phenomenology but no obvious evaluative content. Finally, flag candidly which parts of the dispute strike you as merely verbal — for instance, if the judgment theorist is permitted to count non-propositional, appearance-like states as judgments (in the ballpark of what Tamar Gendler calls alief), do the two positions actually converge? Keep the agreed claims substantive rather than platitudes, and the disagreement statements non-slanted rather than winnowed.
