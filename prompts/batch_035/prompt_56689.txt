For several months now I have been recording my gut verdicts about when an agent counts as irrational, as opposed to merely limited, for failing to believe something that follows from what the agent already believes. The list has grown long enough that I would like to know whether some single principle captures the pattern, and, if not, which of my verdicts has to be sacrificed. Here are the cases and my verdicts on them.

1. A logician believes the axioms of Peano arithmetic. There is a forty-line derivation of a certain arithmetic sentence from those axioms; she has never run through it and holds no view whatsoever on the sentence. Verdict: no rational failing here at all.

2. A philosopher, while drafting a paper, writes that P, writes that if P then Q, has both before her mind within the same hour, and in the very next paragraph asserts not-Q. Verdict: irrational, full stop.

3. A chess master, with ample time on the clock, overlooks that she has mate in three. Verdict: this is a lapse, perhaps an embarrassing one, but it is not a failure of rationality.

4. Frege, before Russell's letter reached him, believed each of the axioms of his system. The contradiction follows from those axioms by a derivation that is, by mathematical standards, extremely short. Verdict: this is the one I keep going back and forth on. Sometimes it seems obvious that Frege was exactly as rational as the logician in case 1; sometimes the shortness of the derivation makes it feel closer to case 2.

5. An economist models households as agents who know every logical consequence of their preferences and beliefs. Verdict: I think this idealization is perfectly legitimate model-building, and I also think the actual households are not irrational for failing to live up to it. I feel no tension about this, though I half suspect I should.

6. A student believes that a long propositional tautology is false, because she has misparsed the placement of parentheses in it. Verdict: not irrational — though I notice this case is uncomfortable for me, since the shortest path from her situation to the truth is a mechanical truth-table check.

7. A bettor accepts a system of wagers under which she is guaranteed to lose money; the guarantee rests on a logical equivalence between two of the propositions involved, an equivalence she has never contemplated. Verdict: she is not irrational to accept, but there is some real sense in which her credences are defective, and I cannot tell whether that sense is the same one operative in case 2.

My tentative principle runs something like this: an agent is rationally required to believe whatever follows from her beliefs by a single explicitly performed inference step, and is rationally permitted to miss anything whose derivation would require a search. But this principle condemns the chess master in case 3, since mate in three is a short derivation, and it acquits the philosopher in case 2 so long as she never actually performs the modus ponens — which suggests I have drawn the line in the wrong place.

So I am asking: is there a principle, or a small set of candidates, that captures all or nearly all of these verdicts? For each candidate, state it precisely, say which case motivates each of its clauses, and say specifically what it rules in the Frege case. Then tell me which of my verdicts your favored principle cannot accommodate, and be blunt about whether the verdict or the principle should give way. Finally, tell me what the principle predicts for a case I have not listed: a large language model that asserts a proposition in one conversation, asserts its negation in another, and never once entertains the two together — is that irrational, or merely limited, or does the principle show that the question is ill-posed as it stands?
