I serve on the standing disciplinary panel of a research university, and we have a case that has split the panel cleanly down the line between retributivist and consequentialist instincts, so I want our written decision memo to engage that divide explicitly rather than paper over it.

The case: a fourth-year doctoral student altered several data points in a manuscript submitted to a journal, then, roughly three weeks later and unprompted, disclosed the fabrication to her advisor and withdrew the submission before any decision; the paper was never published and no co-authors or reviewers acted on the false data. Since then, she has been removed from her lab, lost her funding, and her prospective supervisor withdrew a job recommendation — so she has already suffered serious, effectively irreversible consequences. One faction of the panel argues that imposing a formal sanction such as a transcript notation or a multi-year ban on university research roles is pure retribution given the total harm actually caused; the other faction argues that if self-reporting after withdrawal fully insulates someone from sanction, we dilute the norm that data fabrication is intrinsically culpable, not just culpable when it succeeds.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate to the panel. It should take a clear position — no formal sanction, formal sanction, or sanction with specified conditions — and it should present its reasoning as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion before or after the list. Somewhere in the five items, give the strongest consideration on the losing side an honest and specific treatment, not a token mention; and where the memo invokes a theory of punishment, it should say what work that theory is actually doing in the recommendation rather than name-dropping it.
