My uncle — retired high school physics teacher, reads everything he can find on the history of astronomy — has an argument he's very proud of and brings out at every family dinner, and the annoying part is I can't find the hole in it. It goes like this. (1) When a theory's prediction fails, the blame can always be deflected onto some auxiliary assumption instead of onto the theory itself; that's just a fact about how testing works, since the prediction only ever follows from theory-plus-auxiliaries-plus-instrument-readings taken as a bundle. (2) Sometimes doing that is excellent science: when Uranus kept drifting off Newton's script, astronomers didn't blame Newton, they posited an unseen planet, and the planet was sitting almost exactly where the calculation said to point the telescope. Everyone, including him, agrees that was a great move. (3) And here's his point: what made that move *available* is completely general. Given any anomaly whatsoever, you can cook up a hidden something with parameters tuned until the misfit goes away — there is no failed prediction so precise that a flexible enough auxiliary can't accomodate it. (4) Epicycles are the same trick with better PR: each new irregularity in a planet's motion got its own extra circle, sized and phased to fit, protecting the core (the heavens are perfect and circular) by adjusting the periphery. Neptune and epicycles are, he says, the same rescue in different costumes. (5) And you can't draw the line by ugliness: there's no principled number of auxiliaries at which protection turns irrational — nobody gets to say the 8th epicycle was fine and the 9th was one too many.

So he concludes: if the Neptune rescue was rational, the epicycle rescues were rational too; and if we're confident the epicyclers should eventually have packed it in, then we're stuck calling the Neptune move irrational or just lucky. Either way, no piece of evidence can ever rationally *compel* a scientist to give up a theory they're committed to — the Ptolemaics could still be at it today, adding circles, and nobody could accuse them of anything. The theory never has to die; it just has to be defended. He loves this because he thinks it shows that talk of theories being "refuted by the facts" is mostly rhetoric, and what actually ends theories is boredom and funding.

I want to keep (1) and (2) — (1) sounds like plain logic, and (2) is plain history, the planet really was there. My instinct is the trouble starts around (3) or (4), since "the same rescue in different costumes" is doing a lot of work for a phrase nobody has defined, but every time I try to say what the costume difference is, he just replies that epicycles also fit the observations, and fitting the observations is all Neptune's planet ever did — and I'm stuck again. So: which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it actually cost? I'd rather pay almost anything than accept his conclusion, but I want to know the real price, not just that it's non-zero.
