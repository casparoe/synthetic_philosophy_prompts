I teach a second-year metaphysics course, and every year the unit on vagueness goes the same way: students choose whichever label sounds least ridiculous — sharp boundaries they can't find, truth to degree 0.62, the word "supervaluation" — and then defend the label rather than the commitments. For next year I want a decision-tree handout that starts from the sorites argument itself and forces them through cases before any position is named.

The tree should begin at the argument, not at a question about words: a clear case of a heap, the premise that removing a single grain never turns a heap into a non-heap, the downward march to one grain. The first branching should be over which limb the student abandons — the major premise, the starting point, the inference pattern, or the falsity of the conclusion — and every subsequent node should be a concrete case or question they can answer without knowing where it leads. Please build in, at minimum, the following diagnostics: whether a student accepts that some single removal makes a difference even if no possible evidence could tell them which one (the "God whispers the cutoff number" case); what to say about a borderline case when asked point-blank whether "that is a heap" is true, false, both, neither, or true only to a degree; whether the felt moment of flipping from "heap" to "no heap" during a forced march through the series reflects a shifting standard, a genuinely defective concept, or mere psychological noise; and whether "borderline" is itself a borderline predicate, and whether that embarrasses them.

The leaves must include epistemicism, supervaluationism, a degree-theoretic treatment, contextualism about shifting standards, the nihilist conclusion that nothing is a heap, and an incoherentist option on which the concept is simply inconsistent, plus any unnamed cells the logical space requires — and please say for each unoccupied combination whether nobody holds it because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Finally, flag honestly which pairs of leaves the tree cannot actually separate: if a student's answers to all the diagnostic questions leave supervaluationism and degree theory indistinguishable, or epistemicism and the naive view that some step must matter "because logic," say so rather than inventing a question that decides it. Ten to twelve leaves is about the most the handout can carry.
