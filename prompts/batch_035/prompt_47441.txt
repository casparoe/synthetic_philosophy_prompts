My cousin and her husband had a twenty-minute argument on a road trip last weekend and I heard the whole thing from the back seat. Context that matters: we were driving to a wedding, and the forecast for the next day — the one with the outdoor reception — was 90% rain. At some point my cousin says, half joking, "It'll rain tomorrow, but I don't believe it." Her husband immediately goes "that's the Moore thing, you can't assert that." She goes "I just did." He says you can utter the words, but you can't assert them: a straight assertion of p expresses belief in p, so by asserting the first half she's committed herself to believing it will rain, and the second half then cancels her own assertion. She says assertion doesn't require belief — lawyers argue positions they don't believe in, actors deliver lines, a poker player talks up a hand he knows is trash. He says those are contexts where everyone knows a game is being played; she wasn't role-playing, she was telling us the weather, and in a straight assertion the second conjunct is self-defeating. She then pivots: fine, it's wierd to SAY, but that doesn't make it false — "it will rain and I don't believe it" could easily be true, and it's probably true of her right now: she packed two sundresses and no umbrella, so whatever comes out of her mouth, her actions suggest she doesn't really believe the rain. He says: exactly, so the sentence is true but not assertible, that IS the paradox, Moore noticed it back in the 1940s. She says: then we've agreed for the last fifteen minutes, so what are you still arguing about? He says no — they agree about the phenomenon but not the explanation. Her story, he says, is purely about speech acts: asserting p implies belief in p, so asserting the conjunction shoots itself in the foot. But the weirdness doesn't live at the level of saying: imagine just BELIEVING "it will rain and I don't believe it," without asserting anything to anyone. Still absurd. And a speech-act story can't touch that, because there's no audience for anyone to implicate anything to. Her comeback: believing it would just be an ordinary irrational belief, like someone who believes two contradictory things — people manage that all the time, it's not some deep paradox, it's garden-variety self-inconsistency, and he's romanticizing it. He says that's exactly what it's NOT: a plain contradiction is one thing, but his sentence isn't a contradiction at all — "it will rain" and "I don't believe it will rain" can both be true — yet it's still unbelievable in a way that neither her speech-act story nor her "garden-variety irrationality" story captures. And if she can't explain the believing case, she hasn't explained the asserting case either, since the assertibility problem is presumably inherited from the belief problem.

That's basically where it ended, because we hit the rest stop. They both told me to settle it later and I honestly can't. Can you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right at each step — not just who wins overall?
