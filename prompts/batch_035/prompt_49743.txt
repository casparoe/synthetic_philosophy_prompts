Next week I'm leading a fifty-minute discussion section for a second-year cognitive science course on animal minds, and my job is to introduce the students to Daniel Dennett's tower of generate-and-test — his distinction between Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures, developed in Darwin's Dangerous Idea and Kinds of Minds. The audience is twenty students with a term of intro psychology but no philosophy of mind; their only prior exposure to Skinner is the caricature version (levers, pellets, "we're all just conditioned"), and several of them, I suspect, will want the taxonomy to be a Great Chain of Being with amoebas at the bottom and humans at the top — precisely what Dennett says he wants to avoid, since for him the categories pick out design strategies that can be stacked or added rather than rungs a species climbs. I don't want you to explain the taxonomy to me; I understand it. I want a plan for explaining it.

Specifically: what has to come first in the sequence and why — for instance, whether the framing move about competence without comprehension needs to be in place before the Popperian level can make sense, or whether that's a detour I can't afford in fifty minutes; and what single example should carry the whole session. Dennett's own touchstone is the Popperian thought that an organism can let its hypotheses die in its stead, which I find genuinely illuminating but fear my students will find too abstract. I've been toying with a replacement from machine learning: a model-free reinforcement learning agent that burns through millions of trial-and-error failures in simulation, where each failure costs nothing. Part of me thinks this is perfect — the training environment is functionally a place where hypotheses die for free — but another part worries it muddies the very distinction it's meant to illustrate, since the agent itself never does the generating-and-testing in the way the taxonomy demands, and I can't decide whether that subtlety is a feature of the example or a bug. If you think it's a bug, propose what the carrying example must have (and must lack) instead, and say why.

Beyond the sequencing and the example, I want a misconception map: the errors this audience will predictably form, in the order they'll form them, and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off. The ones I anticipate are (a) reading the tower as a phylogenetic ladder that sorts species rather than architectures, so that a dog is "a Skinnerian" full stop; (b) assuming Skinnerian learning requires an external trainer dispensing rewards, rather than anything that implements differential reinforcement; (c) collapsing the Gregorian level into mere tool use, which crows and chimps would then satisfy; and (d) taking Popperian foresight to require conscious deliberation or something like literal hypothesis-formation, so that the category ends up empty for animals. If there are misconceptions I'm not anticipating, name them. Finally, give me one question I can put to the section at the end that would reveal whether they've grasped the taxonomy rather than merely memorized its rungs — I'd like it to force them to apply the categories to a case they haven't seen — and tell me what I should openly decline to cover at this level (I'm guessing the question of whether the tower has moral implications, and where language fits, are both traps) and how to say so in a way that reads as principled rather than evasive.
