I am preparing a seminar paper on relational conceptions of personhood and have become stuck on a passage from Hegel's Phenomenology of Spirit (1807), in the Baillie translation. In §178, Hegel writes:

"SELF-CONSCIOUSNESS exists in itself and for itself, in that, and by the fact that it exists for another self-consciousness; that is to say, it is only by being acknowledged or 'recognized'."

And later, at the end of §184, he summarises the outcome of the process: "They recognize themselves as mutually recognizing one another."

I have two questions. First, what exactly is the claim in §178? In particular, I cannot work out whether the dependence on being acknowledged is meant to be constitutive (there is no fully developed self-consciousness at all until recognition occurs) or merely developmental (self-consciousness exists beforehand but only comes to know itself through the other). The distinction between existing "in itself" and "for itself" seems crucial, but the prose defeats me. Second, I want to relate this to the Southern African idea captured by the maxim that a person is a person through other persons. Are these the same claim about personhood being constituted by others, or do they come apart — for instance, because Hegel's account arrives at mutual recognition only through a life-and-death struggle and the unequal relation of master and bondsman, whereas the Ubuntu tradition does not seem to make conflict the route to personhood? Please explain the passage and then assess whether the comparison holds.
