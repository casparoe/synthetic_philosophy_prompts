I am preparing a seminar segment on the Third Man argument in Plato's Parmenides, and I want to close it with a verdict rather than a survey. Below are five strategies for blocking the regress, sketched in my own words; I have deliberately left the scholars' names off, and I do not want them supplied back to me either — the reasoning is what I am after. Rank the five strategies from strongest to weakest as replies to the regress, where "strongest" means the one that stops the regress at the least cost to the theory of Forms as the middle dialogues actually need it, and not merely the one that is easiest to assert. Give exactly five numbered items, one per strategy, each at most two sentences: within that space, state where the strategy ranks, restate it in a few words so I can check we have the same one in view, and give the decisive reason it lands there. No introduction and no conclusion.

The five strategies:

(1) The Form of Largeness is not itself large. The argument's first step quietly converts a property into a further thing that possesses that property; refuse the conversion and the regress never gets started.

(2) The Form is large, but not in the way its instances are: the instances are large by partaking, while the Form is what it is in itself, so the shared predicate that would force a new Form never applies across the two levels at once.

(3) The many large things are not made large by a further entity standing over and above them but by a character present in them; since there is no "one over and above the many" to serve as the extra individual the regress requires, it is the second premise, not the first, that should be surrendered.

(4) Grant the argument. It shows not that there are no Forms, but that Forms cannot be the self-predicating, separately existing perfect instances that the Parmenides' premises make them out to be; the theory survives with Forms as principles of structure, and the regress is a lesson about how not to read the middle dialogues.

(5) The trouble lies in the comparison step: a further Form is needed only when the Form is grouped with its instances as though it were one more instance of the same kind, and conflating "one over many" with "one apart from the many" is exactly what the separation of the intelligible from the sensible rules out.
