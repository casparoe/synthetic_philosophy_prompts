I've been reading a popular book about the economics of the far future, and one chapter sums up the dispute over discounting like this:

"Ramsey settled the ethical side in 1928: valuing the wellbeing of future people less simply because it arrives later is a failure of imagination, not a principle of impartiality, so the rate at which we discount future utility should be zero. The problem is that actual people are impatient, and it was later established that only one shape of discounting is internally consistent — exponential discounting at a constant rate — which is roughly what markets reveal when you compare returns across different horizons. With the ethics settled and the functional form fixed, the remaining debate is therefore empirical: which number to plug in. That is what the fight over climate policy really comes down to. On Ramsey's view, weighing the present against the future at zero means almost any sacrifice today is worth making to spare even distant generations harm, and the aggressive findings of some cost-benefit analyses follow directly. Critics such as Nordhaus reply that future people will be much richer than we are, so transferring resources from a poorer present to a wealthier future is perverse; but that misses the point, since Ramsey's impartiality principle applies no matter how rich future people turn out to be. The critics have not really answered him."
