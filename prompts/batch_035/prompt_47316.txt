A friend of mine, a mathematics graduate, explained the Pasadena paradox to me over coffee, and I'm inclined to believe him, but a couple of things he said don't quite hang together and I don't have the background to locate the problem. Here is his account, as close to word for word as I can reconstruct it:

"You know the St Petersburg game, where a fair coin is flipped until it lands heads and the payoff doubles with each additional flip, so the expected value comes out infinite even though you're almost certain to win very little? The Pasadena game is the sequel, and it's worse. Same setup: flip a fair coin until the first heads, on flip n. But now the payoff is 2 to the nth power, divided by n — and the sign alternates. You win that amount if heads first appears on an odd-numbered flip, and you lose it if heads first appears on an even-numbered flip. So the expected value is the alternating harmonic series: one, minus a half, plus a third, minus a quarter, and so on. That series diverges — the partial sums oscillate and never settle on anything. And because the series never settles, you can add the terms in a different order and get any total you like; Riemann proved that for series of this kind. So the game's expected value is undefined rather than merely large, and since decision theory tells you to choose the option with the highest expected value, decision theory is refuted. A theory of rational choice that can go completely silent on a perfectly well-defined question has failed."
