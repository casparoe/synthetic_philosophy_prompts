so i've been following mech interp stuff for a while (yes I'm one of those people who reads the LessWrong interpretability posts instead of sleeping) and there's this analogy absolutely everyone in the field leans on, and I want to know if it can actually carry the weight being put on it. The analogy is: features are concepts. You train a sparse autoencoder on the activations, it finds directions, some of them respond to things we recognise, and suddenly everyone talks as if the network has a mind full of concepts -- the famous case is the Golden Gate Bridge feature, you crank it up and the model will describe literally anything as being made of bridges. People say things like the model "has a concept of the Golden Gate Bridge the way you or I do." Fine as shorthand maybe, but here's the thing: the analogy is doing real argumentative work. Folks use it to conclude that alignment research is literally psychology, that we are reading the model's beliefs off its internal states, that deception worries are worries about *beliefs* it has and hides. That's a strong realist claim about what SAEs find, not just a cute way of talking.

But when I poke at it, the mapping feels wobbly. Human concepts are norm-governed and inferentially integrated -- my concept of *chair* isn't a direction anywhere, it's something that gets deployed in reasoning, can be applied to novel cases, can be criticised as a misapplication. A "feature" is a direction in activation space that happens to make the residual stream more linearly decodable. Different SAE dictionaries trained on the same model find different features; nothing guarantees there's one privileged decomposition. And there's the superposition thing -- the network may not be storing anything in the basis the SAE untangles at all, in which case "the feature" is partly *our* grid laid over the model, which is exactly what the realist about features has to deny. Meanwhile the pro-realist side has a reply I find uncomfortable: our own concepts might also just be whatever decomposition best predicts and controls behaviour, and if a steering vector reliably makes the model talk about bridges, in what sense is that not a concept doing causal work?

So audit the analogy for me. Give me the actual correspondence table -- what plays the role of the concept, the thought, having a belief vs merely using the representation, attention, misapplication of a concept, whatever maps and whatever has no counterpart on either side. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from the ones that don't -- I don't care that features aren't conscious or whatever, I care whether the "interp is psychology and the model literally has beliefs" conclusion survives. Is the analogy load-bearing for that conclusion or is there a leaner version that survives (maybe something like: representations yes, but only in the way a thermostat represents temperature, scaled up)? And if the feature-concept analogy is really broken, tell me what the better analogy actually is -- features as... instruments? coordinates in a compression scheme? I genuinely want to know what the defensible version of the realist position is, and what it costs to hold it.
