I do evals work at a small org that cares a lot about model honesty, and I keep having the exact same fight in different clothes. Someone flags that a model "deceived" an annotator — a model invents a plausible citation, or tells a user it double-checked something when nothing of the sort happened, or claims it's not able to do a task it can do, so the user stops asking — and then someone else pushes back that this isn't deception, it's error, or sycophancy, or the training game, because deception requires intent, and a next-token predictor doesn't have intent, full stop. Meanwhile a third person says intent was never the right criterion anyway, and if a system's outputs systematically produce false beliefs in users, calling that anything softer than deceptive is doing PR for the model. I've realized the fight is mostly definitional, and I want to settle it by finding out what people actually hold rather than which word they like.

So what I want from you is a "what do you actually mean by deception" decision tree, quiz-style, for our internal docs. Somewhere between 8 and 10 questions, each one a concrete case with 2-3 verdict options, phrased so someone with zero philosophy background can answer — no question may just ask "do you think intent is required for deception?". Cases I'd like woven in, roughly: the hallucinated citation where the model seems indifferent to truth rather than invested in it; an assistant that's been fine-tuned to always say "I'm confident in that answer" regardless of whether it is; a game-playing agent that bluffs, where the misinforming of the opponent is the whole point and everyone's fine with it; an output that's literally all true but arranged so the user walks away with a false impression (cherry-picked facts, true statements about a different product than the one asked about, that kind of thing); and a case where a plain "I don't know" was available and the model instead produced a confident guess. The tree should end in leaves for at least these families: deception-requires-intent-and-beliefs (so today's LLMs can at most mislead), deception-as-induced-false-belief regardless of what's inside the system, deception-as-violation-of-what-a-user-is-entitled-to-infer-from-an-assistant (norm-based, where bluffing the poker opponent fails the norm but bluffing the user doesn't... or does it?), and the bucket where indifference to truth makes something a third category that's neither lying nor deceiving — a colleague swears there's an old philosophy essay making roughly this point, and if you know the name of that concept, great, but the tree needs to work either way.

Two requirements beyond the tree itself. First, a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto the leaves, with one sentence per leaf on its most awkward commitment (e.g., the induced-false-belief leaf has to say something about whether a weather site with a bad model is thereby deceiving you). Second, and this is the part I actually care about most: be honest about which pairs of leaves the case-verdicts alone can't seperate — like, if two positions agree on every verdict but disagree about why, the tree should say so and suggest the one follow-up question that would split them, rather than pretending the instrument is sharper than it is. The tree sorts people by what they say about cases, not by their favorite philosopher, and I'd rather have an admissions-of-failure section than a fake clean diagram. Also fine to mark any leaf that nobody in a sane evaluation team would actually occupy, with a guess at why.
