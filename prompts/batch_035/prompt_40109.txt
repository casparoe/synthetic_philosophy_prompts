I'm a retired microbiologist who, since leaving the bench, has taken up philosophy of science and decision theory with something between amateur enthusiasm and mid-life seriousness. My retirement reading group recently worked through material on what is sometimes called the unilateralist's curse: the phenomenon whereby, if each member of a group of competent judges has some independent chance of wrongly concluding that a harmful action is permissible, the probability that the action gets taken by at least one member rises steeply with the size of the group — even when every individual is more likely than not to judge correctly. We met it in the context of publishing dual-use biological research, where the willingness of a single journal to publish suffices to release the risk to everyone.

The discussion has become unexpectedly practical. I sit on the council of our regional natural history society — roughly 150 members, with a twelve-member governing council — and we are quarrelling over whether to publish, in our quarterly bulletin, the precise coordinates of a rare orchid colony and an endangered bat roost discovered through our citizen-science surveys. The data would be genuinely useful to conservation researchers and fellow naturalists, but it could equally invite collectors and vandals. Under our bylaws, any single council member may submit material for publication subject only to a light editorial check, so the standing arrangement is effectively unilateral. One colleague, who took the same reading as I did, has proposed that sensitive location data henceforth require a supermajority vote of the council before publication — her argument being that the unilateralist's curse makes our current default irrational. Another colleague objects that this cures the disease by inducing the opposite one: a rule under which a single mistaken or overly timid objector can block publication simply relocates the problem, and it is not obvious that the epistemic errors run asymmetrically in the direction of over-publication.

Please describe the most important implications of the unilateralist's curse for how our society should structure this class of decisions, and for small volunteer organizations generally, taking both of my colleagues' positions seriously along the way. To help me carry your analysis back to the council, I would like the substance of your answer presented as a table with the following columns: "Implication" (a concise statement of the consequence you draw); "Mechanism" (why it follows from the structure of the curse rather than from generic caution about group decisions); "Prescription" (what this would concretely mean for our council's procedures); and "Caveat" (the strongest counter-consideration, including where relevant the objection that a blocking rule empowers single mistaken objectors). Please include a final row giving your overall verdict on whether the supermajority proposal is sound, or whether some intermediate arrangement — for example, a rule requiring the objecting faction to meet some evidential burden, or a graded decision procedure rather than a binary one — would better balance the errors. Feel free to add a paragraph of prose after the table summarizing your recommendation.
