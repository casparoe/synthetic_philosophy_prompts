I teach intro ethics at a community college — 24 students, mostly non-majors, one 75-minute session. We just finished the consequentialism/deontology unit, and next week I want to run an in-class game around the paradox of deontology, the one Scheffler pressed: if each of a bunch of agents refuses to actively inflict a small harm in order to prevent a bigger harm that would otherwise happen, and everybody refuses, the world ends up with a lot more harm in it than if everyone had been willing to dirty their hands. Constraints that look admirable one agent at a time look terrible in the aggregate, and the deontologist either has to bite the bullet ("the extra harm isn't my doing") or quietly start counting other people's harms — the exact thing she refused to do.

Here's roughly what I have in mind, and I want you to turn it into something runnable. A points game where each student is an agent who can either (a) press a button that actively docks everyone a small number of points but cancels a much bigger loss that would otherwise occur through nobody's choice, or (b) sit tight, in which case the big loss lands unless somebody else presses. Consequentialists press, deontologists sit, and the interesting rounds are the ones where nobody presses and everyone eats the big loss — and the rounds where one kid presses every time and the rest of the class free-rides on his dirty hands. I want multiple rounds with rotating roles, including a phase where half the room is forbidden by rule from pressing (they're the constrained agents) and half isn't, so the constrained half gets to watch themselves being used. And a final phase where they get to talk before the round: can they collectively agree on a policy, like everyone takes turns pressing? Does that feel like a rescue of deontology or a concession to consequentialism? That last bit is where I think the real philosophical action is, but I'm honestly not sure a classroom game can carry it.

Practical stuff: no real money, no apps, materials limited to index cards and a whiteboard, and it has to work with 24 students of whom maybe 6 will voluntarily speak in a debrief. The paradox should reach them through the scoreboard before I say anything — I dont want to lecture up front. The debrief should map specific moments in the game onto specific parts of the problem: whose harms counted and whose didn't, whether the doing/allowing distinction actually showed up in their choices or only in their excuses afterwards, whether free-riding on someone else's button-press felt different from pressing it yourself. And tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — my guess is one student presses immediatly every round just to see what happens, and I need a plan for that that doesn't kill the exercise.

One more thing, and I mean it: if you think the game as I've sketched it is rigged — that my payoff structure already smuggles in the consequentialist frame, or that the free-riding rounds show something different from what I think they show — say so straight out. Don't just design around my framing and hand me a fun activity that quietly begs the question. Same if you think the last phase flat out can't work with non-majors in 75 minutes and I should cut it and save it for a written exercise.
