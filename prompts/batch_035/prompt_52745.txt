For a class project, each student in my twelfth-grade philosophy elective (sixteen of us, one 50-minute session each) has to run a discussion on a topic drawn from a hat; mine is whether it is better to save five lives than one. I've read that some philosophers deny that the larger number, just as such, gives you a reason to save the many, and I find that strange but harder to dismiss than I expected. I don't think my classmates will feel the argument if I just present it, so I'd like an activity instead. Design a game with small real stakes (candy, bonus points, or something else that doesn't cost actual money) in which participants make a series of forced rescue choices between a group and an individual, where the setup at first gives them reasons to save the one that have nothing to do with numbers — the one has a name, a face, some special claim on the chooser — and then removes those reasons one at a time, until the only difference left is the number itself. I want to see whether their choices keep tracking the things they cited or quietly start tracking the count. Then design a debrief that sorts the group's actual decisions into three positions: that numbers count directly, that numbers count only through something else (fairness to each person, or each person's equal claim), and that numbers don't count at all; I'd especially like to see whether anyone arrives on their own at the idea of flipping a coin between the two sides, and whether the rest find that acceptable or insulting. Please don't have me name any philosopher or paper until the very end. Finally, tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — people treating it as a joke, or saying "save the five" because that's the answer you're supposed to give — and how I should handle each problem.
