hey, so I'm putting together material for a grad seminar I teach on reasons and rationality (sort of a philosophy of action / metaethics hybrid), and I need a good essay prompt for the unit on Dennett's free-floating rationales. You're going to write the essay for me. Take the role of a professional philosopher who works on teleology, normativity, and the philosophy of biology -- someone comfortable with both the metaphysics and the semantics of "because" statements. Before you write anything, spend a moment brainstorming the argumentative terrain: sketch the main positions, pick the strongest version of each, THEN commit to writing the essay itself.

The topic: are free-floating rationales really reasons? Dennett's thought is that reasons can exist before (and independent of) any reasoner who grasps them. The bird's wing has a rationale -- it's shaped the way it is because that shape solves an aerodynamic problem -- long before any aerodynamicist existed to articulate why. The rationale isn't in the bird's head, isn't in anyone's head, and isn't supernatural; it's just there, discoverable in advance of being thought. So far so slogan. The question I want you to actually litigate is whether this is a genuine ontological category or a clever compression. Critics basically say it's the as-if move wearing a trench coat: calling the wing's shape a "rationale" is just a shorthand for the selection history that produced it, and shorthand doesn't become a reason just because it's useful. Reasons, on this view, are normative through and through -- they can be good or bad reasons, they can fail to justify what they're supposed to justify -- and nothing that can't be wrong for anyone can have a reason at all. No reasoner, no normativity, no reasons.

To make this concrete, I want you to work through two cases of your own choosing that aren't the standard examples (no bird wings, no cuckoo chicks): one case where the "rationale" is arguably non-biological (grammar before grammarians? common-law doctrines that operate purposively before any judge says so? economic structures like prices carrying information nobody computed?), and one case where things get weird for the view -- I'm thinking something like vestigial traits whose original rationale has lapsed, or exaptations where the rationale we'd now cite isn't the one that explains the trait's origin. The vestigial case is where I suspect the view either earns its keep or dies: if the wing's rationale survives the wing's usefulness, rationales look spooky; if it doesn't, it's hard to see what's left of them beyond selection history.

And here's the twist I've been chewing on and want you to engage with seriously: suppose we grant free-floating rationales exist in nature. Does that give us a route to reasons in artifacts and even AI systems that don't depend on anyone's intentions? If a selection process -- natural, cultural, or market -- is enough to float a rationale, then maybe a trained model has reasons it's not aware of in the same sense the wing does, and maybe the whole debate about whether AI systems "really" have reasons was misframed. I'm honestly not sure whether that's a profound extension or me sneaking anthropomorphism back in through the side door, and I want the essay to take a real position on it, not just gesture at it in the conclusion.
