I am the treasurer of my school's student council, and I chair the committee that decides how to spend roughly $7,000 a year raised from concessions, dances, and a small annual grant from the alumni association. Our disagreement is never about arithmetic; it is about one question, and I want a procedure for it. The question is how much a benefit to future students counts, simply because it arrives later. One faction wants to spend nearly everything now — pep rallies, subsidized prom tickets, a pizza day each term — on the grounds that the students who elected us are here now, and students in ten years never voted for us and will never thank us. Another faction wants to build a permanent fund, so that in six or seven years there is enough to replace the auditorium sound system, which everyone agrees is dying, without passing the hat. In my economics class we discussed the idea, which I associate with a philosopher named Parfit though I may be misremembering the attribution, that discounting a benefit just because it lies further in the future is irrational in the same way caring less about a pain because it happens on Thursday rather than Tuesday would be — what I gather is called pure time preference — while discounting for the possibility that a future benefit never materializes (the fund gets raided, the school closes the auditorium) is perfectly legitimate. We also touched on the experimental finding that people prefer smaller-sooner rewards in the moment and regret it later, and on the complication that our future beneficiaries are not the same people as our current ones: the students who will enjoy the new sound system do not exist yet, and some of my colleagues think a benefit to someone who does not exist is a strange thing to trade against a benefit to a person standing in front of you. Please treat my descriptions of the ideas, not my attributions, as the thing to work from; I am not asking for sources.

What I want from you is a decision procedure the committee can actually run on a spending proposal in about ten minutes at our monthly meeting: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops with a no; and where it hands the decision back to a human because the question is one of values that no checklist can settle. It has to be usable by twelve students with no economics training, and it has to produce a written rationale of a few sentences, because our faculty advisor must sign off on anything over $500 and will not accept "the committee just felt that way" — though, honestly, on some proposals that will be the truth, and I would rather the procedure say so than pretend otherwise. Please do not give me a menu of options; make definite choices and defend them — pick a specific treatment of time and uncertainty, decide whether future students count the same as present ones, and commit, even though I can tell you now that whatever you decide, half my committee will think it is obviously wrong. Then run the procedure on three proposals: $900 for a spring carnival this year; $650 a year into an equipment fund targeting the sound-system replacement around year seven; and $1,200 taken from that fund now for a one-time graduation event. At least one of these should come out the way I personally do not want it to — I will confess I want the carnival — and I would like you to show that the procedure reaches that result anyway, plus a closing note on where the procedure will misfire, especially if a future committee tries to use it to justify anything whatsoever by calling it an investment in students not yet born.
