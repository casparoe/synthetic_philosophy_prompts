I'm teaching a second-year seminar on collective choice this term — 14 students, one 80-minute session, a room with movable chairs. We've just done Condorcet cycles and Arrow. Next I want them to feel Sen's liberal paradox before anyone has heard its name: nothing about Sen, Paretian liberalism, or any impossibility result gets said by me until the debrief, and ideally the punchline comes out of a student's mouth first.

Here's the scenario I want staged, and I need its preference structure kept exactly as I give it, because the whole thing lives or dies on it. Two characters, Milo and Juno, the only two employees of a tiny firm. The boss has one spare ticket to the industry gala. It can go to Milo, go to Juno, or expire unused — those are the only three outcomes, everbody knows this from the start. Milo's strict preferences: ticket expires, then Milo goes, then Juno goes. He thinks the gala circuit is corrosive nonsense, so best if nobody goes; but if the ticket is going to be used at all, he'd rather take the hit himself than watch Juno get swallowed by that world. Juno's strict preferences: Milo goes, then Juno goes, then ticket expires. She'd enjoy going well enough, but she'd enjoy Milo being dragged to it even more. Never show these as a ranking or a table — the class has to work them out from what the characters do and say. That means the character briefs have to make each ordering come off as locally reasonable, and thats a real writing job, especially Milo's middle term.

Roughly what I want to happen: early on, the room ratifies two rules — some version of "if every single one of us prefers X to Y, then X beats Y, full stop" and "what a person does with their own evening is their own business." I want the students to propose and vote on these themselves, so they feel like the rooms rules and not mine; you work out how to get there without me lecturing. Then the trap. However the decision gets run, the class should end up landing on the outcome where Juno goes and Milo stays home — and then discover that every single person in the room, Milo included, prefers a different available outcome (Milo goes, Juno stays home), and that the only route to that better outcome is ordering Milo to go, which breaks a rule they just ratified with their own votes. The forced choice should be real: the RSVP is due before the end of class, so they cant park the decision, and after they commit I want them to audit which of their two rules the final answer violated.

Deliverables: rules and materials (nothing that needs real money), a minute-by-minute timeline, the cast — I'm guessing two role-players plus everyone else as the deciding body, but you structure it — a way of eliciting the character preferences without ever printing them, and then the debrief. The debrief should finally name Sen and the result, map each moment of the game onto the formal conditions (which ratified rule was which condition), and then make them seriously weigh the escape hatches — I especially want real time spent on Sen's own suggestion that a unanimity built on nosy preferences about other people's lives shouldn't count the way normal Pareto unanimity does, versus the alternative of weakening or making rights tradeable. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about which feature of the actual impossibility the game can't reproduce — the preferences here are frozen, nobody can bargain or waive anything, and I want the debrief to own that limitation instead of papering over it.
