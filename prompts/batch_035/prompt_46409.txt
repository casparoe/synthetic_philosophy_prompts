I'm a second-year doctoral student in economic history, and a question I used to regard as a museum piece has recently become my daily problem. My thesis concerns two neighbouring valleys with, as far as I can establish, effectively identical access to water power, comparable guild traditions, similar soils, and the same property law, one of which adopted mechanised cotton spinning in the 1860s while the other remained proto-industrial for another two generations. My supervisor thinks the difference is 'cultural' — a divergence in what counted locally as respectable economic conduct, who was trusted with credit, whether leaving one's father's trade was seen as betrayal or as initiative. My external examiner, I suspect, thinks this is a confession of ignorance dressed as an explanation. As I read for my field exam I've discovered the disagreement I've stumbled into is very old: it is essentially Weber's Protestant ethic controversy, still unresolved, with modern data.

Our economic history reading group has now been circling this for two terms, and the stall is striking. The culture side holds that values can be genuinely causal: Weber's claim, as I understand it, was not that Calvinists saved more capital, but that a religiously rooted asceticism redefined profit-seeking and disciplined labour as morally obligatory rather than merely permitted, and that this ethos was plausibly decisive where capitalism had not yet taken root on its own. The materialist side answers that values are endogenous — people adopt the ethics that suit their livelihoods — so citing culture as a cause runs in a circle; or that we only ever detect a 'culture' after we already know the economic outcome, which makes the appeal unfalsifiable, mere naming rather than explaining. A third participant, a hardcore microeconomist, adds a methodological individualist complaint: cultures don't act, only people do, so 'culture caused it' is at best a placeholder for a mechanism nobody has given. Everyone has read the same case studies, nobody has moved an inch, and the debate has started to feel less like a disagreement about the Valais or about seventeenth-century Württemberg and more like a standoff about what an explanation must supply. My working hypothesis — and I offer it only as a hypothesis — is that the sides mean different things by 'cause': one wants something that could have gone otherwise (a difference-maker), the other wants a set of sufficient initial conditions, and each keeps refuting the other's claim as though it were their own kind.

Is that diagnosis right, or is there a hidden substantive question underneath — perhaps an empirical one about identification that better data on, say, religiously mixed villages could in principle settle, or perhaps a genuinely different question about persistence versus origins (why capitalism survived where it started may not be the same question as why it started)? I'd like you to engage the positions as I've given them rather than survey the literature, tell me what kind of disagreement this really is — verbal, methodological, empirical-in-principle, or two questions travelling under one name — and be honest about what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a sharper concept of what culture-as-cause would even be, a natural experiment, or nothing short of a change of question. Please write your answer in British English.
