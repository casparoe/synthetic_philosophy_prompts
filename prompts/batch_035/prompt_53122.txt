I'm a philosophy PhD student who has just started a part-time internship with a firm that certifies the safety architectures of autonomous warehouse vehicles, and the weekly case clinic run by my supervisor has abruptly become relevant to my day job. In the clinic she rules on causation cases, and her track record so far, as best I can reconstruct it, is this. On the ordinary pain-and-wincing case she granted mental causation, but with the gloss that the pain just is the neural process under a coarser description — one causal chain, not two, and nothing left over at the mental level. On the marksmen case, where two independent shots were each sufficient for the death, she said no single decision caused it — remove either and the death stays — and added that she tolerates such overdetermination only as coincidence, since overdetermination by distinct sufficient causes becoming routine is something she refuses to accept. On the pigeon that pecks a picture, she ruled that the picture's being of a bee, not the specific pixel array, was the cause, because the array varied across the training slides while only the bee-ness did any work; and she rejected the evenness of the crowd as a cause of the home win, on the ground that nothing in the system was organized around that property.

Next week's case is mine to bring, and it comes straight from the internship. Our forklifts stop twice on every occasion: the planner module's representation of an obstacle ahead issues the brake command, and a hardwired interlock, wired separately from the same sensors, issues the same command; either alone would brake the vehicle, and this happens on every single stop, all day, by design. The question the seminar will vote on is whether the planner's representation causes the stopping. My office mates disagree about what she will say. One expects her to grant it the way she granted the pain — the representation just is the planner hardware coarsely, and the interlock is ordinary engineering redundancy of the kind built into everything. The other expects her to apply her own marksmen principle: this is precisely overdetermination by distinct sufficient causes gone routine, so she must break the tie, and, caring about sufficiency, she will keep the physical story and let the representational one ride along without doing anything.

Before I place my bet, I want the analysis done properly: what are the candidate rules that fit her entire track record, what does each predict for my case, which is the best guess and why, and what further case, if I could put one more to her, would most cleanly separate the readings? Please keep your reply under 500 words; I'm less interested in the prediction itself than in seeing the rules carved so that the disagreement between my office mates becomes tractable.
