I run the data desk at a mid-sized news organization that covers science and public policy, and I want a written procedure my reporters can actually follow before we publish any story whose centerpiece is a comparison of rates, proportions, or averages between two groups. The recurring failure mode is Simpson's paradox and its milder cousins: a comparison that holds in the aggregate reverses, or shrinks toward zero, once the data are broken out along some third variable, and we find out from an angry statistician or a rival outlet two days later. None of my five reporters is trained in statistics beyond a college course, we typically have hours rather than days between receiving a dataset and publishing, we have no in-house methodologist, and our corrections policy means an error of this kind is genuinely costly to us. So the procedure has to be runnable by a smart non-specialist with a spreadsheet and no more than an hour: it should say what to check, in what order, what counts as passing each check, when disaggregation is mandatory before publication, and when disaggregation itself is the trap — for instance when the variable you would condition on is itself affected by the thing being studied, or when the disaggregating variable is a post-treatment quantity, or when the strata are simply too small to support any claim at all. It should mark honestly which steps are mechanical and which are judgment calls dressed up as arithmetic, and it should say where the procedure stops and hands the story to an outside statistician or to an editor, because the question has become one of policy framing rather than statistics.

Then run the procedure on two hypothetical stories so we can see how it behaves. First: a transit agency announces that a new bus-priority corridor has cut average commute times by six percent, and a reporter discovers that when the data are split by borough, average commute times actually rose slightly in both boroughs — the aggregate improvement appears to come entirely from riders shifting from the faster borough to the slower one. Second: our deputy editor has spent a month on an investigation showing that a county lead-abatement program reduced juvenile offenses in the aggregate, and a critic now disaggregates by neighborhood income and finds the effect gone or reversed in every income stratum — but income is precisely the variable through which the program selected which homes to remediate. I want the procedure applied to both, and I should warn you that at least one of these applications had better come out against what the desk will want to hear, because what I need to know is where the procedure defends us from our own enthusiasm rather than merely confirming it. Finally, say plainly what the procedure cannot settle: the cases where two statistically literate people would still disagree, and the point at which a reporter has to stop following steps and make a call.

End your response with a single machine-readable line summarizing the procedure's ruling on each case, using labels the procedure itself defines — for example, a last line beginning "Verdict:" and formatted roughly as "Verdict: CASE1=<label>; CASE2=<label>", so that I can scan a batch of these at a glance.
