Something's been bugging me about the many-worlds interpretation and I want to check whether I'm missing something. If every outcome of a quantum measurement happens in some branch, then the probability of each outcome is trivially one — it definitely happens somewhere — so the whole business of assigning probabilities looks like a category error. And the obvious patch, saying each branch is equally likely so a two-outcome measurement gives even odds, actually contradicts experiment: real QM gives you the Born rule, where probabilities track the squared amplitudes, and for unequal amplitudes equal branch-weighting just predicts the wrong numbers. So to me it looks like many-worlds is falsified either way — either probability becomes meaningless or you get the wrong probabilities — and yet serious physicists keep endorsing it. What gives?

To be clear, I dont want a textbook overview of MWI — I know the splitting picture and the usual decoherence handwave about how branches stop interfering. What I want is this: is the probability question a genuine open problem that people are actively working on, or did it get solved? I've seen references to decision-theoretic derivations of the Born rule (Deutsch and Wallace, I think) — do those actually count as a solution, or do they quietly build the answer into the setup, e.g. by assuming rational agents must care about branch weight in just the way the Born rule requires? And if it's a live problem, why doesn't it just sink the interpretation outright?

Please be honest about where the standard replies are weakest. Tell me what would change your verdict that MWI survives this — which argument or result would flip you — and where your own explanation is most likely to fail. Also feel free to say if my framing is off before you even start: I might just be equivocating between different senses of "probability" (chance vs. how much I should expect to see something). One more small thing that's nagging me: do branches even come in well-defined numbers, such that you could count them and say "equally likely" in the first place?
