I hold that interpersonal comparisons of utility are legitimate in principle and often achievable in practice: that claims like "this procedure relieves more suffering for her than it would cause for him" can be literally true or false, that we can sometimes know them with reasonable confidence, and that the discipline of economics committed a methodological overreaction in the interwar period when it declared such comparisons outside science. Tell me what would count against this position, with the objections ranked from fatal to merely awkward. I would expect the answer to consider at least: the classic worry that utility is revealed only through choice, and that no pattern of choices can ever compare two private scales; the fact that social choice theory was rebuilt on ordinal, non-comparable preferences and seems to have flourished without the comparisons, which would suggest they were dispensable; and the possibility that our actual instruments — verbal pain scales, empathy-based projections, symmetry assumptions about physiologically similar people — track something other than the cross-person comparisons they purport to make. I would also like an honest assessment of whether the position has been insulated by qualification: if every failed comparison is excused as "possible in principle but epistemically hard," has the view stopped risking anything, and is that a problem for a view of this kind? Note where defenders of the position would themselves concede that something counts against it, as opposed to merely having a rebuttal ready. Finally, please separate what is settled among people who know this literature from what is genuinely contested — for instance, whether the Robbins-era objection was a semantic point about unverifiability or a normative point about the state's license to compare — and do not present either reading of that episode as the consensus view.
