Construct a thought experiment about the grandfather paradox in which the metaphysical version of the paradox is stipulated away from the start, so that all of the real difficulty must relocate elsewhere. The setup: backward time travel is physically possible, and the universe runs on a single self-consistent timeline — no branching, no parallel histories, no exotic loops beyond the one the traveler creates. The standard contradiction (he kills his grandfather, so he is never born, so he never travels back) is therefore assumed unreachable, and the thought experiment must not try to reach it. Your job is to build the case so that the paradox survives anyway, as a problem about rational deliberation rather than about metaphysics.

Work to the following specifications. First, no fate-enforcing devices: whatever stops the murder, if anything does, must be an entirely ordinary chain of mundane causes — a missed connection, a rusty firing pin, a chance misidentification in a crowd — never a logical barrier, a guardian, or a psychosomatic compulsion. Second, the traveler must be rational and well-informed rather than reckless: he is to be an experienced researcher with access to records showing that every one of the handful of previous attempts by other travelers to prevent their own birth failed in some banal, statistically unremarkable way, and he must know this before setting out. Third, give him stakes that a normal person could compute: the trip is one-way and expensive, and he has exactly one reason to make it — a prudential reason strong enough that attempting the murder is rational unless his own evidence about its outcome defeats the plan. Fourth, keep the whole scenario to a paragraph or two, in a setting a reader can picture (a rented room above a shop in his grandfather's town, not a laboratory).

After the scenario, demonstrate that it meets each specification. Walk through what a causal decision theorist would say about whether he should pull the trigger, treating the decision as he would treat any other; then show how his evidence — the records of past failures, and above all his own existence and his knowledge of it — argues for a different verdict, and explain the structure of that argument: what it is evidence *about*, and why it cannot be dismissed as ordinary pessimism. Then identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work of relocating the paradox from metaphysics to epistemology, and describe what happens to the scenario if you remove that feature — specifically, whether the traveler who knows nothing of his genealogy or of past attempts faces anything more than an ordinary murder plot that happens to require a machine. Close with the strongest reply available to a branching-timeline theorist who denies the single-timeline stipulation: on their view, what should the traveler do, exactly which step of the self-consistency reasoning do they reject, and say honestly whether their reply defuses the deliberative paradox or merely refuses to enter the room where it lives.
