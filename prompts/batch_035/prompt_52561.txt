I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I keep getting assigned versions of the same story: whether we might be living in a simulation. Every few months a physicist announces the hypothesis is testable in principle, a tech founder funds something in that direction, and I call philosophers for comment, who mostly tell me the underlying question was either answered or dissolved decades ago. Writing these pieces has made me realize I'm standing on top of an older argument that, as far as I can tell, has not moved in forty years: brain-in-a-vat skepticism, the scenario Descartes dressed up as a demon and that Putnam, in the early eighties, tried to defuse with the argument that a lifelong vat-brain's word 'vat' couldn't refer to vats, so the skeptic's hypothesis could never be truly stated by the very brain it is about. Since then the field has produced Moorean appeals to common sense, contextualist accounts on which 'knows' shifts with conversational stakes, reliabilist views on which knowledge never required ruling out skeptical alternatives in the first place, and a few revisionists who concede the skeptic the word 'knowledge' and carry on. Nobody has changed camps.

I want a diagnosis of this impasse rather than a verdict on it, because a verdict is exactly what I do not need for the long-form piece I'm planning. Concretely, the skeptic's side says: no possible experience distinguishes the vat scenario from ordinary life; knowledge requires being able to rule out relevant alternatives; so absent an argument that the vat is not a relevant alternative, we know very little. The anti-skeptical side says one of: the skeptic's claim cannot be coherently expressed by anyone it threatens (Putnam's line); knowing does not require what the skeptic demands, so the demand was inflated from the start; or the standards shift with context and the skeptic exploits the shift. My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the dispute is really about the burden of proof and what would even count as an answer — the skeptic constructs a challenge that by design cannot be met from inside the situation it describes, and the two camps disagree over whether that unanswerability is a discovery about our epistemic position or a sign that no question was asked. But I also suspect something messier is going on: the new public discourse about simulation treats the scenario as an empirical hypothesis about which physics and computing might eventually say something, while philosophers treat it as a conceptual possibility about which no experiment could say anything, which suggests the stalled debate may be two different questions traveling under one heading — one modal or semantic, one about whether the scenario could ever matter for how we live or what we should do.

So: is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about the word 'knowledge', or two research programs answering different questions under a single name? Diagnose it using what each side actually says, not as a survey but as evidence for the diagnosis, and tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result, some kind of convergence between the philosophers and the physicists and technologists who keep re-litigating the scenario as though it were new, or an argument that the impasse should simply be expected and why. I'd also like to know whether you think the current public moment is a genuine natural experiment on the dispute or just noise, and why. Please do not give me a bibliography or recommended reading; I want the reasoning, and I'll source the piece myself.
