I write novels — midlist literary-ish stuff, the kind with a moral dilemma on the back cover — and lately I've gotten obsessed with philosophical thought experiments, because I've slowly realized they're my job done by other means. Before I structure my next book around that idea I want someone to check whether my beliefs about all this actually hang together. If there's a crack I'd rather find it now than in the reviews.

Here's my working set:

1. A thought experiment and a piece of fiction work by the same mechanism: you get the reader to concretely simulate a scenario and then report — or just feel — their response. Philosophy just pretends otherwise.
2. Vividness is legitimate. If I write the guy on the footbridge well enough that the reader physically feels the shove, their reaction is real data about them. Making the scene vivid isn't cheating, it's the method.
3. But I also believe the experimental philosophy results, at least as I understand them: run the same dilemma with different framing, different order, different demographic groups, and the intuitions flip. And evidence that flips when you re-stage the scene isn't evidence.
4. Some of the classic cases are unfilmable. I genuinely cannot visualize Mary knowing every physical fact about color while having never seen red, and I can't visualize a world physically identical to ours with nobody home inside the people. My claim: a case you can't render concretely can't elicit an intuition at all — there's nothing there to respond to. Those pumps pump nothing.
5. And yet my reaction to the teleporter case — step in the booth, scanned, destroyed, replica walks out the other side — feels rock solid to me. If I frame it as death-plus-copy I'm horrified; frame it as instant travel and I'm fine. I would honestly trust the horrified version in an argument.
6. I'm aware of the Dennett-style complaint that intuition pumps get rigged by tendentious details smuggled into the setup, and I rig scenes for a living — it's called characterization. That feels like it ought to be hypocracy but I don't think it is, and I'd like someone to check that for me.

The plan for the book itself: a sequence of escalating cases — the protagonist's memories get replaced gradually, then all at once, then there's a copy of her walking around — and the readers shifting sympathy IS the argument. The novel as one long pump, roughly 90,000 words of it.

So: which of these actually fight each other, which only look like they do, and how bad is each fight? For the real ones, tell me what has to give and what giving it up costs. And if the whole project rests on something confused — say (5) and (3) can't both be true and the book collapses with them — say so plainly, don't soften it for my sake. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, no throat-clearing first; I get enough of that from my agent.
