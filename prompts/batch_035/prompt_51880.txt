I've been persuaded by aggregate consequentialism: when a huge number of people each suffer a tiny harm, the total can outweigh a single severe harm, and this seems to follow from the same additive structure that makes me care about each person's suffering in the first place. My local disaster-relief committee has just started allocating a limited budget between preventing a handful of catastrophic injuries and preventing widespread minor ailments, and I find myself actually committed to this view in practice, not just in seminar. Before I defend it in the committee's report, I want its exposure mapped honestly. Tell me what would count against it: the cases and intuitions it must absorb (the speck-versus-torture case is only the most famous), the results it would have to endorse elsewhere — in population ethics, in interpersonal comparisons, in how it treats probability and risk — and where a committed aggregator would concede real trouble versus bite the bullet. Also tell me whether the escape routes available to me (lexical orderings, discontinuous thresholds, agent-relative prerogatives) save the core view or quietly abandon it, and whether the view has been so qualified that it no longer risks anything. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which one should move me most if I want to remain defensible in that committee room rather than just consistent in the abstract.
