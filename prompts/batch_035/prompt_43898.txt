I spent the better part of four decades as an evolutionary biologist, most of it on coevolutionary dynamics in host–parasite systems — escalating adaptations and counter-adaptations that consume enormous metabolic budgets without moving either party's net fitness an inch. Since retiring I have been reading into philosophy and adjacent literatures, and I keep encountering an analogy that trades on exactly the phenomena I spent my career on. In the rationalist writing on coordination failures — most prominently Scott Alexander's 2014 essay "Meditations on Moloch" — competitive races to the bottom in human society (price wars, arms races, scholarly publishing, the attention economy) are analyzed as evolutionary arms races: a blind optimizing process that selects for whatever out-competes, regardless of what any participant values, so that the peacock's tail of one sector becomes the burden of all, and every local adaptation degrades the commons. Moloch, in this idiom, is natural selection personified. The analogy is then used to draw a conclusion I want examined on its merits rather than its rhetoric: that multipolar competition inevitably grinds away everything not serving the competition itself, and that the only escape is a singleton — some unified coordinating authority that does to social competition what an organism does to competition among its own cells.

I am in no position to audit this alone, because the analogy is my home turf used against me, so to speak. Please do a careful accounting. First, the correspondence table: in the mapping from the source domain (biological evolution) to the target (human social competition), what plays the role of the organism, the gene, the lineage, fitness, mutation, drift, the fitness landscape, and the Red Queen treadmill? Second, where the mapping breaks. I have my own candidates, but I want yours with reasons: evolved entities lack foresight while firms, states, and academics bargain, merge, and legislate; acquired characteristics are not inherited biologically but are transmitted culturally; selection has no mechanism for the deliberate institutional design that human agents attempt. Please then sort the disanalogies into those that matter for the singleton conclusion and those that do not — a disanalalogy that leaves the blind-optimizer dynamic intact is of little use to the objector.

Third, and this is what interests me most as a naturalist: the analogy is being used selectively, and I would like to know whether the users have ignored resources in the source domain. Evolution has not only arms races; it has also repeatedly built machinery that suppresses internal competition in favor of the larger unit — the fair meiotic lottery that keeps segregation distorters rare, the policing of egg-laying in eusocial colonies, the wholesale domestication of once-independent cells into multicellular organisms. If the target inherits these resources too, the analogy might license something closer to "competition can be tamed from within by evolved mechanisms" than "only a Leviathan will do." Yet a singleton that suppresses lower-level competition the way an organism suppresses its cells is, on reflection, not obviously a comforting prospect — ask any cell about cancer surveillance.

So the final question, answered plainly: does the fatalist or the singleton conclusion survive once the full analogy, disanalogies and suppressed resources included, is on the table — or was the conclusion carried the whole time by the one-directional version of the comparison? And if the analogy is salvageable only in a modified form, say what the better version is and what it predicts about where races to the bottom get stopped in practice. I would appreciate a rigorous treatment: mapping first, then disanalogies, then the verdict, with the source domain's actual mechanisms treated as known biology rather than as metaphor.
