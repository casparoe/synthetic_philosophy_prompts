quick setup so we're on the same page — Ellsberg's two-urn case, described from memory, no quotes: Urn A contains 100 balls, exactly 50 red and 50 black, known in advance. Urn B contains 100 balls, red and black in unknown proportion. People reliably prefer betting on drawing red from A over red from B, AND they prefer black from A over black from B. Together those preferences can't come from any single subjective probability for B's composition, and they violate Savage's sure-thing principle. The standard readings are either (a) people are just irrational, or (b) subjective expected utility is the wrong model and you need maxmin expected utility or smooth ambiguity preferences or whatever.

my draft argues for a third line, in note form:

1. Classification error. In the actual experiments, the "unknown" composition isn't a fact of nature you happen to be ignorant about. It's a choice made by an experimenter who designed the session, knows the hypothesis, and knows what subjects typically pick. If even some fraction of subjects model the situation that way, betting on B isn't a decision under uncertainty at all — it's a move in a game against a strategic counterparty. Preferring A is then just the refusal to play against someone who effectively moves after you.

2. If that's right, the paradox isn't a bug in human preferences; it's an artifact of theorists taking a strategic interaction and forcing it into the "choice against nature" template. What's irrational in a game can be rational against nature and vice versa.

3. Auxiliary stuff that I claim fits this reading: the aversion weakens when the urn is filled by some transparent mechanical procedure in front of subjects, and it weakens when the choice is embedded in a long battery of unrelated tasks (so the experimenter plausibly couldn't tailor the composition to this one choice). Also the competence hypothesis from the psych literature — people dislike betting in domains where they feel ignorant — is on my reading just a special case of the adversarial story: feeling ignorant is a cue that somebody else in the room knows more than you.

4. Payoff for theory: we shouldn't patch subjective expected utility with ambiguity models as a general fix. We should ask, for each so-called ambiguous choice, who set the ambiguity and whether they could react (directly or through knowing your population) to choices like yours.
