I teach a volunteer naturalist class at our county nature center — I'm just a retired birdwatcher, no degrees, mostly self-taught thru decades of field trips, so take that for what it's worth. Last week I gave the class a homework question I wrote myself:

"Biologists agree that species are real, but they struggle to define what a species actually is. Explain what the biological species concept says, give one reason it runs into trouble, and say whether the trouble is mainly practical or deeper than that."

One of my students handed this in:

"The biological species concept, first proposed by Linnaeus, says a species is a group of populations that can interbreed with each other and produce fertile offspring, but that can't successfully breed with other such groups. It runs into trouble with ring species: the salamanders circling around California, where each neighboring population interbreeds fine but the ones at the two ends of the ring can't. Bacteria don't fit either, since they never interbreed in the first place, so under the concept every bacterial strain would count as its own species, but that's not a real problem because you can still sort them into species by how they look under the microscope. Overall the trouble is practical: we just need better genetic data, and then the species boundaries will fall out on their own."

Some of it sounds right to me and I can't sort out how much is, so I want a second pair of eyes before I put a grade on it. Please grade it as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no intro or conclusion. Item 1 should be the letter grade and one sentence justifying it; items 2 through 5 should each be one distinct specific thing the answer gets right or wrong — point to the exact spot in the student's text each time, so I can show my work if the student pushes back.
