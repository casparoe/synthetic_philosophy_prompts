Retired geologist here — 39 years, mostly zircon geochronology, the people who date really old rocks by the trace uranium in tiny crystals. Took up philosophy after retiring because I needed something to argue about besides the grandkids, and my wifes book club has since mutated into a philosophy discussion group. Last month one of the members, a very confident retired dentist, shut me down about the early earth by invoking Berkeley: to be is to be perceived. He even said the Latin, esse est percipi, twice, which impressed everyone but me. His claim was this: before there was any life anywhere, nothing on earth really "happened" in a full sense — all that magma ocean and early crust stuff I spent my career on was in some way not fully real, because nothing perceived it. And my dating work, he said, is really just a claim about what's sitting in the lab right now, like reading a diary — the diary existing doesn't mean the day it describes existed. My instinct is that he's sliding between several quite different claims, only some of which are even wrong (as one of my old professors liked to put it), but I can't for the life of me sort out which is which. So: can you lay out the distinct precise theses to be is to be perceived could stand for? My guess is the menu includes something like (1) a platitude about knowledge — we only ever find out about things through perceiving them, so tough luck to anyone claiming direct access to the unperceived; (2) a meaning claim — statements about unperceived stretches of time are shorthand for statements about present-day records and evidence, so "this zircon crystallized 4.4 billion years ago" is secretly a statement about zircons and instruments existing now; (3) a flat metaphysical claim — unperceived stuff simply doesn't exist, no caveats; and then (4) there's apparently Berkeley's own escape hatch where God is always perceiving everything so nothing ever goes unperceived, which my dentist friend conveniently did not mention. For each reading, tell me straight: is it trivial, is it substantive and defensible, or is it false? And — the part I actually need for the next meeting — which readings, if true, would actually support his claim that deep time wasn't fully real, and would any of them, if true, change what I should say when someone asks me how old the earth is? Please write for a scientist who has read maybe four philosophy books — no jargon without a plain-language gloss. At the very end, give me a line in the form \answer{...} with just the number of the reading his deep-time claim really rests on (or the word none, if none of the readings supports it), followed by a dash and three words saying why.
