My study group spent last meeting on causation by omission, and by the end one of us had an argument that I can neither accept nor refute. It starts from the textbook case — the plants die because the gardener, hired to water them, never does — but it does not stay there.

The argument, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) The gardener's not watering the plants caused their death. He was engaged to water them, he didn't, and they died for lack of it. This is the paradigm of causation by omission, and I am not prepared to deny it.

(2) What makes his omission causal can only be this: had he watered them instead, the plants would not have died. An omission is nothing, and nothing can contribute to an effect except through the difference it makes to what happens instead; the counterfactual is the whole story, or I do not know what else could be.

(3) But I never watered those plants either. Had I watered them, they would not have died. So, by exactly the test in (2), my not watering them caused their death — and so did the refraining of everyone else who could have watered them, which is to say nearly everyone.

(4) Nor does it stop with people. Had it rained that week, the plants would have lived. So the rain's not falling caused their death too — unless omissions require omitters, in which case it is strictly false that droughts cause famines, which I also cannot believe.

(5) Generalize: every preventable death is caused, in part, by the refraining of everyone who could have prevented it, including those who never knew the victim existed. A child drowns in a pond on another continent; I can swim; had I been there and pulled her out, she would have lived; so my not being there caused her drowning as surely as the gardener's negligence caused the plants' death.

The conclusion is that each of us is a cause of that child's death, and of thousands like it: every tragedy has, among its causes, the whole non-intervening world. That seems to prove too much. It inflates causation until it is everywhere at once, and the word cause no longer tracks anything. Yet I cannot find the flawed step.

Each premise looks defensible. (1) is the case everyone teaches. (2) looks close to analytic: what else could an absence do? (3) and (5) merely apply the test without favoritism — and if I exempt myself there, I need a principled line between the gardener's not watering and mine, and the only lines I can see (duty, proximity, knowledge) make what caused what depend on norms and locations rather than on the world's causal structure. If forced to choose, I would keep (1): I cannot bring myself to say the gardener did not cause the death.

One further complication. A friend offered what looked like an escape: the counterfactual in (2) is evaluated in the closest worlds, and the nearest world in which the gardener waters the plants is close — he simply does his job — whereas the nearest world in which I do is distant and outlandish, stuffed with coincidences; so (3) illicitly swaps in the wrong worlds. But suppose the gardener was on holiday a thousand miles away that week and never gave the plants a thought. His nearest watering-world is now just as outlandish as mine, yet he still seems the cause and I still do not. So either closeness was never doing the real work, or it abandons us precisely where the case gets interesting.

Where does the argument go wrong? Name the step to reject, explain why it looked fine, and price the alternatives. And if you think the argument is valid and the conclusion must simply be swallowed, say so — but then tell me what is left of the distinction between causing a death and failing to prevent one.
