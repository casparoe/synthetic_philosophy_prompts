My friend Dana and I got into it about utilitarianism last night, like 3 hours past when we should've stopped, and she ended on an argument I still can't get out of my head. She's very into Rawls, the separateness of persons thing. Here's her argument as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) When I accept a small harm now for a big benefit later — skipping dessert to avoid a cavity, taking a miserable week of chemo to avoid dying — that's fine, because both the cost and the payoff land in one and the same life, mine. There's someone for whom the trade nets out positive, and it's the someone who actually has to go through the bad part.
(2) With distinct persons there is no such someone. The person who bears the cost and the people who recieve the benefit never add up into one life.
(3) So costs and benefits falling on different people can never be netted against each other, and no one can be pushed below where they started just so that others are made better off — no matter how many others, or how much better off.
(4) But then, she admits, her view seems to condemn basically every cooperative scheme we think is obviously fine: compulsory car insurance (the safe drivers subsidize the ones who crash), vaccination requirements (some people take on side-effect risk for herd immunity they personally don't need), even taxation for emergency services. In each case somebody ends up worse off than they'd be if the scheme didn't exist, and better-off strangers are the reason.
(5) Dana says that's not a problem for her: those schemes are justified by a different route — everyone could reasonably accept the arrangement in advance, not knowing which role they'd occupy — and (3) only ever ruled out summing utilities, not cooperation as such.

My problem is I can't tell whether her move from (2) to (3) is valid or whether something gets smuggled in — maybe "netted against each other" is doing different work in the two steps? And is her escape hatch in (5) real, or is "everyone accepts the risk in advance because they might have been the one who needed the rescue" just utilitarian aggregation smuggled back in through ex ante probabilities? Honestly I'd rather keep the separateness intuition than keep aggregation, but I want to know exactly what I'm paying for it. Which step should I attack, and what does rejecting it cost her? She's already texting me asking if I've conceded, so I need an actual position, not a survey.
