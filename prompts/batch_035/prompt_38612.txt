Write a satirical management-consulting deliverable, roughly 600 words, from a fictional firm (invent a bland corporate name) retained by an unnamed client — omniscient, omnipotent, maximally good — to advise on which possible world to actualize. The conceit is that the engagement itself is an exercise in middle knowledge: the firm's analysts know, for every possible creature in every possible circumstance, exactly what that creature would freely do, but by the terms of the engagement the client can neither determine those choices nor renegotiate the conditionals. Format it as a real consulting report: engagement number, executive summary, methodology, findings, a risk register, and recommendations, plus at least one pull-quote from a satisfied client.

The humor must come from the actual Molinist machinery, not from caricature, and the piece should stay deadpan throughout — no winking, no asides to the reader. Specifically:

- The three logical moments of divine knowledge should appear as the firm's workflow: a prior deliverable prepared before any creaturely facts were on the table (purely necessary truths), the present engagement surveying the conditionals, and a follow-up report to be issued after actualization — billed as a separate phase, of course.
- The grounding objection should surface as an audit finding: every conditional of creaturely freedom in the firm's database is stamped TRUE, but the provenance field is blank. Nothing, including the client, made these conditionals true; they came in as they are. Compliance flags this repeatedly; Compliance is thanked for its thoroughness.
- The deliverable should conclude that no surveyed world in which free creatures always freely choose the good appears actualizable: every portfolio of genuinely free creatures leaks some wrongdoing somewhere, and the analysts cannot patch this without adjusting the conditionals, which would void the contract.
- At least one analyst should note, in a marginal comment or appendix, the worry that the whole methodology is somehow circular — that in surveying what free creatures would do they have quietly presupposed the very freedom they were retained to protect — and be reassigned to the follow-up phase.

A philosopher of religion who knows the Molinism literature (the grounding objection, the actualizability problem, the circularity worry) should recognize the real disputes rather than a parody of them, and a reader who knows nothing about Molinism should still come away understanding, correctly, how middle knowledge is supposed to work. The client should emerge from the piece neither as a fool nor as a villain: the joke is on the structure of the doctrine, not on anyone in it.
