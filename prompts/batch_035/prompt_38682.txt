I'm a third-year student taking a seminar on irrationality, and we are finishing a unit on self-deception: the classic paradox (that to deceive yourself you must in some way register the very thing you are hiding from yourself), Sartre's bad faith, and the deflationary accounts on which self-deception is just belief-formation steered by motivation, with no mind partitioned against itself. Outside class, I work as a peer mentor in my university's academic support program: six first-year students on academic probation, one twenty-minute meeting with each per week, my job being to keep them engaged and to refer them onward when something looks serious. The hardest part is knowing what to do with a mentee's optimism. Some tell me they will get into the nursing program although their GPA sits far below the published admission average and they avoid opening the program's website; some explain a string of failing grades by saying each professor had it in for them; some appear to perform the sour-grapes move in reverse, deciding that a major they are failing was never what they wanted anyway; and some, I believe, hold a plain and reasonable hope that is simply keeping them going. My seminar says these are different states with different remedies — wishful thinking, self-deception, adaptive preference, justified hope — but it says so in the abstract, and in twenty minutes with a nineteen-year-old I need something I can actually run. Please write a procedure for the mentor-training binder, usable by other mentors with no philosophy background: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure ends with a no (for example, where the facts are genuinely contested, as with the professor's grudge, and the pattern of evidence-handling is all I can classify, not the truth of the claim), and where it hands the decision to a person because what remains is a question of values — such as whether a sustaining but false hope should be punctured at all. Ground it in what the philosophy actually supports: selective treatment of counterevidence in this domain versus others, avoidance of the sources that would test the belief, whether the belief tracks the available information the way the same student tracks it elsewhere, and whether the belief formed to fit a motive rather than the reverse. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and about the deeper limit — that from the outside, self-deception, sincere conviction, and clumsy hope can look identical, so the procedure at best licenses predictions about how a mentee will handle evidence, never claims about what they privately believe. Then run it on three mentees: one certain she will get into the nursing program despite the GPA gap; one who attributes each failing grade to a different professor's grudge; and one whose belief that he will make the pre-med track is, by my lights, probably self-deception, yet is visibly the only thing getting him to class in the morning. On that last one, I suspect the procedure will tell me to leave the belief intact, which I will not like — if so, say it plainly, and close by stating what the procedure cannot do at all.
