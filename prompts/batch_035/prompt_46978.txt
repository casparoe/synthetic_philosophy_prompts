After thirty years pricing catastrophe exposure for reinsurers, I recently became chair of the three-trustee board of a small philanthropic fund that disburses roughly £600,000 a year, and whose founding statement commits us, in plain terms, to funding whatever does the most expected good per pound. A proposal now before us has split the board, and I must circulate a written decision memo before our meeting on the 14th.

The applicant is a retired geoscientist working outside any institution. She is seeking £75,000 over three years to maintain a small monitoring network for a hypothesized cascade — a seismic trigger for abrupt methane clathrate release, a mechanism never observed — which she estimates at roughly one chance in ten to the fifteenth per decade but which, if it occurred, would foreclose most of humanity's future. When our second trustee objected that no evidence can meaningfully constrain a probability that small, she responded that even at one in ten to the fortieth the arithmetic still dominates every other use of our funds, and that our charter leaves us no grounds to refuse. The third trustee wants to fund her, arguing that declining would betray the principle we all signed.

Please draft the memo I should circulate: a concrete recommendation (decline, fund, or fund under stated conditions), a serious engagement with the strongest argument for funding, and a standing policy the fund can adopt so that future proposals of this shape are settled on principle rather than on whoever argues longest. The memo needs to be actionable at our meeting, not a tour of the philosophical literature.
