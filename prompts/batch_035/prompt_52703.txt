For most of my career I was a neuroscientist studying memory consolidation, and I retired before I ever seriously read philosophy. In the past year I have been working through Ayer's Language, Truth and Logic, and I have hit a passage in the chapter on the a priori that I cannot fully digest, particularly because of how it seems to connect with what Ayer says elsewhere about life after death. Here is the passage:

"I exist" does not follow from "there is a thought now." The fact that a thought occurs at a given moment does not entail that any other thought has occurred at any other moment, still less that there has occurred a series of thoughts sufficient to constitute a single self. As Hume conclusively showed, no one event intrinsically points to any other. We infer the existence of events which we are not actually observing, with the help of general principle. But these principles must be obtained inductively. By mere deduction from what is immediately given we cannot advance a single step beyond. And, consequently, any attempt to base a deductive system on propositions which describe what is immediately given is bound to be a failure.

I take Ayer to be denying that the existence of a persisting self can be read off from any single moment of experience, and to be insisting that any claim of a continuing self rests on inductive, defeasible principles rather than on anything deductively airtight. Where I stumble is in joining this up with his treatment of survival after death. My understanding, gleaned from the book's treatment of verifiability, is that Ayer refuses to classify "we survive death" as meaningless metaphysics: he seems to regard it as a genuine empirical hypothesis, one that could in principle be confirmed in a scenario involving communications attributed to the dead — as I recall the example, messages relayed through a medium that reliably conveyed information only the deceased could have known. At the same time, he evidently thinks the evidence for survival is worthless and that the dependence of our mental lives on our bodies makes survival, if not strictly impossible, vanishingly improbable.

So here is what I would like help understanding. First, what exactly does the word "intrinsically" do in the claim that no one event intrinsically points to any other, and why does that make the bridge from one thought to a "series of thoughts sufficient to constitute a single self" an inductive rather than a deductive matter? Second, if personal identity over time is constituted by memory-continuity, and memory-continuity itself is only inferentially reconstructed through inductive principles, as the quoted passage seems to imply, then what would ground an identity judgment in the medium scenario? That is, when a communication purports to come from a deceased person, is Ayer entitled to say it is that person surviving, rather than merely that some experiential sequence resembling him continues — and is there a real difference between those two descriptions on his account? Third, in my professional life a memory was a physical trace laid down in tissue, and I find myself assuming that the self is parasitic on that machinery; Ayer's bundle picture seems to abstract the self away from any substrate at all, which makes me wonder whether disembodied survival is, on his own terms, more coherent than his dismissiveness suggests. I would appreciate a patient explanation of how these pieces fit together. I should also mention that I have read Ayer had a near-death experience near the end of his life and afterwards granted that his conviction death is annihilation had been somewhat shaken, though apparently not overturned; if that bears on how his earlier framework should be read, I would be glad to hear how.
