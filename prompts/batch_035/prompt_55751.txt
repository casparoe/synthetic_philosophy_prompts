I've been following the coherent extrapolated volition conversation since it lived on mailing lists rather than on LessWrong, and I've slowly come to accept that it has run aground in a very specific way: the arguments on all sides are fully aired, everyone can recite everyone else's objections, and nobody has moved in roughly two decades. I'd like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is, not another round of the arguments themselves.

To fix the target: on one side are the idealization-friendly views. CEV is the rationalist version, but it sits on top of a much older line in ethics — the full-information accounts of welfare going back through Railton to Sidgwick's idea of regarding desire from the point of view of someone fully informed. The shared thought is that a large fraction of our current preferences are the product of ignorance, fatigue, and akrasia, and that the normatively authoritative preferences are the ones we would have under ideal conditions: better informed, reasoning longer, in some sense better versions of ourselves. On the other side are the idealization skeptics, represented both in the academic literature (the objections that the fully informed agent may be a different person altogether, and that a preference I would have after idealization is not ipso facto a preference of mine now) and, in a more corrosive register, in rationalist spaces themselves, where the complaint is that extrapolation smuggles in the extrapolators' conception of what counts as knowing more or being better, so that CEV amounts to the programmers' values run through a coherence launder.

My working hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that the stall comes from an unargued fork: everyone is using the phrase "what we would really want," but the two camps implicitly disagree about whether reflection discovers values that are already yours or constitutes new ones, and this turns on contested views about personal identity and the normativity of idealization that neither side can defend non-circularly. But I can't tell whether the disagreement is substantive or verbal, whether it's really two different questions — an engineering question about what to build versus a metaethical question about what makes a preference authoritative — sharing one heading, or whether one side has simply made a bedrock error about whose preferences matter.

Given whatever diagnosis you find best supported, I'd also like an honest answer about what would count as progress: a conceptual distinction, a formal result in preference aggregation or value learning, a decision about what to do when actual and extrapolated preferences demonstrably diverge, or nothing at all, and why.
