I work in ML, specifically on abstention — the stuff where a model gets to say "I don't know" / decline to answer instead of always giving its best guess. So I live in a world of selective prediction, calibration curves, conformal prediction, a rejection option bolted onto a classifier, and coverage-vs-risk tradeoffs: you set a threshold on the model's confidence, only emit answers above it, and you're trading how many questions you answer against how often the emitted ones are wrong.

Lately I've been reading about the philosophical debate over the "norm of assertion" — the rule that supposedly governs what you're allowed to assert in conversation. One camp says the rule is knowledge: you may assert that p only if you know that p (and they lean on the fact that Moore-paradoxical utterances like "p, but I don't know whether p" sound terrible, which they take to show knowledge is the standard being violated). A second camp says the norm is just truth: assert only what's true. A third says it's rational belief. And yes, before you ask, this is literally a fight over a rule nobody wrote down and nobody enforces — which is roughly why I find it interesting, it's an argument about the abstention policy that human speech already runs.

Restate this debate in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Treat an assertion as the model emitting an answer, and treat the three candidate norms as three abstention policies. What is "emit only what you know" as an operating characteristic? Is "emit only what's true" even a policy, or is it more like the metric you evaluate policies against — and if that's right, does that asymmetry already tell us something about the debate? Is "emit only what you believe" just "never abstain," since the model always believes *something*? Where do the standard moves land in my world — Moore-paradoxical data, the fact that speakers retract assertions when challenged (is that a post-hoc filter?), and lottery cases, where asserting "my ticket will lose" seems wrong even though it's almost certainly true and I fully believe it. Do we have anything like lottery cases in selective prediction, or does our setup make them dissapear?

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. What in the philosophers' debate has no counterpart in my vocabulary? My guess is it's the anti-luck condition on knowledge — Gettier-style cases where you have a true, justified belief that's true only by accident — but I honestly don't see what "assert only what you know" adds over "assert only what's calibrated and turns out true." So either tell me I'm wrong and what knowledge-as-a-deployment-policy would look like, or tell me the knowledge norm is doing less work than its fans think. And then the meta-question: is the residue a defect of my vocabulary (confidence scores and calibration just can't express what knowledge is), or a sign the philosophers' claim is less clear than it looked?

Finally, commit. If you had to deploy one of the three norms as the abstention policy for a question-answering system — emit only if known, emit only if true, emit only if believed — which one do you pick and why? Don't give me a neutral survey with the choice left open at the end. Pick, defend it, and tell me what you expect to lose by picking it.
