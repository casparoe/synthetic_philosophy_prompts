So a bit of background: I'm a nurse, I've worked ICU and now hospice, and my brother-in-law is one of those guys who read a philosophy book once and now thinks he's a utilitarian. He keeps saying stuff like "well if you could add up all the happiness and subtract all the pain" and I keep telling him it doesn't work like that, at least not for me, but I can't actually say what my view IS. So I want to lay out my gut reactions to a bunch of cases and see if there's a principle behind them. I told him suffering just counts differently than happiness, and he said that's an actual thing called negative utilitarianism or something, but I don't want a philosophy lecture with names and book titles, I want you to actually figure out my position. Pretend you're a professional ethicist doing this properly.

Here are my verdicts. If a patient has a brutal final week and then the dementia means they completely forget it, that week still mattered to me — the suffering happened, it wasn't erased by the forgetting. But flip it: if someone has an amazing vacation and then loses the memory, I somehow feel that was more "wasted" than the painful week was "made unreal." Can't explain it but there's an asymmetry there. Second: I think creating a new person who'll be happy is, like, fine and all, but it feels nowhere near as morally urgent as making life bearable for someone who already exists and is suffering — like if I had to pick between fertility programs and pain clinics I'd pick the clinics without hesitating. Third: I do NOT think any amount of happiness can make up for torture — no pile of joy anywhere in the universe makes one tortured person's situation okay, compensation just isn't a thing for suffering. But here's my problem: I also clearly think a life with wild highs and one awful decade is better than a totally flat life with zero feeling either way. So happiness counts for something! And I can't say how much or in what way without contradicting the no-compensation thing.

So that's basically it. Can you brainstorm first and then give me ONE principle (or honestly a couple of candidates if one can't do it) that captures all or most of these gut reactions, stated plainly enough that I could use it at Thanksgiving? Tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to give up, if any, because I suspect the compensation one and the wild-highs one can't both survive. And then tell me what this principle says about a case I haven't figured out my answer to: my hospice patient who says, completely lucid, that her painful final months are worth it to her because of the love in them — is she confused, or am I?
