So I write the little myth-busting column for our school paper, and lately every idea people send me is the same thing in different words: neuroscientists proved free will is fake, your brain "decides" before "you" do, sometimes seconds before, etc. I did some digging and it basically all traces back to this one old experiment by a guy named Libet, where people flicked their wrist whenever they felt like it while watching a dot go around a clock, and a brain wave (the readiness potential?) started building up before they said they consciously decided to move. Libet himself thought maybe we still have a last-second "veto" over the urge, which people call "free won't." But then I found out there's all this stuff that makes it way messier — like that the readiness potential might mostly be background noise that builds up till it crosses a threshold, and you only see the buildup because they average across tons of trials (I think a researcher named Schurger showed something like this?), or that watching the clock itself can shift when people report deciding, or that deciding *when* to flick your wrist is a really different thing from choosing between options like tea vs coffee, which is what people actually mean by free will most of the time. Also there's a newer study with brain scans that supposedly predicts simple choices like 7 seconds early, and that one gets the "seconds before" headlines.

The problem is I keep winging it in my columns and I don't want to just debunk stuff reflexively anymore. So: write me a procedure I can actually run in like 15 minutes when a new "science disproved free will" claim lands in my inbox, before deadline. It should have the questions to ask in order (like, what experiment is this actually about, what exactly did it measure, what does that measurement have to do with free will, how strong is the claim being made vs. what was found), what counts as a good enough answer to each, and where the procedure should just stop and say "this part is philosophy, not settled by the experiment" so I can report it honestly instead of pretending. Then run it on the three headlines below — I made these up but they're exactly the kind of thing I get:

1. "Your brain knows your decision 7 seconds before you do"
2. "The famous free will experiment had a fatal flaw"
3. "Brain scans prove your choices are an illusion"

And here's the part I actually care about: include at least one case where the procedure gives me an answer I won't like. I really enjoy debunking the debunkers, but I want to know when a pop-science claim is basically *right* and I'd be the one misleading people by dunking on it. Also be upfront about what the procedure can't do — like whether a checklist run by a 16 year old in 15 minutes can honestly settle anything about free will, and which calls it has to hand back to me as judgment calls.

One formatting thing: start your response with the bottom line — one sentence saying what the procedure overall concludes / what its core move is — and only then give the reasoning and the steps. No preamble, no restating my question back at me, just go. And don't bother with a works cited, I track down my own sources anyway since the paper makes me.
