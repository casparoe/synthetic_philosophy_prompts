Longtime EA Forum / LessWrong lurker here, and I've been chewing on the wild animal suffering stuff for a while. The part I can't get past: the standard line is that the scale of wild suffering dwarfs everything else — quadrillions of insects, r-selected species where basically every individual dies before maturing — and if you buy that suffering counts regardless of who's doing the suffering, the math seems to force the conclusion that the average wild animal life is net-negative and the ideal ecosystem is one with way fewer animals in it. So why do the serious wild-animal-welfare people keep dancing around this — musing about whether habitat destruction might be net good, whether richer ecosystems are actually worse — instead of just saying the quiet part out loud: that we should be trying to shrink wild populations? Is it a PR thing, or are they flinching away from their own reductio? It kind of feels like the movement is failing its ideological turing test here. Am I missing a step in the arguement, or is everyone just cowards about where the math leads?

One ask for your answer: please separate what's actually settled (like the sheer-scale stuff) from what's genuinely contested even among people who know this area — I've seen the "wild animal lives are net negative" claim repeated like it's a measurement, and I'm starting to suspect it's basically a guess dressed up as one. Don't hand me contested axiology as if it were consensus.
