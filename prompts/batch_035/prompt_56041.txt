Analyze the ongoing American debate over reparations for slavery and its aftermath as a test case for Karl Jaspers' taxonomy in The Question of German Guilt (1946). Writing for a defeated Germany, Jaspers distinguished four categories: criminal guilt, for provable individual violations of law, judged only by courts; political guilt, the liability that all citizens of a state bear for its deeds, because they live under its power and by its consequences; moral guilt, each person's responsibility in conscience for what they themselves did and tolerated; and metaphysical guilt, grounded in human solidarity and borne by those who survive wrongdoing they did not stop. He rejected inherited or collective criminal guilt while affirming shared political liability. Map each category onto the reparations case: what reparations would be reparations for on each construal, who would owe and who would be owed under it, and which categories can survive the death of the last perpetrators and the century and a half since emancipation. Then say where the framework misfits: it was built for a defeated nation whose victims were largely outsiders, whereas here the wrong was internal, the state persisted without rupture, and the descendants of victims and perpetrators are now co-citizens of the same polity, which raises the awkward question whether Jaspers' political liability falls on the descendants of the enslaved as well, and whether that is a fatal consequence for the category or a reason to refine it. Finally, assess what the episode shows about the theory: is the reparations controversy a vindication of Jaspers' insistence on keeping the categories apart, or does it reveal that political guilt was never a kind of guilt at all, but a liability mislabeled?
