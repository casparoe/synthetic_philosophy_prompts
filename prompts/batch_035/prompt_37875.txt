A pop-neuroscience book I'm reading explains the extended mind thesis like this: Clark and Chalmers argued in a 1998 paper that whenever an external tool does the same cognitive job as some internal process, the tool literally becomes part of your mind. Their thought experiment is Otto, who has Alzheimer's and writes everything he needs to remember in a notebook, consulting it as automatically as other people consult memory; since the notebook does for Otto what biological memory does for Inga, its entries count as his beliefs. So far so good I thought. But then the book goes on: since nowadays everyone depends on their smartphones just as heavily, our phones are literally parts of our minds, so a dead battery literally means your mind temporarily shrank, and glancing at your phone to check a fact counts as introspecting your own thoughts. It wraps up by saying the whole paper was refuted not long after by critics who pointed out that Otto merely relying on his notebook doesn't make the notebook part of his mind, any more than relying on a gas station makes it part of your car. I'm inclined to accept the Otto part but the smartphone stuff feels like a stretch, and the "refuted" bit also seemed oddly quick for a thesis that I keep seeing people still defend. Can you go through it and tell me which parts are accurate to what Clark and Chalmers actually claimed, where the book quietly slides from what they said to something they didn't, and whether its conclusions (the phone claims, the refutation) follow from the accurate parts?
