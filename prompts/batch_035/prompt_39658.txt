Here's my situation: I'm a PhD student in history and philosophy of science, writing a seminar paper on whether the standard textbook telling of the phlogiston story is a case study in Whig history. Everyone knows the version: phlogiston was a foolish pseudo-substance, Lavoisier came along with oxygen and the balance and swept it away, chemistry became a real science, the end. Butterfield's whole complaint about Whig history was that it organizes the past as a march toward the present, dividing historical actors into winners who "saw the light" and losers who stood in the way, and reads earlier theories as merely botched approximations of what we now believe.

So what I want from you is a careful two-way analysis:

1. Map the episode onto the Whig history framework. What exactly is the "present" that the textbook story is teleologically aimed at -- is it modern chemistry, or actually Lavoisier's chemistry, which is itself obsolete in various ways? Who gets cast as the progressive (Lavoisier alone? the whole French school?) and who as the obstacle, and is that casting fair given what phlogiston theory actually did for its practitioners? Note that phlogiston unified combustion, calcination, respiration, and reduction under one heading, and Priestley's "dephlogisticated air" was a perfectly reasonable reading of his own experiments. Do the actual epistemic roles (who had the better predictions, who had the better explanatory scope, at what moments) match the winners/losers casting?

2. Then run it the other way: does the phlogiston case vindicate Whig history rather than indict it? Here's what's nagging me. Sometimes the past really does culminate in the present, and phlogiston genuinely does not exist. If a historian refuses to say "Lavoisier was basically right and Stahl's theory was basically wrong" on anti-Whig principle, hasn't the principle overshot into a kind of relativism? Is there a difference between judging actors (Stahl was an idiot) and judging theories (phlogiston is false), and does Butterfield's framework respect that distinction or blur it?

3. And then the part I most care about: where does the Whig history lens break down on this case? What features of the actual episode does it have no purchase on -- e.g. the fact that phlogiston theory kept being a live, productive research program for decades after Lavoisier (Cavendish, Priestley never converted), the fact that the chemical revolution was partly about instruments and pedagogy and the redefinition of "element" rather than a simple falsification, the fact that Lavoisier's own oxygen account of acids turned out wrong too. If Whig history can't explain why a "wrong" theory stayed productive for so long, is some other framework (Kuhn's paradigm shift, Lakatos's research programmes, whatever you think is the best contender) genuinely better here, or do they each just redistribute the Whiggism rather than eliminating it?

Please don't just tell me "both sides have a point" -- I need you to actually commit to where the balance comes down, and to be explicit about what a non-Whig telling of the phlogiston story would concretly look like, paragraph by paragraph, versus the textbook one. If you think some parts of the textbook story survive anti-Whig scrutiny fine, say which and why.
