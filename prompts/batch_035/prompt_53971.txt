I've been going back and forth with my housemate for weeks about which preferences count as what a person *really* wants, and I figured I'd do this the honest way: go by my gut on a bunch of concrete cases first, then find the principle after, instead of picking a theory and bending the cases to fit. The question originally came up from the AI value learning stuff — the idea that instead of asking people what they want right now, you ask what they'd want if they knew more, thought faster, had grown up more, that kind of thing — but you can ignore all that; the question is old. Anyway, here are my verdicts. My uncle smoked for forty years, and at the time he sincerely said he enjoyed it and would hate being a non-smoker; after the heart scare and quitting, he says the smoking self was just wrong about what he wanted, and I count the post-quit preference, easy. Same for my friend who was raised in a strict sect and at twenty sincerely wanted the sheltered life: I count her post-exit preference, and here I dont even feel the pull of the sincere earlier one. If someone's current preference rests on a false belief — a friend avoided a whole career because of a bogus statistic, and revised the moment I showed her the real numbers — I count the revised one, obviously. And I think it was fine for my aunt to stop my cousin getting the face tattoo at sixteen even though he wanted it at the time, and fine in the Ulysses-and-the-sirens way for someone to bind their future self against a preference they know is coming. So far the pattern looks like: later, more informed, more reflected wins.

But now the ones that break it. My friend from the sect has a twin sister, same upbringing, who married a guy who over about six years talked her out of her job, then out of her friends, until she now sincerely prefers staying home and says the old life was a mistake. I refuse to count this one — I want to reach back to the pre-marriage her — and I can't see what separates it from the sect case except that in the sect case I happen to like where the change landed. From the inside she'd tell you it feels exactly like her sister's deconversion. And the Ulysses thing cuts the same way: the tied-to-the-mast preference loses because of how it was formed, which seems right to me, but that already means "later, more informed wins" can't be the principle, since there the later one loses on its formation story. The only rules I've managed to come up with are circular — the preference counts if it's the real her's, and the real her is whoever would hold the preference that counts.

So here's what I'm asking for. Is there a principle, or a short list with maybe one clause per case, that gets all of these? If you can't get them all, tell me which of my verdicts has to go and why that one rather than another — I'd rather overturn a single verdict than adopt something with a bespoke clause per case, but I also don't want you to quietly save a verdict with a clause that's just the verdict restated. Then tell me what the principle says about the case I keep flip-flopping on: someone whose preferences were changed by years of honest argument with a mentor they admit is smarter than them and who never lied to them or isolated them, but who now says "I can't rebut a word he said, and I still miss who I was." Does the new preference count there, or does the nostalgic self get a vote? And throughout, please tell me how confident you actually are in the principle you land on and single out the point you're least sure of — I'd definately rather know where it's shaky than get a confident rundown of what are mostly judgment calls.
