My friend Daniel and I spent most of Friday evening arguing about what he calls "the composition question," and I left more confused than I started. He's been reading a lot of analytic metaphysics lately and now talks about it with total confidence, and I couldn't tell during the argument whether I was losing on the merits or just being steamrolled by his certainty. At some point he gave me this summary of the territory, delivered more or less word for word, and I'd like to know how much of it to trust: "Philosophers call the special composition question the question of when some smaller objects add up to one further object — it's basically the question of what a whole is. Peter van Inwagen put the question on the map with a book in the early nineties, and he himself defended the contact answer: composition happens whenever things are fastened together, which is why he says tables don't exist but we do. The two main live answers are nihilism, which says composition never occurs and only the fundamental particles exist, and universalism, which says any two things, no matter how scattered, compose a further thing — so there's an object scattered across your left shoe and the Moon. Universalism has been pretty much refuted, since the scattered-object consequence is obviously absurd, and nihilism is refuted by the plain fact that you and I exist and aren't particles. That leaves the common-sense answer as the only serious option: composition happens exactly when things form a unified whole, the way the parts of a chair do and the way your shoe and the Moon don't. There's also some fringe view that composition is just a brute fact that can't be explained, but that's obviously cheating — an answer that says 'it just happens' isn't an answer. So the field has basically converged on common sense; the holdouts just don't want to give up their intuitions about weird cases."

I don't know the literature well enough to audit this myself, but a few things he said felt off. I vaguely recall that van Inwagen's own view has something to do with life rather than contact, and I also suspect the field hasn't converged on the common-sense answer the way he claimed — but I'm not sure, and he was very sure. Please go through his summary claim by claim and tell me which parts a working metaphysician would accept, which are simplifications a specialist would wince at but let pass, and which are just wrong. In particular I'd like to know whether the "absurdity" of scattered objects is actually treated as a refutation of universalism or as a disputed cost; whether nihilists really are committed to denying that people exist, and whether they have anything to say in response; and whether his dismissal of the brute-composition view as "not an answer" is fair to the people who defend it. Also tell me whether his conclusion — that common sense wins by elimination — follows from the parts of his summary that are accurate, or whether it depends on the parts that aren't. Please write in American English, and as you go, keep a clear running distinction between what is settled among people who know this literature and what is genuinely contested, so that I can tell whether Daniel was reporting a consensus or stating one side of an open debate as if it were the end of the matter.
