I'm putting together a seminar unit on personal identity and mind uploading, and I want a thought experiment that stresses a particular seam in the gradual-replacement literature. The usual argument runs: if neurons are replaced one at a time with functionally identical silicon, and the subject reports no change at each step, then either consciousness and identity fade imperceptibly at every step (which seems incredible) or they are preserved throughout — so the final silicon being is you. Parfit-style fission cases, meanwhile, teach that branching psychological continuity threatens identity while arguably leaving what matters intact. I want a single designed case that forces these two lines of thought to collide, rather than merely citing them side by side.

Construct the scenario to the following specifications: a hospital-lab setting, no more than one paragraph, no exotic physics beyond what gradual replacement already assumes. Crucially, the surgeons do not destroy the neurons they remove. Each excised neuron is kept alive in a supportive medium, and via a radio link carrying exactly the signals it used to send and receive, it remains causally coupled to its old neighbors. So at every intermediate stage there are, running in parallel, a hybrid brain in the skull and a growing "shadow brain" of biological neurons in the lab, both sustaining the same pattern of activity as the original. By the end there are two complete, functioning systems — one silicon, one biological — each of which stood to the original in the very relation the step-by-step argument was supposed to certify. You may not stipulate any verdicts about identity or consciousness in the scenario itself; whatever the case is supposed to show must fall out of the mechanism you describe.

After the scenario, demonstrate that the case meets its specifications rather than smuggling them in: explain what verdict the simple step-by-step functionalist argument now delivers and why it can no longer deliver that verdict cleanly; what a Parfitian concludes once the fission structure is noticed; and what a theorist who ties personal identity to biological continuity should say about which, if either, of the two final systems is the original person. Then do the engineering post-mortem: identify precisely which feature of the construction is doing the work (I suspect it is the radio link, but I want the argument, not just the label), and describe what happens if that feature is removed — if the excised neurons are simply destroyed as they are taken out, we should presumably collapse back to the standard uploading argument, and I want you to show why the collapse occurs. Finally, give the strongest available reply on behalf of someone who wants to keep both the gradual-replacement argument and a non-branching view of identity: is the right move to deny that the shadow brain is causally integrated in the relevant sense, to bite the bullet and say the subject gradually ceases to exist as the shadow grows, or to follow Parfit in conceding that identity was never what the argument was about? Recommend one of these and defend the choice, and tell me whether the case, in the end, teaches us anything about consciousness itself, or only about the logic of identity claims.
