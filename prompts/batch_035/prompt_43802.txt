I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science, and I keep running into people (mostly at conferences, and one guy in my department who works on quantum logic) waving around the line from around Quine's Two Dogmas that no statement is immune to revision. Last month someone used it to argue we could in principle revise the law of non-contradiction if the data pushed hard enough, and last week a different person used it to claim mathematics is empirical in just the same sense physics is. Both feel like overreaches but I couldn't say exactly where the one-liner stops doing work for them. Can you lay out the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing -- from the weak versions basically everyone accepts to the strong versions hardly anyone should -- and for each one say whether it's plausible and whether it actually supports the use it was put to in those two cases? A list I can keep in my seminar notes would be perfect, and please flag which readings are genuinely contentious versus which are just truism-adjacent. Also, if some readings only go through given the rest of Quine's package (like the confirmation holism or the attack on analyticity), say so, since the people quoting it usually aren't buying all of that.
