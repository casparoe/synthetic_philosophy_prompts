A member of my reading group has produced an argument to the effect that a perfectly rational agent can starve to death between two bales of hay, and I cannot find the false step. Here it is as he states it. (1) To choose rationally is to choose for a reason, where a reason is a consideration that counts in favour of what you choose. (2) A consideration counts in favour of an option only if it counts against the alternatives to that option: a consideration that told with equal force for every option you face would not guide you towards any of them, and something that guides you towards nothing is not a reason for choosing. (3) Buridan's ass stands between two bales alike in every respect the ass can perceive, so every consideration available to it tells with exactly equal force for each bale. (4) By (2), the ass has no reason to choose either bale; at most it has a reason for eating rather than not eating, which is indifferent between the bales. (5) To choose an option for which you have no reason is to choose arbitrarily, and it is irrational to choose arbitrarily when refraining remains open. (6) So the rational ass makes no choice and starves. (7) But it cannot be that practical reason requires starvation while standing between two bales of hay: if it did, the more perfectly rational the agent, the more reliably it would perish, and rationality would be a defect rather than a guide.

He accepts (7) and concludes that practical reason issues no requirement in cases of perfect symmetry — the ass's predicament is neither rational nor irrational to resolve, and reason simply runs out. That conclusion troubles me less than the route to it, because the route seems to prove far too much. If (2) is right, then whenever two options are so close that I cannot tell them apart — two restaurants that seem to me equally good, two near-identical job offers — every consideration available to me tells with equal force for both, and by (5) my eventual choice is irrational. That is a great deal of everyday choosing to convict. Yet I cannot simply toss (2) aside: it seems to capture something real about what guidance is, and if instead I say that the ass does have a reason to take the left bale, I cannot say what that reason is, since nothing distinguishes the left bale. I have considered letting the ass just pick — I gather some people hold that picking without preferring can be rational — but if picking is not done for a reason, it is exactly what (5) forbids, and if it is done for a reason, I am back to needing the reason.

Go through the argument and tell me precisely where it goes wrong, if it does. Name the premise or inference you reject, explain why it looked acceptable to us when taken on its own, and then price the alternatives: what my colleague keeps and what he gives up if he is right that reason runs out in ties, and what I have to swallow if I cling to (2) and insist that tied choices are somehow made rationally after all. Please answer in continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the sort of thing one could send as a long letter.
