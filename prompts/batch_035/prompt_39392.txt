I'm a PhD student in HPS working on a chapter about Carnap's politics, and I've got notes for an essay that I'd love feedback on before I draft it properly. The idea is that Carnap's principle of tolerance and his views on social/economic planning aren't two separate things but one package. Roughly: Carnap treats the choice of a linguistic framework as a practical decision to be made on pragmatic grounds (fruitfulness, efficiency, fit with purposes) rather than a matter of discovering the one true structure, and I want to argue the same "planner's stance" runs through his politics -- economies, like languages, are artifacts that can be deliberately redesigned, and the question of which arrangement to adopt is settled by open deliberation about shared purposes, not by discovering the "natural" economic order. So the tolerance principle is, in a way, political philosophy smuggled into logic: anti-absolutism about frameworks, anti-absolutism about social orders.

Supporting threads I've got: his youth in the socialist-leaning circles around the Vienna Circle (Neurath especially, with the whole planning economy debate in the background), the early-30s talk where he first aired the tolerance idea and explicitly analogized theoretical positions to practical decisions, his later social-democratic gradualism, and his world government activism once in the US. There's also the fact that he mostly kept politics out of print, so the case has to be partly reconstructive, which I actually want to own rather than hide.

The tension I want to face head on: tolerance says no framework is intrinsically correct, only more or less useful for purposes, but Carnap's pragmatism quietly leans on values like fruitfulness and simplicity that function like quasi-objective constraints. Same in politics -- his planning needs some substantive value commitment (basically welfare, less suffering) that the tolerance principle itself can't ground. I defintely don't want to resolve this by fiat; I want to argue he's fine with the value commitments being themselves revisable, but I'm not sure that isn't just Neurath's boat without the sailing metaphor.

So, comments please: (1) does the framework-planning analogy do actual argumentative work, or is it just a nice rhetorical parallel that a referee will call decoration? (2) Is the tension in the last paragraph fatal or survivable, and are there resources in Carnap (explication? instrumental vs. value rationality?) that help? (3) Where would a historian of the Vienna Circle push back hardest on the political half of the reconstruction? And if you think the whole thing collapses into "Vienna Circle leftism was a known thing, so what" -- the so-what is supposed to be that tolerance gets a non-formal motivation -- tell me whether that's actually a new claim or something people have already argued. Also happy to hear if some part of the notes is just wrong, I'm seperating the philology from the philosophy as I go and might have gotten sloppy somewhere.
