A friend who took a philosophy seminar on death has been pressing Samuel Scheffler's Death and the Afterlife on me, and he summarized the central argument like this: "Scheffler's claim is that what sustains the value and meaning of our lives is not the assumption that we ourselves keep living, but the assumption that humanity keeps going after we die — he calls this the collective afterlife. To bring it out, he asks you to imagine a pill that makes everyone infertile: once you knew humanity only had about thirty years left, life would lose its meaning altogether. Nobody would bother finishing the cathedral, or searching for a cancer cure, or saving for retirement, so those things evidently never had value independent of humanity's future. And that's the payoff: it shows that our own survival matters less to us than we think, and that the fear of death is really, at bottom, a fear of extinction."

I'm inclined to find this persuasive as stated, but a couple of things nag at me. He speaks as if Scheffler flatly predicts that civilization's confidence would collapse, when I'd gathered the book is more tentative about how we would actually react; and I have a feeling the book distinguishes several doomsday scenarios — one where everyone dies shortly after you do, and one involving infertility — and my friend may have merged them. Please go through his summary piece by piece: say what a defender of Scheffler would accept as accurate, where it overstates a hedged or speculative claim into a confident prediction, which distinctions the book insists on that the summary flattens, and — most importantly — whether his final conclusion, that the fear of death is "really" a fear of extinction, follows from the defensible parts of the argument or is instead a distortion of what Scheffler says about which assumption is more deeply embedded in our valuing. I would also like to know whether the summary's inference from "the pill would undermine our projects" to "those projects never had independent value" is a step Scheffler takes himself or an error my friend has introduced.
