I'm preparing a lecture on deflationary theories of truth for my philosophy of language seminar and want to use Collodi's The Adventures of Pinocchio as the running example. Read the novel through deflationism. The story gives truth an observable physical signature — most famously in the scene where Pinocchio lies to the Fairy with the blue hair about the gold coins and a thousand woodpeckers have to chisel his nose back down — and this seems to present truth as a robust, detectable property, exactly what the deflationist denies when claiming the truth predicate is a mere device of disquotation and generalization. Then push the fairy's rule one step further: stipulate that the nose grows exactly when Pinocchio asserts something false, and have him say, "My nose is growing now." That is a liar-style paradox built entirely out of empirical vocabulary plus the story's biconditional, with no semantic predicate in sight. Does this break the deflationist? Also note that Collodi's nose actually tracks lying rather than falsity — it grows when Pinocchio intends to deceive, including about matters he has no way of verifying — so the story quietly conflates insincerity with falsehood, a distinction the deflationist arguably needs. I'd like a reading that takes both the novel and the debate seriously: where the story resists the deflationary reading, where it quietly supports it, and what it dramatizes about truth that the standard arguments alone don't.
