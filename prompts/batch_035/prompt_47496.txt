I'm a first-year grad student and I'm TAing intro ethics for the first time this semester — up till now I only ever graded for symbolic logic, where an answer is either a valid derivation or it isn't, so the whole partial-credit-on-essays thing is new territory for me. We just gave the midterm, and there's one answer I want a careful read on before I commit to a number. The question was:

"In a short essay: (1) Explain Hume's point about the move from 'is' to 'ought' in the Treatise. (2) Explain what Moore called the naturalistic fallacy and how his Open Question Argument is supposed to support it. (3) Say whether these are the same point or different points, and whether either one establishes that no ethical conclusion can follow from purely descriptive premises. Illustrate with an example of each."

Here's the student's answer, typos and all:

"Hume points out in Book 3 of the Treatise that writers on morality go along making claims about what is the case and then suddenly shift to claims about what ought to be the case, without ever explaining how the transition was made. He doesn't say the move is impossible, just that you can't get there without smuggling in some normative premise that nobody bothers to state. That's the is-ought gap.

Moore's naturalistic fallacy is a different complaint. In Principia Ethica he argues that 'good' names a simple, non-natural property, so it can't be defined in natural terms like pleasure or desire-satisfaction. His Open Question Argument: for any natural property N you try to identify good with, it remains an open question whether things that have N are good, and if the question stays open the definition has failed. Note that this isn't a fallacy in the strict logic sense at all, it's more a mistake about meaning. So the naturalistic fallacy is committed whenever someone claims something is good because it's natural, like saying herbal medicine must be better than synthetic drugs because it comes from plants. The is-ought gap is about inference and the naturalistic fallacy is about definition, so they're two separate worries.

But Moore's argument goes further. Once we know 'good' can't be defined in natural terms, we also know that no ought-statement can be derived from is-statements, because without a natural definition of good there's no valid bridge from facts to values. So Hume and Moore were really after the same conclusion, one through logic and one through meaning. Example of the gap: humans are naturally aggressive, so we ought to let our kids settle things with fights."

It's the kind of answer that reads smoothly, which is exactly the kind I don't trust my gut on.
