My philosophy reading group is doing Mill this month and I want to bring a case to argue about, but I want to bring it in a disciplined way instead of everyone just shouting. So here's the assignment I'd like you to do:

Take the Skokie affair of 1977 and run it through Mill's harm principle. Quick facts so we're on the same page: a small group of neo-Nazis announced they wanted to march in uniform in Skokie, Illinois, a Chicago suburb that happened to be home to a large number of Holocaust survivors. The village threw up legal obstacle after legal obstacle to stop it; the ACLU, to enormous public blowback, took the Nazis' side and got the restrictions struck down, and in the end the march in Skokie never actually happened (they held a small rally in Chicago instead).

Here's the wrinkle I keep snagging on. Mill himself, in On Liberty, draws this distinction I want you to work with — this is him, chapter 3, verbatim:

"An opinion that corn-dealers are starvers of the poor, or that private property is robbery, ought to be unmolested when simply circulated through the press, but may justly incur punishment when delivered orally to an excited mob assembled before the house of a corn-dealer, or when handed about among the same mob in the form of a placard."

So Mill's caveat is about *instigation*: the same words are protected in the press but not in front of the house, because there they're likely to trigger a mischievous act. Now map that onto Skokie, and notice that the geometry comes out weird. In Mill's example the speech is dangerous because of what the *audience* will do to a third party. In Skokie, the claim was about what the speech does *to the audience itself* — psychological injury to the survivors standing there watching swastikas go past their houses. So the questions I want answered, carefully:

1. Who's the corn-dealer in this mapping? Is it even the same kind of case, or does Mill's instigation caveat not actually reach it? Be honest about whether Mill's own tools cover "speech that re-traumatizes a specific, identifiable audience chosen precisely for their vulnerability."
2. Can psychological harm to the survivors count as *harm* in the harm principle's sense, as opposed to mere offense? Spell out what turns on that. If it's only offense, the standard reading says restriction isn't justified; if it's harm, it might be. Where exactly is the line, and is there any principled way to place Skokie on it that doesn't collapse into "restrict whatever hurts people's feelings," which Mill obviously can't accept?
3. Where is the harm principle just silent here — and I mean genuinely silent, not stretched to speak. If the best move is to say Mill's framework needs something it doesn't have (like a separate account of offense or of the special claims of particular audiences), say that plainly rather than pretending the principle handles it.
4. And then run it the other direction: what does Skokie show *about* the harm principle? Is it an anomaly it never anticipated, a case that quietly reveals the principle needs an amendment, or — the reading my friend keeps pushing — a case that actually vindicates Mill, because the ACLU position turned out fine in the end?

One more constraint: don't smuggle in the standard First Amendment law doctrine (strict scrutiny, fighting words, all that) as if it settles the philosophical question. The village of Skokie lost in court; that tells me what American law says, not what Mill's principle implies. Keep the analysis philosophical.

Doesn't need to be super long, maybe 600-800 words, but I want the mapping done for real — actors, harms, options — not vibes. Thanks!
