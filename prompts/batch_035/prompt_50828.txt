I write historical novels, and I've got a weird problem that I think philosophers actually have names for. My new book is set in 1890s Vienna, and it's stuffed with little factual claims -- how long the train to Prague took, whether the Graben was lit by electric arc lamps by then, what coffee cost, whether a bank clerk would've had Sunday off. Individually I'm maybe 97% confident in each one; I checked most at some point. But there are something like 600 of them, and from past books I know that whenever I do find an error, it was always in something I'd been exactly that confident about. So by my own lights I basically know some sentences in this manuscript are false -- I just don't know which. And then there's the acknowledgements page, where convention says I write something like "any remaining errors are mine" while simultaneously standing behind every sentence in the book. A friend told me this is the preface paradox, and that it's cousins with something called the lottery paradox, and that whether it's okay to assert each of 600 claims at 97% while accepting that the whole lot together contains falsehoods is an actual live issue.

Write me a procedure I can actually run on the manuscript before it goes to copyedit. For each factual claim: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as good enough to assert flat-out; when to hedge in the prose; when to just cut or make the claim uncheckable (fudge the street name, pick a different battle); and where it's genuinely just a judgement call I can't dress up as a rule. I want to be able to run it on a single sentence in a few minutes, since I can't re-research 600 things on deadline. Then run it on three claims from the actual draft: (a) the travel time of the morning express from Vienna to Prague in 1893, which I got from a schedule I found once and can't refind; (b) a line where a character mentions his father dying at the Battle of Wagram, where I picked Wagram because it fit the birthdate I'd already locked in for the father; and (c) the coffee prices, which I honestly half-invented and then lost track of which ones are which. I want at least one of these to end with the procedure making me do something I'd rather not do. And finally tell me straight: is the acknowledgements sentence -- believing there are errors in the book while asserting the book -- actually coherent, or is it just a polite lie we let authors tell, and should the procedure change how I write it?
