I'm in a reading group that's been on the origins of WWI for about a year now and we are completely stuck on the Fischer thing. Quick setup for you: Fritz Fischer argued in his big 1961 book (Griff nach der Weltmacht, English title Germany's Aims in the First World War) that the German leadership deliberately used the July crisis to start a world war, that the annexationist aims were already there beforehand and the September Program of 1914 just wrote down what the elite had wanted all along, and that you have to explain German foreign policy through German domestic politics — the power elite, the pressure groups, continuity running back before 1914. The West German historical establishment went ballistic at the time; people basically accused him of recycling Allied propaganda from Versailles. Then sixty years later Clark's The Sleepwalkers comes out (2012), redistributes agency back onto everyone — Russia mobilizing first, France egging Russia on, Austria hungary wanting a localized war — and suddenly respectable people are saying we're back to shared responsibility again. And our group, after a year, is exactly where we started: the pro-Fischer people say the documents settle it, the anti-Fischer people say the same documents settle nothing, and nobody has moved an inch.

My working hypothesis is that this was never one debate in the first place. It looks like a fight about documents, but I think it's at least three questions fused together since Versailles: (1) a causal question — who did what in July 1914 and whose risk-taking was decisive; (2) a moral question — what it even means to hold a nation responsible, given that Fischer himself granted Germany didn't want war with Britain, they just knowingly ran the risk, so how does knowingly running a catastrophic risk map onto culpability versus negligence versus bad luck; and (3) a liability question that Article 231 jammed into the whole thing, because war guilt there was a reparations clause, and everyone's intuitions about blame have been contaminated by who-pays ever since. There's maybe a fourth thing too: is the unit of analysis even coherent — holding Germany responsible treats a state like a person, and the whole fight in the sixties was soaked in anxiety about whether that lands on the German people, the government, or a class of elites, which is presumably why the reaction to Fischer was so unhinged.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Verbal, factual, different questions hiding under one heading, or bedrock intuitions about collective responsibility that nobody on either side can argue for? Diagnose it using what each side actually claims, tell me which of the disanalogies between the causal question and the blame question are doing the real work, and be honest about whether anything — new archival material, a clean conceptual distinction, just dropping the liability framing entirely — could get people off their positions, or whether the fact that the same fight restarted from scratch in 2012, decades after the archives were opened, is itself the answer.
