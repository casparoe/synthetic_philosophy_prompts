Arguments about whether "the alignment problem" is one problem or several never reach a conclusion, and I suspect that is because nobody has stated what a characterization of the problem would have to get right. Fix the purpose as follows: a characterization is adequate if it lets us judge whether a given research agenda counts as progress on the problem. Under that purpose, write the requirements list. It must say which pairs of cases are kept apart: a capable model pursuing the wrong goal versus an incapable model producing bad outcomes; a model that defers to its operators versus one that happens to agree with them; ordinary engineering failures, like a biased classifier, versus failures we would want to call misalignment. It must take some position on a model that faithfully serves an operator with bad ends — is that alignment? It must either stay neutral between moral theories or openly take a stand. It must tell us whether "aligned" is checkable in principle — whether there could be evidence that a system is aligned — or whether demanding that imports a standard from philosophy of science that does not belong here. And it must settle, not straddle, whether the near-term failures, like a model telling users what they want to hear, and the long-run ones, like a very capable optimizer drifting from anything anyone wanted, are the same problem. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it; mark which are non-negotiable and which are the preferences of one camp; and flag any subset you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, since that would mean the list itself is the finding. Close with a few sentences on how the main framings in circulation — value learning, corrigibility, instruction-following, general reliability — do against your list.
