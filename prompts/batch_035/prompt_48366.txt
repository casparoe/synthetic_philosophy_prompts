Write a parody of a software terms-of-service agreement for a startup that sells truth as a subscription service — call the company something like Equivalence Technologies or Truth-as-a-Service, Inc. The founders are committed deflationists, and the fine print must quietly take positions: the service consists of nothing over and above the equivalence schema (it is true that snow is white if and only if snow is white), so the warranty adds nothing to the deliverables, and every clause should be structured so that invoking the truth predicate discharges the obligation without adding any property, relation, or content. The humor must come from getting the philosophy right rather than inventing features of the view: the redundancy of the predicate, its expressive role in generalization (a blanket endorsement clause along the lines of "everything in Clause 4 is true" that exists purely to enable semantic ascent, with the company refusing all other uses), the exclusion of correspondence, coherence, and pragmatic conceptions of truth from the list of covered services, a refund policy for subscribers who expected an account of truth's essence or explanatory role, and a dispute-resolution clause that handles the normativity worry — that truth is the goal or norm of belief and assertion — by simply asserting the object-level sentence and closing the ticket. At least one clause must resolve a customer's complaint that truth must be something deeper by replying with the original sentence minus the truth predicate. The satire should exaggerate what deflationists actually say (their habit of answering every substantive question by disquoting), not caricature it, so that someone who works on truth recognizes each position and finds the piece fair. Format the piece as exactly five numbered clauses, each a sentence or two of dense fine print, with no preamble, headings, or sign-off — the whole thing should read like an excerpt someone screenshotted to post online.
