The demarcation problem is supposed to be a dead debate, right? Laudan basically declared it hopeless back in the 80s, and for a while everyone agreed. But it keeps shambling back anyway — intelligent design court cases, the replication crisis, people arguing about whether evolutionary psych or string theory or economics is "really science" on twitter. So here's my hypothesis about why the underlying debate never moved despite being officially over: "is X scientific?" is secretly doing three different jobs at once, and no single criterion can do all three. Job one: what courts and school boards should treat as science. Job two: whose epistemic authority we should defer to as laypeople. Job three: what standards a practicing researcher should hold themselves to. A mark that makes sense for a judge (something about testability, maybe, but Popper's own criterion looks shaky here — astrology arguably makes falsifiable predictions and fails them, and falsified theories like Newton's get kept and used anyway) might tell a researcher almost nothing useful. What I want: a diagnosis of whether the disagreement is substantive or just three questions wearing one label, whether the recent revivalists (the philosophy of pseudoscience people, family-resemblance approaches, etc.) actually changed the question or just relabeled it, and what would genuinely count as progress — a distinction, a reframing, or an argument that no progress should be expected. And please be careful to mark what's actually settled in this area versus what's genuinely contested among people who know the literature — don't hand me the standard textbook narrative about Popper if phil sci people don't actually agree on it.
