Nomic is a game, invented in 1982 by the philosopher Peter Suber, where changing the rules IS the game. You start with a deliberately boring ruleset: players take turns proposing amendments, everyone votes, whatever passes becomes binding, and you win at 100 points accumulated by die rolls (Suber made the win condition dull on purpose so people would immediately start rewriting it). The initial rules also split rules into immutable and mutable tiers — immutable ones can only be changed by first converting them — but no tier is finally immune, and even the rule saying players must obey the rules can itself be repealed by proper procedure.

I keep going back and forth on what this practice presupposes about rule-authority. On its face it's pure proceduralism: a rule is in force because it was passed under whatever rules were in force, full stop, with zero substantive constraint on content. But everyone at the table only started obeying any of it because they agreed to the initial ruleset, and noone thinks the game could pass a rule that binds you after you stand up and quit. So either the binding force of every passed rule traces back to that original agreement, in which case it's revocable consent wearing proceduralist clothing, or each rule binds simply in virtue of being passed, in which case the whole chain lifts itself by its own bootstraps and I can't see why any random group's "we voted, so now you must" wouldn't work equally well. Which reading does the practice actually presuppose, is the presupposition coherent, and do real legal systems presuppose the same thing at their root or have something Nomic lacks?
