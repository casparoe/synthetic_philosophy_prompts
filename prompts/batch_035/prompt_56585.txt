I teach an upper-level seminar on the philosophy of death and I'm writing the final. I want one essay question on wether death is bad for the person who dies, but I'm sick of answers that just recite "Epicurus says death is nothing to us, Nagel says it deprives you of goods." The question should force students into the timing problem: if you go the deprivation route, when exactly is death bad for you — before it happens (so you're already being harmed while alive), at the moment it happens, or afterwards when there's no subject left to be harmed? And I want the question to make them realize this isn't a puzzle about death only: whatever story they tell has to also cover posthumous harms, like a friend betraying a confidence after you die or your life's work being trashed, since a lot of students treat those as a seperate side issue without seeing the two stand or fall together. Please write the question, a model answer of around 600 words that actually commits to one particular solution to the timing problem and defends it (not a neutral survey of the options), and a marking scheme with five must-hit points worth 4 marks each (total 20), each with a one-line note on how candidates typically fumble it. Also spell out what separates an A answer from a B answer — the B answer being the competent one that name-drops the right people but never takes a stand.
