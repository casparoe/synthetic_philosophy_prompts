At work last quarter our service went down because nobody had configured failover to the backup region. In the postmortem, everyone — including me — kept saying the outage was "caused by the missing failover configuration," and a debate about blame got me trying to figure out whether that way of talking is literally true. Working through it, I built an argument that seems to prove far too much, and I can't find the clean way out:

(1) The outage counterfactually depended on the on-call engineer's not configuring failover: had she configured it, the outage wouldn't have happened.
(2) Whatever an effect counterfactually depends on is a cause of that effect.
(3) So the on-call engineer's omission caused the outage.
(4) But the outage also counterfactually depended on my not configuring failover. I'm on an unrelated team, but had I configured it, the outage wouldn't have happened either.
(5) So by (2), my omission caused the outage too.
(6) By the same reasoning, every employee's omission — indeed every person on Earth's — caused the outage.
(7) The causes of a given effect can't be indiscriminate in this way; whatever causation is, it has to single out some contributors rather than others, or causal explanation and responsibility become vacuous.
(8) Therefore (2) is false: counterfactual dependence is not sufficient for causation.
(9) But if (2) fails, (3) collapses along with (5), and the on-call engineer didn't cause the outage either. By extension nobody ever causes anything by failing to act — which makes negligence law, our postmortems, and every accusation of "letting it happen" track nothing real.

I want to keep (2), or at least the conclusion of (3), since it seems obvious that the person who was supposed to configure failover caused the outage in a way I didn't. Yet I can't see how to accept (2) without accepting (6), and I can't see how to keep (3) while rejecting (5) without already having some principled difference between her omission and mine — which is exactly what I was hoping the argument would give me. Which step or premise should I give up, and what does giving it up cost? If there's a difference between her case and mine, I'd like to know whether it's a difference in the causation itself or only in something we layer on top of it.
