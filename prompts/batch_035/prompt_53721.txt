I spent most of last Saturday arguing with a close friend, a condensed-matter physicist, about whether philosophy is of any use to science. He came armed with the standard artillery: Weinberg's chapter titled Against Philosophy in Dreams of a Final Theory, Hawking's book that opens by declaring philosophy dead and then spends hundreds of pages defending a view about how scientific theories relate to reality, and a quip he attributed to Feynman comparing philosophy of science to ornithology as written for the benefit of birds. What struck me afterwards, when I was replaying the argument, was not who won but a recurring structure I could not name, and I would like to know whether philosophy or anyone else has named it.

The structure is this. At one point I asked him how he decides that string theory counts as science while astrology does not, given that both are unfalsifiable at present. He answered without hesitation with a set of criteria about explanatory unification, fruitfulness, and immersion in a tradition of puzzle-solving. This is, of course, philosophy of science, and the criteria he offered are recognizably drawn from the philosophy of science literature, though he had never read any of it. Later, asked why the philosophy of science is worthless if he had to use it to defend the worth of physics, he shifted ground and produced an argument about the epistemic authority of working practitioners, which is itself a position in the philosophy of expertise. The same structure appeared when he cited Weinberg approvingly: Weinberg's chapter is not a refusal to engage in philosophy but a substantive philosophical essay about what science is and what kinds of intellectual values it should track, delivered as an attack on philosophy. And it appeared again with Hawking, whose pronouncement on the death of philosophy is issued in the course of taking sides on scientific realism, a paradigmatically philosophical dispute.

So the pattern is: a practitioner of field X argues that field Y is useless or worthless to field X, but the argument itself is conducted using the concepts, standards, and methods of field Y, and could not be conducted otherwise, because the questions the argument must answer (what counts as a good argument here, what the boundaries of X are, what value consists in) are Y's questions. The dismissal is not simply false; it is the case that performing the dismissal refutes nothing, since there is no contradiction in saying philosophy is useless and then doing some philosophy badly. But something is going wrong, and I want a term for that something.

I can think of near misses but none of them fits. Performative contradiction does not fit, because there is no strict contradiction: my friend's premises do not entail that he cannot sincerely say what he said. Self-refuting seems too strong for the same reason. Ad hominem or boundary-policing describes the social move but not the epistemic one. And there is a related but distinct phenomenon I have seen people gesture at, where the attack on a field damages itself by its own lights (a relativist thesis asserted as objectively true), which is closer but not identical, because here the attack is not damaged by its own content but parasitic on its target: it must borrow the tools of the very activity it devalues in order to get any purchase at all.

What I want to know is whether this configuration has an established name in philosophy, sociology of knowledge, argumentation theory, or anywhere else, and if not, I would like you to coin a term for it and define it precisely. The label itself is not what matters to me; what matters is the carving. What exactly is the phenomenon, what is it not, and where are its edges? In particular: does it require that the borrowed activity be philosophy, or does it generalize (a mathematician disproving the value of statistics by statistical means, a theologian arguing that reason has no place in theology)? Does it require that the borrower be unaware of the borrowing, or does knowing self-service count too? And how is it distinct from the ordinary fact that criticizing a field requires saying something about it?
