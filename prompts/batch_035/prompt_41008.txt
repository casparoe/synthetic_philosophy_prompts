I'm a PhD student in early modern history, and I'm responsible for the historiography unit of our undergraduate "Introduction to Historical Study" course — 18 students, one 80-minute section. Next week's topic is periodization: whether the 'Middle Ages', the 'Renaissance', and the 'Scientific Revolution' name real features of the past or later inventions that froze particular agendas into the curriculum. I don't want to lecture them into skepticism about period labels; I want them to make cuts first, and only then watch their own cuts betray their assumptions. Design an in-class activity built around a deck of roughly sixty cards, each carrying a dated event, work, or practice from about 400 to 1700 CE — some famous, but many deliberately mundane (changes in plough design, notarial practices, a run of unremarkable letters, an agricultural technique that persists across the entire span) — with the deck composed so that different value-weightings (artistic style, political organization, technology, religious practice, proximity to 'modernity') push the boundaries to visibly different places. Working in small groups, students must produce a museum floor plan with exactly three rooms and labeled doorways between them, plus one sentence of wall text defending each cut, and each group must also place six 'awkward' cards that resist clean assignment — publicly stating, for each, whether the card gets forced into a room or displayed in a doorway. Then give me a debrief that maps what the groups actually did onto the historiography: why 'Middle' is only meaningful relative to someone's chosen endpoints, what it means that these labels were minted by writers arguing about their own present rather than neutrally sorting the past, and how a group's omitted criteria function like an unstated thesis. Close with an honest note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and on which feature of the periodization debate — for instance, that real period labels also do downstream work, licensing what counts as a legitimate research question — the activity structurally cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself.
