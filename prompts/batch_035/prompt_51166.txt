I've come around to a view I'd summarize like this: on the whole, religious people and religious societies tend to behave better than secular ones — more honest, more willing to keep promises, more charitable — and I doubt a society that goes fully secular can sustain the level of everyday trust we currently rely on. I didn't get this from a preacher; I worked it out myself from things I've noticed over the years, and I want to know what would count against it. I'm not looking for a defense of my position. I want the observations, arguments, and possible cases that should move me, ranked by how decisive they'd be.

A few complications I'm already aware of, so you can build on them rather than repeat them back to me. First, I know some of the most trusting, lowest-corruption countries in the world are ones where hardly anyone goes to church anymore, and I've never been sure what to do with that — is it evidence against me, or is it that those societies are "living off" religious capital built up earlier, as people sometimes say? Second, I've heard that when researchers test generosity and honesty experimentally, the difference between religious and nonreligious participants often shrinks or vanishes, and that when it doesn't, the religious participants are mainly nicer to people they take to be co-religionists — that bothers me more than the first thing, because it suggests the effect might be tribal rather than moral. Third, there's the "real believer" problem: whenever a religious society behaves badly, the response is that those people weren't truly following the religion, and I can see that move being made forever without anything ever counting against the view.

So beyond the ranked list of defeaters, I'd like you to address three specific questions. (1) Which of the things I mentioned, or others you raise, would a reasonable holder of my view have to concede genuinely counts against it, as opposed to merely being awkward? Please be honest here — I'd rather know my view is exposed than be comforted. (2) Has my view, with all its available escape hatches (the inherited-capital story, the "real believers" story, the "religion is really about inner transformation, not social statistics" story), been qualified to the point where nothing could ever disconfirm it — and if so, is that a fatal problem for a view of this kind, or is it acceptable for views like this, the way it arguably is elsewhere? (3) What does my view actually predict that its rivals don't — what would have to happen, or be observed, for it to come out ahead rather than merely survive? I ask that last one because I've realized I don't know the answer, and I suspect a view that can't say what it predicts is in trouble even if every objection so far has been beatable.

Please write your answer in American English.
