My reading group has spent the better part of a year on Wittgenstein's On Certainty, and my friend and I have deadlocked on what we've come to call the hinge epistemology question. We both accept the core hinge idea: some certainties (that the earth has existed for a long time, that I have two hands, that other people have minds) function not as things we have established but as the untested background against which we test everything else, and doubting them is not a moment of intellectual caution but a breakdown of ordinary epistemic practice. Where we split is on the status of these certainties. My friend holds that hinges are non-epistemic: arational, animal certainties that show themselves in our acting rather than in our reasoning, and that treating them as items of knowledge is precisely the confusion Wittgenstein was diagnosing. I hold that hinges are epistemic after all: they can be rationally held, and even known, just not in the way ordinary empirical propositions are known — say, because knowing them is compatible with being unable to transmit that knowledge, or because their justification is structural rather than evidential.

Several times we discovered we were arguing past each other on sub-questions neither of us had properly separated: whether calling a certainty non-epistemic removes it from the domain of knowledge, or only from one particular theory of knowledge; whether Moore, in raising his hands and announcing that he knows he has hands, says something false, something idle, or something true that fails to do the work he wanted it to do; whether the image of a riverbed of thought that can harden into sand and sand into rock tells in favour of my friend's position (hinges are simply part of our unreflective animal nature) or mine (since riverbeds shift, we can sensibly track how hinges are held, lost, and replaced, which looks like rational assessment at some level); and whether our dispute is partly verbal, hinging on what we each are prepared to count as an epistemic norm.

Please draft the document we should have produced after a good-faith adversarial collaboration: a joint statement setting out, first, the claims both of us can sign about hinges — about their role in testing and doubting, about how they differ from ordinary certainties, about the diagnosis of Moore and of Cartesian scepticism; second, our remaining disagreements, each stated in wording the holder of the view would accept as fair; third, the genuine cruxes, distinguishing, where you can, disagreements that are substantive from ones that turned out to be about words (and say which words and why); and, for each crux, the kind of argument or case both of us would agree bears on it, with the prediction each side would commit to in advance — for instance, what each of us should expect if a hinge is genuinely lost, as perhaps when someone stops trusting their own memory or the continuity of the world around them. Please format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table, and write the whole thing in British English.
