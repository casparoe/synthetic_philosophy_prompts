I am a professor of philosophy who has, for the past two years, convened a graduate seminar on the interpretation of probability. Within it, two doctoral students have hardened into the champions of opposing camps, and their running argument has become the seminar's central spectacle. The first — call her the Keynesian — holds that probability, as Keynes presented it in the Treatise on Probability, is a logical relation between a proposition and the body of evidence for it; that some such relations are not numerically measurable and not even comparable with one another; that the weight of an argument, the amount of relevant evidence one possesses, varies independently of its probability and can matter to action on its own; and that the later Keynes of the 1937 Quarterly Journal of Economics paper went further still, claiming that for some economically consequential prospects there is no basis at all on which to form a calculable probability, so that the decision-maker is confronted with genuine uncertainty rather than risk. The second — call him the Bayesian — takes his bearings from Ramsey's response to Keynes: degrees of belief are real psychological states, manifest in betting dispositions and constrained by coherence into conformity with the probability calculus; any agent who is not Dutch-bookable has precise probabilities for every proposition, whether or not frequencies or logical relations are available to ground them; and where an agent claims to have no probability at all, this is either vague possession of a precise probability or a failure of rationality, not a third epistemic condition. Between them they have argued about the weight of argument, about one-off unrepeatable events, about whether the imprecise-credence literature of recent decades vindicates Keynes or merely relabels his vagueness, and about whether any part of the dispute is merely verbal.

I intend to hold them to an adversarial collaboration before the seminar dissolves. Please draft the joint statement the two of them could sign after such a collaboration. I want the following components:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — about what Ramsey established, about what the Treatise actually committed Keynes to, about the difference between vagueness and non-measurability, and about the existence of situations that defeat naive frequency reasoning — formulated so that neither signatory could complain of slant.

2. A table of their residual disagreements, each row phrased so that its own side would accept the wording of its own view; include their disagreement over whether the practical demands of choice force precise probabilities even where evidence does not supply them.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party, with an argument, case or thought experiment both would accept as bearing on each, and each party's prediction about that case recorded in advance. I suspect the weight of argument is one crux and the Dutch book machinery is another, but you should determine this rather than defer to me.

4. A settled treatment of their recurring seminar case: a small university endowment committee must decide whether to pay a substantial premium for insurance against a specific one-off geopolitical contingency over the coming decade — no frequency base, thin analogues, and reputational costs on both sides of the decision. Say what each camp would have the committee do, what each would count as the committee having decided badly, and whether anything in the joint statement helps the committee beyond the honest admission that it must decide anyway.

5. An explicit verdict on whether any part of the dispute is verbal — about the word 'probability' rather than about decision, belief or evidence — and if so, which part, and what survives the dissolution.

Please write in British English throughout. Do not rely on quotations or textual citations: where you attribute a view to Keynes, to Ramsey, or to the imprecise-credence tradition, characterise it in your own words, and do not append any bibliography or reading list. The test I shall apply is simple: I will read the draft to each student in turn, and if either can identify a formulation that concedes too much or smuggles in a victory for the other side, the draft has failed. Write me the version that passes.
