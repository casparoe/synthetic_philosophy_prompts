me and a friend have been arguing about Fitch's paradox of knowability for a couple weeks and I want a second opinion, with numbers.

Quick setup so we're on the same page: assume every truth is knowable — that is, for any true proposition, it's possible that someone comes to know it (this is roughly the anti-realist/verificationist thought that meaningful truths must be accessible to us somehow). Add very minimal epistemic logic: knowledge is factive (you can only know what's true) and if someone knows a conjunction, they thereby know each conjunct. Now suppose some truth p is in fact never known by anyone. Then the conjunction of p with the claim that p is never known by anyone is also a truth. By knowability, that conjunction could come to be known. But if someone knew it, they'd know p — so p would be known by someone, contradicting the second conjunct. So the possibility is impossible, meaning that conjunction can't be knowable, meaning it can't be true, meaning no truth is never known — omniscience, from two premises that looked totally innocent. Fitch published it in the 60s, though apparently the proof was sitting in a referee report from Church years earlier.

My friend says: this is a clean refutation of verificationist anti-realism, the knowability principle is the whole position, done, move on. I say: the anti-realist never meant the principle to apply to truths that are themselves about what gets known — restricting it that way seems to save the spirit, and the paradox is really a lesson in how not to formalize a slogan.

What's your actual credence that the anti-realist really has to abandon the core knowability idea, as opposed to restructure it? Decompose it for me, at least into:

- the probability that the unrestricted principle (knowability applies to literally every truth, including the ones about our own knowledge states) was essential to the position and not just an overreach in how it got stated
- the probability that the known patches — e.g. restricting to truths that don't embed knowledge attribution, or moving to "for all we know, p could be known", or the versions with the actuality operator / tense restrictions — still capture enough of the anti-realist thought to be worth calling anti-realism
- the probability that those patches either reintroduce triviality somewhere else or end up so weakened that they can't do any real anti-realist work (like underwriting meaning-theoretic claims)
- anything else you think belongs in the product

Give me a number and a one-line justification for each term, then combine them, then tell me which term the final credence is most sensitive to and what argument or result would move that term the most. And no "depends on your favored theory of truth" hedging — I want the number and where you're least sure of it. If you think the whole framing is off because e.g. the interesting question isn't salvaging the principle but what work it was supposed to do in the first place, say so, but still commit to the number first.
