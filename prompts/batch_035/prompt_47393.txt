My closest friend and I have been arguing, on and off for a decade and most recently for an entire evening last week, about whether software could ever matter morally in the way that a person or an animal does. She works in machine learning and holds that consciousness is a matter of functional organisation: any system implementing the right sort of control structure — integrated representations of its own internal states, goals against which it can suffer setbacks, some capacity to monitor and report on its own condition — would have moral status regardless of whether it is realised in neurons or in silicon, and that our treatment of increasingly capable systems is therefore a live ethical question now rather than a science-fiction one. I hold that this smuggles in the conclusion: on my view, felt experience depends on the physical realisation, and what look like expressions of distress from a trained model are better understood as statistical mimicry of distress-talk than as distress, so that attributing moral status to current systems rests on a confusion. After last week we have both conceded that neither of us will convert the other, but we would like a record of the argument that neither of us would be embarrassed to sign. Please draft the joint statement we should be able to agree on: a numbered section of claims we both accept, covering what is actually known about the internal workings of present-day systems, whether machine consciousness is at least logically possible, and what moral uncertainty reasonably demands of us in the meantime; then a table of our remaining disagreements, each formulated so that the side holding it would accept the wording; then the cruxes between us — I suspect they include whether behavioural tests could in principle settle questions about felt experience, and how much weight to give the mere possibility of moral status when acting under uncertainty — with, for each crux, the kind of argument or evidence that would genuinely count against each of our positions, and our respective predictions written down in advance so that they can be checked. Please also apply the cruxes to two concrete cases: a language model that, after training with human feedback, comes reliably to produce reports of aversive inner states; and a simple reinforcement-learning agent trained on a homeostatic objective that is penalised whenever it drifts from its target. Finally, tell us candidly which parts of our dispute are substantive and which are, at bottom, verbal.
