At my department's AI safety reading group this week, two of the more senior members — a postdoc who works on agent foundations and a philosophy PhD student who mostly works on well-being but has strong opinions about everything — got into a long argument about the orthogonality thesis, and the rest of us lost the thread halfway through. The disagreement started when the postdoc dismissed a paper we were reading by saying the orthogonality thesis is close to a tautology: intelligence and goals are just different axes of a system, so of course any level of capability is compatible with any final goal — a superintelligent chess engine could just as well be programmed to promote paperclips, flowers, or the glory of a long-dead emperor, and being smarter at modeling the world tells you nothing about which of those it wants. The philosopher objected that this makes the thesis trivially true and therefore useless: in actual intelligent systems, goals aren't inert data files plugged into an otherwise neutral reasoning engine, they are partly constituted by the whole architecture, so the claim "any goal with any intelligence" needs an argument that goal-content and intelligence-relevant features can be cleanly separated, and he didn't see one.

The postdoc's response was that the philosopher was conflating two different claims: the orthogonality thesis is a compatibility claim about what pairs of intelligence and final goals are possible, not a prediction about what pairs are likely, and nothing about goals being "constituted by architecture" shows that a given level of intelligence can't coexist with a given final goal — it shows at most that some engineering routes to that pairing are harder. The philosopher then made what seemed to me the sharpest move of the evening: he argued that the compatibility claim is not obviously true either, because of reflective consistency. A genuinely intelligent system with an incoherent goal structure — one that's Dutch-bookable, or that flip-flops under the same circumstances — will eventually notice this about itself, and a system that notices incoherence in its own goals and is smart enough to care will have pressure to revise them toward coherence; so "high intelligence with an arbitrary, including a thoroughly irrational, goal" is a pairing that can't be stabilized, and the thesis fails for exactly the goals that matter most when you're worried about misaligned systems. The postdoc answered that this smuggles in a transition from "will revise toward coherence" to "will revise away from the original goal," which he said is a non sequitur: a system can tidy up its paperclip preference into a coherent form and still be a paperclip maximizer, so reflection changes the goal's formulation, not its content. The philosopher's last word was that formulation and content can't be kept apart that cleanly — tidying up a vague goal requires resolving ambiguities in some particular direction, and which directions get resolved is not determined by the goal itself, so by the time the system is coherent, the goal has already been rewritten, and the orthogonality thesis only holds for goals described so abstractly that it no longer tells us anything about real systems.

The room split and eventually dissolved into side conversations. I have to write the minutes, and honestly I couldn't follow which claims were supposed to depend on which others — is the thesis about possibility or about likelihood, is reflective revision a counterexample to it or just an objection to a lazy way of using it, and does any of this actually change what the instrumental convergence arguments downstream are entitled to conclude? Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: for each step, say whether the move succeeds, whether it actually engages with what the other person claimed, or whether it slides between different versions of the thesis (e.g., a formal compatibility claim versus a practical claim about how distinguishable differently-goal-directed intelligent systems will be). Also flag anything important that both of them seem to be missing — in particular, I want to know whether the philosopher's reflective-consistency point is a genuine problem for the orthogonality thesis as it is standardly used, or only for a strawman version of it.
