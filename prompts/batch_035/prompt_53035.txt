I am a psychometrician. My day-to-day work is cross-cultural survey methodology: construct validity, reliability, measurement invariance and differential item functioning across countries, response styles, acquiescence and extremity biases, questionnaire translation, mode effects, and response shift in longitudinal quality-of-life studies. I have no philosophy background. Every spring I get dragged into arguments about the World Happiness Report — journalists treat a single league table as measuring "happiness," our own item-level work suggests the life-evaluation ladder and the affect items are not tapping quite the same construct, and last month a policy colleague asked me whether a government should explicitly set out to top the rankings. I suspect there is a real philosophical debate underneath all of this that I am only ever hearing in caricature, and I want it translated into my language.

Please restate, in my vocabulary, the main philosophical theories of well-being: hedonism (well-being as the balance of pleasant over unpleasant experience), the desire- or preference-satisfaction view, and objective-list theories — and also the position, to the extent it is one, that what the reports measure is best understood as life satisfaction, a construct distinct from both momentary affect and preference fulfillment. For each theory, tell me what claim it is making about the latent construct — what the true score is, so to speak; what would count as psychometric evidence for or against it (for instance, whether a global life-satisfaction item and experience-sampling measures of momentary affect should be expected to load on a single factor, or whether divergences between them — the familiar pattern where a country reports high life evaluation but unremarkable positive affect — should be treated as a validity problem, a definitional dispute, or ordinary measurement error); and where the translation forces a distortion — places where the philosophical claim smuggles in something normative that my vocabulary cannot carry without reinterpreting it as an empirical claim. Where the philosophers' arguments map onto things I already know — the experience machine as a discriminant-validity probe for whether affect and preference measures are separable constructs; adaptation and the hedonic treadmill as a form of response shift or scale recalibration; the observation that life evaluations track national income much more tightly than daily affect does — say so, but be honest about what the mapping smooths over.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the philosophical debate have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all: the question of whether well-being is the sort of thing that admits one correct operationalization rather than several admissible ones; whether a self-report can be invalid not because of bias or noise but because it is registering contentment with an unjust arrangement rather than the quality of a life; the worry that satisfied preferences can nonetheless be bad for the person whose preferences they are. For each such residue, say whether it points to something my vocabulary is missing or to something the original claim never made as precise as it sounded. And — the part I most want you not to fudge — the normative question of whether a country should try to top the rankings: can that be rendered as a measurement problem at all (Goodhart-style indicator capture, teaching to the test, reactivity of the measure to being targeted), or does it come through the translation untouched? If it comes through untouched, I want your view on whether that is a limitation of the psychometric framework or a sign that the question was never an empirical one to begin with.
