For most of my working life I was a cellular physiologist — I spent thirty years measuring the turnover of synaptic proteins and the migration of membrane constituents in cultured neurons, and I retired with the firm conviction that the brain I studied was, materially speaking, a standing wave: nearly all of its matter exchanged over years, its organization all that persisted. Since retiring I have taken up philosophy of mind in earnest, and I have found, to my mild embarrassment, that this background makes the prospect of mind uploading seem far less exotic to me than it does to most of my philosophical reading. A former colleague from my department, a committed skeptic of the whole enterprise, has lately been pressing an argument on me over our weekly coffee. He concedes the ground I care about and then tries to show it collapses. It troubles me because I can see that it proves too much — it seems to entail that I could be turned, neuron by neuron, into two people — and yet each individual step looks defensible to me. Here is the argument as he states it:

(1) The molecular constituents of my brain are continuously replaced — ion channels, vesicle proteins, cytoskeletal elements — and I demonstrably survive this, so my survival cannot depend on which particular matter constitutes my brain. (2) It must depend instead on the preservation of my brain's functional organization: the pattern of causal roles that its components occupy. (3) In gradual uploading, each biological neuron is replaced, a small fraction at a time, by an implanted device occupying exactly the same causal role — the same afferent and efferent connections, the same input-output profile. (4) At every such step, the functional organization of the system is exactly what it was before, and if the person existing after some step were not me, then some single step — some one-thousandth of my brain, say — would have to be the difference between my existing and not existing, a cutoff no principled reason could possibly mark. (5) So each step preserves me, and by transitivity the fully artificial person at the end of the process is me. (6) But the reasoning is indifferent to what fills the causal roles, so long as they are filled: it would run identically if the replacement neurons were drawn, one at a time, from a living clone of me, with each of my removed neurons simultaneously installed in the corresponding position in the clone's brain. Every step preserves functional organization in my head, and no step is any more "the fatal one" than before. (7) At the end of that process there are two functioning adults, each standing to me as the silicon person does — each reached from me by a chain of steps, none of which could be my last. Both would be me. But they are two, and I cannot be two. (8) Therefore the step-by-step reasoning that supported gradual uploading was a sorites illusion all along, and gradual replacement no more preserves me than teleportation or a destructive scan would.

My colleague presents this as a refutation of uploading outright, and I confess I cannot locate the error with any confidence. My own suspicion is that something slips between (4) and (5) — between "no single step could be the fatal one" and "each step preserves me" — but he anticipates the objection: if I deny that slide, he says, I must either identify which one-thousandth of a brain is the fatal one, which neither of us believes, or admit that there is no fact of the matter about when I cease to exist, which he takes to be a reductio of the entire functionalist picture. I have gathered from my reading that philosophers have worried about branching cases of this general shape, though I have not worked through the primary literature carefully enough to know whether they dissolve the problem or merely pay a price to avoid it. I would very much rather keep the conclusion of (5) than accept the conclusion of (8) — I find the gradual-replacement result congenial, and my old laboratory work inclines me toward it. So tell me plainly: which step or inference in the argument should I reject, why did it look sound to begin with, and precisely what do I give up in rejecting it? I want to know what the least costly exit is, and I want to know what my colleague has to pay for his own exit, so that I can judge whose bill is smaller.
