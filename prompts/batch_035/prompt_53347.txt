My coworker, who has read a bit of Dawkins, keeps pressing a line on me: ideas are like genes. They pass from head to head the way genes pass from parent to child, they vary in the copying, and the ones that end up everywhere are the ones that are best at getting themselves spread -- catchy, memorable, good at hijacking our emotions -- not the ones that are true or good for us. He uses this to draw two conclusions. First, any belief that is genuinely widespread -- religions, national myths, folk wisdom, even the stuff everyone in our office just knows -- should be suspect on arrival, since popularity tracks catchiness rather than correctness. Second, bad ideas are nearly impossible to kill, because they have been shaped by centuries of selection pressure to survive exactly the counterarguments we throw at them, the way bacteria end up resisting antibiotics. I can feel the analogy doing a lot of work here, but I have no sense of where it stops holding. Please set out what is supposed to map onto what -- the gene, the organism, reproduction, fitness, adaptation, the antibiotic -- and then tell me honestly where the mapping breaks down. In particular I want to know which of the breaks actually matter for his two conclusions and which are just places where the comparison is loose but harmless: I can see that ideas spread horizontally between peers rather than only downward from parents, and that an idea's fitness depends on whether the person holding it stays attractive to others, not on whether the idea helps the person, but I don't know how much either difference should worry me. Then say whether the conclusion that widespread beliefs are suspect survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether it was being carried by the analogy the whole time, and if there's a better comparison for what he's trying to say.
