I've just joined the investment committee of a mid-sized private foundation whose charter requires it to operate in perpetuity, and I'm trying to understand what our governing rule actually commits us to philosophically. The practice, concretely: each year we distribute about 4.5% of a three-year trailing average of the portfolio's market value, smoothed precisely so that grantees receive a stable, inflation-adjusted stream and the corpus is preserved in real terms forever. The investment office behind that rule allocates roughly 60% of the portfolio to risky assets, rebalances annually, and is evaluated almost entirely on Sharpe ratios and drawdown behavior; borrowing is effectively prohibited, and a year in which the portfolio falls more than about 20% triggers serious board consternation even when long-run expected returns are unchanged. One of my colleagues, who has been reading about the Kelly criterion, insists the whole apparatus is just fractional Kelly betting dressed up in committee language — that a perpetual owner with no heirs should size bets to maximize the geometric growth rate of the corpus, that our 60% allocation is simply Kelly with a fudge factor set by collective loss aversion, and that the spending rule is the institutional embodiment of log utility. Another colleague finds this recklessly quantitative and says our real commitment is a moral one: no generation of grantees may consume resources that impair any later generation's capacity to do the same work.

What strikes me is that these two rationales seem to pull in different directions, and I can't tell whether our practice is coherent under either. The spending rule treats the future as nearly deterministic — it calibrates the payout to an assumed long-run real return of about 5% and behaves as though the corpus must never decline in expectation — while the portfolio it draws from is aggressively risky and its evaluation is path-sensitive in a way pure expected-return maximization wouldn't be: given two portfolios with identical arithmetic expected returns but different volatility, we reliably prefer the smoother one, and over an infinite horizon that preference looks exactly like preferring the higher geometric mean. So here is my question: what theory of rationality, or of intergenerational obligation, does this practice presuppose? Is it, as my colleague claims, effectively log-utility maximization across generations with the spending rate as the consumption policy that maximizes long-run geometric growth? Or a quasi-Rawlsian constraint that the worst-off future generation must be no worse endowed than the present one? Or is it just institutionalized loss aversion that happens to mimic one of these? I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, then tell me where the practice is underdetermined — which genuinely different views of what the endowment is for would license the very same spending rule and allocation policy — and whether the spending rule and the asset-allocation policy are even consistent with each other in what they presuppose, since one seems to assume stability and the other embraces variance. Finally, tell me concretely what would have to change in the rule if the presupposed view were replaced by one of two rivals: a spend-down view on which the foundation should deliberately exhaust itself over a fixed horizon, or a discounted-utilitarian view on which the expected present value of all future grants is maximized with a modest exponential discount rate. Please lead with your bottom-line diagnosis in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, and be explicit about which parts of the practice are strictly entailed by the view you attribute to it and which merely sit comfortably alongside it.
