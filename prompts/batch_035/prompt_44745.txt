I'm TAing an upper-level seminar on decision theory and the foundations of rational choice, and im grading take-home midterms this week (~40 of them, and we split the stack between graders, so I need to be able to justify whatever I give). The question they answered was worth 30 points and read: "Explain the Dutch book argument for probabilistic coherence, being careful about what it does and does not establish. Then assess whether coherence arguments (Dutch books, money pumps, and anything else in that family) show that a fully rational agent must be an expected utility maximizer. Be explicit about any substantive assumptions these arguments need beyond mere consistency." In the review session we definately flagged that precision about the difference between being incoherent and actually losing money would be rewarded.

Here is one student's answer, pasted as-is:

"The Dutch book argument starts from the idea that your credence in a proposition can be measured by your betting dispositions: your credence in A is the price at which you'd buy or sell a bet that pays $1 if A is true. Suppose your credences violate the probability axioms -- say your credence in A plus your credence in not-A comes to more than 1. Then a bookie can sell you both bets at your own prices, and you've paid more than $1 for a combined payoff of exactly $1, so you lose money in every state of the world. The same holds generally: any violation of the axioms (nonnegativity, additivity over exclusive alternatives) leaves you open to a sure-loss book. Note what the theorem actually says, though: it says you're exploitable, not that you'll be exploited. The bookie has to know your prices to construct the book, and no real bookie can see inside your head. It's also a pragmatic argument rather than an epistemic one: the incoherent bettor is shown to lose money, not to have false beliefs.

Coherence arguments for preferences run the same way. Take an agent with cyclic preferences: A over B, B over C, and C over A. A trader offers them B in exchange for A plus a small fee, then C for B plus a fee, then A for C plus a fee, and they end up right back where they started, minus three fees. So intransitive preferences are exploitable, and in the long run agents with intransitive preferences get driven to bankruptcy -- a competitive market will weed them out, so the argument even gets an evolutionary vindication: the surviving agents are the coherent ones.

Putting the pieces together, coherent credences plus coherent preferences give you the expected utility theorem. The independence axiom is just coherence applied to preferences: if you prefer A to B, you must prefer a lottery that yields A with some probability to the same lottery with B substituted in, and otherwise you're exploitable -- Allais-type preferences violate independence, and sure enough they can be pumped the same way cyclic preferences can. Completeness is forced too, since an agent who can't compare two options can be talked into accepting whatever price they're quoted for either. So the coherence arguments jointly prove that a rational agent maximizes expected utility, and the only assumptions they make are consistency requirements -- nothing substantive about rationality sneaks in beyond consistency itself."
