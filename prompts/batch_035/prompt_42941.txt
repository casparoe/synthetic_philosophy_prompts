so I have to write up my stance on Mary's room / the knowledge argument for a seminar, and before I do I want someone to check whether my five commitments actually fit together. here they are, in no particular order:

1. Physicalism: every fact is a physical fact, including facts about conscious experience.
2. In the Mary case, take the full-strength version — before she leaves the room she has complete knowledge of all the physical facts about color and color vision. no hedging with "well, nobody could really know all that."
3. When she walks out and sees red for the first time, she learns something new, and it's genuinely propositional — a truth she now knows that she could not know before. I reject the ability hypothesis (the "she just gains skills at recognizing and imagining red" move); that always seemed to me to miss the point of the case.
4. Nothing that happens at the release amounts to a new fact coming to light. what's new is her grip on an old fact, not a fact that wasn't already covered by what she had in there.
5. The explanatory gap is real and permanent — no physical story about the brain will ever make phenomenal character intelligible the way molecular motion makes heat intelligible — but that's fine for physicalism becuase the gap lives in our concepts, not in the world.

I can feel that some of these are pulling against each other but I genuinely can't tell wether which of them has to go, or whether they can all be kept. go through them one at a time: exactly five numbered items, one per commitment, a sentence or two each on whether that one is where the trouble is and what it would cost to keep it. no intro, no conclusion, and no separate verdict section — I want the verdict to come out of the five items themselves. and if two of them only look like they clash, say that in the relevant item instead of flagging everything as a problem.
