After reading about Bertrand's paradox, I've concluded that the principle of indifference has been mathematically refuted, but no one seems to have told the statisticians. As I understand the setup, you inscribe an equilateral triangle in a circle and ask for the probability that a randomly drawn chord is longer than the triangle's side. If you draw the chord by picking two random points on the circumference, the answer is 1/3; by picking its midpoint uniformly along a randomly chosen radius, 1/2; by picking its midpoint uniformly from the interior of the disk, 1/4. Since the very same state of total ignorance yields three different probabilities, it seems to me that assigning equal probabilities to cases about which we know nothing is not a neutral default but an outright error. So why do statistics courses still teach us to use uniform priors for parameters we know nothing about? If every flat prior is a disguised Bertrand mistake, shouldn't statisticians be warned off them as firmly as we're warned off the gambler's fallacy?
