been stuck on this one for a while. in my philosophy of language seminar we spent like three weeks on Quine's gavagai argument, and the debate around it seems to have been at a standstill since basically the 70s. Quine says all possible behavioral evidence leaves translation underdetermined: rival manuals can fit every speech disposition a community could ever display, yet translate a rabbit-sentence as rabbit, undetached rabbit part, or rabbit-stage — and there's no extra fact to break the tie. Chomsky's old reply was that this kind of underdetermination is just what science looks like generally — the same data also underdetermines theories about the interior of stars — so it tells us nothing special about meaning. The Quinean comeback is that indeterminacy is meant to run deeper than ordinary scientific underdetermination: even with all the physical facts fixed, translation is still not settled. And there the whole thing has sat for 50 years. my hypothesis is that it's at least partly a verbal dispute about what counts as a fact of the matter — Quine ties facts to physics, while Chomskyans let the best explanatory theory, including psychological ones, settle what the facts are — so maybe the two sides aren't even disagreeing about the same thing. Is that diagnosis right? or is there a genuine substantive issue here about whether meaning is a real natural kind? and given whatever diagnosis you land on, what if anything would count as progress here — some new distinction, a thought experiment, anything — or is this just an ill-posed question we should expect to stay open forever?
