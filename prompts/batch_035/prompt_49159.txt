So here's my situation: I write novels for a living, which mostly means I spend all day inventing people who disagree with each other and never resolve it, and last weekend I got cornered at a book club afterparty by a guy who does quant finance and reads a lot of LessWrong. We were arguing about whether a character in the book we'd just read would really have covered for her sister the way she did — a judgment call, I thought, the kind of thing two people can just see differently. He eventually played what he clearly thought was his trump card: rational people can't agree to disagree, it's some theorem from game theory, so since we'd each heard the other out and neither of us had budged, one of us was, quote, definitionally being irrational — and he smiled in a way that suggested he knew which one. It worked, in the sense that I shut up and got more wine. But it's been bugging me all week, partly because I'm a pushover for people who cite theorems at parties, and partly because, professionally, endless disagreement between reasonable people is my raw material, so if there's a proof it can't happen I'd like to know.

The thing is, I genuinely can't tell what the slogan is even claiming. Is it a math thing about idealized agents who share all their information and end up with identical beliefs — in which case fine, but what does that have to do with the two of us and this book? Is it the claim that actual human disagreement is always evidence somebody's being irrational? Is it something about how argument is pointless because you should just split the difference with whoever you're talking to? He said something about how "once you know what I know and I know what you know, we have to land in the same place," which sounds almost trivially true and also sounds like it could be doing a lot of sneaky work.

So that's what I want from you: lay out the distinct precise claims that one-liner could be making, from the humdrum to the radical, and for each one tell me whether it's actually plausible and whether it gets my book-club guy his conclusion — that one of the two of us was being irrational, rather than something weaker like "neither of us had shared everything relevant." I'm especially curious where judgment calls fit in: is my sense of whether a character rings true even the kind of thing the theorem is about, or does it only govern beliefs about facts out in the world? And tell me which reading the guy at the party was probably reaching for when he used it to end an argument, because if I ever see him again I'd like to have the menu ready. No need to be gentle — if the honest answer is that he was misusing it, I want to know exactly how, in a form I can use at the next afterparty.
