I have spent the better part of fifteen years in the rationalist corner of the internet — the LessWrong threads on functionalism and personal identity, the recurring arguments about uploads and whole-brain emulation, the periodic dust-ups about whether future AI systems will deserve moral consideration — and I have come to believe that the debate over whether the brain is a computer is exactly the kind of stuck debate that adversarial collaborations were invented for. Both sides have long since stopped generating new arguments and mostly generate new ways of restating their priors. Worse, I suspect a decent fraction of the shouting is verbal, because the word "computer" is doing at least double duty: sometimes it means an abstract automaton, sometimes a physical symbol-manipulating device, sometimes a system with a particular kind of causal organization, and the participants freely slide between the senses.

So: please draft the joint statement that a committed computationalist (who holds that the brain literally is a computer, that computation of the right kind suffices for mind, and that artificial systems running the right computations would have experiences) and a committed anti-computationalist (who holds that computation is at best an observer-relative description we project onto physical systems, and that what matters for mind is the specific biological and causal organization of brains, which formal-state talk necessarily misses) could both sign after a good-faith collaboration run under proper adversarial-collaboration norms.

The document should contain:

1. A disambiguation section, agreed by both parties, distinguishing the senses of "computer" and "computation" at play, and stating which sense the debate is actually about. Each party should confirm that its own view survives this cleanup rather than being an artifact of the ambiguity.

2. A numbered section of agreed claims — but not vacuous ones. Both sides should accept claims with some bite: about what Turing machines and formal automata are, about what physics and neuroscience have established about neural implementation, about multiple realizability as an empirical matter, and about which naive versions of each side's position are dead.

3. A disagreements section, ideally as a table, where each remaining disagreement is stated twice — once in wording the computationalist would endorse as a fair statement of the computationalist view, once in wording the anti-computationalist would endorse as a fair statement of theirs. No strawmen, no hedged mush.

4. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one party. I suspect one crux is the implementation problem — that if mappings between physical states and formal states are left unconstrained, any sufficiently messy physical object can be described as implementing any finite computation, which both parties find unacceptable but for opposite reasons. Ask them to state precisely what constraint on state-mappings each side endorses, and what each predicts happens to the other side's constraint under scrutiny.

5. For each crux, concrete cases both parties agree would bear on it, with each party's prediction written down in advance. Good candidates: a neuromorphic analog chip implementing a brain emulation at a thousandth of biological speed with continuous, noisy state variables; a giant lookup table implementing the same input-output function as a human brain; a system whose "computations" are spread across a slow, distributed, geographically separated network; an embodied robot with rich sensorimotor loops versus a disembodied system with identical internal processing. For each, both parties should say whether there is something it is like to be the system, and why, and where their answers would come apart.

6. A closing section in which the parties flag which of their residual disagreements are substantive and which are merely verbal, with reasons — and, if they can manage it, a statement of what kind of argument, thought experiment, or future development both agree would actually move the debate, rather than merely recommitting each side to its prior.

The point of the exercise is a document both parties could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact enough that a reader could tell, five years from now, which party's recorded predictions came true. Please take the drafting seriously enough that neither party would refuse to sign.
