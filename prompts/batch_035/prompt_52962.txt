I am a labor economist whose native method is revealed preference: I think in terms of Samuelson's program, Afriat's theorem and the generalized axiom of revealed preference, McFadden's random utility models, and the distinction between testing rationality and recovering preferences. My department has just made a joint hire who does what she calls "inverse reinforcement learning," which she glosses as inferring an agent's preferences from its behavior. On its face this sounds like revealed preference with fresh notation, but the surrounding culture feels foreign: she speaks of a reward function defined over states of the world, of the demonstrator being approximately or noisily optimal, of "Boltzmann-rational" policies, of observing a single agent's trajectory over time rather than choices from menus, and of using the recovered reward to train a new agent rather than to test the old one. She also complains about an identification problem — that many reward functions are consistent with the same behavior — which she addresses with representational constraints and regularization rather than with ordinal equivalence theorems. Restate inverse reinforcement learning in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can: what is a reward function over states next to a utility function over commodity bundles; what is the assumption of approximate optimality next to GARP; what is Boltzmann rationality next to a random utility model; what is her identification complaint next to Afriat's result that finite choice data test rationality and recover preferences only up to monotone transformation; and is discounting and trajectory data anything more than intertemporal choice in another dress. Then give me the residue report: which parts of her field map cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all — for instance, the fact that the recovered reward is a dense function expected to generalize to choice situations never observed; that the point of the exercise is to retrain or advise an agent rather than to test or predict it; and that the observed agent's systematic suboptimality may itself carry information, whereas in my tradition it is either a rejection of the hypothesis or noise. Finally, tell me whether the residue suggests that revealed preference theory is missing something real, or that inverse reinforcement learning is quietly doing something other than preference inference and borrowing the prestige of my tradition while doing it. Throughout, keep the translation honest: do not let her field's assumptions pass as mere vocabulary, and flag every point where you had to fudge.
