A friend of mine who's really into astronomy forums shut me down in an argument about extraterrestrial life last week with what he called the principle of indifference: we have no evidence either way, so it's 50/50 whether technological aliens exist in our galaxy, case closed. I'm a backend engineer, not a philosopher, but that smelled off to me even before I went down a rabbit hole afterwards. The rabbit hole: apparently there's this old puzzle called Bertrand's paradox where you ask for the probability that a "randomly chosen" chord of a circle is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, and depending on how you set up the randomness you get 1/3, 1/2, or 1/4, and everyone agrees the setup is symmetric and we're totally ignorant about which chord we get. So "we know nothing, therefore equal probabilities" can't just be obviously right, or the paradox wouldn't be a paradox.

Here's what I want: treat "no reason to favor one outcome over another, so assign them equal probability" as a slogan and unpack the distinct precise claims it could be making. Like, is it a claim about what rational agents are permitted to do, or required to do? Is it a definition of what we mean by probability in cases with no data, or an empirical-ish thesis that uniform priors tend to wash out okay? Is it maybe just trivially true by convention, the way I might default-initialize a weight to zero in code? And where does the "outcomes" part sneak in all the work — because in the aliens case you could take the outcomes to be "aliens exist / aliens don't" (giving 50/50) but just as easily partition things by number of alien civilizations (0, 1, 2, ..., N), and the uniform choice over that partition gives some tiny probability to zero. I suspect the whole action is in who gets to pick the partition, and the slogan is silent about it.

So lay the readings out as a menu, from the trivial to the radical, and for each one say whether it's plausible, whether it actually licenses my friend's 50/50 move, and whether it survives the Bertrand chord thing. I keep feeling like there must be a reading that's both defensible AND does what my friend wants, and I'd like to know if that intuition is just wrong. Keep it at a level I can follow — I've never taken a philosophy class, but I do probability for a living (the applied kind, hypothesis tests and A/B tests), so you can assume I'm comfortable with the math and just need the epistemology spelled out slowly.
