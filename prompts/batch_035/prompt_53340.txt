In my epistemology seminar we spent an hour on a closure-driven skeptical argument — if I know that I have hands, then I know that I am not a handless brain in a vat; I do not know that I am not a handless brain in a vat; so I do not know that I have hands — and when someone voiced sympathy for the second premise, a classmate shut the discussion down by quoting Wittgenstein: "If you tried to doubt everything you would not get as far as doubting anything. The game of doubting itself presupposes certainty." The TA nodded as though the line had done some real work, but I honestly could not tell what work. Please lay out, as a list I can bring to the next session, the distinct precise theses this one-liner could be advancing — for example: a psychological claim about what finite human beings are in fact capable of doubting; a normative claim that genuine doubt requires grounds, so that some propositions are rightly exempt from the demand for justification; a claim about the intelligibility conditions of the practice of doubting, such that radical doubt is not refuted but rendered idle; or a constitutive claim that doubt only functions against a background of things held fast. For each reading, say whether it is plausible on its own terms, whether it can be sustained by what Wittgenstein actually wrote in On Certainty or hangs more on a particular extension of the book than on the book itself, and — most importantly — whether it actually damages the skeptical argument: does it show the second premise false, show it to be meaningless, or merely explain why we find ourselves unable to live by it? Please also keep clearly separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested in this corner of the subject: I understand there is a whole literature on hinge epistemology that cannot agree even on whether the hinges are beliefs, and I do not want one contested reconstruction passed off to me as the standard Wittgenstein.
