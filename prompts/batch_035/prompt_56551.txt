I'm setting the final for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of death (about twenty students; the anchor text is Fred Feldman's Confrontations with the Reaper, supplemented by the standard Epicurean literature). Draft one essay question, answerable in ninety minutes, on whether death is bad for the one who dies. It must not be answerable by reciting the symmetry argument, nor by reciting the deprivation account's textbook slogan. The question must be built around the following case, which I want printed verbatim on the paper:

Marisol is sixty-eight. Next Tuesday an aneurysm will rupture, and she will die instantly and painlessly. In the nearest world in which she does not die then — the surgical team intervenes — she lives eight more years: seven of them dominated by a degenerative illness she experiences as unrelieved misery, containing exactly one thing she values, a single afternoon, three years in, when her estranged son visits and they reconcile. By any reasonable aggregation the eight years are much worse for her than nothing; the afternoon is, by her own lights, worth a great deal; and there is no nearby world in which she lives longer or better.

The question should force three confrontations. First, the comparative structure of the deprivation account: if death is bad for its victim when, and only when, the life cut short would have contained more good than the life actually lived, Marisol's death comes out good for her, not bad — and the student has to decide whether the account can really say that, and whether the one irreplaceable good afternoon changes the verdict or only the story we tell about it. Second, the timing problem: if her death is bad for her — or good for her — when is it so, given that there is no moment at which she is both existing and dead; candidates must not be allowed to get away with either "it is bad at no time" or "it is bad at all times" without arguing for which and paying the costs. Third, the antemortem move: the proposal, familiar from the literature on posthumous harm, that the subject of the misfortune is the living Marisol, whose interests can be set back after her death — the student must say whether this rescues the timing problem or merely relocates it to the question of when the living Marisol suffered a setback that no one, including her, ever noticed.

Then give me: (a) the exam question exactly as it would appear on the paper, in about 150 words; (b) a model answer of roughly 600 words that represents the standard a top student could actually reach in ninety minutes, not the answer an examiner with unlimited time would write; (c) a marking scheme of five must-hit points totaling 25 marks, each with its mark allocation and a one-line note on how candidates typically botch it; and (d) a short closing note on what separates an A from a B — the one specific move the A answer contains that the B answer, otherwise competent and well-informed, always lacks. One further constraint: no part of the question or the model answer may rest on a quotation from Lucretius, Epicurus, Nagel, or anyone else; paraphrase only.
