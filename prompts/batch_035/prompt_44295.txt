I write, under a pen I do not acknowledge in company, a serial of Victorian village mysteries — quarterly installments in a small magazine, a modest but devoted readership — centered on Miss Elspeth Gow, a retired schoolmistress whose talent for quiet detection is inseparable from her character. Because continuity across five years of installments is difficult, the magazine's editor, Mrs. Hartree, serves as a tribunal of last resort: I submit a scene, she rules it in character or out, and her rulings have been remarkably stable. Her record on questions of Elspeth's honesty runs as follows: the scene in which Elspeth lies to a constable to shield a former pupil — ruled in character; the scene in which she refuses her dying brother the comfortable fiction that their estranged sister had written to him, telling him the plain truth instead — in character; the scene in which she keeps silence, under direct questioning by a neighbor, about having seen the man's wife board the early ferry (the wife was fleeing him) — in character; and the scene in which she tells a small lie to a visiting antiquarian to conceal that she herself, in her youth, wrote a set of scandalous verses — out of character, and I rewrote it.

For the next installment I have drafted a scene I cannot judge. The estranged sister having died, her daughter — Elspeth's niece, whom she has come to love — asks her directly whether it was Elspeth who, thirty years before, gave the alibi that allowed the girl's father to escape a criminal charge. In one draft Elspeth lies, gently, to spare the girl the knowledge that both her parents and her aunt were party to it; in the other she confesses. I can see what Mrs. Hartree should say only if I know what rule she has been running, and I find that at least two rules fit her record equally well and diverge precisely here. On one reading, Elspeth may deceive to protect others but never herself — which licenses the lie to spare the niece and explains the rejection of the verses scene. On another, she may tell only such falsehoods as she will never have to repeat or inhabit — which licenses the one-off alibi of thirty years past and the misdirection of the constable, but forbids the lie to the niece, since the girl will go on asking and the truth sits in the parish records. Predict the ruling, name any further rules that fit the record as well, and tell me which further scene — one I could write cheaply, as a discarded draft, without committing the installment — would most cleanly separate the candidates.

I confess the question has stopped being merely practical. A friend who studies philosophy has been reading me the rule-following passages of Wittgenstein's Philosophical Investigations and the skeptical literature they provoked, and I now cannot shake the thought that nothing in the printed record of Elspeth's five years of conduct fixes what she would do next — that for any rule I name, there is a gerrymandered variant that re-describes her every past act as readily, and that Mrs. Hartree's rulings, however stable, are so far consistent with each of them. I know what I intend Elspeth to be, but when I try to state it I mostly find myself paraphrasing her history. So tell me also whether, in your judgment, there is a fact of the matter my editor is tracking — something in the character as written, or in my authorial intentions, that makes one draft the right one — or whether her ruling will not report a truth about Elspeth but create one, retroactively styled as discovery; and whether that distinction even survives the skeptical point or collapses under it.
