could you write me a parody agony-aunt column, around 650 words, for the christmas edition of my department's unofficial newsletter? the agony aunt is a strict revealed-preference economist — call her something like Aunt Utility — and the column is called something along the lines of Ask What You Actually Want.

she gets maybe four letters. ordinary life problems: someone who regrets an expensive purchase, someone who procrastinates and hates themselves for it, someone who keeps cancelling dates with a partner they claim to love because work always wins, someone stuck in a job they say they hate. her replies have to run the doctrine completely straight and internally consistent: if you chose it you preferred it, full stop. regret is just a subsequent preference for making regret noises at parties. akrasia can't exist because the will isn't a separate thing that can be weak — it's just whatever choice got made. saying you love someone is cheap talk, and cheap talk is itself just a low-cost choice, so the calendar doesn't lie. there's no gap between what you do and what you want, so there's nothing left to advise her readers on, which is part of the joke — she dispenses advice while technically holding that advice-seeking is itself a choice, and a free one.

one letter should come from someone who was mugged or coerced into something, and her answer should note, perfectly deadpan, that knife-point is simply a very steep budget constraint, and that all choice is choice under constraint anyway, so it's not obvious where you'd draw the line even if you wanted to. she never breaks character, never winks, never concedes a thing — the satire should land by getting the doctrine exactly right, not by strawmanning it. the running gags should be real features of the position: nothing could ever count as evidence against it, the theory explains every choice and therefore predicts none of them, welfare analysis collapses into an inventory of receipts. if you can work in the transitivity problem — a reader who chose a over b, b over c, and c over a, and aunt utility reassuring them that intransitivity is also a preference, definately not an inconsistency — that would go down well with the micro crowd.

she should close each reply with the same quietly sinister sign-off, something like "yours in revealed preference" or a tagline about how she'd love to hear from you but knows you'll only write if you do.

no citations or name-drops of actual economists, the jokes should carry it on their own. bristish spelling please since the newsletter copy-edits for that. around 650 words but dont stress if its a bit over.
