I lead the user research team at a company that builds a health-tracking app, and I've become uneasy about how much of our evidence about users consists of their own explanations of themselves. Our findings routinely hinge on questions like why someone abandoned an onboarding flow, what made them distrust a permissions screen, whether they would use a proposed feature, and how a red notification badge made them feel. My product leads treat the answers as windows directly into users' minds, and honestly, so did I for a long time. But I've read enough psychology to know the field has been skeptical of this for decades: the old Nisbett-and-Wilson worry that when people explain why they did something they are reporting a plausible theory about themselves rather than observing an inner process; experiments where people confidently justify choices they never actually made; the general finding that reports about one's own motives track what a person thinks would make sense of them. At the same time, I don't want to overcorrect into dismissing everything users tell us. It seems like some introspective reports are in much better shape than others -- say, what just passed through my mind a second ago versus why my mind did what it did over the past week -- and I can't run a lab, so I need the distinction usable in a conference room.

Please turn the philosophy and psychology of introspection's reliability into a decision procedure my team can actually run each time they design or interpret one of these self-report questions. My team has no psychology background, sessions and surveys run weekly, and whatever survives has to fit in a one-page summary that product leads will actually read, so it needs to be a short ordered list of questions with clear stopping points: what to ask about a proposed or collected self-report, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure concludes that the report shouldn't be used as evidence at all (or only with an explicit caveat attached), and where it should hand the call back to a human because the issue is a judgment about stakes or product context rather than something the literature settles. I'd like the distinctions that actually matter to survive the translation -- for instance, reporting a just-completed experience versus reconstructing one after a delay, reporting what was in mind versus why the mind did it, characterizing a specific episode versus a general disposition, whether the person could have arrived at the answer by observing their own behavior and circumstances rather than by looking inward, and whether the way we elicited the report could have planted the very state or explanation being reported. But you should decide which distinctions earn their place in a ten-minute procedure and which belong in a footnote, and say so. Also please be explicit about where the procedure will misfire -- in particular, questions about preferences and feelings for which we have no behavioral substitute, where a skeptical procedure would leave us with no evidence at all.

Then run the procedure on three cases from our recent work: (a) a think-aloud session where users are asked, immediately after completing a task, why they hesitated on a particular screen; (b) a cancellation exit survey sent three days after a user downgraded their subscription, asking them to select and explain their reasons; (c) a focus group shown a mockup of a proposed privacy dashboard and asked whether they would use it and why. Include at least one case where the procedure tells us to downweight findings that are vivid, unanimous, and that everyone on the product side loved. I'd rather know now where my own habits of interpretation fail than after we've shipped something on the strength of them.
