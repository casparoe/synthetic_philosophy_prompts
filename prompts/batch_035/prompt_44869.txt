I teach a graduate seminar on population ethics, and I need a dialogue for my students to critique. Write a dialogue between two philosophers of roughly equal ability: Amara, who defends the procreation asymmetry — creating a life not worth living wrongs someone, while failing to create a happy life wrongs no one and is not positively bad — and Tobias, a total utilitarian who thinks the asymmetry is an unprincipled concession to intuition. The dialogue should be anchored in two cases rather than drifting into generalities. In the first, a couple can conceive now, producing a child with a life barely worth living, or wait five years, producing a different, much happier child; Tobias should press Amara on whether her asymmetry can say anything at all about the choice, given that neither prospective child exists to be wronged, and Amara should respond with her best person-affecting machinery rather than retreating to bare intuition. In the second, a happy child's existence would impose a tiny, imperceptible cost on each of billions of existing people, with total losses exceeding the child's gains; Tobias should argue that if tiny harms to existing people can aggregate into something that outweighs a new happy life, then tiny benefits to existing people should equally be able to be outweighed by creating one, collapsing the asymmetry. Require both characters to actually concede points when their opponent's argument lands, and have the dialogue end unresolved but with each stating precisely what evidence or argument would change their mind. Avoid having either character merely recite textbook formulations; they should be doing original-feeling philosophy on the cases.
