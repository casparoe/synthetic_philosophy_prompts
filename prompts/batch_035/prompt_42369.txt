Please argue for a position I half believe: that the Allais preferences don't refute the independence axiom as a norm of rationality, they just show the people holding them haven't finished individuating outcomes. The thought is that losing a sure thing is a genuinely different outcome from failing to get a probable thing — some structural feature of the option, not just its payoff, is part of what you're choosing between — so once outcomes are sliced finely enough the "violation" evaporates and expected utility theory comes out untouched. You'll need to defend the individuation move against the obvious objection, that it makes EU theory vacuous because you can always redescribe outcomes to save it; maybe say individuation is constrained by something external to preference (the causal structure of the choice, or what the agent could be paid to accept as compensation), so it can't just be reverse-engineered. If you decide the position can't actually be defended, argue that instead and say where it breaks. End with a final line beginning "Verdict:" giving a one-sentence statement of what the Allais data do and don't establish about expected utility theory if your argument works.
