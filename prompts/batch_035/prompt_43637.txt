I've recently found myself, of all things, in a property dispute where a two-thousand-year-old Chinese paradox is being used as a live move. I sit on the board of my condominium association, and we are revising the pet policy for the common garden: the draft rule bars "animals kept as pets" and exempts, without saying more, "service animals." A unit owner has an emotional support cat that her therapist has certified in writing, and she takes the position that the rule does not restrict her at all, on the grounds that an assistance animal is a medical accommodation, not a pet — it is in the unit in a different capacity entirely, whatever species it belongs to. At our last meeting a neighbor, a retired professor of Chinese philosophy, grinned and said that this was straight out of the School of Names: a white horse is not a horse, and an emotional support animal is not a pet. He clearly thought he was being clever; the trouble is that none of us could tell whether he was mocking her reasoning or endorsing it, and I have not been able to let the thing go since.

I know the white horse slogan only in its bare, oft-repeated form; I never read the original and would not trust my memory of it anyway. But I want to understand what work the sentence could actually be doing. Lay out for me, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise claims "a white horse is not a horse" might be expressing — starting with readings so weak they are trivially true (perhaps just that the two expressions are different expressions), through substantive semantic readings (that "white horse" and "horse" differ in meaning, or in reference, or in what they pick out at a world), up to radical metaphysical or logical readings (that the class of white horses is not part of the class of horses, or that horsehood and white horsehood are different kinds of thing, or that no compound term ever applies to what its head noun applies to). For each reading, say whether it is plausible, whether it is false, and — most importantly — whether it would actually support the inference my neighbor's opponent or my neighbor would need: concluding that a creature can fall under a general term's extension while being excluded by a rule that uses the general term, i.e., that a member of a subclass is not a member of the class.

Here is what worries me, and I would like you to address it directly: whatever reading licenses "a white horse is not a horse" also seems to license "a black cat is not a cat," "a trained guide dog is not a dog," and even "an indoor plant is not a plant" — and once we are licensed to say that, it is hard to see how any bylaw, statute, or ordinary generalization survives, since almost nothing is a plain F without being an F-of-some-particular-sort. Does one of the readings escape that collapse — perhaps one that turns on something special about how the compound is formed, or on what rules and terms are for, rather than about reference? And is there a reading under which the sentence is true in a way that is legally or practically inert, so that my neighbor could be right about the words and still lose about the cat? Finish by telling me, with reasons, which reading you think the School of Names practitioners themselves most likely intended, and which reading the professor at our meeting was probably invoking when he used the line — as a reductio of my neighbor, as support for her, or as a well-placed joke at the expense of the whole exercise of drafting rules with general terms in them.
