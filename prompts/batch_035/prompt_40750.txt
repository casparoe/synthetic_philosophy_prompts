My philosophy reading group is working through the mind–body problem, and the comparison that keeps doing the real work is the old Huxley-style one about the steam train: consciousness is like the whistle on a locomotive — the machinery does all the work of moving the train, the sound is just a byproduct, and everything would run exactly the same if the whistle fell off. One member in particular leans on it to defend epiphenomenalism: the physical facts about the brain fully determine behavior, experience is real but causally inert, and the paradox of phenomenal judgment — that if experience has no effects, then my reports about my experience can't be caused by the very experiences they are about — is just a harmless consequence of the picture rather than a defect in it. Audit the analogy for me: lay out what is supposed to map onto what (engine, steam, whistle, driver, passengers, people along the track), say where the mapping breaks, and pay special attention to the fact that in the train case the whistle's sound does enter the causal story — the driver and bystanders can hear it, and things can happen because of it — whereas the epiphenomenalist denies that anything analogous can "hear" consciousness. Given that disanalogy, does my groupmate's conclusion survive: is the paradox of phenomenal judgment something epiphenomenalism can absorb, or does it show the view can't explain why our talk about experience tracks experience at all? Throughout, please separate what is settled among philosophers of mind from what is genuinely contested — for instance whether the causal closure of the physical actually excludes mental causation, and whether the various rescues (pre-established harmony-style accompanying laws, overdetermination, deflating what "reports about experience" really are) are live options or widely regarded as dead — and don't present a contested verdict as the consensus one.
