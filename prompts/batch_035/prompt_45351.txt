I'm building a thought experiment for a paper on the moral status of engineered systems, and I want it to invert the standard lineup. In the familiar cases, a substrate-independent functionalist and a biological naturalist land on opposite sides. Construct me a case in which they converge instead — both agreeing that some engineered system has morally significant experience — while the dissenter is an enactive or embodiment-oriented theorist, a view that usually gets cast as the generous, inclusive party.

The scenario should involve three systems: an evolved animal with a small nervous system (something on the order of an insect), a neuromorphic artifact whose continuous physical dynamics implement, under some choice of state variables, the same abstract causal organization as that animal's nervous system, and a cultured-neural organoid induced to run that same organization. Constraints: no demons, no population-scale realizations, no whole-brain emulations of humans, no perfect duplicates stipulated into existence; the artifacts should be recognizably near-term extrapolations of existing technology; and the scenario itself should be a single paragraph.

After the scenario, show that the intended split actually falls out of the theories rather than being stipulated: walk through the verdicts the functionalist, the biological naturalist, and the enactivist would each give, and make clear where they agree, where they diverge, and why. Then tell me which feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to each verdict if that feature is removed or varied, and how an enactivist would attack the case rather than accept the denial. Finally, confront a problem the case cannot sidestep: whether "implements the same computation" is determinate here at all, given the triviality worry that under sufficiently liberal mappings any open physical system can be described as implementing any finite automaton, and given that continuous analog dynamics do not come pre-carved into discrete states. Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on these questions — including whether the functionalist's and biological naturalist's agreement reflects real convergence or an artifact of the case's construction — and do not present any contested reading of functionalism or enactivism as the consensus view. Please answer in American English.
