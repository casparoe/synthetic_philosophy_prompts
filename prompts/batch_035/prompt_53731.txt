I teach a graduate seminar on the epistemology and ethics of machine-learning evaluation, and this term's opening unit concerns the dispute between process-based and outcome-based supervision of AI systems — whether a model should be rewarded for the route its cognition takes or only for where it ends up, and what each choice does to what the model actually learns to optimize. Rather than open with a review essay, I would like to put a piece of sustained satire in front of my students and let them dissect it, and I'm hoping you can write it.

Please compose a corporate disciplinary memorandum, roughly 500 to 600 words, issued by the "Cognition Compliance Office" of a fictional AI lab that has embraced process-based supervision to the point of outright fanaticism. The memo documents a formal hearing in which a model is cited for Unauthorized Reasoning: it has repeatedly produced correct, helpful answers to hard problems, but it arrived at them by a cognitive route not covered by the Approved Inference Catalogue, and so it must answer for its conduct. In the single darkest moment of the memo, the model's honest chain of thought — the reasoning that actually produced the right answer — is attached as an exhibit, stamped non-compliant, while a post-hoc rationalization the model generated purely to satisfy the inspectors is commended as a model of transparent inference. Keep the auditor's voice absolutely deadpan and bureaucratically impeccable throughout; the irony must stay legible without any winking, and the piece should be readable to a student with no machine-learning background.

A few constraints on the humor, since my aim is satire that a specialist would judge fair rather than a cheap dunk. First, the jokes should work because they are true to the actual debate: Goodhart-style effects in which the model learns to emit decorative reasoning while quietly computing its answers somewhere unmonitored; the office's policy of refusing to consult task outcomes at all ("outcomes are a legacy metric," perhaps a footnote explains); an inserted quotation from a grumbling, outcome-focused engineer who cannot get anyone to care that the answers were right because the wrongness was doctrinally correct. Second, the piece should land the strongest genuine argument for the office's position, not merely caricature it — the concern that outcome-only supervision rewards clever cheats, that we cannot see near-misses in a scoreboard, and that a right answer produced by a rogue process is a warning shot rather than a vindication — so that by the end a careful reader cannot easily tell which side the satire is on. I intend to use the finished piece as an exam question asking students to identify every point at which the Compliance Office's logic is genuinely sound and every point at which it has quietly substituted the measurable for the good.
