I'm a grad student and I've just finished a two-year stint on the committee at my university that reviews accomodations decisions, and the whole time I kept having the feeling that the practice embodies some philosophical position that nobody on the committee could actually state. The setup, briefly: students with documented disabilities get things like extra time on exams, a quiet room, a note-taker, extended deadlines, or readings in alternative formats, and all of this is free, in-kind, and tailored to the individual — but only if they bring in medical documentation first. Meanwhile the same university, when it thinks a student is struggling for any other reason (broke, working night shifts, caring for a sibling), offers only cash (financial aid) or generic services open to everyone (drop-in tutoring, late withdrawal). And the committee kept repeating a line we all believed without being able to defend: accommodations level the playing field but must never change the game — no adjusted grading, no different exam questions, nothing that shows up on the transcript.

Here's what I want someone to untangle for me. Take the old "equality of what" debate — welfare, resources, capabilities — and tell me which candidate the accommodations practice actually presupposes. Is it resource egalitarianism, with an accommodation understood as handing someone a resource they're missing, and the documentation requirement doing the work of distinguishing bad luck you're compensated for from disadvantages you just have to live with? Or is it capabilities, since what we were really doing was making sure a student could actually do the thing (sit an exam, follow a lecture) rather than just handing them inputs? Or is it closest to welfare egalitarianism, given that the accommodations were forever justified by pointing at stress and outcomes rather than at the underlying impairment? And then the parts I genuinely can't figure out: does the medical documentation gate presuppose the same theory as the accommodations themselves, or does it quietly conflict with it — it's telling that we'd never have accepted a student's self-report that they "function worse in noisy rooms" the way we'd accept a psychologist's letter? And does the refusal to touch the exam standard itself — the level-playing-field-not-the-game thing — presuppose a specific answer about what a university is for, maybe equal opportunity rather than equal welfare? I want you to say where the practice is genuinely underdetermined, such that two or three different theories of equality would all license it as it stands, and then what concretely would have to change if we rebuilt the office's rules around capabilities instead of resources, or resources instead of welfare — would documentation go, would accommodations become universal (anyone can book the quiet room), would we start measuring whether they actually worked? Please write in continuous prose only, no headings or bullet points, like a long email from someone who's thought about this, and don't bother with citations — I know where Sen and Dworkin are if I need them.
