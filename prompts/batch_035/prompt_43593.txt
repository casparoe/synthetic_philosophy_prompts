A friend and I spent an entire evening arguing about why promises bind, and I came away with a puzzle I can't find a label for. Her position was the Humean one: promising is a convention, like money or driving on the right, and the obligation to keep a promise is simply what the convention prescribes; asking why the convention binds, she said, is like asking why chess requires the king to move only one square — the rule does not bind you from outside the game, since being subject to it is just what playing the game is. I kept pressing on a pattern I noticed in her replies: every time I asked what makes the obligation real rather than merely expected, she answered in terms of a further norm — the convention obliges because entering into it obliges, entering into it obliges because saying "I promise" under the convention is the kind of act that obliges — so whatever is supposed to do the obliging always seems to need the same sort of obliging in place in order to do it. Is there an established name for this circularity charge against convention-based accounts of promising, and for the standard replies to it? The nearest concepts I can think of are constitutive rules and self-verifying performatives, but neither seems to carry the specific worry that the story of how the institution gets authority over you quietly assumes it already has that authority. If there is no settled label, say so plainly, coin one with a definition and some boundary cases, and throughout please distinguish what is actually agreed upon among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested — I would rather not walk away believing one side of our argument won when the field itself has not decided the question.
