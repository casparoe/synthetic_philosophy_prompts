Two members of my experimental philosophy reading group, Marianne and Theo, got stuck on the Knobe effect at the end of last week's session, and their disagreement has continued into our group chat. I'll report it as fairly as I can and would like a verdict, because I can't work out who is right about what.

For context: the effect involves the familiar case of a chairman of the board who is told that a new programme will increase profits and that it will also harm the environment; he says he doesn't care at all about harming the environment, he just wants to make as much profit as possible, and starts the programme. Most people asked whether he intentionally harmed the environment say yes. In the mirror version, where the programme will also help the environment and he says he doesn't care about helping it, most people asked whether he intentionally helped the environment say no. That's the phenomenon they were arguing about.

(1) Marianne's opening move: in both versions the chairman knows about the side effect in advance and decides to proceed anyway, so on a purely descriptive concept of acting intentionally — foresight plus a settled decision — the two cases should get the same answer. The only thing that flips between versions is the moral valence of the side effect, and the answers flip with it. So the folk concept of intentional action is partly constituted by moral concerns; the competence itself is moralised, and armchair analyses that treat it as a purely descriptive concept got the folk wrong.

(2) Theo's reply: the asymmetry shows nothing of the sort, because the survey question doesn't reach the concept cleanly. When you ask whether he intentionally harmed the environment, respondents hear a question about whether he's answerable for it, and answer that question instead; in the help version, saying yes would sound like a compliment he hasn't earned, so people say no. The composite answer differs even though the underlying concept may be untouched. The correct moral for experimental philosophy, he said, is that questionnaires are contaminated by conversational pragmatics, not that the concept has morality built in.

(3) Marianne's second move: the pragmatic story predicts the asymmetry should disappear wherever blame is off the table, but it leaks everywhere. She claimed the effect has been found in children far too young to be strategic about blame attributions. And she cited a well-known follow-up study about office stationery: in a department where pens keep going missing, two people each take the last pen on the same day — one of whom is permitted to take pens, the other of whom is forbidden — and asked who caused the problem, respondents overwhelmingly name the person who violated the norm, even though both took a pen. A who-caused-it question is not plausibly heard as a covert blame question, yet norms bend the causal judgement all the same.

(4) Theo's second reply: he was unconvinced that causal talk is the pristine register she needs. Calling the norm-violator the cause of the problem is itself a way of laying the outcome at his door; ordinary people say the driver, not the icy patch, caused the accident precisely when the driver broke a rule, and nobody concludes that the concept of causation is moralised — the moral layer affects what we choose to highlight, not what we represent. As for the children, he said that if the effect is a habit of ordinary conversation, we should expect it early, since children learn the habits of conversation before they learn anything else; the developmental data cuts both ways.

(5) Marianne's closing move: she said the two views then seem to make the same predictions everywhere. If you build a version where the respondent is told the harmed party had it coming, her view predicts the intentionality judgement should track the respondent's own norms and soften, and she was confident Theo's view predicts the same, since on his story the respondent hears the question through those same norms. She pressed him: if no possible observation could separate the two accounts, his position isn't a rival hypothesis, just a refusal to accept hers.

(6) Theo's closing reply: he said she had it backwards. If the folk practice is pragmatic through and through, there is no further fact about whether the concept is really moralised — the question is ill-formed, not undecidable, and his account is more parsimonious because it keeps one neutral concept plus an explanation of the enrichment, whereas she multiplies moralised concepts for every norm-sensitive judgement we make.

That's where they left it. Please adjudicate move by move: say who is right about which specific point, not just who wins overall; treat any empirical claims they made as their claims, and note where the verdict would have to be qualified if a claim turns out not to hold. Then say what, if anything, both of them are missing. Finally, tell me where your adjudication is most likely to go wrong, and what argument or finding would change your verdict. Please answer in British English.
