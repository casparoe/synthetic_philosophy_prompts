My uncle Viktor is retiring after 42 years of running the local chess club, and I've been asked to give the speech at his retirement party. Some background so you understand the problem. He's a strong amateur player, and for most of my life he was famously sour about computers: he insisted they'd never really play chess, and when Deep Blue beat Kasparov in '97 it cost him about a month of grumbling. But what's interesting is how he came around — not with "the machine understands chess" but something more like: it plays like an idiot who somehow always wins. And then he started making the juniors at the club study engine lines anyway. He's also a big biology guy and used to go on about Darwin at family dinners, how Darwin's whole move was explaining design away: you don't need a designer to get eyes and wings, just inheritance plus variation plus competition plus an enormous amount of time. Once he told me Turing pulled the same trick on the mind — you don't need comprehension to get competence, just the right kind of blind process grinding away. I half remember Dennett writing somewhere that Darwin and Turing each made some kind of big inversion of reasoning along these lines, but I might be garbling it, so don't lean on that or treat me as a source.

Here's what I want the speech to do, and it's trickier than it sounds. Half the room is club people, some very strong, who train with engines every day and will roll their eyes if I suggest the machine doesn't really play chess in any sense that matters for their prep. The other half is family, several of whom basically think chess skill = being smart, and they'll tune out anything that sounds like a lecture. And Viktor himself is the trap. I can't say the engine understands chess the way he does, because he thinks that's flatly false and will literally correct me from the head table. But I also can't say it's just a calculator that got lucky, because then why did he spend the last twenty years making kids copy its moves? I want the speech to use his own Darwin point — competence without comprehension — to square the circle: to give the machine its due as the strongest "player" around, while saying clearly that what Viktor taught for 42 years was the other thing, the comprehension, and that this is why his students still beat kids who only parrot engine lines. Keep it around five minutes spoken, warm, funny if you can manage it, and no philosophy vocabulary — if the word intentionality shows up I will simply not read that paragraph aloud. He would hate that, and this speech is for him.
