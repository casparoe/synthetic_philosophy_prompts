I'm a third-year PhD student writing a dissertation on the badness of death, and I've settled into what I take to be the mainstream, broadly Nagelian and Feldmanian position: death is bad for the one who dies, and its badness is privative rather than positive — it consists in the goods of further life the person is thereby deprived of, not in any unpleasant experience (there is none) or in some intrinsic badness of nonexistence. I've fortified the view with the standard qualifications: the misfortune need not be located at any particular time within the person's life (it accrues holistically, to the life as a whole), and the relevant counterfactual is the closest world in which the death doesn't occur, not some maximally wonderful world in which the person lives forever in bliss.

I want a serious stress-test, not a survey. Please act as a professional philosopher of well-being and think carefully before writing — brainstorm the full space of defeaters first, then structure the answer. Specifically:

1. Rank the defeaters from potentially fatal to merely awkward. I can think of at least five candidates, and I'd like your ranking plus anything I've missed: (a) the Lucretian symmetry argument — if posthumous nonexistence deprives, why isn't prenatal nonexistence an equally great misfortune? I currently lean on an asymmetry of relation: the future victim has a life whose later goods are threatened, while the not-yet-born has no determinate goods under threat. Does that response survive the pulsed-existence problem (see (c))? (b) The comparability problem: perhaps the goods of a longer life are incommensurable with, or only marginally better than, the goods of the actual life, so the deprivation is trivial for most people — the view may then entail that death is barely bad for the ordinary person, which strikes me as a reductio. (c) Intermittent existence: imagine two beings each of whom exists for the same total duration of pleasant experience, but one exists continuously for eighty years while the other flickers in and out of existence in alternating-year pulses. If gappy existence isn't bad, what exactly does the deprivationist say death deprives us of — duration, continuity, or something else — and can any answer avoid collapse into either Epicureanism or an ad hoc attachment to continuity? (d) Guaranteed resurrection: a person is annihilated and reconstituted, with experiences and memories intact, after a century-long gap. If the gap is no misfortune, why is a death that would have been followed by an equally long stretch of further life a misfortune — and if the gap is a misfortune, does the view misclassify dreamless sleep? (e) The indeterminacy of the baseline: the closest world in which I don't die tonight is not the closest world in which you don't die tonight, and small changes in the baseline can swing the alleged deprivation from enormous to negligible, which suggests the view makes the badness of death hostage to counterfactual trivia.

2. Distinguish what a committed deprivationist should concede as genuinely counting against the view from what they would, and reasonably could, dismiss. Which of the above would the view's serious defenders treat as a cost, and which as a mere application of the theory?

3. Tell me what the view predicts that its rivals — the strict Epicurean view that death cannot harm, the desire-fulfillment account on which death is bad only when one wants to live, and the narrative-value account on which the shape and closure of a life matter independently of its aggregate goods — do not. Which observations, cases, or lived reactions would differentially support or undermine it?

4. Be honest about whether my qualified version has been insulated into unfalsifiability — and, if it has, whether that is a defect for a claim of this metaphysical and normative kind, or simply what rigor costs in this corner of axiology.
