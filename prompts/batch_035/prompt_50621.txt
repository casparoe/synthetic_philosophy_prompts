My friend went on a Gödel kick after reading some pop-math stuff and now has an argument he's very proud of, and the conclusion smells wrong to me but I can't put my finger on the broken step. It goes:

(1) First incompleteness theorem: any consistent formal system that can express arithmetic is incomplete — there are arithmetical truths it can't prove. (2) Second theorem: such a system can't even prove it's own consistency. (3) Any rule-governed practice with enough recursive structure — rules that apply to other rules, unbounded input — hits the same wall, because that's the machinery the theorems need. (4) A legal system is such a practice: statutes plus precedent get applied to arbitrary cases, including cases about how the rules themselves apply (appeals, constitutional review, self-amendment). (5) So the law can't settle every case, and can't certify its own consistency from inside. (6) Therefore in the hard cases something outside the rules has to decide, and that's what judges are really doing — the rules run out and equity or sense-of-justice takes over. (7) And since that thing isn't itself rule-governed (or it'd hit the same wall), the final say in law rests on something no set of rules could capture. He thinks (7) also explains why constitutional systems always end up with some unreviewable apex — the Supreme Court, the sovereign people — the "outside the system" the argument predicts.

Here's what bugs me: if this worked, it seems like it would prove too much. Mathematicians use arithmetic every day without anyone proving PA's consistency from inside PA, and bridges don't fall down. Same for a compiler that can't check itself, or chess engines that provably can't solve chess. None of these look like they need a magic human standing outside them, they just do fine at 99.9% of cases, and law looks more like that than like a machine demanding a completeness proof. It also feels like (7) moves the goalposts — the judge's equity is still reasoning happening somewhere, in a head, and if heads are in principle describable by rules, the regress just continues and he's shown there's always another level, not that there's a level of rule-less wisdom.

But when I try to name the false premise I keep finding myself believing all of them. So: where does this go wrong? If you tell me to reject (3), I want to know why the legal system doesn't have "enough structure" given that appellate review and self-amendment look a lot like self-reference. If the real issue is that "formal system" doesn't mean what he thinks it means, say what it does mean and why the difference matters. I'd rather keep (4) and (5) if I can — they seem right about law even apart from all the Gödel stuff, so if they have to go, tell me what they're really claiming when shorn of the incompleteness framing.
