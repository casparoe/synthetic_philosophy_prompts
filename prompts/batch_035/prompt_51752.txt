I'm a PhD student in political philosophy writing a chapter on comparative approaches to the regulation of sex work, and I keep hitting a structural question I can't resolve with my existing vocabulary. Since 1999, Sweden has criminalised the purchase of sex while leaving its sale legal, an asymmetry since adopted by Norway, France, Northern Ireland and others. Yet the same legal system treats the seller as a full legal agent nearly everywhere else: the tax authorities treat income from prostitution as taxable income in the ordinary way, and procuring offences criminalise third parties who facilitate or profit from the trade. So the criminal law seems to presuppose that the seller lacks whatever agency would make their participation in the transaction culpable, while the tax authorities presuppose exactly that agency; and the procuring laws seem to presuppose that the transaction is wrongful in a way that implicates everyone connected to it except one of its two participants. What I'd like is a reconstruction of the implicit theory: what view of moral agency, consent or exploitation does this pattern presuppose, if any? Is it that consent to sex for payment is not valid consent to the relevant wrong, that culpability tracks who is doing the using rather than who is participating, or something else again? Please distinguish what the asymmetry strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined such that several rival theories of the seller's agency would license the same legal outcome, and explain what would have to change in the doctrine if the presupposed view were replaced by the picture the German licensing regime appears to act on, where sex work is a regulated trade and both parties are ordinary contracting agents. I'd also be grateful if you would write in British English throughout.
