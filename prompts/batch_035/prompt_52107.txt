Since a seminar on moral luck last term, a friend of mine — call him D. — has been deploying a single argument everywhere: against blaming the drunk driver who kills a pedestrian, against blaming our grandparents for the prejudices they were raised into, against criminal sentencing, and eventually against his own resentment of people who wronged him. Stated carefully, it runs:

(1) A person is a fitting target of blame only for what is within their control.
(2) What a person does — as opposed to what merely happens — is what issues from their choices, intentions, values, and character.
(3) Values and character are fixed by genetic constitution and formative circumstances, none of which the person chose or controlled.
(4) Whatever issues from a cause you did not choose is itself not within your control.
(5) So nothing a person does, as opposed to what merely happens to them, is within their control.
(6) So no one is ever a fitting target of blame — or praise.
(7) So every desert-based practice — blame, praise, retributive punishment, admiration for virtue — rests on an illusion, and the only honest criticism is forward-looking: this conduct causes harm, so arrange things to prevent it, the way we treat a medical condition.
(8) So there is no principled difference between blaming a person for their cruelty and blaming them for their height or their birthplace.

D. is delighted with (8) and now refuses to blame anyone for anything; he says anger at a person, as opposed to opposition to their conduct, is like being angry at the weather. The rest of us think (8) proves too much, but we cannot agree on where the chain breaks. I can see two candidate weak points. Step (4) looks like it trades on an ambiguity in control — the sense in which I control my typing even though I never chose to have hands — but whenever I push on it, D. asks me to name one thing I control for which I did not receive the capacity of control from sources I did not choose, and I have no answer. And step (5) seems to slide from every input to an action being unchosen to the action itself being uncontrolled, which does not obviously follow — though D. insists that if character and circumstances fully settle the choice, there is nothing left over for the agent to be doing.

There is also a wrinkle. Someone in the group claims that a conclusion very close to (6) is defended in print by serious philosophers, not as a reductio but as the truth, and that at least some of them keep something recognizably like blame by reconceiving what blame is for. If that is right, the argument may not be broken at all, and the live question becomes only what accepting (6) costs, and whether (7) and (8) actually follow from it — D. runs those last two steps very fast, and abolishing all desert does not obviously entail treating cruelty exactly like height.

So I have two requests. First, tell me where the argument goes wrong, if it does: name the step, explain why it looked fine, and price the rejection — what D. gets to keep, what he has to pay, and what the rest of us pay if we keep (1) and reject something else. If several steps are vulnerable, say which is cheapest to give up and why. Second, do not smooth over what is actually disputed. In particular, separate what is settled from what is contested among people who work on this: whether the inference in (5) from every contributing factor being unchosen to nothing being controlled is a straightforward fallacy or a defensible aggregation; whether the philosophers who accept a conclusion like (6) can coherently retain the practices D. wants to abolish; and whether rejecting (4) requires a substantive account of control or merely relocates the problem one level up. Where the standard diagnoses genuinely disagree, say so, and do not present any one of them as the consensus.
