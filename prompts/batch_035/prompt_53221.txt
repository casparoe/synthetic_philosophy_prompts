i'm a phd student in history and philosophy of science and my dissertation chapter at the moment is about what Dennett calls the strange inversion of reasoning — darwin's move of explaining design in nature by getting rid of the designer, and turing's move of explaining thought by getting rid of the thinker, so that competence gets explained without comprehension ever being posited anywhere. anyway, our reading group is closing out the term and somehow i got volunteered to open the last session with a poem instead of a summary, so here i am.

i want a pantoum, iambic pentameter, six stanzas, on this inversion. the pantoum seems like the exact right form for it and i'd like the form to do the philosophical work rather than just carry it: lines come back verbatim, but because the lines around them have changed, they mean something different on their second appearance. i want the reader to feel the poem itself acquiring comprehension that was never inserted into it anywhere — comprehension arriving as a product of the machinery, not as an ingredient. and of course in a pantoum the last line is the first line again; i want that to land as the point, so that when the reader circles back to it, they now understand a line that was sitting there, blank and unexplained, at the top. some of the stanzas should track darwin (blind variation, selection, the appearance of design without a mind) and some turing (syntax from which meaning falls out, no homunculus inside), though id honestly be happiest if you render the two inversions well enough that a philosopher of biology and a philosopher of mind each recognize their own field in it without being told which stanza is whose — naming darwin or turing outright is fine but not necessary, and name-dropping alone won't cut it.

one more thing: if the darwin stanzas and the turing stanzas can mirror each other across the middle of the poem, so the two inversions rhyme structurally, that would be great — but don't break the poem to force it. no sermons about wonder, no cheap ai-is-coming stuff, and no wincing from people who actually know dennett's work. its for an audience that has spent a semester on this, so the bar is: someone who knows the ideas should find the poem right, and someone who doesn't should still find it a good poem.
