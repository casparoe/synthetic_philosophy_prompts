I'm a senior in my school's philosophy elective, and our teacher has us trade in-class essays for anonymous peer grading before he looks at them himself. I've been given a classmate's essay to grade, and since I want to be fair rather than just impressionistic about it, I'd like a careful point-by-point assessment I can model my own comments on. The essay question, which we had forty-five minutes to answer under exam conditions, was:

"Sociologists and historians often appeal to 'the Protestant work ethic' to explain why some societies treat long hours of diligent work as virtuous in themselves. France, by contrast, has written a statutory 35-hour workweek into law, and Japan has had to coin a word, karoshi, for death from overwork. Taking these three cases together, is there an answer to the question of how much a person ought to work, or is the right amount purely a matter of what a society happens to value? Defend your view."

Here is the essay I have to grade, copied out exactly as written:

"Weber argued in the early twentieth century that Protestantism, and Calvinism in particular, created the work ethic behind modern capitalism. Calvinists believed in predestination, meaning God had already decided who was saved. Since they could not know whether they were among the elect, they worked obsessively at their worldly calling, because hard work and the success it brought were how they earned their way into heaven. This anxious ethic hardened into the spirit of capitalism, and it survived even after people stopped believing, which is why we still feel vaguely guilty on idle Sundays. Weber's own conclusion was that we should shake off this leftover guilt and get back to a more relaxed, pre-capitalist pace of life.

France has gone in the opposite direction. The 35-hour week shows that the French decided as a society that leisure matters more than work: they legislated the view that the good life is one with plenty of free time. Japan shows the cost of the opposite view. Karoshi proves that a culture can prize work so intensely that it literally kills its workers, and the fact that the Japanese state compensates the families amounts to an admission that the work culture itself is at fault.

So who has it right? Nobody, really. Each society simply has different values: the French find meaning in leisure, Americans in work, the Japanese in company loyalty. Since the Protestant work ethic is just one culturally specific value system among others, there is no neutral standpoint from which to judge them, and no one can say that France or Japan is objectively wrong. The most we can say is that overwork is medically dangerous, since karoshi is a documented fact, so every society should cap hours at a safe level — but beyond that, how much to work is a matter of taste."

Our teacher grades on an A–F scale and has told us the rubric cares most about two things: whether views are attributed accurately, and whether each step of the argument actually follows from the last. Please structure your assessment with the following sections, in this order: first, "Overall grade," where you state the letter grade you would assign together with a one-sentence justification; second, "Point-by-point comments," where you work through the essay paragraph by paragraph, naming what it gets right as well as what it gets wrong (about three short paragraphs, or a numbered list with one item per significant point); third, "The most serious problems," where you identify and carefully explain the one or two deepest flaws in the essay, the ones that a quick reading might let slide but that a careful grader should not (roughly a short paragraph each); and finally, "Comments for the writer," two or three sentences of constructive guidance about how to improve the essay, phrased the way you would actually say it to a classmate. Please be specific throughout — a comment like "the Weber discussion has some issues" is not useful to me, whereas one that pinpoints exactly which claim is wrong and why is exactly what I need.
