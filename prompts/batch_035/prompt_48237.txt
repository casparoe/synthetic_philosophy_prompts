My political theory reading group has spent most of the semester on populism and popular sovereignty, and by now two members have dug into trenches. One is basically a Laclau-style populist theorist: on her view "the people" is always and everywhere constructed — there is no pre-political popular will sitting there waiting to be polled, and the work of democratic politics IS articulating a demos out of heterogeneous demands, which elites denounce as "simplification" only because it threatens their own, better-hidden form of aggregation. The other is a liberal anti-populist in the Jan-Werner Müller vein: populism is a specific pathology, not a style — it's the claim that only some of the people count as the real people and that the populist alone represents them, which makes it anti-pluralist at its core regardless of how participatory it looks. They've been arguing past each other for about two months now, and honestly the rest of us suspect half the fight is about what the word "populism" should pick out rather than about anything substantive.

Write the joint statement that these two should have produced after a properly run adversarial collaboration. I want: (1) a numbered section of claims both can actually sign — I'd guess they agree that all representation is partial and mediated, that popular sovereignty isn't a fiction elites invented, that referendums are neither pure democracy nor mere advisory theater, but you work out the real list; (2) the precise disagreements, each phrased in wording its own side would accept without wincing; (3) the cruxes — sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them. My hunch is one crux is whether "the will of the people" is discovered (a pre-existing majoritarian preference that institutions obscure) or constructed (built by the very act of articulation), and another is whether the claim to exclusive representation is definitional of populism or just one variant of it. For each crux, give a concrete case or piece of evidence both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Brexit is the obvious shared case study for both camps (the 52-48 referendum, "the will of the people" hardening within weeks into a mandate that Parliament was somehow betraying, the whole 2019 prorogation standoff), so lean on that or any similarly well-known episode — but tell me what each of my two theorists would predict about it, not just what it shows.

Two more things. Be ruthless about separating genuine disagreements from verbal ones, and tell me explicitly which parts of this particular dispute you think would dissolve once the words were fixed and why. And because I'm going to show this to both of them and they will each be looking for slanted phrasing: end with a short note on where your own drafting is most likely to have failed — an agreed claim that's secretly vacuous, a crux that's just the whole disagreement restated, wording that leans one way under pressure — and what would make you restructure the document if it turned out that way.
