Quick background: I teach philosophy, mostly ethics, at a state school, and last week our medical ethics colloquium ended with the two speakers arguing past the Q&A, into the hallway, and finally over drinks. One is a psychiatrist who runs an inpatient unit (I'll call him D.), the other a philosopher of science who just finished a dissertation on the concept of disease. The ostensible topic was Szasz — whether "mental illness is a myth" — but I honestly couldn't tell you who was winning, and I'd like a proper referee's report on the exchange itself.

D. opened by saying Szasz's whole argument collapses at the first premise, because it equates disease with a lesion or a bodily pathology. Modern medicine is full of diseases that don't work that way: hypertension is a diagnosis you get from a number on a cuff, migraine from a pattern of complaints, and half of sleep medicine runs on self-report. If a blood-pressure threshold can be a disease with no lesion in sight, then Szasz's "no pathology, no illness" rule was dead on arrival, and mental disorders can be diseases in exactly the same sense as anything else in the hospital. She replied that this misses where the value judgment sits in each case. The blood-pressure number is pegged to downstream events — strokes, kidneys failing — and the instrument doesn't care what you think a good life looks like. A DSM diagnosis, by contrast, turns partly on "clinically significant distress or impairment," which is a normative claim about how a person should be functioning, built into the detection itself and not just the threshold. Szasz's deeper point, she said, was never really about lesions — it was about medicine claiming authority over "problems in living," and that problem survives the lesion point entirely.

D. didn't dispute that the criteria involve judgments, but pointed out that lots of legitimate specialties lean on the patient's report and the clinician's judgment — pain medicine, insomnia, fatigue — and nobody calls those myths. He also said the field demonstrably polices its own boundaries: he brought up the big public fight over whether ordinary grief should be diagnosable as depression after a few weeks, saying that's just what a young science looks like, arguing in the open about where normal suffering ends. She said he'd handed her the example: notice what that whole debate was about — not biomarkers, but whether it's acceptable to medicalize sadness two months after a spouse dies — and the boundary got drawn by argument over values, wearing a lab coat. That, she said, is Szasz's prediction confirmed: the scope of illness tracks what we want medicine (and sometimes the state) to take over, which is exactly how you get drapetomania in the 1850s and homosexuality in the DSM until the early seventies. D. closed by saying those two cases cut his way — the drapetomania case especially shows the problem is bad values, not the concept of illness, since bad values corrupted plenty of non-psychiatric medicine too — and that abandoning the disease frame would leave millions of suffering people with no vocabulary for getting help. Nobody conceded anything and I left before it looped.

So: please go through the exchange move by move — the lesion premise and the hypertension reply, the "value judgment in the detection vs. the threshold" distinction, the self-report comparison to pain and sleep medicine, the grief/DSDN fight and her reading of it, the drapetomania/homosexuality point and his rejoinder, and the closing suffering argument. For each move say who's right about that specific point, then tell me who is more right overall and what, if anything, both of them are missing. I don't need a history of Szasz scholarship, just careful adjudication of what they actually said.
