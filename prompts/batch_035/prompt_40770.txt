Longtime lurker on LessWrong and the physics threads here, and I've been basically on board with many-worlds for years except for the one thing everyone keeps handwaving: the Born rule. The debate over whether you can actually derive it from within Everett has been running forever — Deutsch's decision-theoretic argument, then Wallace's big book-length version where any rational agent living in a branching universe is supposedly forced to bet as if squared amplitude matters, the Sebens-Carroll self-locating uncertainty approach, Zurek's envariance stuff deriving the weights from entanglement symmetry — and on the other side Kent and company saying the decision-theoretic axioms just smuggle in what they claim to derive, plus the Bohmian/GRW crowd saying this whole literature is itself evidence that many-worlds is empirically underdetermined so they win by default. And as far as I can tell nobody has moved an inch in like two decades. Everyone has the same wavefunction and the same unitary dynamics and the same experimental data.

So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? My working theory: it's not a physics disagreement at all, it's a disagreement about what a derivation of an empirical postulate even has to look like. The Wallace camp thinks it's enough to show that coherent preferences plus branching structure force amplitude-weighting on you; the critics think that's just renaming the postulate as an axiom of rationality and then claiming you derived it, which is definately not the same thing. If my theory is right this is closer to a methodology fight than a quantum mechanics fight. But I'm not sure, and there's a second layer I cant sort out: whether self-locating uncertainty even makes sense in a deterministic theory where branches don't come with a natural measure on them until after you already have the Born weights. Like, "how many copies of me are in that branch" seems like it presupposes the answer you were trying to derive. Also there's the branch counting problem — if you just count branches you get garbage weights, which nobody on any side disputes, but the camps read opposite morals into it.

Diagnose the disagreement using what the sides actually argue, tell me whether it's verbal, methodological, or a genuine hidden formal question in disguise, and be honest about whether anything — a theorem, a better decision theory, some clever experiment, a conceptual distinction I'm missing — could actually end it. If the answer is that the impasse is permanent because the two camps have different standards for what counts as an explanation, I want to know why that's more interesting than just saying they're talking past each other. Please answer in American English.
