So here's my situation. I teach a grad seminar on rational choice, and this term I finally carved out room for the absent-minded driver problem, which I've always loved because it's the rare decision-theory puzzle where the math is trivial and the conceptual mess is enormous. Let me lay out the setup so we're working from the same page.

A driver is on a highway with two identical exits. If he exits at the first intersection he gets payoff 0 (long detour), if he exits at the second he gets 4 (home in time for dinner), and if he sails past both he gets 1 (a longer detour than exiting at the second, but not a disaster). He's absent-minded in a specific, idealized way: while at any intersection he cannot tell which one it is, he remembers nothing from between intersections, and he knows he'll decide the same way at each one. Ex ante, the best he can do is a mixed strategy -- continue with probability 2/3 -- for an expected payoff of 4/3. That part is not controversial.

The mess starts once he's actually AT an intersection. If he knows he's been executing the 2/3 policy, then conditional on being at an intersection, his credence that this is the first one is 2/3. And now the tempting thought: "I'm probably at the first one, so I'll keep driving now and exit at the next one" -- which seems to promise (2/3)(4) + (1/3)(1) = 3, massively beating the 4/3 his best feasible policy delivers. But no feasible policy can do better than 4/3, so something in that little calculation has to be rotten.

What I want from you: write up an argument defending the ex ante optimal policy even from the driver's seat at the intersection. Specifically, I want you to pinpoint exactly where the 3 calculation goes wrong -- is it double-counting a benefit, treating your current action as mere evidence about a past action you can no longer cause, helping yourself to a correlation you can't act on, or something else entirely? And then say what general lesson about planning, commitment and self-locating uncertainty the case is supposed to teach us. Finally, stress-test your own argument: construct a three-intersection version with your own payoff numbers, work out the ex ante optimal policy and what the corresponding "naive" at-the-intersection calculation yields there, and confirm that whatever diagnosis you gave still localizes the error. I'm planning to hand whatever you produce to my students as a target to attack, so don't leave any soft spots in it -- I'd rather they find a genuine objection than a sloppy one.
