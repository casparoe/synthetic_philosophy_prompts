I have spent some weeks reading my way through the literature on our emotional responses to fiction, and I have come away able to recite the standard moves — that the responses are quasi-emotions generated by make-believe, or genuine emotions directed at thoughts, or irrational lapses — without being able to say which of them, if any, describes what happens to me. So I have decided to work from my own reactions and see whether they fall under a single principle, and I would like your help.

My verdicts on the cases, as honestly as I can render them:

1. I weep at the end of a tragedy I have read many times, knowing precisely what is coming and knowing all the while that nobody involved exists. The grief seems entirely genuine — as genuine as grief gets — and I do not regret it.
2. I feel real suspense rewatching a thriller whose outcome I remember perfectly. This puzzles me more than the grief, because suspense seems essentially to involve uncertainty, and I have none.
3. I am genuinely frightened during a horror film, in the sense that my heart races and I flinch. But if I heard a scream in my actual house at that moment, the fear I would feel would be a different emotion altogether, not a stronger version of the same one. Fictional fear and real fear seem to differ in kind, whereas fictional grief and real grief do not — or so I am inclined to say, though I cannot defend it.
4. I read a piece of reportage about a refugee family and was deeply moved; when I later learned it was a composite, invented for a magazine feature, my emotion collapsed, and I felt vaguely deceived. Yet when a novel I loved turned out to be closely based on the author's own life, my emotion intensified. I cannot see why those two should go in opposite directions.
5. I feel unambiguous anger toward certain villains, and I regard that anger as rational. But after finishing an interactive narrative in which my own choices led a character to ruin, I felt a stab of guilt that I am inclined to call confused. Anger at the villain seems fine; guilt over my own choice within the fiction seems somehow mistaken. I cannot locate the difference.
6. A fictionalised account invents a dying scene for a real historical figure who did not die that way. My pity for him feels pity for him — a real man — even though the occasion of it never occurred. Pity for a wholly invented character, by contrast, does not feel like it has the same sort of object.

My tentative principle, offered in the spirit of something to be repaired rather than endorsed: emotional responses to fiction are genuine when directed at the contents of our own imaginings rather than at nonexistent objects, and their appropriateness is assessed by how well they track what the work makes available to be imagined, not by whether anything is the case.

I have three requests. First, state what principle, or small set of candidates, best captures the verdicts, with the case that motivates each clause — and say honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, since I suspect at least one of the six has to go. Second, tell me what the resulting account predicts for cases I have not listed: fear during a lucid nightmare, where one knows one is dreaming; grief that lingers for days after finishing a novel, intruding into one's non-engaged life; and being moved by a passage one has just learned was generated by a machine rather than by an author. Third, and most importantly: tell me where your own reasoning here is most likely to fail, and what argument or consideration would move you to abandon the account you have recommended. I would rather be told that my verdict in case 3 or case 5 rests on a distinction I cannot really draw, and shown why, than be handed a principle that flatters all six of my reactions at the price of untidiness.
