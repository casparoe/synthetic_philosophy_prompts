And here's the student's answer, copied word for word:

"Arendt's point about the banality of evil is that evil on the scale of the Holocaust does not require evil people. Eichmann was not a demon or a monster; she describes him as terrifyingly normal, a careerist who wanted a promotion, who spoke in clichés, and who, as she puts it, could not think. That is what banal means: the evil is ordinary, administrative, done by a man at a desk. The subtitle is sometimes read as diminishing Eichmann's guilt, and Arendt is partly to blame for this misunderstanding, because she also says he was not Macbeth -- not a wicked man who knows he does wrong. But her actual view is the opposite of excusing him. She explicitly rejects the excuse of orders: she notes that in the Third Reich 'just following orders' was a lie, because Hitler's will was not written into any law that ordinary citizens were required to obey, so everyone had a choice, and the men who refused to participate generally got away with it -- they were not punished severely, at most they had to accept slower promotion. That is why she says the legal concept of the superior-orders excuse was born in Germany, but she is arguing it should be recognized as a fraud.

On the Kant point: Eichmann in his testimony tried to invoke Kantian ethics, saying he had lived his whole life by the categorical imperative. Arendt observes that what he had done was replace Kant's formula with a different one -- roughly, act as if the principle of your actions were that of the legislator, or, in the formulation associated with the Nazi jurist Hans Frank, act so that the Führer would approve of your action. That, she says, was known as the categorical imperative of the Third Reich. So Eichmann did not even get Kant wrong by accident; he was giving the court a morality in which conscience is replaced by the will of the leader.

Her own theory of guilt is that Eichmann's real crime, beyond the deeds, was a failure of judgment. He was incapable of looking at his own conduct from the standpoint of another person; he could not say 'I would not want to be a murderer,' because to him the category murderer did not apply -- the administrative language of the Final Solution had abolished such concepts, so that he could insist he had a clean conscience regarding the killing itself. The deeper meaning of the banality of evil is therefore that the greatest evils are committed without motives: he acted not from cruelty or hatred of Jews but from careerism and the desire not to miss out, and this absence of thought is what made him into the desk murderer he became. And her conclusion is the chilling one: the lesson of the trial is that evil comes from the failure to think, which is why thoughtlessness is dangerous, since such thoughtlessness can be found everywhere, and its spread will one day become the criterion by which we decide whom to eliminate. If we want to resist it we need only take Eichmann as an example: he showed that evil is what happens when someone surrenders his autonomy, which Kant had placed in the will, and chooses to let another person define right and wrong. For Arendt the answer to Eichmann is that those who refused did so because they were still capable of judgment, and she recommends that we all practice that capacity."
