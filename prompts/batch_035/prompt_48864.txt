I'm setting the final exam for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of mathematics. Draft one essay question, answerable in about sixty minutes, on the continuum hypothesis, together with a model answer of roughly 700 words and a marking scheme worth 25 points.

The question must not be answerable by reciting that CH is independent of ZFC. To force genuine engagement, have it center on a short invented exchange between two fictional colleagues: a logician who argues that, since Cohen's forcing shows CH can be neither proved nor refuted from the standard axioms, the question of its truth is not merely unsettled but empty, so that adopting any new axiom to decide it would be legislation rather than discovery; and her Platonist colleague, who replies that an independence proof is itself just another theorem about a particular formal system, and that it is a category mistake to read off from it anything about the sets themselves. Students should be asked to adjudicate.

The question should be constructed so that a top answer must (i) distinguish unprovability in a formal system from indeterminacy of truth, without simply asserting that the latter doesn't follow from the former; (ii) assess whether Gödel's own program of new axioms — his suggestion that additional axioms might earn acceptance through their fruitfulness and consequences rather than self-evidence — answers the legislation charge, or merely restates it in friendlier terms; and (iii) say what a multiverse or conditional conception of set-theoretic truth would have to concede to the logician's position, if it is to resist her conclusion at all.

Two further constraints: the model answer should treat the history (the constructible universe, forcing) only insofar as it does argumentative work, not as narrative filler; and the marking scheme should name the specific moves separating an A answer from a B answer, identify the single error that should cost marks outright — treating "independent of ZFC" as if it meant "shown to have no truth value" — and close with a one-line note on how students typically fail at each of the three required tasks.
