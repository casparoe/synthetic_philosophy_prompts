At my local policy reading group we spent an evening on a proposal to replace our city council with a rotating citizens' assembly chosen by lot, and two members — call them Dana and Marcus — fought it out over six moves without either giving ground. I'd like a careful referee's report.

The exchange, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Dana opened by saying elections are aristocratic in effect if not in name: they systematically select for the wealthy, the credentialled, and the professionally ambitious, while a random sample would give us a council that looks like the city — the mechanic and the nurse alongside the lawyer — which she called descriptive representation, and claimed it is the deeper form of political equality. (2) Marcus replied that selection by lot ignores the one thing we should be selecting for, namely competence in governing: nobody would pick a surgeon or an airline pilot by lot, and legislation shaping a city budget is at least as consequential as surgery, so we should expect the deliberative quality of a lotted council to collapse toward the least-informed member. (3) Dana answered that this misunderstands what elected politicians actually contribute: the drafting, modelling, and legal vetting is done by civil servants and aides regardless of who holds office, so the scarce input from the officeholder is judgment about tradeoffs and a sense of what constituents can live with — capacities ordinary people plainly have, and which we already entrust to randomly selected jurors whose verdicts decide life and liberty. (4) Marcus said the jury comparison proves the opposite of what she wants: a jury deliberates inside a rigid scaffold — a judge's instructions, rules of evidence, a courtroom with the state on one side — whereas assembly members would face lobbyists and PR professionals with no such structure, and capturing twelve or thirty randomly chosen citizens is vastly cheaper and easier than capturing an electorate of hundreds of thousands. (5) Dana countered that capture-by-donors is precisely the disease elections already have and randomization is the cure: a member chosen by lot never runs for anything again, so there is no campaign to fund and nothing she can credibly sell to a donor — which is why, Dana said, the opponents of sortition have always been the people who are good at winning elections. (6) Marcus closed with accountability: an elected official who betrays the city can be thrown out, and that sword hanging over them is what makes responsiveness anything more than a hope, whereas a lotted member faces no possibility of removal and no prospect of reward for good performance, so the rational strategy for her is to please whoever is in the room — the very lobbyists Dana worries about — with no electoral punishment to fear.

I genuinely couldn't decide who was winning as it happened, and I noticed both of them kept switching between what a citizens' assembly would do in our small city and what lottocracy would mean for a whole polity, without flagging the switch. Please structure your response as follows, in this order:

Section 1, "Overall verdict" — no more than three sentences stating who is more right overall and, in one clause, why.

Section 2, "Move-by-move adjudication" — take each of the six numbered moves in turn; give each a short label for its central claim, a rating on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and two to four sentences of justification that engages the argument as actually made, not a better version of it.

Section 3, "What both are missing" — one paragraph on the considerations neither of them raised that you think would most change the analysis, including whether the small-city-versus-nation ambiguity matters as much as I suspect it does.
