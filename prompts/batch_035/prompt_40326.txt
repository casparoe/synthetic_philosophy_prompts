I should say at the outset that I have no academic background: I left school at sixteen, never attended university, and have spent the last several years reading about mathematics and its philosophy on my own, as far as my untrained understanding allows. Through this a few neighbours and colleagues grew curious, and we now meet fortnightly as a small discussion group. At our last meeting I set the group a written question, and I have been asked to mark the responses. Since I have no experience as a grader, and no way of consulting anyone with training, I would like you to do the marking for me. Please give the answer a grade out of 100 and then provide the specific comments that justify the grade, point by point — setting out what the answer gets right and exactly where and how it goes wrong — so that the comments could be handed back to the author as they stand.

The question I set the group was this:

"Suppose a hotel has infinitely many rooms, numbered 1, 2, 3, and so on, and every room is occupied. Explain how the manager can nevertheless make room for one additional guest, and then for infinitely many new guests arriving together on a coach. Does this thought experiment show that an 'actual' or 'completed' infinity is a coherent notion? Where do you stand between Cantor's acceptance of actual infinities of different sizes and the finitist insistence that only the finite, or at most the potentially infinite, is genuinely meaningful? Give your reasons."

And here, reproduced word for word, is the answer submitted by one member of the group:

"To make room for one more guest, the manager asks every guest to move from room n to room n+1. Every guest has a next room and there is no last guest, so nobody is left without a room, and room 1 is left empty for the newcomer. If a coach arrives carrying infinitely many passengers, the manager asks the guest in room n to move to room 2n; this frees every odd-numbered room, and since the odd numbers form an infinite set, the coach's passengers can be settled one per odd room, with some rooms still to spare. The lesson is that an infinite hotel can be full in one sense — no room without a guest — and yet never full in the ordinary sense, since more can always be added.

This shows that a completed infinity is coherent. Hilbert devised the hotel precisely to make this point: he held that the infinite, handled with the right methods, is no more paradoxical than the finite. Since the hotel involves no contradiction, an actually infinite hotel is possible in principle, and if it is possible in principle, actual infinities as such cannot be incoherent, which is just what the finitists deny. The finitist says that only what can be completed can be real; but the hotel shows that completion need not be anyone's doing, since the rooms are simply all there at once.

Cantor, going further, proved by his diagonal argument that the real numbers cannot be put into one-to-one correspondence with the natural numbers, so there are at least two sizes of infinity, and he showed how to generate a hierarchy of ever larger ones. Beyond them all he placed the Absolute Infinite, which he regarded as exceeding every set and as not properly a mathematical object at all — on this point his view is close to the finitists' own. Even so, the standard mathematics of today rests on his set theory, and the combined lesson of the hotel and the diagonal argument is that Cantor was right: actual infinities are coherent, and they come in different sizes, which is why a hotel with a room for every natural number could never accommodate the reals."
