After a career spent running a condensed-matter spectroscopy lab, I retired three years ago and have since been working through philosophy of physics with the diligence of a convert — Putnam, then van Fraassen, then far too much philosophy of quantum mechanics for my wife's taste. What strikes me, coming to the literature late, is how much of the debate about the measurement problem consists of quietly burying things: assumptions, postulates, and above all the poor collapse of the wavefunction, which every interpretation eulogizes in its own way while insisting someone else was the murderer. I'd like you to write a satirical obituary for the collapse postulate, in the style of a serious broadsheet's death notice — the kind with the sober typeface, the survivors listed, the requests in lieu of flowers. Around 550–650 words.

The humor should come from the actual content of the measurement problem, not from cartoonish misrepresentation, because I want a philosopher of physics to read it and nod rather than wince. Some material you might draw on, though I leave the selection to you: the deceased's lifelong refusal to say precisely what counted as a "measurement," which made both its achievements and its death strangely hard to pin down; the family dispute over the year of birth, since no two sources agree on when collapse entered the formalism; the Everett cousins, who send every possible condolence card and attend in every possible branch; the Bohmian branch of the family, who decline to mourn at all on the grounds that nothing ever actually collapsed, the trajectory was decided from the start; the GRW mourners, who grieve sincerely but keep collapsing spontaneously at random intervals during the service; and Wigner's representative, who refuses to confirm the death until a friend has looked inside the casket. Feel free to note that decoherence was the deceased's longtime caretaker — diligent about suppressing interference, but, as the family privately admits, never able to decide which of the surviving branches actually got anything.

Please keep it deadpan throughout: no winking at the reader, no "physicists, am I right?" asides, just the flat gravity of an obituary page, so that the irony remains legible without being announced. And the satire should be fair — the obituary can be a loving one, written by someone who used the postulate every day for forty years and only ever wanted to know what it meant. That is, incidentally, more or less my own position, so if the piece carries a faint undertone of a man reading his own past complicity into a death notice, that would be entirely appropriate.
