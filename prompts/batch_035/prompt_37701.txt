I am a novelist at work on a book in which Adam Smith, having drifted into an unexplained sleep in Edinburgh in the summer of 1790, wakes on a bench outside the University of Glasgow in 2025. The central chapter is a long, combative conversation between Smith and a present-day empire-nostalgist columnist who wants to enlist him as the headline speaker for a lecture series titled Britain's Inheritance: What We Gave the World. Before I can write a word of dialogue, I need to know what the man would actually say, rather than what his admirers or his detractors would put in his mouth — and his writings on empire and colonies, in the Wealth of Nations and the student notes published as the Lectures on Jurisprudence, are the closest thing I have to a script.

Please give me his likely positions, each argued from his actual commitments rather than from his modern reputation, on the following posthumous questions:

1. The dissolution of the empire itself — decolonization, the Commonwealth, and the nostalgia-fueled claim that Britain surrendered its colonies too quickly. Recall his view that the American colonies were the wrong shape of possession for a commercial nation to govern from afar, and his striking suggestion in Book III that the natural course of things might one day carry the wealth and power of the empire westward across the Atlantic.
2. Reparations — whether present-day former imperial powers owe compensation to former colonies, and where his accounting of colonies as a scheme of expense borne by the British public, alongside his treatment of injustice between nations, would push him.
3. The return of looted artifacts, along the lines of the Benin Bronzes or the Parthenon marbles — a question of property, restitution, and what an impartial spectator would judge.
4. Foreign aid and development policy — whether aid to poorer nations is debt repayment, beneficence, or, given his lifelong suspicion of schemes arranged by merchants and ministers, something to be scrutinized before welcomed.
5. The idea of rebuilding preferential trade with former colonies now that Britain has left the European Union — is this simply the mercantile system he dissected, with the monopoly map redrawn, or something his framework would tolerate?
6. A wildcard: given how the westward shift of power he glimpsed actually played out, how would he assess present-day American dominance, and would he recognize any modern analogue to the ungovernable imperial project he thought Britain had undertaken?

Constraints for the draft: keep him recognizably Smithian — neither a twenty-first-century progressive nor a libertarian pamphleteer; show at least one place where his framework genuinely strains to answer a question (his concession that the Navigation Acts, though commercially harmful, could be justified on grounds of national defense strikes me as the kind of precedent a modern question would press hard upon); and mark clearly where you are extrapolating beyond what he wrote, ranking each extrapolation by confidence.

Close with what I need for the jacket copy: a single machine-readable final line of the form

\answer{SHORT PHRASE}

stating, in a phrase a publicist could print, Smith's verdict on the central question of the chapter — whether, on his own accounting, the empire was on net a mistake for Britain itself.
