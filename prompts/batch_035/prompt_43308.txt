My philosophy club spent a month on Lewis Carroll's short dialogue in which a tortoise refuses to accept a conclusion from premises, even after accepting "if A and B, then Z" as a further premise, then a premise about that premise, and so on forever. Our debate about what the tortoise actually shows has now stalled completely, and I would like to understand why. One member says the regress proves that deduction cannot be justified by adding premises, so the justification of deduction must come from somewhere else — convention, or the psychology of how minds actually move from thought to thought. A second says the regress is just a pun on "if": the tortoise treats a rule of inference as though it were another statement to be believed, when it is really a fact about what we do with statements, so nothing follows about justification at all. A third insists both are wrong, because the real lesson is that accepting a rule is a commitment or capacity rather than a belief, which she calls a substantive discovery about logic, though she can't say what would falsify it. We each think the others are obviously confused, but when we argue, we mostly talk past each other. Is this a genuine disagreement about logic, a verbal one about the difference between a valid inference and a justified one, or three different questions being asked under the single heading of what the tortoise shows? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a sharper distinction, a formal treatment of rules, a different example — or should we expect no resolution, and why? Please give a diagnosis with reasons rather than a survey of positions.
