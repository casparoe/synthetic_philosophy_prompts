Every spring I convene a small working group on free will and moral responsibility, and two members have spent three years arguing past each other in a way I can no longer watch. One, call her Professor S, is a Strawsonian impossibilist: she holds that the Basic Argument is sound, that genuine or true moral responsibility requires being ultimately responsible for one's own character in a way that would demand being, in effect, self-created, that this is logically impossible, and that therefore no one ever deserves, in the ultimate sense, either blame or praise. The other, Professor C, is a compatibilist in the reasons-responsiveness and ownership tradition: he holds that responsibility requires only that one's actions flow from capacities for recognizing and responding to reasons, and from a character one has, in a suitable sense, made one's own — a sense that does not require the impossible self-creation S demands. They have finally agreed to a structured adversarial collaboration, and I have been asked to produce the draft of the joint statement they will both be asked to sign at our final session.

Please draft that document. I want, first, a numbered section of claims both could sign — including whatever both accept about the logic of the Basic Argument itself, about the role of constitutive luck, and about the distinction between desert in some weaker sense and the kind of ultimate desert S says is impossible. Second, a table of the precise residual disagreements, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording; be especially careful with the dispute over whether C's "ownership" of character is genuinely weaker than what S demands or merely the same thing described with softer words. Third, the cruxes: I suspect one is whether the impossibility of self-creation actually entails the impossibility of ultimate desert, rather than simply of the former concept; another is whether chains of self-forming decisions, each individually reasons-responsive, accumulate any desert at all, or merely relocate the fortune of one's initial constitution. For each crux, specify at least one concrete case or argument both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance — I would particularly like you to include the case of the serial self-reformer who over three decades reshaped her own character through deliberate, reasons-responsive steps, and the case of the agent who locks in her present values through a single irrevocable act of self-binding. Finally, tell me frankly whether any portion of their dispute is, in your judgment, merely verbal, and if so which portion and why.
