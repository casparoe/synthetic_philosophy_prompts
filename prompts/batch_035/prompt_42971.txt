I teach an undergraduate course on reasoning under uncertainty, and I am weighing whether to assign a chapter from a pop-economics book on prediction that a colleague recommended. My notes reproduce the chapter's core passage as follows; since these are notes rather than the book itself, treat the wording as close to the original but not verbatim:

"Forecasters go wrong by reasoning about their case from the inside: from its details, its special story, the plan as written down. The outside view corrects this. You find a class of similar cases, take the base rate, and bet on that. Richard Gott turned the idea into a formula that works on anything: if a thing has lasted t years and you know nothing else about it, expect about t more, and be surprised if it lasts much longer than a few t. So a forty-year marriage should be good for roughly another forty years and a five-year one for another five; on the same principle, divorce is just as likely the day after the wedding as the day after the ruby anniversary. Scaled up, the formula is eerily prescient about geopolitics: in 1990 the Soviet Union was sixty-nine years old, so the outside view put it in the final stretch of its life, and it was gone within two years. The critics concede the arithmetic but reply that the whole apparatus is induction in a costume: it assumes the future will resemble the past, which is exactly what is in dispute, and it is caught flat-footed whenever a regime or a technology genuinely changes — which is why the outside view always predicts that mature things are about to end and looks wise only in hindsight."

Some of this sounds right to me — the Soviet arithmetic appears to check out — but the marriage claim and the closing sentence strike me as the kind of thing a popularizer sharpens for effect, and I don't trust myself to locate the seams. I would like a careful audit before I decide.

Please go through the passage claim by claim and tell me: what a working forecaster or statistician would accept as stated; where the author conflates positions that the literature keeps apart — I suspect the central conflation is between Kahneman-style reference-class forecasting, which requires choosing a reference class and taking its base rate, and Gott's Copernican argument, which deliberately refuses to condition on anything but current age, but correct me if that is not the distinction doing the work here; and which claims a defender of the outside view would insist be retracted before this went in front of students. I am particularly unsure about two points: whether Gott's principle implies anything like the equal-divorce-likelihoods claim, and whether the Copernican argument, applied in 1990, would really have placed the Soviet Union in the final stretch of its life, or would instead have assigned it decades. Please be explicit about the numbers wherever the method actually yields them, rather than leaving things at the level of the author's adjectives.

When you reach the critics' objection at the end, please distinguish what is settled among people who work on these questions from what is genuinely contested — for instance, how much of Gott's argument is fairly described as just induction, and whether the choice of reference class is a methodological decision or a substantive judgment — and do not present either side's position as the consensus view. Finally, tell me whether the closing claim, that the outside view always predicts that mature things are about to end and looks wise only in hindsight, follows from anything that survives the audit; and if you think the chapter is nonetheless salvageable for teaching purposes, say what I would need to correct, qualify, or delete.
