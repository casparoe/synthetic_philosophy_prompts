I'm a high school junior taking an introductory philosophy elective, and we just finished a unit on Kant's ethics. The part that has stayed with me is his view of animals: my teacher explained that, for Kant, animals are not ends in themselves and have no direct moral standing. Our duties, she said, are only "regarding" animals, never "to" them — what makes cruelty to an animal wrong, on this view, is not what happens to the animal, but what the cruel act does to the agent: it corrodes the compassion we need in order to fulfill our duties to human beings. When I first heard this I thought it was an elegant way of getting animal ethics out of a system built entirely around rational agency. Then I sat down to work through it and produced an argument I can now neither accept nor refute, and I would like to know where it goes wrong.

Here is the argument. (1) On the indirect-duty view, the wrongness of how we treat an animal must be traceable entirely to some damage done to a human being or to the agent's own moral character insofar as that character is needed for duties to human beings. (2) So if no such damage occurs, the act toward the animal is not wrong at all — at most it is in poor taste. (3) Now consider a researcher who performs painful experiments on animals strictly to develop a surgical technique that will save human lives, who takes no pleasure in the animals' suffering and is if anything made more devoted to human welfare by his work; by (1) and (2), what he does involves no wrong. (4) The same reasoning extends to anyone who uses animals, however painfully, as long as the use serves human ends and the agent's standing compassion for humans is left undamaged. (5) Yet Kant himself, and my teacher with him, insists that wanton cruelty to animals is genuinely wrong — that we may not treat living beings that can suffer as if they were mere things. (6) Therefore the indirect-duty view either permits what it was supposed to condemn, or else the phrase "damage to one's moral character" must be stretched so far that it ends up forbidding cruelty to animals as such — which is precisely what a direct duty would forbid, under a different name.

I have tried to locate the weak step, but every candidate diagnosis looks expensive. If I deny (2), the view seems to rest on a substantial empirical claim in moral psychology — that being cruel to animals, even in the line of duty, reliably deforms one's character toward humans — and if that claim is even slightly false in a single case, the Kantian must say that case involves no wrong. If I accept (6), it begins to look as though the famous distinction between direct and indirect duties does no real work, and that Kant simply had two ways of describing one prohibition. If I hold onto (5) by giving up (1), I have abandoned the official Kantian position, which is the part of the unit I found most attractive and would most like to keep. Please tell me which step I should reject, why that step looked secure to me when it is not, and what the least costly rejection is. If you think the argument is actually sound and the Kantian should simply live with (4), say so — I would rather know that than be comforted.
