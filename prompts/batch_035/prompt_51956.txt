I am a philosopher of language preparing a departmental talk — my first invitation to speak at a linguistics-heavy program — in which I will defend the Gricean analysis of non-natural meaning against its classic counterexamples. My thesis is that the recursive intention analysis — a speaker means something by an utterance insofar as she intends to induce a belief in her audience by way of the audience's recognizing that very intention — is not refuted by the reflexive-regress objection or by the showmanship-style cases from the early counterexample literature, because those objections trade on misreading the reflexive intention as a further psychological state stacked behind a first-order intention. Read self-referentially — as one state with self-referential content rather than an infinite nesting of distinct intentions — the analysis survives, and it still cleanly separates genuine telling from merely causing the belief, the way smoke means fire without anyone meaning anything by it. My planned positive move, which I would also like you to pressure-test, is that the reflexive intention should be understood functionally: as whatever intention-type plays the recognition-and-trust role within a communicative practice, not as a state speakers must be able to introspect or report.

The audience: about a dozen formal semanticists, for whom meaning is compositional truth-conditional content and for whom the whole intention-based layer smells of circularity (how can one analyze meaning in terms of intentions, when intentions are themselves individuated by the very contents at issue?); two neo-Griceans, who accept the framework but will insist that most implicature traffic runs on shortcut heuristics rather than real-time intention-reading, and may charge that my reading makes communication too intellectualist; and one committed relevance theorist, who holds that ostensive stimuli communicate by raising expectations of relevance and will argue the recognition-of-intention clause is doing no independent work. In the back row, a cognitive scientist who studies theory of mind and will want to know whether creatures that demonstrably cannot represent second-order intentions can nonetheless mean things, and what my functionalist move licenses me to say about that.

Please prepare my objection sheet. Give me the objections in roughly the order they will actually arrive, phrased the way each audience member would put it — a semanticist's circularity worry sounds different from a relevance theorist's redundancy charge, and I want to recognize the objection in the words it arrives in. For each, provide a two-minute reply, a note on where the reply is genuinely soft rather than merely unfinished, and the follow-up each objector will make if my first answer lands. Tell me which objection I should concede ground on, which I must hold at all costs, and where those two come apart. Close with the question I am hoping nobody asks: the one that my reading of the reflexive intention as functional rather than psychological makes it hardest for me to answer well.
