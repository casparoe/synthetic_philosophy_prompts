I'm a novelist four drafts into a book about a brother and sister estranged by politics, narrated in strict alternation, each chapter voicing one sibling's worldview as persuasively as I can manage it — I've interviewed people on both sides, transcribed their arguments, and done my best to give each narrator the strongest honest version of the other's least favorite positions. My working theory is that echo chambers work by filtering out contrary views: people only ever encounter strawmen, so if I place the strongest versions of both cases inside a single book that readers on either side will actually pick up, the filter is defeated — the contrary evidence is finally in the room. My publisher's copy promises the novel will pierce the reader's echo chamber, and I've structured the whole thing around that promise.

My agent, who read philosophy before she went into publishing, returned the manuscript with a note saying the book is designed to cure a disease the reader doesn't have — that I have confused two different conditions, and that the one I'm actually up against won't yield to this treatment no matter how fairly I write. She flatly refused to elaborate and told me to work it out for myself, which is either a kindness or a cruelty. So before I commit another year: is she right that the project rests on a mistaken model of what an echo chamber is? If she is, I want to understand precisely what an echo chamber is doing if it isn't simply keeping contrary information out — and, since I'm not abandoning the book, what a novel could realistically accomplish against the real condition, given that I can't control who reads me or in what spirit.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — whether my agent is right, and what exactly I've misunderstood — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my setup.
