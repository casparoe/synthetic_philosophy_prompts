And here's the student's answer, typed out exactly as I recieved it:

> Periods are not found in nature; historians make them by looking backwards. Nobody in 1600 knew they were living through a "Scientific Revolution," just as nobody in 1400 knew they were living at the end of the "Middle Ages." The whole ancient–medieval–modern scheme goes back to the Renaissance humanists, who thought of their own age as a revival of antiquity and so needed a "middle" age in between, which they described as a sterile interval. Later this hardened into the phrase "Dark Ages," which medievalists now rightly reject: the Middle Ages saw the founding of the universities, the recovery and assimilation of Aristotle, the scholastic natural philosophy that Galileo and Newton were actually trained in, and even what some historians call a twelfth-century renaissance.
>
> The Renaissance itself became a period when Jacob Burckhardt coined the term in his 1860 book The Civilization of the Renaissance in Italy, where he argued that in Italy, for the first time, the individual emerged as an individual — the modern self was born there, in the Italian city-states, and the rest of Europe eventualy caught up. This picture is now heavily criticized. Social and economic historians stress continuities with the Middle Ages rather than a clean break, and the celebration of Renaissance individualism is widely seen as a nineteenth-century projection onto the past.
>
> Something similar has happened with the "Scientific Revolution." The label only became standard in the twentieth century, mainly through Alexandre Koyré and Herbert Butterfield. Koyré matters most here: taking up Pierre Duhem's archival work on medieval physics, he showed that the new science of the seventeenth century grew continuously out of late medieval scholasticism. Copernicus, Galileo, Descartes, and Newton were reworking problems the scholastics had already formulated — impetus, the latitude of qualities, doctrines of quantity.
>
> The empirical record is also less revolutionary than the label suggests. Galileo's telescopic observations of 1610, above all the phases of Venus, proved the heliocentric system observationally, and once that was settled the remaining debate was essentially mopping up. So what we call the Scientific Revolution was mostly the working out, in mathematical detail, of a conclusion that observation had already delivered.
>
> The upshot is that the whole idea of a Scientific Revolution is a later construction. Steven Shapin put it best in the opening line of his book: "There was no such thing as the Scientific Revolution, and this is a book about it." If even the most careful students of the period tell us the event never happened, there is not much left to retain. A label whose boundary dates are contested — 1543? 1600? 1687? — cannot do any real explanatory work; it can only mislead, by implying a unified, coherent, Europe-wide transformation that the sources do not show. My conclusion is that historians should drop the label and describe what changed in narrower regional and disciplinary terms.
