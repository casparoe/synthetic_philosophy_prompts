I'm taking a behavioral economics course and we just covered information cascades — the Banerjee / Bikhchandani-style models where people observe others' choices, infer that those others had private information, and herd even against their own signal. For the final paper we have to apply the framework to a real episode, and I picked the Silicon Valley Bank run of March 2023. The more I look at it, though, the less sure I am the cascade lens actually fits, and my professor said she wants a committed analysis, not a both-sides survey where I list considerations and refuse to land anywhere.

Here are the facts as I understand them, so we're on the same page: SVB had loaded up on long-term bonds that lost value when interest rates rose, then announced it had sold securities at a big loss and needed to raise capital; the stock cratered, and in about a single day depositors tried to pull out something like $42 billion, with regulators shutting the bank down the next morning. Some of it looks cascade-ish: virtually none of the depositors could have independantly assessed the bank's solvency, and most of them were startups with balances way above the FDIC's $250k insurance cap, so being slow to withdraw meant possibly losing everything — a strong reason to copy whoever moved first. But other features look off for the model. The decisions weren't really sequential; they were near-simultaneous and coordinated in the open — founders comparing notes in Slack channels and group chats, and some VCs tweeting (and then deleting) advice that everyone should pull their money immediately. And the "private information" wasn't private: the interest-rate risk was sitting in the bank's own public filings, so what depositors were reacting to was less hidden bad news and more a shared update about publicly known facts. So which is it — an information cascade where depositors rationally infer insolvency from each other's withdrawals, or a self-fulfilling bank run in the classic Diamond–Dybvig style, a coordination failure where racing to the exit is rational for each depositor given the fundamentals?

Please give me a real verdict for the paper. Map the episode onto the cascade framework as carefully as you can first (who the decision-makers are, what the observed actions are, what the supposed private signals would even be), then tell me plainly whether the cascade model explains what happened or misdescribes it, and which framework does better here. If the honest conclusion is that the cascade lens only captures a slice of the run, say exactly which slice and how the two mechanisms divide the labor — don't retreat to "it's a bit of both" without spelling out the division. And where you think the episode actually teaches the cascade literature a lesson (e.g., about what happens when information is public and moves are simultaneous instead of sequential and hidden), say that too, and commit to it.
