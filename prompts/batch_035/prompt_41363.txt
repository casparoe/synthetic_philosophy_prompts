I do risk policy work at a state environmental agency, and after losing the torture vs. dust specks argument at a bar for the umpteenth time I realized something funny: my side of that argument basically won in actual regulatory practice decades before the internet started fighting about it. But now I cant tell if that's philosophically meaningful or just an accident of administration.

Here's the practice I mean. Environmental and health regulators routinely draw a line under which a risk is treated as acceptable and no further reduction is demanded — the de minimis threshold. In US practice this shows up as roughly a one-in-a-million lifetime cancer risk for carcinogens: if a chemical in the drinking water gives each exposed person a 10^-6 chance of cancer over their lifetime, regulators treat that as effectively zero, stop there, and dont order a cleanup — even though across a large exposed population that adds up to a fully predictable number of excess cancer cases. Similar moves elsewhere: tort law has its de minimis doctrine for injuries too trifling to be worth anything, and analysts zero out impacts below whatever their measurement floor is.

But here's what bugs me. The very same agencies, in the very same analyses, happily use the value of a statistical life. They'll take a rule that reduces each of 100 million people's annual risk of dying by one in ten million, count that as ten statistical deaths, multiply by something on the order of ten million dollars per statistical life, and justify the rule by the sum. Tiny risk to each person, big aggregate — and this time the tiny bits DO get added up, precisely because adding them up justifies acting. So we refuse to aggregate when aggregation would demand even more regulation, and aggregate when it supports the regulation we already wanted? That cant be the principled line.

What view of harm and aggregation does the de minimis practice actually presuppose? Does it presuppose that tiny harms genuinely don't add up — a real threshold or non-aggregative view of badness, basically the side that says no number of dust specks is worth one instance of torture — or only that there's an epistemic and administrative floor, below which we can't measure, attribute, or cost-effectively remove risks, in which case the practice is compatible with full aggregation in principle and the dust-specks utilitarians lose anyway? Reconstruct the implicit theory as best you can, say which rival theories of aggregation would and wouldn't license the practice, and tell me whether the two halves of it (de minimis thresholds over here, statistical-life addition over there) presuppose consistent things or quietly contradict each other. And if we replaced the threshold with a straight aggregative view, what would concretely change — would regulators be obligated to chase infinitely many vanishingly small risks with no stopping point, or is there some principled way to keep drawing the line while admitting the underlying harms are real?
