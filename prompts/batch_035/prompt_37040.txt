I've been stuck on this since Thursday's decision theory seminar, and I'd like a proper diagnosis rather than the wave-of-the-hand I got in class. We were discussing the Death in Damascus case: you're in Damascus, and you learn that Death, who never errs in his predictions, is expecting you tomorrow in Aleppo. A classmate claims he can extract from this the old fatalist conclusion that effort is pointless, and I can't find the step that breaks. His argument, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Death made his prediction before you decided, and his predictions are infallible. (2) If he predicted you will be in Damascus tomorrow, you will be in Damascus tomorrow no matter what you now decide. (3) If he predicted Aleppo, you will be in Aleppo tomorrow no matter what you now decide. (4) One or the other prediction was in fact made, so whichever place you are in tomorrow, you would have been there whatever you decided. (5) Your decision therefore makes no difference to where you are tomorrow, and so no difference to whether you meet Death. (6) When a decision makes no difference to which outcome you obtain, deliberating between the options is pointless. (7) But notice that nothing in this reasoning turned on Death being supernatural: whenever an outcome is fixed in advance by earlier states — a prediction, a causal chain, a deterministic history — the same argument shows deliberation to be pointless. (8) Since in a universe like ours every outcome is fixed in advance by earlier states, no effort is ever worthwhile: not the flight to Aleppo, not the seatbelt, not studying for the exam.

The Lazy Argument has been dismissed since antiquity, yet each step here reads like ordinary causal reasoning to me, and our professor only added that causal decision theory has its own embarrassment in this case — it tells you to flee to Aleppo, and then, once you've arrived and reflect again, tells you to flee back. If I have to sacrifice something, I'd most like to keep (2) and (3), which seem to state nothing beyond infallibility, and (6), which seems like common sense about wasted deliberation. So tell me: which premise or inference do you reject, why did it look innocent, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, if you think the argument is sound through (5), I want to know how you stop the slide to (8) without simply reasserting that effort matters.
