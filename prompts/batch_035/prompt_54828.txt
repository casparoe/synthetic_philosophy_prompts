A friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about Bertrand's chord paradox, and we ended exactly where we began, so I have decided to try to settle it in verse instead. I would like you to write me a sestina.

For context, in case the setup matters to how you shape it: inscribe an equilateral triangle in a circle, draw a chord at random, and ask for the probability that the chord is longer than a side of the triangle. Depending on how "at random" is construed, the standard answers are 1/3 (choose the two endpoints independently and uniformly on the circumference), 1/2 (choose a radius uniformly, then a point uniformly along it, and draw the chord through that point perpendicular to the radius), and 1/4 (choose the chord's midpoint uniformly over the disk's interior). All three methods share the same criterion for "longer than the side" — the midpoint must fall within the concentric circle of half the radius — and differ only in the distribution they place over midpoints. My friend's position is that this indeterminacy refutes the principle of indifference outright: if distributing ignorance equally over the same event licenses three different probability assignments, then the principle is not merely incomplete but self-undermining, and the edifice of "uninformative priors" built on it inherits the rot. My position was narrower: the paradox shows that "a random chord" is a description of no procedure, and that the three constructions are three different experiments that happen to share a sentence, not three answers to one experiment; indifference is silent between procedures, which is a limitation of its scope, not a contradiction in its content. Neither of us moved the other an inch, and at some point the argument became about which of us was treating the other's position as a caricature.

The formal requirements are as follows. The six end-words should be: circle, chord, endpoints, midpoint, radius, random. Stanzas one through three should each carry one of the three constructions, and a reader who knows the paradox should be able to identify which is which from the geometry alone — from where the uniform measure is placed and what counts as the favorable region — without the fraction ever being stated outright, though each stanza should quietly earn its number by the time its sixth line closes. Stanzas four through six should stage our argument: stanza four, my friend's charge that a principle which licenses contradictory verdicts has abdicated its authority; stanza five, my reply; stanza six, the impasse we actually ended at, which was less about probability than about what each of us counted as the same question. Here I want the sestina's own rule to do philosophical work rather than illustrate it decoratively: the same six end-words recur in every stanza, in different positions, and mean differently each time by position alone — which is, in miniature, my whole case, and I would like the poem to know this without announcing it. The envoi must use all six end-words in its three lines, and it should be the dissolution rather than a verdict for either of us: the observation that the question as posed named no procedure, and that this — not any of the three numbers — is what the evening was actually about.

A final constraint: the mathematics must stay honest. A probabilist reading the poem should find nothing to wince at — no fudged criterion, no fraction attached to the wrong construction. Someone who knows the paradox should find the poem right; someone who doesn't should find it a good poem.
