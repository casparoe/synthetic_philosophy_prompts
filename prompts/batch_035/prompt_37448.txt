I'm on a little committee in my town deciding whether to rename our elementary school. It's named after a guy who was basically the big civic figure here in the 1920s -- donated the land for the library, got the hospital built, that kind of thing. But we just found out he was also on the state eugenics board for over a decade and lobbied pretty hard for the sterilization program that our state ran. Some of his letters from that era are genuinely nasty, talking about certain families in the county needing to be "bred out" of existence.

The debate at our last meeting got pretty heated. One side says look, this was mainstream respectable opinion back then, supreme court justices and university presidents were into eugenics, so its unfair to judge him by standards he couldnt have known about. The other side says that's a cop-out, because there WAS opposition at the time -- there were always people, including in his own era, who thought sterilization programs were cruel and scientifically bogus. A retired history teacher on the committee put it this way: if his neighbors could see the problem, the fact that lots of powerful people didn't isn't much of an excuse.

I keep going back and forth. When I read his letters I'm horrified, but then I think about how many things I probably do now that people in 2100 will find monstrous, and I wouldn't want my whole life judged by the worst of it. On the other hand renaming a school isn't the same thing as retroactively jailing the guy -- nobody's claiming he was evil by the standards of 1925, just that we don't have to keep honoring him by 2025 standards.

Can you help me think this through? Specifically I want to know: when, if ever, does the "product of his time" defense actually work, and does it work here? Is there a real difference between condemning someone and just declining to keep honoring them? And does the fact that critics existed in his own day actually change the argument, or is that a red herring?
