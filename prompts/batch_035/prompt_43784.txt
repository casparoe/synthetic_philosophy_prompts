So I've been in a reading group on causation for about a year and a half now, and we've gotten completely stuck on the counterfactual vs. interventionist thing and I'm starting to wonder if the whole dispute is somehow confused. Let me lay out where we are and then ask what I actually want to know, which is not "who wins" but what kind of disagreement this even is.

The counterfactual side (think Lewis-style, and its later patched versions) says c causes e roughly when: had c not occurred, e would not have occurred, and there's some chain of events connecting them. The interventionist side (Woodward's programme) says c causes e when there's a possible intervention on c that would change e (or the probability of e), holding other things fixed appropriately. So far so good, these sound like cousins. But here is where it stops moving:

1. The interventionists say counterfactuals about "had c not occurred" are ill-defined without saying what else you hold fixed, and that only interventions tell you what to hold fixed. The counterfactual crowd replies that interventions are themselves defined in causal terms (you have to say the intervention doesn't affect e except through c, which is a causal claim), so interventionism is circular or at least smuggles in what it was supposed to analyse. Both sides have heard this a hundred times and nobody budges.

2. On the hard cases — late preemption, trumping, whatever — both camps have patched their accounts over and over (Lewis going from 1973 to his later "Causation as influence" stuff, the interventionists adding "active paths" and contrastivism and whatnot) until, to my untrained eye, the two theories give the same verdicts on basically everything, just with different machinery inside.

3. When pushed, people on each side retreat to a different emphasis: the Lewisians talk about the metaphysics of causation (what causation IS in the world), the Woodwardians talk about methodology (what's useful for constructing models, especially in the social and biomedical sciences, where you rarely see the full causal web). My hypothesis, and I want you to evaluate it, is that this is less one debate than two projects sharing a word: one is doing metaphysics and the other is doing something like an account of causal *reasoning and modelling*, and the "who's right" question only makes sense once you've specified which project you're in. But I'm honestly not sure, because the circularity charge in point 1 looks like a genuine substantive clash and not just a framing difference — if interventions really do presuppose causal facts, the interventionist can't claim to have reductive ambitions, and that's a real concession, not a change of subject.

So: diagnose this for me. Is it (a) a verbal dispute about the word 'cause', (b) two groups answering different questions under one heading, (c) a genuine substantive disagreement that's just very hard, or (d) something else, like a disagreement about standards of success for an analysis (reductive vs. non-reductive accounts, and whether non-reduction "analyses" are even doing philosophy)? Use what the two sides actually say, not textbook summaries. And then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a class of cases that cleanly separates the theories, a formal result, a distinction somebody should be drawing, or an argument that we should expect nothing to move and why. Feel free to tell me my hypothesis in the previous paragraph is wrong, half right, or badly stated.
