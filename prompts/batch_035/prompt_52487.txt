I teach philosophy of science, and something happened at our colloquium reception last week that I honestly couldn't referee at the time, so let me lay it out and ask for a ruling. My colleague Marta, from the molecular biology department, got into it with one of our postdocs, Elias, about whether the genetic code is literally a code.

Marta's opening move: obviously it is. A code just is a systematic mapping between one set of symbols and another, and the genetic code is exactly that, sixty-four three-letter combinations mapped onto twenty amino acids plus the stop signals, with fixed rules for where to start reading. If Morse code counts as a code and this doesn't, she said, the word has stopped doing any work, and she suspects that's a philosopher's aesthetic preference dressed up as a finding.

Elias said that's too cheap. Human codes are conventions: somebody sat down and decided dot-dot-dot means S, and the decoding table exists because people keep agreeing to it. There's no convention in the ribosome. The codon-to-amino-acid hookup is fixed by chemistry and the shapes of the carrier molecules, and nobody agreed to anything, so "code" there is a frozen metaphor that got reified because it sounds technical. Strip away the word, he said, and the resemblance to a real code is one feature: both are lookup structures.

Marta came back with arbitrariness. The mapping is not chemically forced, in something like the way a river's course isn't forced: there are variant codes in some lineages ( mitochondria reassign a codon or two), the whole table evolved, and labs have now reassigned codons on purpose, in living organisms. If the hallmark of a code is that the mapping could have been otherwise and can be deliberately changed by an agent, the genetic code has both. And she flagged that "arbitrary" here doesn't mean "random," so Elias shouldn't here it that way.

Elias wasn't moved. A river's course also could have been otherwise, he said, and that's exactly his point: "could have been otherwise" is nowhere near sufficient for "code," otherwise every contingent fact in nature encodes something and we've just defined information out of existence as a useful concept. As for the lab recodings, he thought those handed him the argument: in those cases there IS a codifier, namely the scientists, which concedes that codes need codifiers. His suspicion, which is where he got going, is that biologists trade on the design overtones of "code" and "information" to make the cell sound like an engineered artifact, and that the whole vocabulary comes as a bundle, code, program, blueprint, information, that does that rhetorical work together.

One more lap. Marta granted him "blueprint," since a blueprint is a plan of the whole finished structure, and DNA is more like a recipe: it doesn't specify the outcome, it specifies ingredients and steps whose result depends on the environment. But she dug in on "code" as the one term in the cluster that has earned its keep. Elias replied that it's odd for the one literal term in the cluster to be doing exactly the same rhetorical job as the metaphors, and that a term can be technical and still be metaphorical at bottom, "electron orbital" being his example, which he claimed nobody treats as a license to say electrons go around in little circles.

I stood there with my drink and couldn't call it. Can you go through their exchange round by round and say who's right at each step? I'd also like your view on two things they left dangling: whether Elias's bundle point survives Marta's blueprint concession, and where information in the copying sense fits in, since heredity needs high-fidelity copying of the sequence and neither of them mentioned it, though it seems like it might cut for one of them. Say what, if anything, both of them are missing, and who comes out more right overall.
