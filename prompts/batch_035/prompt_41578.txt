In an online seminar on evolutionary explanation, a discussant has been pressing the following analogy, and I want to test how far it can really carry before I respond to them.

Their starting point is the idea, associated with Daniel Dennett, of free-floating rationales: the reasons why an organism has some trait are often real reasons — there is a genuine answer to why the elk grew those antlers, why hemoglobin is shaped the way it is — even though no reasoner ever entertained them. Natural selection accumulates good designs without comprehending them; the rationale exists out in the world, attributed by us through reverse engineering, and only much later, if ever, does any mind catch up to it. My discussant grants all of that. Then they extend it: the market is a mindless designer in exactly the same sense. Nobody ever sat down and reasoned out why the standardised shipping container, the middleman, the corporate hierarchy, or prices ending in .99 are good ideas; these practices were selected, retained, and spread because they worked, and the reasons they work were nobody's reasons. So there are, they conclude, free-floating rationales in the economy: reasons without reasoners, this time minted by market competition instead of natural selection. And from this they draw a further conclusion: we should treat economic practices the way biologists treat adaptations. Reverse-engineer them; expect them to have functions; and be humble about redesigning them, because the rationale is typically invisible to the person proposing the redesign.

I would like an audit of this analogy.

First, the mapping. Lay out explicitly what is supposed to correspond to what: the designer to the selection process, the adaptation to the practice or institution, fitness to whatever it is that makes a practice persist and spread, the record of past variants to the history of commercial life, and so on. Where the correspondence is strained, say so.

Second, the disanalogies. Some will matter for the conclusion and some will not, and I want them sorted. Consider, at least: the market contains intentional agents at every level, so unlike the gene pool it is made of reasoners even if none of them holds the rationale — does that change the epistemic situation, or is a crowd of comprehending parts still sufficient for a rationale that no one comprehends? There is no clean heredity mechanism and no generation time — practices are copied imperfectly by firms and imitators with foresight, and a designer can anticipate selection rather than undergo it. Regulators, culture, and law are exogenous to the selection story in a way that has no comfortable biological counterpart. And in the market, unlike the gene pool, the selected variants often arrive already comprehended by their sponsors. For each disanalogy you identify, say whether it undercuts the humility conclusion, or merely decorates the analogy without bearing its weight.

Third, the equivocation question. It seems open to my discussant to slide between there being a reason for a practice and someone having a reason for it — and it also seems open to their opponent to reply that a rationale attributed to the market is just a good causal story told with intention-suggestive vocabulary. Address whether the free-floating-rationale framing earns its keep here or is a redescription of ordinary functional explanation dressed in Darwinian clothes.

Finally: state whether the humility conclusion survives once all this is on the table, whether it survives in weakened form, or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole time; and if there is a better analogy available for what market processes actually do with reasons — one that keeps what is true in my discussant's picture — say what it is.
