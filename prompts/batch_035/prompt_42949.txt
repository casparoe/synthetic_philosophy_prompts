I am a psychoanalytic psychotherapist by training, and I think fluently in the vocabulary of my tradition: repression and the return of the repressed, splitting, defense mechanisms, the dynamic unconscious, primary and secondary process, the ego as an agency that can ward off an idea without ever knowing what it wards off. I read almost no academic philosophy, but I have been asked to referee a paper on self-deception that treats the psychoanalytic account as one position among several, and before I write my report I want a firm grip on the philosophical landscape.

Restate the main positions in the contemporary philosophical debate over whether self-deception is even possible — driven by the apparent paradox that deceiving oneself seems to require knowing the very thing one is concealing — in my vocabulary, one position at a time: Sartre's account of bad faith, Davidson's picture of self-deception as a divided mind, and Alfred Mele's deflationary account, on which self-deception is motivated belief formation that involves no intention to deceive and perhaps not even a false belief. For each, tell me what translates cleanly into talk of defenses, unconscious content, and the agencies of the mind; what has to be distorted to fit; and what has no expression at all in my vocabulary. Then give me a residue report: does what fails to translate point to something the philosophical debate is missing, or to something psychoanalytic vocabulary simply cannot say? I am especially interested in whether the philosophers' distinction between intentional and non-intentional routes into self-deception maps onto anything I would recognize, or whether it cuts across my own distinction between repression and other defenses in a way that leaves the two vocabularies partly incommensurable.

Two constraints on your reply. First, keep the whole thing under 500 words. Second, close with a brief confidence note: say how confident you are in each of your translation verdicts, and single out the one point you are least sure of. Please do not invent technical terms on either side to make a translation look smoother than it is — where you have to stretch, say that you are stretching.
