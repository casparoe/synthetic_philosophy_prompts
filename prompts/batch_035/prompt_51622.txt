Some background: I've been reading the rationalist blogosphere since the Overcoming Bias days, so I was around for the original dust specks vs. torture thing and all the rounds since, but a recent argument at our meetup made me realize I never actually resolved it for myself. The setup, if you've somehow missed it: one person is horribly tortured for fifty years, vs. 3^^^3 people each getting a dust speck in their eye (3^^^3 being so large that the tower of exponents itself doesn't fit in the universe; just say unimaginably many people). You pick one.

Maya picks the torture side. (1) Her opener: if a speck is even a tiny bit bad, then specks times that many people swamps any finite amount of torture, and anyone who picks specks is running the scope insensitivity bug -- the exact glitch that makes people donate more to save one named kid than a thousand statistical ones. (2) Dev picks specks, saying nobody would trade literally anything to avoid a single speck -- not one second of discomfort, nothing -- so it's not clear a speck is on the badness scale at all rather than just below the threshold where anything matters. (3) Maya does the chaining move: if specks never add up, tell her where it breaks -- you'd trade fifty years of torture to remove the specks from all but one person, right? And surely a slightly shorter torture to remove one fewer speck? Walk the chain down and you end up choosing torture to avoid a single speck, which she says shows the specks intuition was intransitive all along. (4) Dev bites the bullet on the chain rather than accept the conclusion, and says her argument proves too much: unbounded multiplication of unfelt-able quantities is exactly what generates Pascal's mugging, so the arithmetic is only as good as the commensurability assumption, and that assumption is the thing in dispute -- she can't use math that presupposes her answer. (5) Maya closes with the evolutionary point: intuitions built for a hundred-person tribe are worthless at numbers like this, so trust the multiplication. Dev snaps back that her confidence that multiplication even applies across distinct persons is itself an intuition -- one she happens to like -- so the debunking cuts both ways.

I honestly can't tell who's winning. Please adjudicate move by move, rating each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, saying who's right about which specific points and what, if anything, both of them are missing.
