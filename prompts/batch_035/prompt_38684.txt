I teach critical thinking to a Year 12 class of about twenty-two pupils at a British secondary school — one 55-minute period, movable desks, mixed ability, and no prior exposure to economics or decision theory. I also sit on the working group that redesigned our house-points system last year, and I have spent the past two terms watching it decay in instructive ways: pupils arriving early solely to swipe the register terminals, form tutors nominating their own tutees for community-spirit awards in order to climb the staff league table, and a group of sixth-formers who worked out that points were awarded per hundred words for an extended-reading scheme and began submitting padded drivel by the ream. Goodhart's law and specification gaming have therefore stopped being abstractions for me and become a live professional embarrassment, and I want to design the lesson in which my pupils feel how this happens rather than merely hear about it.

Design that lesson for me, with the following requirements:

1. A competitive game, playable in roughly the first half of the period, in which teams optimise a score that I publish — where the score is an imperfect proxy for what the class is told the activity is actually about, and where the divergence between the proxy and the underlying goal must be discoverable by the pupils themselves before I name anything.

2. The divergence must be able to arise without anyone cheating. I want at least one route by which honest maximisation of the published score still corrupts the activity's real purpose, kept distinct from the route in which a clever pupil spots and exploits a loophole. If you can manage it, build in a third route as well: one where the proxy was only ever a decent correlate of the goal within the ordinary range of behaviour, and it is pushing hard at the extremes that breaks the correlation. The debrief must separate these failure modes cleanly rather than lumping them together as gaming.

3. Materials limited to what an ordinary classroom contains, payoffs in something other than money, and a scoring scheme simple enough that I can administer it while facilitating.

4. A debrief in the remaining minutes that maps specific moments from the game onto those distinctions, and then turns the mirror on the school's actual house-points system — I want the pupils themselves to diagnose that system, prompted by questions rather than by my conclusions, ideally reaching the swiping, the staff league table and the word-count fiasco on their own.

5. An honest note on which feature of Goodhart's law the activity cannot reproduce no matter how well I run it, and what I therefore have to supply by assertion.

6. A prediction of what will go wrong the first time I run it, and how to handle each failure.

There should be no lecture up front: the phrase Goodhart's law should not appear until the debrief, and even then only after the pupils have articulated something close to it themselves. Write everything in British English, and assume I will run the lesson exactly as written, so the rules must be complete and unambiguous.
