My younger brother just had his first solo show — abstract canvases he worked on for six years — and at the opening I stood in front of them feeling absolutely nothing, which was awkward, because he kept asking what I felt. I've been arguing with him about what makes something art rather than a stunt since we were teenagers, and to finally arm myself I read Tolstoy's What Is Art?, the late treatise from the 1890s in which he defines art as one person deliberately re-evoking a feeling she has experienced and transmitting it to others through external signs, judges the quality of the transmission by the sincerity of the maker, and insists that the best art is comprehensible to the simple laborer and not just to a trained and jaded elite. What unsettled me is how many of my own gut verdicts this test seems to predict, because I had thought of myself as a fairly standard "if it wins the attention of the art world, it's art" kind of person. So I sat down and recorded my honest reactions to a bunch of cases, and I'd like you to tell me what principle, if any, captures them — whether it's Tolstoy's, or something in the neighborhood, or something that requires dropping parts of him.

Here are my verdicts. A retired schoolteacher's clumsy, derivative watercolors that reduce her whole congregation to tears at a memorial exhibit: real art, and I'd even say good art. A signed shovel displayed in a museum vitrine, the label explaining that the act of signing is the artwork: I thought about it for weeks afterward, but standing in front of it I felt nothing except curiosity about the label, and my gut says that's not art — it's a philosophical argument wearing art's clothes. The late Beethoven quartets, which floor me every time and mean absolutely nothing to my father, a farmer: great art, full stop. A fast-food jingle, written by a studio musician who genuinely loved composing it, that lifts the moods of millions of commuters every day: by Tolstoy's test this seems to be sincere, universally accessible, universally infectious art, and I find I cannot call it good art no matter how I try. A First World War recruitment poster that fills viewers with authentic, earnest patriotic feeling: sincere, infectious, and I think it's bad — but if I'm not just smuggling in my disapproval of the feeling, I can't say why the jingle verdict and this one point in opposite directions. And a virtuoso forgery of an old-master painting that moved museumgoers sincerely for forty years before being exposed: I keep thinking it was art while they loved it, even though the forger's "sincerity" was in service of a deception. My tentative repair is that sincerity plus infectiousness gets you real art, but commercial or institutional purpose somehow debases the transmission — though I can't state that without it sounding like snobbery, and it would demote the schoolteacher if she'd been paid.

So: is there one coherent principle, or a small set of candidates, that captures most of these verdicts? State it as precisely as you can, note which of my cases motivates each clause, and be honest about which of my verdicts cannot be saved by any principle simpler than an ad hoc list. Please also tell me what the winning principle predicts about a case I haven't listed — a photorealistic image produced by a program, with no human on the other end who experienced anything, that makes strangers cry when they see it — and about the one I keep circling back to: my brother's canvases, made with total sincerity, that do nothing to me. I would like the answer to be in American English, please.
