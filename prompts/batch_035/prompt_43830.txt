For two years I've been in a philosophy of biology reading group that keeps circling the units-of-selection question — whether selection acts on genes, organisms, or groups — and we've hit a wall I'd like help understanding rather than adjudicating. Here's the shape of the stalemate as we've run it. One faction, following the gene's-eye view, holds that group selection is an illusion of bad bookkeeping: anything selection "does" to a group can be redescribed without loss as selection among genes or among organisms, and the famous formal results — partitioning total selection into within-group and between-group components, showing the partitions are inter-translatable — seem to back them up. The other faction counters that the accounting equivalence is precisely where the interesting disagreement begins, because some cases don't look like mere bookkeeping: the literature on evolutionary transitions in individuality, where selection genuinely seems to act on group-level properties (biofilms, early multicellular lineages, the origin of organisms), and the distinction between two different things "multilevel selection" can mean — one where groups are just contexts in which selection among organisms occurs, another where groups are themselves the bearers of fitness-relevant traits and selection among them is doing the causal work. The gene-side members say the second reading collapses back into the first once you pick the right description; the group-side members say the collapse is what's at issue, not something either side is entitled to assume.

My working hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that the disagreement has become two disagreements wearing one label: a verbal one about which description of the same causal process counts as "the" unit of selection, which the equivalence results really do settle; and a substantive one about when a population of lower-level entities becomes a single higher-level evolutionary individual, which the equivalence results don't touch and which is why the fight never ends despite everyone agreeing on the formalism. But I'm not sure the second half of that diagnosis is right, or whether the transitions cases are actually doing the work my side attributes to them, or whether some hidden empirical question — say, about how realistically group-level traits are heritable in the cases we argue over — is what's keeping the two camps apart.

Please write a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is: verbal, substantive, two questions under one heading, or a difference about which cases count as data. Structure your response in four sections, in this order: first, "What each side is actually claiming," where you reconstruct the two positions from what I've described in enough detail to test my hypothesis (~250 words); second, "What the equivalence results do and don't show," where you separate what the inter-translatability of the partitions settles from what it leaves open (~300 words); third, "Diagnosis," where you give your verdict on the kind of disagreement and say explicitly whether my two-disagreements hypothesis survives, and if not, where it goes wrong (~400 words); and fourth, "What would count as progress," where you identify a distinction, a kind of case, or a change of question that could actually move the debate, or argue honestly that no progress should be expected and why (~250 words). Please don't survey the history of the debate beyond what the diagnosis needs, and don't simply pick a side.
