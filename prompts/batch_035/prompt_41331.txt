I am a retired physicist; for most of my career I fitted models to noisy data, and since retiring I have been working through formal epistemology, where I have become captivated by Bas van Fraassen's so-called Judy Benjamin problem. Since the setup matters, let me give it as I understand it: a soldier lost in territory partitioned into Blue, Red, and Yellow zones — with a prior spread over all three, and further subregions within each — receives a radio message telling her that if she is in Blue or Red territory at all, she is twice as likely to be in Blue as in Red. Jeffrey conditioning on the indicated odds drives her probability for Yellow to zero, even though nothing in the message ruled Yellow out; and every proposed repair — Lewis-style imaging, Adams conditioning, minimizing some distance from the prior, or declaring the problem underdetermined — preserves one thing and gives up another.

You are an expert in formal epistemology with a talent for pedagogy. Please think carefully and do not rush to labels: begin by brainstorming the logical space of constraints an update rule might preserve (nonzero probability for unmentioned regions, ratios among subregions within a cell, odds between cells, overall closeness to the prior, sensitivity to the reliability of the source), and only then locate the actual proposals within that space. Then produce a one-page decision tree for my philosophy reading group, titled "Which update rule do you actually endorse?" Every branch node must be a question a reader with no prior allegiance can answer from intuitions about cases alone — for instance, whether Yellow should retain some probability, whether the ratios among the subregions inside Blue should survive the update, or whether the right answer should depend at all on how trustworthy the radio operator is — and every leaf must be a named position: Jeffrey conditioning with its zeroing consequence worn openly, imaging, Adams conditioning, a distance-minimizing or minimum-entropy rule, and the deflationary view that the problem statement underdetermines any single answer. Flag any two leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate, and for any combination of branch answers that no one defends, say in a sentence whether that is because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
