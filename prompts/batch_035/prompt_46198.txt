A friend of mine went deep down the effective altruism rabbit hole and came back with an argument about insects that I can't get out of my head. It runs like this. (1) If I'm unsure whether a kind of being matters morally, I shouldn't treat "unsure" as "doesn't matter" — I should weight my concern by how likely they are to matter, the way you'd weight anything else under uncertainty. (2) Given the kinds of evidence people cite for invertebrates — the experiments where injured animals trade pain relief against other rewards, the wound-tending, the trade-offs between avoiding a noxious stimulus and getting food — her credence that at least some insects can suffer is around 30%, and she'll grant 10% to be conservitive. (3) There are roughly a billion insects per human right now, and their generations turn over in weeks, so the number that exists in any given decade is astronomical. (4) Even if you value an insect's suffering at one ten-thousandth of a human's — a discount she calls "generous to us" — the expected total still beats human suffering by many orders of magnitude. (5) If you're allocating a fixed budget, it should go where the expected benefit is largest. (6) So nearly all of it — the farmed-animal welfare money, the personal donations, everything short of keeping ourselves alive — should go to wild insects: population dynamics research, habitat interventions, whatever actually moves the needle. When I pushed back, she extended the template: if there's even a 0.1% chance that some very simple simulated minds are sentient, and you can spin up as many of them as you like, then the same six steps say we should already be prioritizing those over the insects, and over everyone else. That was the point where I started feeling the argument proves too much — but I can't point at the broken step.

My main suspicion is (4). She framed it as "even a very conservative person can pick one ten-thousandth," but nothing in the argument fixes that number, and if the weight per insect can just keep shrinking along with brain size, the arithmetic in (4) doesn't go through at any fixed discount — it only works if the per-being weight has a floor somewhere in the vicinity of the numbers she happened to pick. Is that the actual flaw, or am I misdescribing it? Or is the chain fine all the way through, and the real price is rejecting (5), which sounds like it ends up costing me my weekends and my donations to the local food bank? For what it's worth, I'd most like to keep (1) — weighting by credence seems obviously right to me, and I'd rather pay somewhere else. But I want to know what each option costs before I concede anything to her.
