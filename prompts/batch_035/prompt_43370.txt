I TA the intermediate ethics course at my university and also run a small philosophy blog, and every year the unit on deception plays out the same way: students arrive convinced that lying is obviously worse than "merely misleading" someone, and by the end they've learned the labels — assertion versus implicature, the Kantian line, the consequentialist flattening — but I can never tell whether their actual views changed or whether they've just memorized the map. I'd like a diagnostic decision tree for the question "is there a morally significant difference between lying and misleading?", built so that a reader's answers to concrete cases, not their allegiance to a school, determine where they land. The point is to sort people by what they say about cases, not by which position they enjoy calling themselves.

On structure: the root should be the question that does the most sorting work — my guess is it's about what makes lying wrong in the first place (violating the norm of assertion by saying what you believe false, intending to induce a false belief, or just producing bad downstream effects), but choose a different root if the logical space is better carved elsewhere. Internal nodes should be cases or claims a reader could evaluate without knowing which branch they lead down. There are specific cases I want on the tree, because the positions genuinely come apart on them: the murderer at the door answered with a sentence that is true but implicates a falsehood (the famous case on which Kant held the true-but-misleading reply permissible while flatly denying a duty to lie); paltering in a negotiation — volunteering true statements selected to induce a false belief, as when a seller emphasizes a used car's new brakes and fresh paint while not mentioning that both were needed after a crash; the scalar case — answering "yes" to "do some of your coauthors smoke?" when in fact all of them do; and a witness who, under oath but replying to an artfully narrow question, gives literally true testimony she knows the jury will read the wrong way.

As for the leaves, these must appear: the Kantian strict-difference position (lying categorically forbidden, misleading permissible at least in the door case); the assertion-norm moderate view (lying is worse per se, though the gap can be outweighed by consequences); the no-intrinsic-difference deception view (whatever is intended to induce false belief counts together, so paltering and scalar cases land with outright lies); the misleading-can-be-worse view (paltering exploits the hearer's own cooperative inference and shifts epistemic responsibility onto them, which some argue is the more corrosive move); and a deflationary leaf on which the assert/implicate boundary is linguistically real but morally inert. Aim for six to eight leaves total, adding others if the logical space demands it. Two further requirements: first, wherever a combination of node answers has no natural occupant, mark the cell as unoccupied and give a one-sentence diagnosis — is nobody there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored? Second, flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate given the questions available, rather than pretending some question discriminates when it doesn't; my suspicion is that the Kantian and assertion-norm leaves will be hard to pull apart with cases alone, and I'd like to see whether you can prove me wrong. Keep the node-level language jargon-free — a reader who has never heard of Grice should be able to walk the whole tree.
