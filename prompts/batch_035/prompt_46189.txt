I have been assembling my considered position on the ethics of meat consumption, and I would like an audit of whether its parts can all be true at once. Here are the commitments as I hold them:

1. The morally relevant cost of animal agriculture is the aggregate of unpleasant experience it causes and pleasant experience it forecloses. Species membership in itself carries no weight; it matters only as a correlate of these.
2. Where welfare data are sparse, I take the number of deaths per unit of food as a serviceable proxy for suffering. On that basis I have concluded that a welfare-conscious omnivore should prefer beef to chicken: deaths per kilogram are lower by orders of magnitude, and I take that to settle the comparison.
3. Crop cultivation kills a very large number of animals — invertebrates and small mammals in fields, through tillage, harvest, and pesticide use — and many or most of those deaths matter in the same way deaths on the slaughter line matter.
4. I take wild animal suffering to be a serious moral problem, and I am genuinely uncertain whether the average wild animal's life contains welfare that is positive or negative on balance.
5. I regard climate change as a grave long-run welfare catastrophe, and I count beef's methane and land-use footprint as a real cost of the diet I have recommended to myself.
6. I support research into cultivated meat as the eventual resolution of this whole tangle, while in the meantime treating my personal consumption choices as doing what good they can.

Several of my friends tell me this package cannot stand, and their complaints disagree with one another. Some say (5) flatly contradicts (2), since beef is the worst offender on emissions; others say (6) is hypocrisy — if I think animal farming is bad enough to want to abolish it, I should not be eating from it in the meantime; and one insists that (3) and (4), taken seriously, must wreck my conclusions in (2) entirely, though when pressed she could not tell me in which direction.

What I want from you is not a survey of these objections but an audit. Tell me which pairs among the six are genuinely in tension, which merely appear to be, and rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally grave. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what conceding it would cost me elsewhere in the set. I will confess that the one I cannot think through myself is the land-use point: a kilogram of beef requires vastly more land than a kilogram of chicken, so switching to beef multiplies my cropland and pasture footprint, and I genuinely cannot determine whether that fact turns my verdict in (2) on its head, washes out as noise, or — given my uncertainty in (4) about the sign of wild welfare — leaves the correction of indeterminate sign altogether. Please work that through explicitly. I am not asking to be talked into or out of anything; I want to know which of the six I should stop believing first, and whether the cheapest revision is a small one or one that unravels the rest.
