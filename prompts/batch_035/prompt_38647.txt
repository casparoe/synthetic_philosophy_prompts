I have no training in philosophy; everything below comes from a year of thinking about Descartes on my own, so please forgive me if I am rediscovering the wheel.

When I first worked through Descartes' attempt to doubt everything, what struck me was not his conclusion but the shape of his move. He tries to doubt his own thinking and fails, and he fails not because his thinking is especially trustworthy but because the very attempt to doubt it is another instance of it. The denial defeats itself by being performed. Since then I have noticed the same shape in other places, and I would like to know whether the pattern has a name.

Here are the instances I have collected. First, the cogito itself: while I am doubting, I cannot coherently doubt that doubting is occurring, even though I can doubt nearly everything else. Second, a person who announces that there are no absolute truths — the announcement is put forward as though it were true, and not merely true for the announcer. Third, someone who says, in plain sincerity, "I do not exist" — something is doing the asserting, so at least that much seems secure. Fourth, and this is the one that bothers me most: a friend of mine, a hard determinist, maintains that nobody ever reasons their way to a conclusion, since every belief is just the effect of prior causes. But then his own statement is either the product of causes, in which case why present it to me as though it were supported, or the product of reasoning, which he says never happens. He tells me this is mere rhetoric on my part, and I never know how to answer that charge.

What I want is the following. Is there an established concept for this family — claims or arguments that are not refuted by their content, which may be unimpeachable as a string of words, but by the act of asserting, doubting, or denying them? If so, I want to understand where its edges lie, because I can see at least three things it must be distinguished from, and I cannot manage the distinctions myself. One is plain self-contradiction, as when someone says a shape is both square and round; that is false whether or not anyone utters it, whereas my examples seem to need a speaker in order to collapse. Another is circular reasoning, where the conclusion is smuggled into the premises; I cannot find any premise being smuggled in when someone says they do not exist. A third is mere awkwardness or hypocrisy, as with a doctor who smokes; the smoking does not refute the medical advice. A bare name would not satisfy me — I would like to know what exactly the pattern is, what it is not, and which of my four instances really belongs to it, if any do not.

A second question, which may or may not be the same question. I have come to suspect that in the case of the cogito the trick proves much less than it is usually taken to prove: at most that thinking is going on now, during the thinking — not that there is a persisting self, not that the thinking belongs to anyone, and not that any of it will still hold a minute from now. Does the concept covering the family above, if there is one, also explain this shortfall, or does the shortfall need a separate diagnosis? And is there a principled way of stating how much such arguments can legitimately be made to yield?

Please begin your answer with your bottom line in the first sentence and give your reasoning afterwards, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
