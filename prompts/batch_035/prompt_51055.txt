I write about physics and its foundations for a general-interest magazine, and a recent article required me to ask working physicists what the laws of nature actually do. One of them, a very able theorist, kept declining the question with a line he plainly found satisfying: the laws of nature are descriptions, not prescriptions — they don't push anything around, they just compress what happens. He used it to wave off two questions of mine: whether anything in nature enforces the regularities, and whether the universe could have failed to be lawlike in the first place. I came away suspecting that the one sentence packs together several claims of very different strength — some he surely believes, some he probably wouldn't, and at least one that would not justify the refusal even if it were true.

Could you separate them for me? Lay out the distinct precise theses that "the laws are descriptions, not prescriptions" could be expressing — from the near-tautology that equations are not physical agents exerting forces, through the substantive doctrine that the laws are nothing over and above the totality of particular facts about what happens (so there is nothing left over that needs enforcing), to the radical claim that there is no necessity in nature for the laws to be carrying in the first place. For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude, a contentious but defensible position, or false, and — the part I care about most — whether it actually supports his refusal to answer my two questions, or only sounds like it does. I would like the readings as a list I can keep beside me, because I want to work out which version to put to him in the follow-up interview, and where, if anywhere, he has a point.
