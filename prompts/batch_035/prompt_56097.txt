I write about science and ideas for a general readership, and I'm preparing a piece on the old Searle–Dennett standoff over whether machines could ever genuinely mean anything. Searle's position, as I understand it, is that intentionality comes in two grades: original intentionality, which conscious minds like ours have intrinsically, and derived intentionality, which words, maps, and computer symbols have only on loan from the users and designers who interpret them — a spreadsheet cell means what it means to us, not to itself. Dennett's favorite rejoinder is his two-bitser: if I remember it right, it's a coin-operated vending machine built in pre-decimal Australia to accept two-shilling coins, which is later shipped to America and, by happy coincidence of size and weight, ends up accepting quarters — and Dennett invites us to ask what the machine's states are about, arguing that the answer shifts with the interpreting community, which suggests that even the original kind of intentionality may be a flattering story we tell about ourselves rather than a metaphysical difference in kind. Please correct me if I've garbled either side's story; I want the background right before I build on it.

What I'd like from you is a thought experiment of my own, built to specification. The target: a case in which it becomes genuinely contestable, rather than stipulable, whether a system's intentionality is derived or original — one where Searle's distinction gives no clean verdict without appealing to some extra premise that isn't already in the distinction itself. The constraints are as follows. First, the setting must be mundane and realistic — an engineering project, a laboratory, an industrial deployment — no room-filling brains, no sudden awakenings, and crucially no stipulation that the system is conscious; the whole point is to keep the case out of reach of the easy answer that consciousness was smuggled in. Second, the system's history must be gradual: whatever content its states acquire should emerge through successive redesigns, retrainings, and re-deployments, such that no single moment marks a transition from borrowed meaning to something else. Third — and this is the feature I care most about — at some point the original source of the derivation must go dark or go wrong: the designers' intentions must become obsolete, misdirected, or simply no longer consulted, so that the system tracks something its makers neither intended nor could specify, leaving the proponent of derived intentionality without a lender to point to.

After you present the scenario in a paragraph, do the walkthrough. Say what Searle's framework implies at each stage of the system's history, and where it starts to strain; then show how Dennett, using two-bitser-style reasoning about etiology and misrepresentation, would treat the same stages. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the philosophical work, and what happens to the case if that feature is removed. Then give Searle his best shot — I imagine it will be some version of the claim that all machine intentionality is observer-relative, and that nothing short of the causal powers of biological brains could make syntax intrinsic to physics — and assess whether that reply blunts the case or merely restates the distinction under pressure. I'd rather you end by telling me honestly whether the case succeeds on its own terms or quietly begs the question, since that judgment is what will decide whether the piece argues or merely illustrates.
