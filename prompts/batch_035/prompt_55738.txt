For a seminar paper I'm writing on the ethics of influence, I'm trying to get clear on the distinction between manipulation and legitimate persuasion, and I keep noticing that the standard accounts seem built for a certain picture: one person, speaking or writing directly to another, who is thereby induced to believe or do something. On the familiar "rational bypass" diagnosis, persuasion addresses the target's reflective capacities — it offers grounds that the target could weigh, reject, or endorse — while manipulation routes around them, working through framing, emotional association, or exploitation of some cognitive shortcoming. I'd like to stress-test that picture against a historical episode, and I want the analysis to run in both directions: what the theory illuminates about the episode, and what the episode does to the theory.

The episode is the 1929 "torches of freedom" campaign. George Washington Hill, head of the American Tobacco Company, wanted to open the female market for Lucky Strikes and hired Edward Bernays, the founder of modern public relations. Bernays took the psychoanalyst A. A. Brill's suggestion that cigarettes could be equated with emancipation, and arranged for a group of women to march down New York's Easter Sunday Parade in March 1929, ostentatiously smoking — a public act that was then taboo for women. Crucially, Bernays hired his own photographers so that good pictures would be taken and widely published, and the stunt was framed to the press not as advertising at all but as news: a spontaneous feminist protest against a sex taboo, complete with the slogan that women should "light another torch of freedom." The women whose behavior was ultimately targeted were not the direct audience of any communication from Bernays or from American Tobacco; they read about the event in their newspapers, or saw the photographs, and took themselves to be learning what was happening in the world rather than being sold something.

Please analyze the campaign through the lens of the manipulation/persuasion distinction, with a disciplined mapping rather than a loose comparison. Specifically, I want the following addressed: (1) Was this manipulation by the lights of the bypass account, given that nothing false was asserted, no argument was addressed to the targets, and indeed the intended targets never received a communication from the manipulator at all — does the standard theory even have the resources to classify a staged event whose message is laundered through journalism, or does it misdescribe this as a scaled-up version of a one-on-one case? (2) A defender might say the campaign merely attached the product to an ideal the audience genuinely held, which is what all value-advertising does — but if legitimate persuasion requires engaging reasons for the action itself, and there were no reasons offered to smoke, does that make the campaign manipulative, and does it threaten to make enormous amounts of ordinary advertising and even political rhetoric manipulative too, and is that a cost for the theory or a correction of our intuitions? (3) Note that the taboo was on smoking in public while many women already smoked privately, so arguably the campaign didn't implant a desire but lowered the perceived social cost of acting on an existing one — can the manipulation/persuasion distinction, drawn for the alteration of beliefs and choices, say anything about the manipulation of norms and perceived costs, or is this a point where the theory is silent? (4) Finally, say where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak, and then assess what the episode shows about it: a confirmation of the bypass account at the scale of mass society, an anomaly, or a case the dyadic picture never anticipated? I'd rather have an honest verdict about the theory's limits than a tidy demonstration that it handles everything.
