For my school's philosophy society I'm preparing a session on whether there can be reasons for action that are entirely disconnected from what an agent already cares about — the internal versus external reasons dispute. I'd like you to construct a thought experiment engineered so that the two sides deliver different verdicts, under constraints that close off their usual escape routes.

Requirements for the case: the agent must be fully informed, free of irrationality or compulsion, and reflectively stable, so that an internal reasons theorist cannot point to any defect in how the agent deliberates; the setting must be ordinary life (a workplace or family situation, not science fiction); and the agent must feel no regret afterward, so that the externalist cannot smuggle the reason in through prudence. The agent's indifference should be toward something we would ordinarily count as mattering, and the scenario itself must not stipulate any verdict — the verdicts should follow from the theories, not be built into the setup.

Please present the result as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion:

1. The scenario.
2. What an internal reasons theorist concludes about the agent, and in one sentence why.
3. What an external reasons theorist concludes, and in one sentence why.
4. The single feature of the case that is doing the work, and what happens to the disagreement if that feature is stripped out.
5. The strongest reply still available to the internalist, stated as fairly as you can manage.
