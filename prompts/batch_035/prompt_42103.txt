I'm a novelist working on a linked sequence of stories about medical decision-making, and I'd like one of them to dramatize the problem of the earlier self's authority over the later self — the question of what, if anything, an advance directive can legitimately bind. I could use your help drafting it.

The premise: Margaret, a retired hospice nurse, drafted an advance directive at sixty-two, shortly after her own father's prolonged final illness, in which she refused antibiotics and hospitalization once she could no longer recognize her children. Now seventy-nine and in advanced dementia, she does not recognize anyone — but she is, by every visible measure, content: she sorts fabric swatches by color for hours, hums along to hymns she can't name, and lights up at the feeder outside the window. She has a chest infection; a week of antibiotics would very likely clear it. Her daughter Ruth, the named proxy, must decide.

What I want the story to take seriously is the genuine two-sidedness of the philosophical dispute here. On one reading, Margaret's directive is an exercise of the autonomy of the person she really was — the one who wrote it — and honoring it respects the integrity of her life as a whole rather than the comfort of its final, diminished fragment. On another reading, it is the last wish of a stranger imposed on a different person who now exists, whose simple contentment arguably counts for something even if it is not the sophisticated wellbeing the earlier Margaret would have called a life. I want the story to give both readings their due without tipping into essayistic narration; the difficulty has to be enacted in the room, not explained to the reader.

To that end, I'd like the story built around a specific device I've been developing: Margaret kept a diary while drafting the directive, and in it she referred to her future demented self as "the tenant" — she was quite deliberate that she was writing a lease for a stranger who would occupy her house, and that the tenant, once moved in, would have no standing to renegotiate the terms. I'd like the story to interleave short excerpts from this diary with the present-day scene, so that the earlier Margaret becomes a character arguing with Ruth and with the woman at the window, while never quite being able to see her. The excerpts should be written in Margaret's voice from her most lucid and unsentimental period, and the tension should come partly from the fact that the earlier Margaret, so confident in her drafting, got the emotional texture of the tenant's life wrong in a way she could not have anticipated — while still, perhaps, being right about the terms of the lease.

A few constraints. Please do not resolve the dilemma by plotting it away — no antibiotic that fails anyway, no convenient pneumonia-induced death, no eleventh-hour lucid moment in which Margaret says something clarifying. The story should end at or just after the point of decision, and whichever way Ruth goes, the story must not tell the reader she chose correctly. Sentimentality is the enemy here; Margaret would have hated it, and the prose should honor that.

Formal requirements: the story should open with the relevant clause of the directive itself, quoted verbatim as a document, before moving into scene. It must not exceed 800 words, and I'd rather it be 600 tight words than 800 loose ones. Please give me just the story, with no accompanying commentary or explanation of your choices.
