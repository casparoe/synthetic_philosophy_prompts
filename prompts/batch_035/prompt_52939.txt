The question as it appeared on the exam:

"A three-judge panel is deciding a tort case. The governing doctrine states that the defendant is liable if and only if both of the following are established: (a) the defendant breached a duty of care, and (b) that breach caused the plaintiff's injury. The judges vote as follows: Judge 1 finds breach but not causation, and votes against liability; Judge 2 finds causation but not breach, and votes against liability; Judge 3 finds both breach and causation, and votes for liability. Explain the paradox this creates for aggregating the panel's votes. Then (i) describe the premise-based and the conclusion-based procedures for reaching the panel's verdict, and state what each procedure costs in this case; and (ii) explain what the dilemma is generally taken to show about aggregating individual binary judgments into a collective judgment when those judgments are logically interconnected."

The student's answer, transcribed verbatim:

"Each judge votes consistently with the doctrine: only Judge 3 accepts both premises, so only Judge 3 votes for liability; Judges 1 and 2 each accept exactly one premise, so each votes against liability. Counting votes issue by issue, a majority (Judges 1 and 3) finds breach, and a majority (Judges 2 and 3) finds causation, while a majority (Judges 1 and 2) votes against liability. The paradox is that the majorities accept both premises, the doctrine says the premises entail liability, and yet the majority of the panel rejects liability.

Under the premise-based procedure, the panel votes on the premises and the verdict follows from the doctrine: since majorities accept both premises, the panel finds the defendant liable, even though a majority of the judges individually voted against liability. The cost is that the panel's verdict contradicts the view of most of its own members. Under the conclusion-based procedure, the panel votes directly on the verdict, and the majority rules: not liable. The cost here is that the verdict can no longer be justified by the doctrine, because the panel, voting issue by issue, would affirm both premises; the group as a whole ends up holding an inconsistent set of positions — both premises, the doctrine, and no liability.

The dilemma is, at bottom, the same phenomenon as Condorcet's paradox of voting: in both cases, majority rule takes individually rational inputs and produces a collectively irrational output. In Condorcet's case the inputs are preference rankings whose majority ordering cycles; here the inputs are yes/no judgments whose majority outcome is inconsistent. Since both are just instances of majority rule generating collective irrationality from rational individuals, the discursive dilemma is best seen as a re-description of Condorcet's paradox at the level of propositions rather than preferences.

What this shows in general is that when individual judgments are logically interconnected, aggregating them issue by issue can saddle the group with a logically inconsistent set of judgments, and this problem arises not only for courts but for any multi-member body — committees, legislatures, expert panels — that decides by majority on interconnected propositions. Since the collective outcome is inconsistent, the inconsistency must have entered somewhere, and the only place it can have entered is the individual votes: at least one judge in the majority must have made a mistake about at least one premise, because if all three judges had reasoned correctly, the group could not have arrived at an inconsistent position. So collective bodies face a structural trade-off: whichever procedure they choose, they must give up either responsiveness to their members' conclusions or the justifiability of those conclusions by the group's own premises."
