For the past few weeks a friend in a physics program has been walking me through what she takes to be a serious problem for the standard resolution of Maxwell's demon, and she has now pushed me to a conclusion neither of us wants to accept: that the second law is saved only by assumptions about the demon's hardware that no physical law actually supplies, or else by a bookkeeping convention that seems to price things wrongly.

Her argument runs as follows.

(1) The second law says the total entropy of an isolated system never decreases.

(2) A demon that measures each molecule approaching a gate and opens it selectively can sort fast molecules from slow ones, lowering the entropy of a gas while doing no work on it — so if such a demon is possible, the second law is false.

(3) The received resolution, running from Szilard through Landauer and Bennett, locates the inescapable cost in the demon's memory: measurement itself can be performed reversibly, but erasing the record to prepare the memory for the next molecule dissipates at least kT ln 2 of heat per bit, which matches the work extractable per bit of information. No net entropy decrease, and the second law survives.

(4) Erasure, however, is forced only on a demon whose memory is finite. No principle of thermodynamics fixes the demon's memory capacity, and nothing in (2) requires the demon to operate in a cycle rather than in a single long run. So consider a demon with an unbounded memory that appends each molecule's record to a register that never fills: it never erases anything, and therefore, by (3), incurs no thermodynamic cost, while, by (2), it goes on lowering the gas's entropy indefinitely.

(5) The only visible reply is that the growing memory itself must be paid for. The currency available is entropy: each bit appended multiplies the number of memory states, so the memory's own entropy rises by k ln 2 per bit, exactly cancelling the demon's winnings. But note what this prices identically. On this accounting, the entropy cost attaches to writing down the outcome, whether or not anything is done with it — so a demon that merely records what it observes and never touches the gate stands in exactly the same thermodynamic position as one that intervenes. That seems wrong: an inert note-taker has compressed nothing, exploited nothing, and it is hard to see what physical fact could compel it to dissipate anything.

She concludes that either the second law is protected only by the contingent fact that real memories are finite, or it is protected by an entropy accounting under which keeping notes is itself somehow costly in the same sense as running the engine. The first option she rejects because she wants the second law to hold as a matter of law rather than of engineering; the second she rejects because she wants Landauer's principle to be a principle about computation, not about scribbling.

I have a vague sense that something in the chain is being counted under one description in one place and a different description in another, and that the word "cost" is where the slide happens, but I cannot make the diagnosis precise, and I notice that (4) and (5) seem to pull in opposite directions about what the memory's entropy growth actually amounts to. Which step or premise should be rejected, exactly? In answering, please address what rejecting it costs: what must then be said about a demon with an unbounded memory, about whether the second law is fundamental or merely statistical, and about the sense in which the inert note-taker of (5) is or is not in the same thermodynamic boat as the gate-keeping demon.
