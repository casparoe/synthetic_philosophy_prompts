I run a weekly discussion group for a mix of second-year maths and philosophy students — 14 people, 75 minutes, room with movable desks. Next session is on the two-envelope paradox, and I want them to run into the problem rather than be told about it. Please design the activity.

Rough shape I'm imagining (improve on it if you see something better): everyone pairs up, each pair gets two sealed envelopes, one containing a slip worth twice the other — amounts in points, redeemable at the end for chocolate coins or similar. Each person takes one envelope without opening it. Then I want a stage where the students themselves articulate the argument that switching is worth 25% in expectation — the "the other envelope is either half or double mine, so my expected gain is a quarter of what I hold" move — and I want that to feel airtight for a while. They should commit in writing to switching or not, with their reasons, before anything is opened. Then the envelopes get opened, results are tallied across the whole room, and the empirical result — switching coming out dead flat on average — has to collide head-on with the argument they just endorsed. After that, a second round with some twist you design, ideally one with an announced distribution so they can see when switching is genuinely worth it and why the earlier half-or-double reasoning couldn't have told them that.

The name "two-envelope paradox" shouldn't appear in anything participants see before the debrief at the very earliest. No lecture up front — the rules of the game are the only thing I say before they're playing.

Structure the write-up in exactly these sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths:

1. "Materials and setup" — ~100 words
2. "Round one: the argument surfaces" — ~200 words, how to get the 5/4 reasoning to come out of their mouths instead of mine
3. "Round two: the tally" — ~150 words
4. "The debrief" — ~300 words, mapping specific moments in the game onto specific parts of the fallacy (why "half or double with equal probability" can't be right at every amount, what it would take for switching to actually be worth something, and the switch-then-switch-back reductio if they find it themselves)
5. "What will predictably go wrong" — ~150 words, first-run failure modes and fixes
6. "What the game can't reproduce" — ~100 words, the parts of the paradox I'll have to supply myself afterwards
7. "Where this design is most likely to fail" — ~100 words on which piece of the probabilistic explanation you'd bet against landing with this particular group, and what I'd see or hear in the room that would tell me the session failed for an interesting reason rather than a boring one — that's the part I'll actually be watching for

One thing I keep going back and forth on: whether pairs should just swap envelopes with each other, or whether there should be some pool arrangement where you hand yours in and draw a stranger's. If you have a view on which is pedagogically better, work it into one of the sections above rather than adding a new one.
