I'm a retired biochemist (protein sequencing mostly, a stint in pharma at the end) and lately I've been reading philosophy for fun and getting into arguements on the internet, which was a mistake. Anyway, here's a pattern I keep hitting and I want to know if it has a name.

The fight I keep having is about whether DNA is a "code". One side says: obviously, the codon table is a code in exactly the sense a cryptographer would use, an arbitrary mapping from one alphabet onto another, and the fact that people literally talk about "cracking" the genetic code (Nirenberg and all that) shows the word was doing that job all along. The other side says: no, a code is something somebody devises, symbols that are about something, minds required — and calling DNA a code smuggles in a coder. That second camp is usually making a creationism-adjacent move, the "codes come from coders" thing, though I've also seen atheists do it in reverse to bait people.

Here's what I've noticed. Neither side ever switches meanings mid-argument. Each is completely consistent. So it's not equivocation, at least not the textbook kind, because textbook equivocation is one person sliding between two senses. What I'm seeing is two people, each locked onto a different but equally old and legitimate sense of the same word, and the whole disagreement is over which sense the word should be carrying. And it matters! The word is load-bearing: "codes require coders" is true on one sense and false on the other, so whoever wins the word wins the argument without doing any further work. I've seen the same shape in fights about whether the universe came "from nothing", where the two sides mean different things by nothing (no matter vs. no matter AND no laws AND no quantum fields), and people wave it off with "oh, that's just semantics" — but in these fights the semantics IS the argument, which seems like the opposite of an idle dispute.

So: is there an established concept for a dispute where two parties hold fixed two seperate senses of one word, each is internally consistent, and the fight is over which sense gets the word — with the twist that the fight isn't idle, because settling the word settles the downstream conclusion? I know about equivocation and it's not quite that. A friend mentioned philosophers have a notion of "merely verbal disputes", but that always sounds dismissive, like the dispute dissolves and nobody was really disagreeing, and my case is the reverse: the parties only stop disagreeing once you force a sense on the word, and which sense you force decides who was right. If there's no exact term, coin one and give me the edges — e.g., how what I'm describing differs from a persuasive definition (I gather that's when someone redefines a word to win, but in my pattern nobody is redefining anything; both senses are standard issue).

For format, please structure your answer in these sections, in this order:

1. "Nearest existing concepts" — a short list (~100 words) of the closest things philosophy already has names for.
2. "Fit and misfit" — roughly 200 words on how my pattern matches each of those and where it pulls away from each.
3. "The carve" — if nothing fits exactly, give this thing a name and a definition, with boundary cases (~150 words).
4. "Back to DNA" — about 150 words applying your carving to my actual argument: on which senses of "code" and "information" is DNA a code or information, is either sense the "real" one, and does "codes come from coders" survive once the senses are pulled apart.
