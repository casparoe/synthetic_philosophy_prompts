I direct our department's interview series with visiting speakers, and our final session of the term is with a philosopher who has made her career defending the procreation asymmetry: the view that there is a moral reason not to create a person whose life would foreseeably be worth not living, but no corresponding moral reason to create a person whose life would foreseeably be happy. She has written on this for decades, fields the standard objections with practiced ease, and is at her best when provoked by a question that doesn't come straight off the syllabus. Please draft ten to twelve questions for me, in a natural running order for an hour-long conversation, going well beyond having her restate the view.

Three pressure points I want covered, each with a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give. First, the structural mirror with the badness of death: she almost certainly accepts that death is bad for the person who dies, on the ground that it deprives them of goods they would otherwise have had; but if the stretch of non-existence after death can be bad for someone by containing deprivation, it becomes hard to see why the stretch before birth can't be good for someone by containing the absence of suffering. I want the follow-up aimed at the reply I anticipate from her, that the dead person's deprivation is anchored in a subject who already existed, whereas no one anchors the nonexistent person's benefit. Second, whether her asymmetry commits her only to a person-affecting restriction — creating the happy child benefits no one, since there is no one it is better for — or also to the bolder impersonal claim that the world containing the happy child is no better, and whether she thinks those two commitments can come apart in cases where they point in different directions. Third, the non-identity problem in its sharpest form for her view: a couple who can either conceive a child with a survivable condition making that life worth not living, or conceive nothing at all. Since no one is made worse off by being created with that life, what exactly does the wrong she insists on consist in — and I want the follow-up keyed to whichever anchor she reaches for, whether it is the badness of the life itself or some broader claim about what we do rather than whom we affect.

I would also like one question built around a case my seminar has been arguing about all term, which I suspect cuts in an unusual direction for her. An embryologist must decide which of two viable embryos to implant, one certain to yield a life just barely worth living and one certain to yield a richly happy one; everything else — the parents' circumstances, the number of children born, the effects on others — is held fixed. The friend of the asymmetry says neither creation is wrong, so no harm is done either way; but it strains credulity to say the choice is a coin flip. Ask her whether she thinks the choice is merely permissible both ways, supererogatorily better one way, or genuinely better one way — and if she takes the last option, press her on whether a view that began by denying that happy lives give reasons for creation hasn't quietly reinstated them at the level of choosing between two lives rather than between a life and no life at all.

Finally, please append to the question list a short note — five or six sentences — flagging which of these pressure points reflect something close to a settled verdict among people who work in population ethics and which remain genuinely open, so that I don't present a contested objection as the received critique in my framing or follow-ups. Where you are unsure whether a point is standard or idiosyncratic, say so rather than guessing; I would rather over-hedge before the interview than be corrected during it.
