I write a weekly column on tech and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I keep ending up in the same argument with my editors. Every time some new system rolls out, one desk wants a piece saying "this thing is political by design" and another says "it's just a tool, politics is what people do with it, write about the people." I've read enough of the "do artifacts have politics" literature to know the actual answer was a taxonomy, not a slogan -- the famous move there distinguishes a technology that *requires* a certain social order to run at all (the classic example being nuclear power, which seems to demand centralized authority and a permanent expert class), from one that's merely *compatible* with an order without requiring it (the solar panels and decentralization comparison), from one where designers just settled a live political question locally through a design choice (the Moses low-bridge story, or the tomato harvester built to need fewer workers). And I know the cautionary tales too: the bridge story got taken apart years later on the facts, which I take as a warning that a too-good anecdote can make a whole literature look dumb, and there's the Latour counter-move where the politics isn't IN the artifact at all, it's been delegated to it -- a speed bump doing a cop's job with no cop present.

So here's what I want: a decision procedure I can actually run in about an hour before deadline, on a single technology in the news, using only public documents (I can't call an STS professor every week and I won't always have interviews). The procedure should sort what I'm licensed to claim into mutually exclusive buckets: (a) this artifact requires a particular political order to exist in any form; (b) it's merely easier or more natural to run under one order than another; (c) its designers settled a live political question in advance via specific design choices, and I can name the choice and the question; (d) the artifact is indifferent but is currently being used as a weapon in a political fight; (e) not political in any interesting sense at all. For each step of the procedure tell me what evidence counts as sufficient, what counts as a stop-and-quote-a-human-because-this-is-a-values-question, and where the procedure will misfire (especially: how do I avoid handing my editor a Moses-bridge style factoid that feels delicious and is wrong -- what would have caught it?). Then run the thing on three cases: a city replacing its parking meters with a smartphone app, a large LLM with a published constitution and reinforcement learning from human feedback, and supermarket self-checkout machines. And I want you to force at least one case where the procedure tells me to write "this one is not actually political in the strong sense" even though the politics angle is what would get clicks -- I suspect self-checkout is the candidate but you tell me. Last thing: be honest about which steps of the checklist are real tests and which are just my judgment call dressed up as procedure, because if it's all judgment I'd rather know now than after I publish.
