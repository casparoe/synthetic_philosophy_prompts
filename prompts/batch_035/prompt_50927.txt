Two friends of mine, Maya and Jordan, spent most of our last board-game night arguing about conditionals, and since I've been deputized as referee and can't do the job, I'm handing it to you. It started mid-game when someone muttered, "If the butler didn't poison the stew, the chef did," and Jordan remarked that the subjunctive version — "If the butler hadn't poisoned the stew, the chef would have" — was a different animal entirely. Maya disagreed: on her view the subjunctive is just the indicative wearing costume, its "counterfactual" label meaning nothing more than that the speaker takes the antecedent to be false, with the past-tense morphology and the "would" serving only to signal that. Jordan answered that the label can't be a definition, since there are perfectly good subjunctives with true antecedents — consider "If the butler had poisoned the stew, he would have shown exactly the symptoms he did show," said by someone who knows full well the butler did poison it. Maya conceded the terminological point but pivoted: fine, the subjunctive merely *presupposes* rather than asserts the antecedent's falsity, and otherwise has the same truth conditions as its indicative twin. Jordan rejected that too, on two grounds: presuppositions are normally cancellable, and this one isn't; and in any case the poison pair seems to show the moods can differ in truth value. He's certain the stew was poisoned, so for him "if the butler didn't poison it, the chef did" is plainly true, while the subjunctive "if the butler hadn't, the chef would have" seems false, because the worlds most similar to ours in which the butler restrains himself are worlds where nobody poisons anything at all — same antecedent, same consequent, different truth value, which looks truth-conditional rather than presuppositional.

Maya's final position was that this just shows the mood shifts the conditional's domain of worlds: indicatives range over worlds compatible with what the speaker knows, subjunctives over worlds closest to actuality, and neither mood has anything essentially to do with how things "actually" went. She accepted, apparently cheerfully, that this makes the indicative's truth speaker-relative — true for the ignorant host, false for the butler himself. Jordan took that as a reductio: a conditional about who did or didn't poison a stew shouldn't be true for one person and false for another just because of what they happen to know, since that would make facts out of ignorance. Neither would give an inch, and they eventually appealed to me, which was a mistake.
