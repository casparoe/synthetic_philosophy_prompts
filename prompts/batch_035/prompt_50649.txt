I am a teaching assistant grading a final examination for an upper-level undergraduate course on moral responsibility and free will. One question asks about P. F. Strawson's essay "Freedom and Resertment" (Freedom and Resentment — apologies for the typo; the essay title is of course "Freedom and Resentment"). Below I give the exam question and then a student's answer, transcribed exactly as written. Please do two things.

First, assign a grade on the standard A–F scale, with pluses and minuses as you see fit. Second, provide the marginal comments you would actually write: a point-by-point assessment that credits what the student gets right, identifies each mistake, explains why each mistake matters rather than merely noting it, and indicates what a fully correct treatment of the relevant part would have said.

Exam question (25 points):

"P. F. Strawson's 'Freedom and Resentment' is often read as arguing that the truth of determinism would not, by itself, undermine our practices of holding one another responsible. (a) Explain the distinction between the participant attitude and the objective stance, and explain what reactive attitudes are and what they are supposed to be responses to. (b) Explain the difference between the two kinds of pleas Strawson discusses: pleas that operate within the participant attitude by modifying or removing a particular reactive attitude, and pleas that call instead for taking the objective stance toward the person altogether. (c) State what Strawson takes himself to have shown about the relation between determinism and our responsibility practices, and by what route he takes himself to have shown it."

Student's answer, exactly as written:

"Strawson's paper is aimed at the pessimist, who holds that if determinism is true, no one is ever genuinely responsible, so our practices of praise and blame are unjustified. Against this, Strawson distinguishes two ways of regarding another person. In the participant attitude, we relate to the person as a responsible agent and respond to the quality of her will: resentment and hurt when she displays ill will or indifference, gratitude when she displays goodwill. These are the reactive attitudes. In the objective stance, we instead regard the person as an object of policy — someone to be managed, trained, or treated — and here reactive attitudes are out of place. Both kinds of consideration come up when people make excuses. If someone steps on my hand, resentment is natural; but if he did not mean to, the resentment is misplaced and should be withdrawn; and if he is in the grip of a severe mental illness, the objective stance is called for. In both kinds of case what has happened is that we have discovered that only the objective stance is appropriate, and the reactive attitude gives way. Strawson's overall conclusion is that the justification of our practices never depended on settling whether determinism is true: since the reactive attitudes spring from our natural human commitment to one another as persons, they stand in no need of the external rational warrant the pessimist demands, and the argument could not be completed in that direction anyway. But Strawson's deeper position is more radical than this suggests. For anyone to be truly responsible for what she does, she would have to be responsible for the way she is — and, going back, responsible for the earlier facts that made her that way — which would require being, in some sense, responsible for her own creation. Nothing could satisfy that requirement. So Strawson is in the end best read as a sceptic about responsibility: he concedes that praise and blame lack rational warrant, and that we go on blaming only because we cannot help it."
