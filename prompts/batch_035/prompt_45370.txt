I teach an upper-level seminar on the philosophy of economics, and this week a student forwarded me an argument she had assembled from our readings on revealed preference. She presents it as a refutation of the entire approach, and although I suspect there is a sleight of hand somewhere in the middle of the chain, I have not been able to isolate it to my own satisfaction, and I would like to put it to the class in its strongest form. The argument runs as follows. (1) On the revealed-preference approach, preferences are not inner states reported by introspection but are defined by choice: when an agent selects x while y was available and affordable, she is thereby revealed to prefer x to y. (2) Utility, in turn, is merely a device for representing these preferences; to say that an option has the highest utility for an agent is just to say that it is the option she chooses. (3) It follows that every voluntary choice is, by definition, utility-maximizing: the hypothesis that an agent chose otherwise than utility-maximizing could not even be formulated without contradicting the definition. (4) Since "contrary to the agent's own good" means, on this approach, nothing more than "contrary to what she prefers," and what she prefers is fixed by what she in fact does, no voluntary choice can ever be contrary to the agent's own good. (5) So when we say of a man that his weekend gambling emptied his bank account against his own interest, the most this can express is our disapproval; it cannot be literally true of a voluntary act. (6) Yet statements of this kind can be literally true: an agent can knowingly, at the moment of action, choose against what he himself regards as his interest, as in ordinary weakness of will. (7) Therefore the revealed-preference approach is false, and utility must be an independent psychological magnitude that choice measures rather than defines.

I have a suspicion the trouble lies somewhere in the middle of the chain, but I cannot state the diagnosis cleanly, and I would rather not hand my students a hand-waving reply. So I have two questions for you. First, which step or steps should be rejected, and why exactly did they look innocuous when the argument was first assembled? Second, what does rejecting them cost the revealed-preference theorist? For instance, does she have to concede that the approach applies only to agents whose choices satisfy certain coherence conditions, and if so, what becomes of the gambling case, which after all consists entirely of voluntary choices? For what it is worth, I would much rather keep (6) than (1): I take weakness of will to be a plain fact of human life and do not want a theory of utility that explains it away. Please answer in continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — in the manner of a letter to a colleague.
