My sister, who did her PhD in philosophy, has started describing my current situation as "a textbook Buridan's ass." Three weeks ago I received two job offers: a teaching position at a college in the town where my parents live, and a research position in another city. The salary is the same, my pros-and-cons spreadsheet has now flipped its ordering four times, and I have re-read both letters so often I could recite them. When she invoked the donkey starving between two identical haystacks because it has no reason to walk to one rather than the other, she meant it partly as a tease and partly as a diagnosis: that my paralysis must be telling me something, that if I really had no preference I would simply have picked by now.

Before I accept the diagnosis, I want to know what the Buridan's ass scenario actually claims. Since the exchange with my sister I've noticed the phrase doing a lot of different jobs, and I'd like a menu of the distinct precise theses it could be expressing. Off the top of my head: a descriptive thesis in decision theory, that an agent with exactly symmetric preferences and no tie-breaking mechanism cannot act; a normative thesis, that choosing arbitrarily between indistinguishable options is a failure of rationality rather than an exercise of it; a metaphysical thesis, that the scenario works as a reductio of the principle of sufficient reason, since if nothing happens without a sufficient reason and the reasons on each side are perfectly balanced, nothing happens at all; and a psychological thesis about people like me, that prolonged indecision is itself evidence of hidden or conflicted preferences rather than of their absence. There may well be readings I haven't listed, and I'd like those included too.

For each reading, please say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false; then say which reading my sister is most plausibly invoking when she uses the scenario to interpret my three weeks of spreadsheet oscillation, and whether that reading actually licenses her inference that I must secretly prefer one offer. Relatedly, two people have advised me to "just flip a coin," with opposite framings: one presented it as perfectly rational tie-breaking, the other as a humiliating concession that I've failed as a deliberator. Does any of the readings settle which framing is right, or is that a further question the scenario itself doesn't touch? Finally, it seems worth noting that actual donkeys in actual fields do not starve between haystacks — real animals appear to come with built-in symmetry-breaking, from noise in their nerves onward. I can't tell whether that fact refutes the scenario, or is the very point of it: that the starving donkey is an idealized rational agent rather than a farm animal, and that the difference between the two is where human freedom, or sloppiness, lives. Please address that too, if it connects to the readings.

Lay the whole thing out as an organized list I can bring to Thanksgiving, since my sister will be at the same table and I intend to win this one on the merits.
