I run a reading group for philosophy grad students — decision theory and epistemology people, comfortable with expected utility and Bayesian conditioning, but none of them have seen the formal literature on imprecise probabilities. Next week its my turn to present, fifty minutes, whiteboard, and I don't want to just lecture at them. I want them coming out the other end actually understanding what the view is claiming, not just having heard the phrase "sets of probabilities."

So don't give me the explanation itself — give me a plan for it. Specifically:

- What has to be in place before "your credence is a set of probability functions" even sounds like a position rather than a confusion? My guess is the second-order uncertainty thing needs to be killed early — a precise Bayesian in the room will say they can already model "I'm not sure what my probability is" with a prior over their own probabilities, so the imprecise view had better be claiming something more than that. Where in the sequence does that get dealt with, and how do I make the "more than that" visible?

- The carrying example. My instinct is the Ellsberg urn, since everyone's half-seen it in behavioral economics, but thats maybe a bug not a feature — they may arrive reading it as "ambiguity aversion," i.e. a psychological bias, and the whole point here is that theres a rational reconstruction of those preferences. So either tell me how to use Ellsberg without it collapsing into "people are biased," or propose a different example that can carry the whole session.

- The misconceptions they'll form, in the order they'll form them, and where in the sequence each gets headed off. Ones I anticipate: reading a credal set as just an interval [lower, upper] of credence (which loses information a set of functions has); thinking the agent should just average over the set and go back to being precise; and the one that worries me most, dilation — conditioning on some event can make your credal set wider, which they will take as flatly irrational. Should I even mention dilation in a first session, or does it do more harm than good?

- At some point someone will ask how the agent picks among acts when the set doesn't induce a ranking. I don't want to teach a zoo of decision rules, but does the view look empty without at least one? If so, which one (E-admissibility? Maximality?) and where does it slot in?

- A check at the end: one question I could ask that would tell me whether they've got the actual idea, as opposed to a vague "sometimes probabilities are fuzzy, I guess" takeaway.

And tell me what belongs in the bucket of things I should openly refuse to cover at this level — I'd rather say "not today" than hand-wave, but I dont want the bucket so big that nothing gets done.
