I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and as the graduate representative on my university's research ethics board I've been asked to circulate a written opinion before next month's meeting on a protocol that has the board split down the middle. The proposal, a collaboration between our medical school and a neurotechnology company, concerns patients with a progressive neurodegenerative condition that destroys cortical tissue in a predictable sequence over roughly three years. Building on earlier work with hippocampal prostheses, the team has developed implants designed to replicate the input-output behavior of the brain regions they replace. The protocol is staged: over some thirty months, each patient undergoes eight surgeries, each replacing roughly an eighth of the affected cortex, with extensive behavioral and cognitive testing between stages. The consent materials lean heavily on what the investigators call the gradual replacement argument — because each step preserves function and the patient remains awake, reporting, and behaviorally themselves throughout, the patient's consciousness and personal identity carry over, just as Chalmers's fading-qualia thought experiment is supposed to show that functional preservation rules out a gradual dimming of experience.

The dissent on the board centers on a gap I keep coming back to but haven't been able to resolve to my own satisfaction. One faction — led by a neuroscientist member — argues that the implants are validated only for matching the coarse-grained input-output profile of the replaced tissue, not for replicating its fine-grained internal dynamics, so the trial secures behavioral equivalence without securing the functional isomorphism that the fading-qualia argument actually needs. If the implants get the right relationship between stimulation and output but achieve it through a different internal organization, then the philosophical safety net has a hole in it, and the thing that might slip through the hole is exactly the thing no monitoring could detect: the patient's own reports would presumably degrade along with their experience, so a fading of consciousness would leave the behavioral data looking reassuring. The other faction responds that this objection proves too much — if you take it seriously, no neuroprosthetic research could ever proceed, and the patients face certain deterioration and death without the intervention anyway. A third worry, raised by our patient-advocate member, is about what honest consent would even look like: can a patient meaningfully consent to a procedure whose worst-case outcome is, by construction, invisible to everyone including themselves?

Please draft the decision memo I'll circulate — approve, approve with conditions, or reject — with a clear bottom line and reasons the investigators and dissenting members could actually respond to. The conditions, if you recommend any, should be enforceable ones the board could write into the protocol (pause criteria, reporting structures, consent language), not vague calls for further reflection. Beyond the ethics-board mechanics, I want the memo to take a real position on the underlying philosophical question: what level of functional fidelity does the gradual replacement argument actually require before it transfers from thought experiment to surgical protocol, and how should a decision-making body proceed when the main risk on the table is empirically undetectable in principle? Please engage the strongest version of the side you reject rather than treating it as a confusion, and be explicit about whether the epistemic problem — the in-principle invisibility of fading consciousness — is a reason for caution or just a reason to stop asking the question.
