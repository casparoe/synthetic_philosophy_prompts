I teach a 200-level social and political philosophy course (22 students, one 80-minute session) and next week we reach Marx. My students arrive with a folk understanding of exploitation as "getting paid less than you deserve" or "being treated badly by your boss," and I want to dislodge that before we open the text. Design an in-class game that makes them encounter exploitation as a structural feature of an allocation of assets rather than as a moralized feeling of unfairness, and that also gives them a bodily sense of alienation as distinct from mere unfairness.

Here is the shape I have in mind, which you should refine, extend, or correct rather than merely fill in. Round one: students live on a token economy where each person, working alone with equal resources, can comfortably meet a "subsistence quota" with effort left over — so that exploitation, when it appears, is visibly not about anyone being below subsistence. Round two: you redistribute the productive assets so that some students own the means of production and others own nothing but their labor, let wages be freely negotiated with no coercion and no deception, and have the game arrange things so that the deals are genuinely voluntary and yet a surplus systematically flows one way. Somewhere in here I want the counterfactual test associated with analytical Marxism — the one where an agent asks whether it would be better off under an equal division of society's productive assets, with the answer determining who is exploiting and who is exploited — built into the game mechanics rather than explained, so students perform the test before they can name it. Round three: turn to alienation proper. Have the owners redesign the product so that workers no longer decide what they make, impose piece rates or a tournament so that workers experience each other as competitors, and add a rule (perhaps a silence rule, or piece-rate tables posted individually) that cuts off the small talk and mutual aid from the earlier rounds.

Deliverables: complete rules and materials (tokens, cards, or paper slips; nothing requiring computers or real money), a payoff scheme with precise numbers so that the surplus extraction is exact and visible on the board, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 80 minutes, and a debrief that maps each round onto parts of the ideas — the structural versus moralized notion of exploitation, the counterfactual test and what it is meant to capture, and each of the strands of alienation from the product, from the process, from others, and from one's own productive activity. The debrief should be honest about where the mapping strains.

Two further asks. First, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: I anticipate things like the student-owners feeling guilty and quietly giving the surplus back, or the workers enjoying the tournament too much to feel anything like alienation, but I want your own predictions with fixes. Second, be candid about which features of the ideas the game cannot reproduce at all — I assume a 40-minute round cannot convey alienation from one's species-being in any but the most gestural way, but I'd like your honest accounting of the gap, including where your design leans on a contested reading (for instance, on the analytical-Marxist reconstruction of exploitation, which some Marx scholars regard as a replacement of the concept rather than an analysis of it). Throughout, where you make claims about how Marx or his later interpreters understood these ideas, say how confident you are in each claim and single out the interpretive point you are least sure of.
