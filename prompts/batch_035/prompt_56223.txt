A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible, and we've concluded that we've been talking past each other long enough that we're going to try the adversarial-collaboration exercise our ethics reading group uses. Let me state the two sides as each of us would accept the statement. She is a dilemma skeptic: every case that looks like a tragic bind — Sartre's student torn between staying with his mother and joining the Resistance, a parent forced to choose which child to pull from the water — dissolves, she says, once you distinguish properly. Either the putative duties are only prima facie, in Ross's sense, so that all-things-considered only one thing is actually owed and what remains is regret, not guilt; or, if you insist both are owed outright, you generate an incoherence, since an obligation to do A together with an obligation to do not-A entails that I ought to do what I cannot, and "ought implies can" blocks that. I am the dilemma realist: I hold that some cases leave an agent doing wrong whichever way she turns; the person on the unchosen branch has a legitimate moral complaint against her, not merely a claim on her sympathy, and the language we reach for in such cases ("I owe it to her either way") tracks a real feature of them rather than a confusion. A theory that reclassifies the residue as mere regret has, on my view, misdescribed the case, however tidy it makes the theory.

Write the joint statement we should be able to sign after a good-faith collaboration. Format it as follows. First, a numbered agreed-claims section: what both of us accept about the phenomenology of hard cases, about what the prima facie / all-things-considered distinction actually achieves, about what the "ought implies can" principle does and does not show, and about the difference between regret and guilt. Second, a disagreements table stating precisely where we still differ, each row worded so that the side holding the view would accept the wording — for instance, on whether the unchosen branch's claim is a moral complaint or only a claim on sympathy, and on whether the sentence "she wrongs him either way" is even coherent. Third, the cruxes: I suspect one is whether the joint satisfiability of moral requirements is a conceptual necessity or only a substantive hypothesis that could in principle fail, and another is what exactly the residue in these cases is a response to. For each crux, name an argument, example, or thought experiment both of us would agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction about it in advance. If you conclude that part of our dispute is merely verbal — say, over what the word "genuine" is doing in "genuine moral dilemma" — identify which part and why; but do not dissolve the substantive disagreement unless you can show it is really made of words.
