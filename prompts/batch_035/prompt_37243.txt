I'm a software engineer with zero philosophy background so bear with me. At work we're prototyping this internal tool for prioritizing feature requests — every engineer rates how painful each missing thing is, and someone suggested we just sum the pain scores across people and build whatever scores highest. My colleague, who did a philosophy minor, said that's naive because of "interpersonal utility comparisons" and I've been falling down a rabbit hole ever since. I care about whether he's actually right or just name-dropping.

The stuff I've found online talks past itself, so I'd appriciate if you could untangle a few things:

1. Obviously when I rate my pain 8/10 and my teammate rates hers 6/10, the numbers aren't on the same scale in some naive sense. But is the philosophical problem just that survey-scaling issue, or is there something deeper that survives even if everyone is forced through the same calibration? From what I can tell, von Neumann-Morgenstern utility theory gets you "cardinal" utility out of gambling preferences, unique up to affine transformation — ok, so each person ends up with a genuine real-valued scale, not just rankings. But apparently that STILL doesn't license comparing across people? Like, why can't I take u(x) - u(y) for me and v(x) - v(y) for her and see which difference is bigger? What exactly blocks that move?

2. There seems to be a distinction between comparing utility *levels* across people ("she's overall better off than me") and comparing utility *differences* ("fixing this removes more of her pain than mine"). And apparently for aggregating by summing, only the second one matters? Why does utilitarian-style aggregation need comparability of differences but not levels, and what precisely goes wrong if each person's utility is allowed to be rescaled independently — I think the phrase was something about affine transformations applied per-person. Does the ranking of outcomes just become undefined, or does something weirder happen?

3. Finally: when people object to interpersonal comparisons, is the claim that they're *impossible*, that they're *meaningless*, or just that they're *unverifiable*? These feel like three pretty different objections (impossible = can't be done, meaningless = there's no fact of the matter, unverifiable = there's a fact but we can't check it) but the texts I found swap between them freely. Did the people who originally pushed this objection — some British economist in the 30s I think — actually hold all three, or did the position drift over time?

Bonus points if you can tell me whether my colleague's objection actually applies to our tool, since our scales aren't vNM utilities derived from bets anyway, they're just survey numbers, and I honestly can't tell if that makes the problem worse or irrelevant.
