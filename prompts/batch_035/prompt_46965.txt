I run a small philosophy society that holds an annual 'metaphysics night' for members who have read a little philosophy but have no technical background, and this year I want to hand everyone a flowchart that sorts them on the special composition question: when do several things compose one further thing, rather than just being many? I'd like you to design that flowchart as a decision tree.

Some requirements on how you build it:

1. Every internal node should be a concrete case the reader can have a reaction to without knowing any jargon — for example, whether the parts of a swarm of locusts moving as a unit compose an organism; whether two statues touching after being glued together compose a third thing; whether a heap of pebbles composes an object distinct from the pebbles; whether a committee, a crowd at a football match, or all the doorknobs in a building compose anything. The reader answers about cases, and only at the leaves discovers which position on composition they actually hold. No question should ask 'are you a universalist?' or similar.

2. Build the tree from the logical space first, not from a list of names: decide which questions carve the space (roughly: does composition ever occur beyond the trivial case; does it occur for everything, whatever the arrangement; if it is restricted, is the restriction principled or brute; and is the pattern in principle statable at all), and only then locate the known positions as leaves — unrestricted or universal composition; the view that composition occurs only for living things; the view that most of the apparent objects around us don't exist (so that tables and chairs go, but perhaps not people); the 'brutal' view that composition occurs and there is no informative answer to when; and the deflationary line that the question itself rests on a mistake about what our ordinary composition talk is doing.

3. For any combination of node answers that no one plausibly occupies, note it in one sentence and say whether you think it is unoccupied because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. And please flag honestly any pair of leaves the tree cannot really separate by cases alone — I'm expecting, for instance, that a tree of this kind cannot distinguish the brute view from unrestricted composition, since they agree on every case and differ only on whether an informative pattern exists; if I'm wrong about that, tell me, but don't paper over separations you can't actually deliver.

Keep it to between six and nine questions, with each question having two or three answer options, and phrase everything so that a reader who has never heard the phrase 'mereology' can still answer every node. In a closing section, please say what would change your mind about the tree itself: which branch placement you are least confident in, and what sort of counterexample to one of your diagnostic questions would force you to rebuild the tree rather than merely trim a leaf. Please write the whole thing in British English.
