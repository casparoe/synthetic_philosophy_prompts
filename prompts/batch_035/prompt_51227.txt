Quick context before the prompt: the domain is interpersonal utility comparisons, the type is the slogan-precisification one. The Robbins attribution checks out (Lionel Robbins, An Essay on the Nature and Significance of Economic Science, 1932, which famously denied the scientific legitimacy of interpersonal utility comparisons), so the prompt can lean on that without a verbatim quote.

So, our RA group had a guy shut down a utilitarian argument for progressive taxation with the old line that you can't compare utility across people — his point was that the whole thing collapses at step one, because you can't actually say an extra dollar does more good for the poor person than for the rich one. Twenty minutes later, same guy, he's arguing we should fund foreign aid over domestic arts subsidies because a dollar goes further for someone in extreme poverty. Nobody called him out and I can't quite put my finger on what's inconsistent, but it smells. I gather the original move here is Lionel Robbins in the 30s saying economics as a science shouldn't touch interpersonal comparisons, so maybe some of it is just a disciplinary boundary thing? Can you lay out the distinct precise claims the slogan could actually be making — like, is it a claim about measurement (utilities have no common unit, so any cross-person ranking is undefined), a claim about knowing (there's a fact of the matter but we can't measure it), a claim about whether there's a fact there at all (the comparisons aren't just hard, they're meaningless), or a boundary claim about what economists qua economists are allowed to assert even if comparisons are fine as ethics? For each reading say whether it's plausible, whether it's trivial or substantive, and — the important part — whether it supports his first move, and if so whether it also undercuts his second, or whether there's a consistent package where he keeps both. No preamble, lead with the bottom line in the first sentence.
