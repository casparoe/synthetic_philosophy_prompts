I was at a dinner where a cognitive science PhD student explained predictive processing and the free energy principle to me, roughly like this: 'The brain is a prediction machine. It constantly generates a model of the world and tries to minimize prediction error — the gap between what it expects and what the senses report. Friston formalized this as the free energy principle, where free energy is basically surprise, so the principle just says that organisms avoid surprises. And he proved that anything that survives has to work this way: any system that resists entropy is minimizing free energy, no exceptions. Perception is when you update your model to fit the senses, and action is when you change the world to fit your model. That's why we're such creatures of habit — the brain likes the familiar and steers us away from novelty, which is why we replay the songs we already know instead of hunting for new ones. Honestly it explains almost everything, which is also why some people call it unfalsifiable: if everything minimizes free energy, you can't ever test it.'

Most of this sounded right to me, but I don't know the literature well enough to spot what's off. Please go through it claim by claim and tell me which parts a working computational neuroscientist would accept as stated, which are conflations or overstatements of a more careful thesis, and which are just wrong. In particular I'd like to know whether the 'creatures of habit' conclusion about music actually follows from the accurate parts of her summary, or whether it depends on one of the distorted ones. And on the unfalsifiability charge: is the version she gave — everything minimizes free energy, therefore nothing can count against it — the strongest form of that objection, or a caricature that a defender of the principle could defuse? If it's a caricature, I'd like to hear what the stronger version would look like.
