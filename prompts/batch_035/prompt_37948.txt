I'm a high school junior working on an extended essay about when several things add up to one further thing, and I've been trying to settle it by listing my gut judgments about cases before looking at what philosophers have said. Here they are. The atoms that make up my body compose one object: me. If those same atoms were scattered by an explosion, they would compose nothing. A heap of sand poured into a pile is one thing — I can point at it, move it with a shovel, say how big it is. The pebbles scattered along a stretch of beach are not one thing, even though there are more of them and they're just as close together. A swarm of bees that coordinates to drive off a predator is one thing in a way that the same bees clustering on separate branches overnight is not. Two books stacked on my desk are not one object, just two books in a stack — and yet I find myself saying a stack of twenty books is a thing I can carry, which is uncomfortable. A car is one object even though I can unbolt a hubcap and the rest is still the car. My grandfather's prosthetic leg is part of him; his cane is not; his glasses I keep going back and forth on. The parts of a bicycle disassembled and spread across a garage floor do not compose anything, but a bicycle missing one wheel still seems to compose a bicycle.

My tentative principle was that objects compose something when they're stuck together and act as a single unit. But it fails almost immediately: the heap of sand isn't stuck together in any way that matters, and doesn't act as a unit doing anything, yet I'm confident it's one thing; while the sun and the planets are gravitationally bound and act together as a system in a fairly strict sense, and I'm inclined to deny that the solar system is a single object the way a bicycle is.

Please tell me what to think. I want a definite answer, not a menu: commit to the single principle you think is closest to true, state it precisely enough that I could apply it to a case you haven't seen, and show me how it handles my cases — especially the heap versus the solar system, the stack of books, and the glasses. If the principle can't save every one of my verdicts, say plainly which ones you would overturn and why those are the right ones to give up rather than the others. If you think my confidence about the sand heap or my hesitation about the glasses is itself the error, argue me out of it rather than working around it. I'd rather have a principle I can defend with one or two admitted counterexamples than a principle so hedged it decides nothing.
