I am writing a novel whose central conceit is a woman who, across fifty years, becomes—in temperament, values, even her sense of humor—someone her oldest friends regard as a stranger wearing her name. I want to stage the metaphysics, not just the psychology. Construct a thought experiment, grounded in her ordinary biography and using no science-fictional machinery (no teletransporters, brain transplants, or fission), under which endurantism and perdurantism deliver different answers to a question the narrator cares about—for instance, whether a sentence like "I was that girl in the yellow dress" is strictly and literally true, or how many people have lived the protagonist's life. Keep the scenario itself to one paragraph, and let the divergence fall out of the two theories' treatments of persistence and temporary intrinsics rather than being stipulated. Then walk through the verdict each theory delivers and why, identify which feature of the case is doing the work, and explain how an endurantist with a relativized-property or adverbialist treatment of temporary intrinsics would try to collapse the difference—and whether, for literary purposes, the residual disagreement would still be worth a chapter.
