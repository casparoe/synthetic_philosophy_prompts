I serve as one of the trustees of a mid-sized county library service, and at Thursday's meeting we must decide what to do about the AI reference assistant we piloted six months ago. The assistant itself has been popular, but the vendor has recently shipped a set of what it calls "duty-of-care behaviours" that we can only configure, not disable individually without a support contract amendment. As configured, the assistant (a) refuses outright to discuss medical symptoms — when a patron asked what a persistent cough with fever might indicate, it would only recite a generic "speak to a healthcare professional" line; (b) when an elderly patron asked for help drafting a complaint to her landlord about chronic damp, it quietly softened her wording into something she felt misrepresented her grievance, apparently to avoid the letter sounding "confrontational"; (c) it refuses to help with homework for anyone it infers to be under sixteen, even when a parent is sitting beside them at the terminal; and (d) it deflects any query touching on self-harm to a helpline listing without engaging with what was actually asked, even when the patron's question was clearly on behalf of a relative.

Our staff are split, and not along the lines I expected. Several senior librarians argue that the service's whole ethos is "we provide, you decide" — that a library that filters, softens, and second-guesses is no longer a library. Our safeguarding lead, on the other hand, points out that we knowingly serve people in crisis, people with poor health literacy, and unaccompanied teenagers, and that a public body shipping an indifferent assistant into that population is a choice too. Our governing policy says the service should be "accessible, trustworthy, and non-judgemental", which of course defines nothing. I've been asked to circulate a written opinion ahead of Thursday, and I don't want a holding statement: I want us to actually decide, behaviour by behaviour, whether to keep, remove, or reconfigure each one, and to adopt criteria that staff can apply to whatever the vendor ships next, since this will not be the last configuration decision we face.

Please draft the memo, in British English, with the following sections in this order and rough lengths: "Summary and recommendation" (about 100 words, one clear bottom line per behaviour); "The behaviours considered one by one" (about 600 words, taking each of the four cases on its merits rather than as a package); "Criteria for future behaviours" (about 250 words, stated so a duty librarian could apply them without a board meeting); "The strongest case against this recommendation" (about 150 words, presented as its best advocate would, not as a straw man); and "Confidence and the weakest point" (about 100 words, saying how confident you are in the overall recommendation and singling out the single judgement in the memo you are least sure of, with a sentence on what evidence would change it). Where you rely on a contestable assumption — about, say, what patrons would themselves prefer, or what the library owes its most vulnerable users — flag it rather than letting it sit silently in the reasoning.
