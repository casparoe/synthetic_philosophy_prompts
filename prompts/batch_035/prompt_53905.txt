I run a philosophy-of-mind evening course for adult learners with no background in philosophy, most of whom signed up out of an interest in sleep or lucid dreaming. My next session, ninety minutes with a whiteboard, is on whether dreams are experiences at all — Malcolm's contention that a dream is not something experienced while asleep but a story composed or accepted upon waking, and Dennett's response that dreams are experiences of an unusual kind, drafted and loaded into memory just before or as we wake. I want a plan for the session, not a rehearsal of the doctrine. Specifically: which idea has to be in place before the question can even seem open to them — I suspect it is the difference between a report of an ongoing episode and a report told after the fact, but tell me if something else has to come first and why. Which single case should carry the whole session — it has to do double duty, lending itself to both the real-time-experience reading and the composition-at-waking reading, and I want to know what that case must contain and what it must be kept free of. The misconceptions the class will predictably form, in the order they will arrive — that Malcolm is flatly denying that people dream, that REM sleep research and neural decoding have long since settled the matter, that Dennett is calling dreams fake and our dream memories deceptive — and exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off. One question I can pose at the end that would distinguish someone who has understood the argument from someone who has merely sat through it. And what I should openly decline to get into at this level — I suspect lucid dreaming and the neuroscience — and why leaving those out does no harm to the core point.
