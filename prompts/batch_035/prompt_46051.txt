Been arguing with my brother-in-law ever since Thanksgiving about the whole judging-historical-figures thing, and I've noticed a pattern I can't put my finger on. Every time someone brings up Jefferson and his slaves, or Churchill and the Bengal famine, or Columbus, the defense is some version of "you can't judge him by today's standards, everyone thought like that back then." And the thing is, that defense is basically never factually true. There were abolitionists in Jefferson's own time telling him to his face that slavery was wrong. If I'm remembering right, Columbus got so many complaints about how he was running things that he was arrested and shipped back to Spain in chains. And Churchill's own people in government were apparently horrified by his attitude toward India during the famine. So the real defense is never "everybody believed it" -- it's more like "enough of his peers believed it that he shouldn't stand out for blame." Which is a totally different claim, and nobody seems to notice the switch.

And then there's the mirror-image thing, which might be related or might not. The "everyone did it back then" logic should apply to the millions of ordinary anonymous people who were doing the exact same stuff -- but it never gets applied to them, because nobody knows their names. We somehow end up arguing about one famous guy, as if the whole society's wrongdoing got condensed onto whoever happened to be famous, while everyone else just evaporates from the moral ledger entirely. Like, my brother-in-law will defend Jefferson with "everyone owned slaves then," but the people that defense actually fits best -- the ordinary slaveholders nobody's heard of -- get neither the blame nor the statues.

So, is there an actual name for either of these? Specifically: (1) excusing someone by comparing them to the moral average of their era instead of its moral best, and the sneaky move of switching between those two baselines depending on whether you like the guy, and (2) the thing where collective wrongdoing gets loaded onto the one person whose name survived history. If there are established terms or recognized debates around these, tell me what they are and where the edges are -- what counts as an instance, what doesn't, how they differ from the nearest lookalike concepts. If there isn't, say so and tell me what you'd call them and why. Don't just list a bunch of candidate labels and leave me to sort it out -- pick the carving you actually think is right and defend it, I'm trying to win an argument here, or at least have one properly.
