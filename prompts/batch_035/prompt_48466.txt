I've been a fixture on LessWrong and the adjacent rationalist forums for about a decade, and somewhere along the way I internalized the standard free-will talking points: the Libet experiment is cited casually as showing that "the brain decides before you do," and the discussion usually ends there, modulo a smug gesture at compatibilism. I have enough philosophy to know that the version of the finding that circulates in blog comments is lossy. But what I actually want is not another metaphysics of free will. I want the practical layer, and I want it done carefully.

Here is the state of play as I understand it, so you can correct me where my picture is off. In the classic Libet-style setup, subjects make a trivially spontaneous movement (a wrist flex) whenever they feel the urge, while watching a dot sweep a clock face, and report the clock position at the moment they first "felt the wanting." Averaged traces show the readiness potential ramping up several hundred milliseconds before the movement, and the reported "wanting" time lands well after the ramp begins — hence the slogan that the decision was unconscious before awareness. There is also Libet's claim that subjects could still veto the movement in a short window after the wanting became conscious. Separately, an influential reinterpretation has circulated for the past decade or so — I associate it with Schurger and colleagues, early 2010s, though please don't lean on my memory — on which the ramp is an artifact of averaging: on any single trial there is no slow buildup, only ongoing spontaneous neural noise, and a movement fires when that noise happens to cross a threshold. On that story, time-locking to the movement and averaging across trials manufactures the ramp out of nothing causal, and the readiness potential is not a decision being made; it is a fluctuation that the averaging makes look like one. And there are the older familiar criticisms: that a wrist flex at a random moment is not a paradigm case of deciding anything, and that the clock-report method for dating a felt wanting is not trustworthy at the relevant hundred-millisecond resolution.

Given all that, please give me the most important implications of this whole literature — original findings, veto claim, and the noise-reinterpretation taken seriously — for how a person like me should actually run my life. Concretely: I build my days on engineered habits, implementation intentions, commitment devices, and a meditation practice whose selling point is precisely noticing an urge arise and declining to act on it, which sounds suspiciously like Libet's veto. I also make use of productivity advice in the "stop deliberating, just start" genre, which on the causal reading looks like exploiting the fact that conscious deliberation is epiphenomenal. I'd like you to sort the implications into two piles: those that survive even if the noise-reinterpretation fully replaces the causal reading, and those that flip or evaporate depending on which reading is right. Be explicit about which practical upshots you're confident in and which you'd only assert if the old reading were correct — I'd rather have an honest "this one rests on shaky ground" than a tidy list. And if you think the honest conclusion is that neither reading has implications for daily life at all, because wrist flexes and life decisions live at different timescales, say that plainly and argue for it rather than gesturing at it.
