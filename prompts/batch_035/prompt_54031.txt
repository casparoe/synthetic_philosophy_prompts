I am a novelist, and I am currently at work on a book in which a central chapter turns on a family quarrel about commemoration: my narrator, the granddaughter of a man implicated (as bystander, then as archivist) in a massacre two generations back, must decide whether to publish his private letters or let them molder, and she conducts the argument with herself by borrowing the vocabulary of the philosophers she half remembers from university. To write her monologue responsibly I have been reading around the debate between those who hold there is a duty to remember and those who counsel letting go — I have Margalit's The Ethics of Memory and Rieff's In Praise of Forgetting on my desk — and I recently came across a review essay in a literary magazine that compressed the whole quarrel into a single paragraph. It reads: "Margalit argues that because memory is the cement of community, humanity as a whole is under a moral duty to remember its crimes; the person who fails to remember betrays the dead a second time. Rieff takes the opposite line: since remembrance mostly feeds grievance and prolongs conflict, societies are better off letting their records of injury lapse into disuse — he admires the amnesty the Athenians swore after their civil war, and faults Margalit for never permitting forgetting at all. In the end the two positions face the same practical question, namely what to do with the archives."

The review is fluent and mostly squares with my impression of the two books, but I have a novelist's suspicion of passages that resolve too neatly, and I would like to know exactly what in this paragraph I can trust before I let my narrator lean on it. Something I half recall from Margalit is that he distinguishes two different levels of how we treat one another — morality and something he calls ethics — and that the duty to remember lives on only one of those levels, though I cannot reconstruct which, or why it would matter for the reviewer's formulation. I also vaguely recall a pair of terms in his book, "common memory" and "shared memory," that were not meant as synonyms. Please go through the reviewer's paragraph claim by claim and tell me which parts a careful reader of Margalit would accept, which a defender of Rieff would wince at, and which are simply misattributions or conflations — in particular, whether "humanity as a whole is under a moral duty" is a fair statement of Margalit's position or a subtle transposition of it, and whether "letting records lapse into disuse" captures what Rieff is recommending or quietly converts a hedged argument against a duty into a prescription of forgetting.

Finally, I want to know whether the reviewer's closing move is earned: does the disagreement between Margalit and Rieff really collapse into "the same practical question" about what to do with archives, or does that framing relocate the dispute to terrain on which it is no longer really being fought — and does anything true that survives your corrections still support the idea that my narrator's choice about her grandfather's letters is the choice the philosophers were arguing about? If the honest answer is that the two books never actually meet on the same ground, tell me that too, because my narrator is exactly the sort of person who would prefer the version that makes her decision philosophically weighty.
