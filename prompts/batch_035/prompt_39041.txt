Construct a thought experiment in which an expected-value fanatic recommends the cautious option while a principled opponent of fanaticism recommends the gamble — the opposite of the usual direction, in which the fanatic backs the long shot and the anti-fanatic counsels prudence. By a fanatic I mean an unbounded expected-value maximizer: no cap on how much value may be at stake, but also no risk aversion beyond the expectational calculus itself. The opponent of fanaticism should be of a specific kind: someone whose resistance to expected-value maximization is grounded in fairness to persons — they hold that when a decision distributes life and death among identifiable individuals, each person has a claim to a fair chance, and an option that gives some of them no chance at all is tainted even if it saves more people in expectation. Constraints: an ordinary, realistic setting (a clinic, an outbreak, a rescue effort — no trolleys, no demons, no perfect predictors); no astronomically large payoffs and no vanishingly small probabilities, so the case cannot trade on the magnitudes that usually generate disputes about fanaticism; the fanatic's choice of the safe option must follow from expected-value maximization alone; and the opponent's choice of the gamble must follow from their fairness principle, not from any stipulated appetite for risk or desperation in the narrative. After the scenario, walk through what each view says and why, identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work, state what happens to the disagreement if that feature is removed, and note how a defender of the fairness view would attack the case. The case is meant to test whether resistance to fanaticism is at bottom a view about probabilities or a view about persons, so it must not stipulate its verdicts: each recommendation must be generated by the theory, not inscribed in the setup.
