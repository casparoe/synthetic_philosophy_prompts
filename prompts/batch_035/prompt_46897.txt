A friend of mine and I have been arguing for years about who should be allowed to vote, and over time I've kept notes on her verdicts because they don't fit any simple pattern I can see. She says tourists should never get a vote, however long they stay, and the same goes for foreign students on four-year degrees and for seasonal workers who come back every harvest. But she says a woman from another country who has lived and worked in our town for thirty years, owns a house, and never took citizenship should certainly be able to vote, at least in local elections. She says citizens who moved abroad decades ago and almost never come back should keep their national vote by mail. She says children obviously can't vote. And when our country debated it, she came down firmly on the side of prisoners keeping their votes while serving long sentences. Last week three new cases came up and we ran out of time before she ruled. First: should the thirty-year resident also get a vote in national elections, or only local ones? Second: should sixteen-year-old citizens with jobs and taxes get the vote? Third: should a man who entered the country without papers but has now lived here fifteen years, working and raising kids who go to the local school, get a vote in local elections? Predict her verdicts for me. Then tell me what rule she seems to be running -- it can't just be citizenship, or the thirty-year resident would be out and the emigrants wouldn't matter; it can't just be where your life is, or the emigrants would be out. Lay out the candidate rules that fit everything she's said so far, say which fits best and why, and tell me which further case -- one I could actually bring up at dinner -- would most sharply distinguish those rules from each other. I'd also like to know what her verdicts suggest she thinks a vote fundamentally is: a say for anyone whose interests are at stake, a badge of membership, or something owed to people who pull their weight. Her answers point in different directions on that, and I can't tell whether she has one principle with exceptions or several principles that happen not to have collided yet.
