I'm on the board of a mid-sized environmental nonprofit — call it the Green River Alliance — about $8M a year, 40-ish staff, three regional offices. Our executive director has gotten really into prediction markets lately and wants us to run what she keeps calling a "futarchy pilot" on a decision we already have to make this fall: whether to consolidate the three regional offices into one. Her proposal: we pick a success metric (donor retention at 18 months post-decision), and we run two conditional internal markets — one on the "we consolidate" branch and one on the "we stay as we are" branch — with staff and some long-term volunteers trading on how the metric turns out in each world. Whichever branch prices higher, we commit in advance to doing that. She even has a board member's kid who did market making at a trading firm willing to help seed liquidity, and the finance committee has provisionally okayed maybe $15k in subsidies so the markets aren't completly dead.

Half the board thinks this is a brilliant way to get honest information out of people who otherwise won't say what they know out loud — everyone knows the regional directors hate the merger, but nobody can actually tell us whether the donors do. The other half thinks it's a mess: 40 traders is barely a market at all, the regional staff whose jobs are literally at stake would be trading in it, and what if the market says consolidate but the staff whose offices close then quietly tank the donor relationships on purpose, so the metric punishes us for following it? And me personally: I like the idea in principle — I've read enough Hanson to think vote on values, bet on beliefs is directionally right for big messy organizations — but I can't shake the feeling it works at the scale where theres thousands of traders and ours would have fewer people than attend our annual gala.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before the September meeting — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with reasons the ED could actually respond to rather than generic risk language. Take the strongest version of her side seriously: the information-extraction point is real, and if you think some version of this is worth doing, say which version. But be honest about which parts of the futarchy idea are load-bearing and which ones we'd be dropping by doing it at this scale, and whether whatever we'd end up running still deserves the name or is basically an expensive anonymous survey with extra steps. If you recommend conditions, make them conditions that fix something specific — trading restrictions, excluding interested parties, a different or slower metric — not just cover for the board. And if the honest answer is that the pilot is harmless precisely because it's too small to matter either way, say that too; I'd rather hear it than have us pretend we're testing something we're not.
