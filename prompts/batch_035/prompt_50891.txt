My department has a reading group that's been fighting about the coherence arguments for expected utility maximization for what feels like forver, and it's stuck in a really characteristic way. Everyone agrees on the math: the representation theorems (Savage, von Neumann-Morgenstern) go through, everyone agrees real people violate the independence axiom in the lab (Allais and all that), and everyone can recite the money pump argument for transitivity and the Dutch book reasoning. And yet the "coherence crowd" and the "permissive crowd" haven't moved an inch in two years. The coherence people say: if your preferences are cyclical, or you violate independence, you're exploitable, and being exploitable just is a form of irrationality; the theorems show any coherent agent is representable as an EU maximizer, so rationality requires being one. The permissive people say: the pumps need contingent contracts and a market-maker who never goes away, a "resolute" agent who commits in advance never gets pumped, and in any case being exploitable shows you're a mark, not that you're incoherent — plus Buchak-style risk-weighted expected utility is supposed to accommodate Allais-type preferences without leaving you open to any exploitable dynamics. Nobody budges, and honestly we keep having the same meeting over and over.

My working theory is that the two camps aren't even disagreeing about the same thing. The coherence people seem to read the axioms as consistency constraints on an agent's complete disposition set — something a god or an outside observer could check off in one go — while the permissive people read them as advice for a bounded deliberator who has to actually make choices one at a time, from the inside, without the whole preference structure laid out in front of them. And an argument's force can be completely different depending on which of those you take rationality to be about. But I can't tell whether that makes the dispute basically verbal, or whether there's a genuine hidden substantive question doing the work — maybe about what exploitation even has to do with rationality, i.e., why losing five bucks to a Dutch booker is supposed to be a worse fate than acting on risk-weighted EU all the way to a slightly suboptimal outcome nobody ever pumps you for.

So diagnose this thing for me. Is it a verbal dispute about the word 'rational,' two different questions (structural coherence of dispositions vs. guidance for deliberation) hiding under one heading, a real formal disagreement about the dynamic choice setup that's been laundered into intuitions about pumps, or bedrock commitments neither side can argue for? Ground the diagnosis in what each camp actually says, not a textbook survey of the theorems, and tell me honestly whether anything would count as progress: a new impossibility result, a dynamic model of sequential choice that both sides would accept, a distinction that cleanly splits the question, or an argument that we should just expect this one to stay stuck. And if you can, name the individual cruxes and say which ones partisans on each side would actually concede versus wave off.
