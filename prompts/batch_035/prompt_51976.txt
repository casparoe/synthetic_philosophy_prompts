my ml reading group keeps hitting the same move whenever specification gaming comes up: someone says Goodhart's law is basically the observer effect from physics — the act of measurement disturbs the thing being measured, so of course optimizing a proxy degrades it, its baked into measurement itself. Last week a guy went further and said you might as well call it an uncertainty principle for metrics: there's a hard floor on how well any score can track the thing it's scoring, and no clever metric design gets you under it. The conclusion this gets used for is basically fatalism — stop wasting time on holdout sets and adversarial audits and periodically rotating benchmarks, the corruption is fundemental, like fighting quantum mechanics. I want an actual audit of the analogy. Lay out what maps onto what — who or what is the measuring device in the ML case, and what is the disturbed system? is the RL agent responding to the reward the analogue of the electron being poked by the photon? Then the disanalogies, and crucially which of them matter for the fatalist conclusion and which dont. The ones I can half see: in QM the disturbance is symmetric and dumb, it doesn't depend on the system strategically anticipating what you'll do with the reading, whereas a big chunk of specification gaming is adversarial exactly because the system is optimizing against you; and there seem to be Goodhart cases with no disturbance story at all, like plain regression to the mean when you select on a noisy proxy — the proxy wasn't disturbed, it was never that good. Also I have a nagging feeling that "measurement disturbs the system" is itself a pop gloss of the actual uncertainty principle, which is something about joint distributions of non-commuting observables, not about clumsy instruments — does the analogy survive contact with the real thing, or is it the pop gloss doing all the work? And if the physics framing fails, say whether there's a better analogy lying around (teaching to the test? rewarding a behavior until the behavior becomes performance?) that can actually carry the weight people wanted the physics one to carry, or whether the fatalism was never supported by the analogy in the first place.
