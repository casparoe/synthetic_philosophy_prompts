I'm writing a term paper on whether states can meaningfully apologize for deeds no living official committed, and I've gotten stuck trying to extract a principle from my own reactions to historical cases. When Kevin Rudd delivered Australia's apology to the Stolen Generations in February 2008, plainly and without hedging, it struck me as exactly what was owed. When Tony Blair issued a statement in 1997 on the Irish Famine that expressed sorrow over the failure of those who governed in London at the time, but carefully avoided the language of apology, I found the gesture faintly evasive — as though half an apology was worse than none. When the U.S. Senate passed its 2009 apology for slavery and segregation with a disclaimer stating that nothing in the resolution supported any legal claim, I had the same reaction even more strongly: an apology that pre-emptively seals itself off from consequences seemed to be apologizing only on the condition that it cost nothing, which struck me as a defect in the apology itself, not just in politics around it. So far my verdicts suggest something like: the apology must come from the continuing body that committed the wrong, and it must be unconditional. Call this the "same legal person, no strings attached" view.

The trouble is that my other verdicts strain it. Willy Brandt kneeling at the Warsaw Ghetto memorial in December 1970 seemed to me one of the most fitting acts of political apology ever performed — yet he had spent the Nazi years in exile and was about as far from personally complicit as a German chancellor could be, and he said nothing at all, which sits oddly with any requirement that the apologizer be the responsible party. Conversely, when a government that has just overthrown a previous regime is pressed to apologize for that regime's atrocities, I find myself thinking it's the wrong addressee for the apology — they're the ones who stopped it, and whatever they express is testimony or commemoration, not apology — even though structurally they seem no less "innocent spokespeople for the state" than Brandt was. And then there's time: 150 years back, as with the Famine statement, feels comfortably within range, but if someone proposed that the United Kingdom formally apologize for the Norman Conquest, or that Italy apologize for Rome's destruction of Carthage, I'd find the demand absurd — though if the state persists as a corporate entity across the centuries, I can't see what in the principle makes the cutoff fall at 150 years rather than 950. A related wobble: apologizing for a 19th-century massacre whose victim community survives seems to me to make sense even with no living victims, while apologizing to a people who have been entirely extinguished seems somehow hollow — yet if the apology settles the state's own moral ledger, the audience's survival shouldn't matter.

Please tell me whether the "same legal person, no strings attached" view can be repaired to cover all of this, or whether it has to be abandoned for something else. I'd like the response to lead with your best single statement of the principle (or of the small set of conditions) and then apply it, clause by clause, to the cases above — noting explicitly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why you'd overturn those rather than the others. Please also tell me what it predicts about two cases I haven't decided on: a university apologizing for its founders' profits from slavery, and a successor state apologizing for the acts of a predecessor state it voluntarily absorbed. Please keep the whole answer under 600 words.
