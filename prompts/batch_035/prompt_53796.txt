I run a reading group on the philosophy of language and computation — about a dozen people, mostly graduate students — and we've spent the last few weeks on whether machine translation systems understand what they translate. We started from Hofstadter's 1990s attacks on "shallow" approaches to AI, moved through his later book built around translating a single short French poem into dozens of English variants, and paired those with Searle's Chinese Room argument and the contemporary defense of large statistical models. For our final session I want a "which position do you actually hold?" quiz: ten or so questions, each a concrete translation case with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions. The point is to sort members by what they say about cases, not by which label they like — so no question may ask directly whether a system "really understands," whether it has semantics or merely syntax, or use any other phrasing that hands over the label in advance.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names. The distinctions I care about — keep these if you find them load-bearing, refine or supplement as you see fit — are at least: whether understanding is all-or-nothing or comes in degrees; whether the question concerns current systems or the architecture in principle, i.e. whether some descendant of the same statistical approach could ever count as understanding; whether meaning requires causal grounding in perception and action, or whether sufficiently rich use within a linguistic community suffices; and whether producing ever-better translations across ever-harder cases is itself evidence of understanding, or only ever more behavior. Choose the cases so that positions genuinely come apart on them: a pun whose two readings cannot both survive into the target language; a sentence carrying a novel metaphor never seen in any plausible training corpus; a poem whose surface sense and its actual point diverge, so that a literal translation is a mistranslation; an untranslatable word the system must paraphrase or lose; a case where the output is fluent but subtly wrong about the world. Every question should be answerable by a member who has never heard the position names.

The leaves should include at least: the classical symbol-grounding skeptic in the Searle mold; the Hofstadterian who grants fluent output but denies the underlying depth of analogy-making; the graded functionalist who holds that understanding admits of degrees and that good translation constitutes some nonzero degree of it; and the meaning-as-use defender, on whom distributional methods confer a presumption of some understanding. Flag any pair of leaves the quiz cannot really separate — I suspect the Searlean and the Hofstadterian will often answer identically for different reasons, and I would rather that be admitted than papered over — and note any combination of answers your logical space permits that nobody plausibly holds, with one sentence each on whether that combination is vacant because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Keep the whole thing to a handout: the quiz on one page, the scoring key with commentary on a second.
