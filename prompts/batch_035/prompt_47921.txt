I am a retired isotope geochemist who spent the better part of my career dating marine sediments, and in retirement I have taken up philosophy of science. I would like your help adjudicating a disagreement I recently witnessed and could not referee myself.

At a dinner held by my old department, two former colleagues argued about when the Anthropocene began and, more importantly, about who is entitled to decide. For clarity, call them the stratigrapher and the historian: the stratigrapher served for several years on the relevant working group, and the historian lectures in environmental history.

(1) The stratigrapher opened by insisting that the only defensible way to fix an epoch boundary is a primary marker in the stratal record: something globally synchronous, unambiguous, and preservable. On his view, the mid-century radionuclide signal from thermonuclear tests is the best candidate anyone has found — the Anthropocene Working Group proposed the annually layered sediments of Crawford Lake in Ontario, with a base around 1950, precisely because the plutonium from the bomb tests appears there as a single sharp line. Dates such as 1610 or the start of the Industrial Revolution, he said, fail the synchrony test: industrialisation reached different continents at different times, and its earlier signals are patchy.

(2) The historian replied that 1610 passes the test by the stratigraphers' own standards. Simon Lewis and Mark Maslin, in a 2015 paper in Nature, identified 1610 as one of only two dates that meet the formal requirements: the dip in atmospheric CO2 that year is recorded synchronously in Antarctic ice cores, and it is tied to a specific event — the collapse of indigenous American populations after European contact, and the reforestation of their abandoned farmland. So the preference for 1950 over 1610, she said, cannot be stratigraphy; it is a value judgement about which origin story is comfortable, dressed up as boundary science. She then pressed a second point: the decision about when the epoch of human history begins had been left to a committee of geologists, and in 2024 that committee, and the International Union of Geological Sciences above it, voted the whole epoch down — thereby settling, by vote, a question about human history in which historians had no formal say.

(3) The stratigrapher answered that the 1610 dip is a trough, not an onset: it marks a temporary reversal of human influence, whereas a boundary should mark the beginning of the sustained new regime that defines the epoch, and on that criterion the mid-century date wins. He added that the 2024 rejection proceeded on just these sorts of grounds — the proposed record was too shallow and the start date too recent — so the vote was not a judgement that humans have failed to reshape the planet, only that the proposal did not fit the rules for an epoch.

(4) The historian's last word was that this criterion is retrospective through and through. The mid-century date looks like an onset only because the Great Acceleration happened afterwards; had the post-war economy stagnated, no stratigrapher would have chosen 1950. The choice therefore presupposes the very narrative it claims to discover, and once onsets-of-trends are admitted as boundary evidence, stratigraphy has quietly become historiography — at which point the question of who decides returns in full force. She noted that the union's own statement announcing the rejection conceded that the term would remain an invaluable descriptor of human impact on the Earth system; ruling that the word is fit for everything except the timescale, she said, looks less like science than boundary-policing.

I could not referee this, and it rather matters to me, because I half suspect the real question is neither 1610 nor 1950 but what a boundary is for. Please act as a professional philosopher of science with a working knowledge of both stratigraphy and historiography. Begin by brainstorming, without commitment, the considerations each party is implicitly relying on at each move; then adjudicate the four moves in order, saying who is right about which specific point and rating each move somewhere between clearly mistaken and clearly right; and close by identifying what, if anything, both of them are missing. Please think carefully and do not flatten the exchange into a winner-takes-all verdict — I want to know where each of them is right, where each overreaches, and where the disagreement is really a disagreement about something else entirely.
