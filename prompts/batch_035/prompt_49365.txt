The exam question was:

"Ruth Chang has argued that when two options are what she calls on a par, they are comparable even though neither is better, neither is worse, and they are not equal in value; she claims this is the situation in what she calls hard choices. Explain what the parity relation is supposed to be, how Chang distinguishes it from incomparability, and what she thinks this shows about the relationship between value pluralism (the idea that some values cannot be measured on any single common scale) and whether options can be compared at all."

The student wrote:

"In a hard choice, like Chang's example of choosing between a career in law and a career in music, neither option is better and they are not exactly equal either. Chang says the options are on a par. This is a fourth relation: better, worse, equal, or on a par. So the options remain comparable — being on a par is a way of being compared, not a failure of comparison.

The reason hard choices arise in the first place is value pluralism. Following Berlin, Chang holds that values like liberty and equality can't be put on a single scale, so when they conflict the options cant be measured against each other. Raz makes basically the same point in his work on incommensurability: when neither option is better, worse, nor equal, the options are incomparable. Chang's parity is really just Raz's incommensurability under a new name — if nothing is better, worse, or equal, then there is nothing left to compare, so the comparison fails.

This means hard choices are hard because we don't know which option is better — the relevant information just isn't available to us. And since there is no fact of the matter about which is better, whatever we pick is beyond rational criticism. Chang concedes that at the end of the day the choice comes down to taste, like preferring chocolate to strawberry, and is in that sense arbitrary. Her only real addition is that we should feel comfortable making it anyway."
