For the final paper in my Western Civilization class, we each have to defend a thesis about the fall of the Western Roman Empire, and before I commit to mine I want to make sure my positions actually fit together. Here is roughly where I stand after a semester of reading:

1. The West genuinely fell. Between roughly 400 and 600, tax revenue, long-distance trade, urban population, and literacy all collapsed in the former Western provinces, and this was a civilizational catastrophe rather than a rebranding.
2. The fundamental cause was internal, in the tradition of Edward Gibbon: the Roman state rotted from within — civic spirit eroded, the citizen-soldier ideal disappeared, and defense came to depend on barbarian recruits with no particular loyalty to Rome.
3. Environmental monocausal explanations are overrated. The lead poisoning theory in particular seems weak to me, since the empire was near its height while lead pipes were already everywhere.
4. The decisive proximate cause was the loss of the Western tax base to the barbarian kingdoms, above all the Vandal conquest of Africa — once that revenue was gone, the Western army simply could not be paid.
5. The year 476 is overrated. When Odoacer deposed Romulus Augustulus, he sent the imperial regalia east and ruled through the old Roman offices; the Senate kept meeting and consuls kept being named. The fall was a long process, not an event.
6. The survival of the Eastern Empire for a thousand years after 476 shows that the deeper problem was not Roman institutions themselves, which were perfectly capable of keeping a state going into the fifteenth century.
7. Justinian's reconquest of Africa and Italy in the 530s shows that imperial capacity remained formidable well after the supposed decay set in — the West was not unrecoverable in principle.

My question is this: which of these seven claims are in genuine tension with each other, which only appear to be, and what is the cheapest way to resolve the worst conflict? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious, and be concrete about which claim would have to give. I would also like to know whether there is a thesis about the fall of Rome that could survive whichever revisions turn out to be necessary, since that is what I am supposed to submit.
