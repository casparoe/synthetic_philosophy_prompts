A request about two distinctions that tend to get run together in the trolley literature: the distinction at the heart of the doctrine of double effect — between harm that is intended, whether as an end or as a means, and harm that is merely foreseen — and the distinction at the heart of the doctrine of doing and allowing — between harm an agent brings about and harm the agent merely allows to happen. It has been suggested that across the trolley family of cases the two doctrines are extensionally equivalent, delivering the same verdicts case by case, so that the choice between them is, within this family at least, partly a verbal matter. I would like you to assess that suggestion.

Work from the following three cases, spelt out so that we share the same material:

1. Bystander. A runaway trolley will kill five people on the main track. You can throw a switch diverting it onto a side track, where it will kill one person; had the switch not been thrown, the trolley would have proceeded to the five, and the one person would have been unharmed.
2. Footbridge. A runaway trolley will kill five. The only way to stop it is to push a heavy person off a footbridge into its path; his body will stop the trolley, and he will die.
3. Loop. As in Bystander, you can divert the trolley onto a side track on which it will kill one person — except that here the side track curves back onto the main track just before the five, so that if the one person's body were somehow not to stop the trolley, it would continue on and kill them. His death is thus causally necessary to saving the five in a way it need not be in Bystander.

Please structure your response with the following sections, in this order:

a. The two doctrines stated (roughly 150 words). Formulate each doctrine precisely enough that a defender of it would accept your formulation, and note the main interpretive question each faces: for the doctrine of double effect, how to draw the intended/foreseen line; for the doctrine of doing and allowing, what counts as doing a harm rather than allowing it.

b. Verdicts on the three cases (roughly 300 words). Present a table with one row per case, giving the verdict each doctrine appears to deliver, the reasoning behind it, and a final column recording agreement or disagreement between the doctrines. Flag explicitly the contested point in Bystander: whether flipping the switch should be classified as doing the one person's death or as merely redirecting a threat with a foreseen death.

c. Extensional equivalence (roughly 250 words). Assess the suggestion above: within these cases and near variants of them, do the doctrines always agree, or is there some construction on which they come apart — for instance, a harm that is allowed but functions as a means, or a harm that is done yet plainly not intended?

d. The residual difference (roughly 200 words). Suppose the doctrines are extensionally equivalent across the whole trolley family: state what substantive difference between them nonetheless remains, and identify the kinds of cases outside that family — omissions, two-agent cases, or threats that would exist regardless of anyone's action — that would reveal it.

e. Overall judgment (roughly 100 words). Say which doctrine you take to be doing more of the explanatory work in our trolley intuitions, and whether the equivalence suggestion survives.
