quick context: I'm a second-year undergrad taking a political philosophy module this term, and we just finished the block on Berlin's two concepts of liberty and then republicanism (Pettit and Skinner came up). I wrote up an argument for my tutorial and my tutor circled the last line and wrote "so which premise dies?" and honestly I can't tell. Please act as a political philosophy professor who specialises in republican theory and thinks Berlin's negative/positive distinction is basically right but overdrawn. Take a moment to brainstorm the possible escape routes before committing to one, and then give me a proper diagnosis.

Here's the argument:

(1) Republicans define freedom as non-domination, not non-interference: you are free so long as no one has an arbitrary power to interfere in your choices, whether or not they ever use it.
(2) Their showcase case is the slave with a kindly master who never actually interferes — the slave is unfree anyway, because she lives at the master's mercy.
(3) What makes the master's power domination is that the slave depends on his goodwill: her choices are only safe because he happens not to exercise a power he fully has.
(4) But dependence on another's goodwill is everywhere in modern life: workers depend on employers not to fire them at will, tenants on landlords, citizens on the discretion of officials, and in a complex economy basically everyone depends on the goodwill of countless people they will never meet.
(5) So on the republican's own account, nearly everyone in any modern society — including the well-ordered welfare states republicans like Pettit praise — is significantly unfree.
(6) Republicans present non-domination as an achievable political ideal for modern states, and this is supposed to be an advantage over Berlin's negative liberty.
(7) Therefore either republicans must bite the bullet and say life in a modern democracy is disturbingly like the kindly master's household, which nobody believes, or they quietly load the word arbitrary with conditions — the power must be contestable, reviewable, exercised on terms the person could veto or challenge — and once they do that, whether you're free depends on whether your institutions match some substantive standard of legitimacy. At that point the sharp contrast with positive liberty collapses, because freedom now measures how well society embodies a contested ideal, which is exactly what Berlin warned against.

I know (7) is doing two different things at once (the absurdity horn and the collapse-into-positive-liberty horn) and I'm not sure they're separable. For what it's worth, I'd much rather keep (2) — the kindly master intuition is the reason I found this view attractive in the first place — but I have a bad feeling about (5), I just can't articulate why without sounding like I'm defining the problem away.

So: which premise or inference should I give up, why did it look fine to me, and what does rejecting it cost the republican? If you think the argument is actually sound in some version, say that instead and tell me which horn I should embrace. Please write in British English, and don't hold back on the technical detail — we've covered interference conditions, the eyeball test, and the worker/employer debate in lectures already.
