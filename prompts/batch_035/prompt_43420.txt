I have taught the socialist calculation debate twice now in a history of economic thought seminar, and both times I came away with the uncomfortable feeling that the class had watched a very long chess game in which neither player ever captures anything. Mises opens in 1920 with the claim that without market prices for producer goods there is no rational way to compare alternative production plans. Lange answers in the 1930s with the trial-and-error model: a central planning board sets prices, adjusts them up where shortages appear and down where surpluses do, and instructs managers to minimize average cost and clear their markets — so the formal properties Lange cared about survive the abolition of private capital markets. Hayek's rejoinder is that this concedes the wrong game: the knowledge needed for planning is dispersed, tacit, local, and constantly changing, so it is never given to any board to feed into an adjustment procedure, while the price system communicates the salient parts of it without anyone having to articulate it. And there the exchange sits. Textbooks in the middle of the century scored it for Lange on the formal question; after 1989 the same exchange got re-scored for Hayek on the practical one; and now every few years the debate restarts over whether modern computing changes the verdict, with one side pointing to work showing that solving the planning equations is computationally tractable and the other pointing to firms like Walmart or Amazon running internally planned economies of continental scale as evidence for, or against, exactly one of the two sides — I have seen both readings of that example in print.

My working hypothesis is that this never resolves because there is not one debate here but two running under a single name: a static allocation question, whether an economy with given resources, technology, and preferences can be steered to an optimum without private ownership of capital, and a dynamic discovery question, whether the knowledge and the innovations such steering presupposes can exist in the absence of the market process the socialists wanted to abolish. Lange and Mises were largely fighting about the first, Hayek about the second, and each side's standard of victory is the other side's irrelevant metric. But an alternative diagnosis also seems live: the disagreement is a hidden empirical question — roughly, how much economically decisive knowledge can be articulated as data — and that question's answer drifts with information technology, which would explain the peculiar pattern of the debate restarting, apparently fresh, with each generation of machines while repeating the same arguments. There is also the awkward fact that Schumpeter raised a third question neither camp addressed: if innovation is what matters, and large organizations seem perfectly capable of it, perhaps both sides were arguing about the wrong thing entirely.

I want a diagnosis, not a retelling. Using what each side actually claims, tell me whether the stall is verbal, a clash of standards of evidence, sides answering different questions under one heading, or a genuine empirical question that happens to be very hard — and if it is several of these, say which part carries which. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: some distinction that splits the debate into decidable parts, some measurement, some formal result, or an argument that the impasse is structural and no progress should be expected. I would particularly like your view on whether the recurring computer-and-big-data revival of the debate is genuine new evidence, or the same positions being re-derived and mistaken for news.
