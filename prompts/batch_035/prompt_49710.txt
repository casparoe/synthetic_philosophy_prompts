quick context: I'm at a small AI lab and our team just grew enough that I actually have to make the calls now instead of deferring up. The decision on my desk is how to train our models on reasoning tasks (math, code, that kind of thing), and my position is that we should supervise the process, not the outcome — grade the individual reasoning steps for being sound, rather than just checking whether the final answer is right and rewarding that. My reasoning: outcome-only feedback rewards a correct answer that was arrived at through garbage reasoning, or even for bad reasons we can't see, and step-level grading gives us something legible we can audit — we know what we actually paid the model to do.

My boss is on board but I want to know what would count *against* this before we sink six months into it. Specifically: what evidence, including things that have arguably already shown up in other labs' models, would show that process supervision is worse than outcome-only? I can think of one worry myself — that graders end up rewarding steps that *look* good rather than steps that actually caused the answer — but I assume there are more, and some might be ones a committed step-grader like me would be tempted to explain away. So I also want an honest check: has the view probably been qualified into unfalsifiability ("grade the steps but sanity-check the answer", "grade steps in domains where steps are checkable"), and if so is that a real problem for a view like this or just how engineering tradeoffs work?

I'd like the defeaters ranked from would-actually-make-me-switch to merely annoying, and please flag which ones defenders of process supervision would concede vs. brush off.
