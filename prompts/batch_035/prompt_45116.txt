So here's my situation. For the last two semesters I've been in a seminar on the badness of death, and the whole thing has frozen in place in a way I find kind of fascinating and kind of maddening. On one side you've got the Epicureans: death can't be bad for you, because a harm needs a subject, and once you're dead there's no subject left — the old idea that when we exist death isn't present, and when death is present we don't exist. They also lean on the symmetry point that nobody thinks the vast stretch of nonexistence before their birth was bad for them, so why is the stretch after any worse. On the other side the deprivation theorists say death is bad for you when it cuts off a life worth living, by robbing you of goods you would otherwise have gotten — no experience of the harm required, since plenty of harms are non-experiential anyway. And then the reply-counter-reply machinery kicks in: the deprivation people try to solve the timing problem (when exactly is death bad for you — during life, at the moment of death, at no time at all?), the Epicureans press back with the question of who exactly is supposed to be enjoying those future goods, and around it goes. Everyone in the room knows every move. Nobody has budged since, honestly, Nagel's paper in the 70s, and I'm starting to suspect nobody will.

I have a working theory I want you to take seriously but not just rubber-stamp: that this isn't really a disagreement about death at all, but about whether value needs a valuer — the deprivation side has an axiological commitment to goods that can accrue to a person without anyone experiencing them, and the Epicurean side reads that as a category error, and neither commitment can be argued for from the other's premises, because they're the premises. But I can also see a competing diagnosis: that the two camps are answering different questions under one label — one about what makes a life go better or worse, the other about what a person has reason to dread or regret — and the fight only looks substantive because both use the phrase "bad for you." Or maybe it's even shallower than that and it's bedrock intuitions about the Lucretius symmetry case all the way down.

First, brainstorm a bit — lay out what each side actually says in their own best versions, since my summary above is no doubt leaving things out. Then diagnose the structure of the disagreement: verbal or substantive, factual or about standards, different questions or shared question with incommensurable starting points? And finally, tell me honestly what progress would even look like from here — a distinction, a thought experiment that splits the intuitions, some result from neighboring debates about personal identity or posthumous harm — or whether the right conclusion is that this question is ill-posed and the interesting work is elsewhere. If your diagnosis says no progress should be expected, say that plainly and tell me why, dont hedge. I've read enough surveys; I want the diagnosis, with reasons.
