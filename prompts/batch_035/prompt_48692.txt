I am a retired experimental physicist — thirty-five years in low-temperature condensed-matter work, mostly on superconducting devices — and since retiring I have been reading seriously in philosophy, particularly formal epistemology. I want to enlist you as an expert assistant.

**Your role:** Act as a professional philosopher specializing in formal epistemology and the theory of rationality, comfortable with both the Bayesian tradition and the literature on bounded rationality, computability, and idealization in science.

**Background for the task:** The problem of logical omniscience fascinates me, partly because it rhymes with something I know well from physics: idealizations. Textbook Bayesians model a rational agent whose beliefs are closed under logical consequence — it assigns probability 1 to every tautology and knows every theorem derivable from what it knows. Nobody could be like this; even checking whether a given string is a theorem can be undecidable or take longer than the age of the universe. The standard defense, as I understand it, is that this is an idealization, like a frictionless plane or an ideal gas: no plane is frictionless, but the model earns its keep. Here is what strikes me as a physicist, and what I want the dialogue to take seriously: in physics, idealizations come with a service contract. We know the dimensionless quantity that measures how badly the idealization misleads us — the ratio of mean free path to pipe diameter, of viscosity to inertia — and we know in which limit the error vanishes and how fast. And crucially, we know idealizations that do *not* have this property and are treated as pathology, not as idealization. My suspicion is that logical omniscience may be more like the second kind. Friction can be reduced arbitrarily far by better lubrication; but the cost of deducing consequences plausibly *grows* with the length of the proof, so the gap between the ideal agent and any real one may not shrink as the real one gets better — it may grow. An idealization whose error term you cannot control seems to me to be in a different category from one whose error term you can. I do not know whether the philosophers have already had this fight, and I do not need the dialogue to adjudicate it for the field; I need the two positions stated as strongly and honestly as they can be.

**The task:** Write a dialogue between two philosophers — call them what you like, but give them names — who disagree about whether logical omniscience in the Bayesian model is a legitimate idealization.

- One character defends the ideal-agent framework: idealizations are allowed to be false, the ideal agent is a normative standard the way a perfect calorimeter is a measurement standard, and complaints about computational cost confuse the evaluation of a credence with the process of getting there.
- The other character presses the objection I sketched above, in whatever form survives scrutiny: that a regulative ideal must be *approachable*, that idealizations need a controlled limit, and that logical omniscience may fail this in a way frictionless planes do not.

Requirements for the dialogue:

1. Both characters should be equally competent. Neither should be a foil who caves in or runs out of replies; each should score genuine points.
2. There must be real engagement: each character should respond to the other's strongest formulation, not to a weaker neighbor of it. If one character makes an objection, the other should either answer it or explicitly accept the cost.
3. The characters should work through at least two concrete cases, e.g. an agent who believes the axioms of arithmetic but has not computed whether some very large number is prime, and an agent who holds a theorem-prover's output in higher or lower credence than the theorem itself. They should also address my physics analogy directly — including the possibility that the analogy is bad, and the possibility that the defender's best reply is to distinguish *approachability in principle* from *approachability by us*.
4. The dialogue should end not with a handshake, but with the disagreement located precisely: by the end, the reader should be able to say in one sentence exactly what factual or evaluative claim the two characters actually differ on, that they did not know they differed on at the start.

**Process:** Before writing the dialogue, briefly brainstorm the strongest versions of both positions, and the two or three strongest specific moves available to each side, so the dialogue is built from the best material rather than the most familiar. Then write the dialogue. It should be long enough to do justice to the exchange — roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words. After the dialogue, append a short paragraph in your own voice, as the professional philosopher, assessing whether my physicist's worry about uncontrolled error terms is a known and serious objection in the field, a rediscovery of something standard, or a confusion — and, if a confusion, of what.
