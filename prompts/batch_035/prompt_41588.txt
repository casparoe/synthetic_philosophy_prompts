I run a small philosophy blog aimed at motivated non-specialists, and I want to build an interactive decision tree titled "Which cosmological argument, if any, do you actually accept?" — emphasis on "actually," since my readers tend to say "the cosmological argument" as though there were only one, and then object to a premise that belongs to a version they don't hold. The tree should start from a single shared point — that contingent things exist, or that the universe is here rather than not — and then branch on questions a reader can answer without knowing any of the technical labels: e.g., does everything need an explanation, or only things that begin to exist; is "the universe began to exist" a claim about evidence (cosmology, thermodynamics) or about the metaphysics of time; whether an infinite regress of explanations would satisfy you if it never terminated in anything self-explanatory; whether a necessary being could be abstract (numbers, structures, moral truths) or must be concrete; whether the conclusion "a necessary concrete being exists" gets you anywhere near "God" without adding premises that the argument itself doesn't supply.

Please construct the tree from the logical space first and only then locate the named positions in it — kalam (with its dependence on a tensed view of time and its horror of actual infinities), the Leibnizian contingency argument built on the principle of sufficient reason, the Thomistic argument that trades on the essence/existence distinction, plus the skeptical exits available at each fork: rejecting the causal principle, accepting brute contingency, treating modal facts as needing no explanation, or arguing the universe itself is the necessary existent. Each internal node should be phrased as a concrete question or thought experiment rather than a request to endorse an ism — for instance, asking what a reader would say about a stubborn four-dimensional block universe with no beginning and no explanation, which should split the kalam supporter from the Leibnizian without either label appearing. Include roughly six to eight branch questions and no more than eight leaves, and where two positions share all their answers on your tree — as arguably happens with versions distinguished only by how they argue for the causal principle — flag them as genuinely inseparable by this instrument rather than manufacturing a fake distinction. Also mark any branch-combination that is coherent but, to your knowledge, undefended, with a sentence on whether that's because the view is unstable, unattractive, or just unfashionable.

A few formatting notes: write for an audience that has read a little philosophy but never taken a modal logic course, so define "contingent" and "necessary" in plain terms on first use; American English throughout; and keep the whole thing to roughly 800–1,000 words including a short intro paragraph my readers will see above the tree. If you want, you can add a closing sidebar of two or three sentences noting the single question you found most diagnostic in constructing the tree — the one where readers who thought they agreed diverge most sharply — since my experience is that the fights in my comments section are almost always one fork being answered two ways at once.
