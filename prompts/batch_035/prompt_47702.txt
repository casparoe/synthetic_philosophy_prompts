You are a philosopher of science specializing in scientific realism, confirmation theory, and the debate over the underdetermination of theory by evidence. I have been recording my gut verdicts on cases of empirically equivalent but allegedly incompatible theories, and I would like you to help me work out what principle, if any, captures them. Please think carefully, begin with some brainstorming of candidate principles before committing to one, and write your answer in British English.

My verdicts, in no particular order:

1. The Reichenbach-style case: a theory on which space is genuinely non-Euclidean, versus one on which space is Euclidean but an unobservable universal force distorts every measuring rod and light ray in exactly the way that mimics curvature. The two are empirically equivalent by construction, but to me they are not genuine rivals — they are one theory in two sets of clothes, and the underdetermination here is a triviality not worth losing sleep over.

2. Many-worlds versus a collapse interpretation of quantum mechanics: also empirically equivalent (grant that collapse is undetectable even in principle), yet I have no hesitation treating these as genuine rivals, and I would happily say that one is true and the other false.

3. Lorentz's theory with an undetectable ether versus special relativity: here I keep going back and forth. Some days I feel these are genuine rivals and that nature simply picked one; other days it seems the ether theory is just special relativity plus a wheel connected to nothing.

4. Suppose a theory of chemical bonding posits an unobservable inner degree of freedom that makes no difference to any possible observation, alongside an otherwise identical theory without it. My verdict: the padded version is not a rival but an inferior edition of the same theory — believing it isn't believing something different, just believing something worse.

5. Two theories that agree on everything observable, where the second achieves the agreement only by a fiat clause declaring that nothing beyond a certain boundary is observable. My verdict: cheap trick, not a genuine rival. But I can't justify this, given that I accepted (2), where a postulate about when collapse occurs looks uncomfortably like a fiat clause about observation.

Is there a single principle that separates genuine-but-underdetermined rivals from notational variants, delivers verdicts on (1), (2), (4), and (5), and settles (3)? Or do some of my verdicts have to go? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which verdict motivates each clause, say what it predicts for a case I haven't listed — say, a phenomenological thermodynamics recapitulated in exactly the opposite terms by a statistical-mechanical underpinning — and be honest about which of my verdicts your preferred principle cannot accommodate.
