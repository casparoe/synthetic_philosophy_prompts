I'm preparing a session for a comparative ethics seminar on what gets called the contrast between German and American folk morality: roughly, the thought that in German moral culture rules and order have a claim on you in themselves — you wait at an empty crosswalk at 3 a.m. because the light is red, and you discharge the duties of your office even when your conscience itches — whereas American folk morality treats individual conscience as the court of final appeal, before which any rule can be hauled and cross-examined. The rule-following side has a famous philosophical self-portrait in Kant's 1784 essay "An Answer to the Question: What is Enlightenment?", which lists the guardians' cry — "The officer says: Do not argue but drill! The tax official: Do not argue but pay! The clergyman: Do not argue but believe!" — and the ruler's great concession: "Argue as much as you will and about whatever you will, but obey!" The essay also diagnoses the comfort of outsourcing one's conscience: having "a spiritual advisor who has a conscience for me," among the perks of remaining a minor. But note that Kant builds in a safety valve — the *public* use of reason as a scholar, which even the drilling officer retains off duty.

For the session's case study I want to put Ishiguro's *The Remains of the Day* on the table and read Stevens through this contrast. Please give me a reading that does several things. First, the mapping: what in the novel corresponds to Kant's officer, his office, the ruler, the drill, and especially the safety valve — is Stevens's creed of dignity and "great man" service the German folk morality in its purest fictional form, with Lord Darlington as the ruler whose concession Stevens never once invokes? Miss Kenton as the voice of individual conscience — and is she really, or does she simply serve a different master? Second, the points where the reading strains and an honest critic must say so: the novel is about English class deference, not German rule-following, so what exactly licenses the mapping; the appeasement plot arguably stacks the deck, since a defender of the order-first folk morality can reply that the fault lies with a corrupt master rather than the ethos — the office was betrayed from above, not discredited; and Kant himself would insist that Stevens's abdication is precisely *not* what the essay endorses, since the safety valve existed and Stevens refused to use it. Third, I want you to weigh whether the novel, taken as a whole, adjudicates which folk morality has the better of it — consider the dismissal of the Jewish maids, Stevens serving through the 1936 conference on the night his father dies, and the final meeting and bus stop in the rain — or whether it shows something the framing of the debate itself misses, such as the fact that the ethos Stevens follows was never really rule-following at all but devotion to a person, which neither the Kantian officer nor the American conscience-first moralist has a category for. Be faithful in both directions: don't reduce Stevens to an illustration of either folk morality, and don't distort the contrast to fit him. End by saying what the novel adds to this debate that the abstract arguments cannot.
