I host a recorded interview series for our graduate program, and our next guest is a philosopher of mathematics who defends a contemporary descendant of the Lucas–Penrose argument from Gödelian limits. In his telling, Hofstadter's MU-puzzle is the emblem of human mathematical insight: after pushing typographical rules around for a while, the human simply steps outside the game, notices that the number of I's in any derivable string is never a multiple of three, and settles the question in a way no maneuver inside the system ever could. He has lately begun calling this capacity JOOTSing with intent: not merely jumping out of one formal system into some wider one, but recognizing in oneself a standing ability to take such a jump whenever a system's blind spots become visible. He argues that no Turing machine can be given that standing ability in advance, because which jumps will matter is not effectively enumerable. Draft eight to ten interview questions that press on the actual mechanics of his position rather than inviting him to restate it. I want at least one question that confronts him with the standard objection that the MU invariant is itself a theorem of elementary number theory, so that the jump lands him in a formalizable metasystem rather than outside formalization altogether — with a follow-up keyed to whichever escape he is most likely to attempt. I also want at least one question about whether there is any principled asymmetry between a human who happens to spot an invariant and a machine that reliably enumerates and tests candidate invariants until one works, and at least one question about whether his view yields any prediction that could turn out to be false — for instance, about what proof assistants will or will not achieve. Please avoid questions about biography or intellectual influences.
