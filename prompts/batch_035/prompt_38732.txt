So here's my situation: I write novels for a living, which mostly means arguing for years at a time with people who don't exist, and on Saturday I'm giving the toast at the retirement dinner of my oldest friend Maya, who just finished forty years in computational linguistics. Maya's career, in her own telling, was one long argument that whatever a machine does with language, it isn't understanding — she was a skeptic about machine comprehension back when the machines could barely finish a sentence, and she stayed a skeptic after they got terrifyingly good at it, which won her a lot of arguments early and cost her a lot of arguments late. The complication is the room: half of it is her old lab, several of whom now actually build large language models for a living and privately think the models do understand something, and the other half is her family plus a couple of us novelists who couldn't define a transformer if you held us underwater. My own position, for what it's worth, is a mess: I use one of these models every day as a sparring partner for drafts, and some mornings it feels like talking to a mind and some mornings like autocomplete with a heating bill, and I genuinely can't tell which of my intuitions about it are worth anything. I even asked the thing to draft this toast, out of curiosity — it produced something eerily warm about friendship, and the fact that I couldn't tell within thirty seconds whether that was understanding or a very good trick is, I've realized, both her point and her opponents' point, which is exactly why I can't write this speech myself.

Constraints, because she has opinions about those too: five minutes, give or take; it has to land as affection and not as a colloquium; it cannot concede the debate in either direction (if I come out firmly on the machines-understand side, half the room feels lectured and Maya feels betrayed; if I sneer at the models, her former students who build them feel erased, and she hates a cheap crowd-pleaser more than she hates being disagreed with); and — this one is non-negotiable — I am banned from using the phrase stochastic parrots anywhere in it, because she says she has heard it approximately four hundred thousand more times than she ever wanted to and wants one evening without it. What I'd love is one good joke about parrots, or prompting, or asking a machine to write a toast, that the NLP half will actually find funny while the family half still gets it, and ideally the whole thing should quietly use my own confusion as the vehicle — the novelist who can't tell whether he's been talking to a mind, honoring the scientist who always could.

Please write the toast, and then after it give me a short plain note (for my eyes, not the room's) telling me where the speech takes liberties with the actual debate — i.e. which side's contested notion of understanding it quietly leans on whenever it gets warm, since some lean is probably unavoidable in anything affectionate — plus how confident you are it clears the bar I've described, and the single line you're least sure lands.
