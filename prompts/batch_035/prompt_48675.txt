I'm preparing a session for a philosophy of mind seminar on the question of whether dreams are experiences, built around the classic exchange between Norman Malcolm's Dreaming (1959), which denies that dreams are experiences occurring during sleep, and Daniel Dennett's "Are Dreams Experiences?" (1976), which answers Malcolm with his composition-and-insertion model, on which dream content is composed at wakeup and retroactively inserted into memory. As our test case I want to use Christopher Nolan's Inception (2010), and I'd like a serious critical reading of the film through that debate—one that does justice to both the film's actual mechanics and the philosophy.

Here is what I want the reading to take on. Inception seems to simply assume the pre-Malcolmian, Cartesian picture: dreams are experiences had in real time while asleep, which is what makes the whole dream-heist premise possible, including the time dilation across nested dream levels (a few minutes at one level buying hours at the next) and the synchronized "kicks" that jolt dreamers awake at coordinated moments across levels. Read as a thought experiment, though, the film may be doing something sharper: Malcolm's central worry was that dream experiences are private and unverifiable, since the only evidence is the sleeper's own retrospective report. Nolan's shared-dreaming technology—multiple dreamers plugged into the same machine, dreaming a common dream that each can later report and cross-check—looks like a deliberate repair of exactly that epistemic gap. So one question the reading must answer: does Inception construct the very machinery that answers Malcolm, or does it merely stipulate the received view away without earning it? I'm also interested in whether the film knows what it is doing—whether the totems (like Cobb's spinning top, a private check designed to be unreadable by anyone else) push in the opposite direction, reinstating a residuum of verificationist anxiety inside the very technology that was supposed to dispel it, and whether the famous cut to black at the end is a concession to Malcolm that the film cannot, even in principle, settle its own question.

I also want the reading to be honest about where the film's mechanics break against Dennett's model. On Dennett's view, there is no fact of the matter about what was experienced while asleep—content is composed after the fact—so the intricate cross-level kick choreography, the counting-down of dream-time, and the characters' conversations mid-dream are all, philosophically speaking, fantasies of a transparency that Dennett denies and Malcolm would say was never there. Where exactly does the film trade on confusions that either philosopher would pounce on, and are there scenes where it is genuinely wiser than both?

Please write the reading as a polished essay of roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words, in American English. Do not pad it with plot summary beyond what the argument needs, and do not reduce the film to an illustration of the debate—I want to know where the film resists the reading as much as where it rewards it. End by saying what Inception adds to the Malcolm–Dennett exchange that the philosophical arguments, taken alone, do not give us. You may refer to scenes and characters by name, but do not attempt to quote the film verbatim.
