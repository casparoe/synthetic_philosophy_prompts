I'm on the exhibits committee at our state historical society (volunteer position, I'm a retired accountant who likes history) and we're planning the big statehood bicentennial show for next year. Our head curator is pushing a "one ordinary day" concept: instead of the usual governors-and-treaties narrative, the main gallery would reconstruct a single day in 1905 through a railroad worker's pay stub, a midwife's account book, laundry ledgers, a tenant farmer's diary, that kind of thing — her claim is that this tells visitors more about how the state actually was than another hall of inauguration portraits and battle flags ever could. Our youngest curator backs her and keeps invoking that famous microhistory of the sixteenth-century Italian miller as proof that one obscure nobody can reveal a whole world. Meanwhile a longtime trustee — donor, and descendant of one of the founding families — wants the marquee space for a retrospective on his ancestor built around the family papers, and he's not-so-subtly reminding everyone that the new wing's fundraising depends on his goodwill. The committee is deadlocked and I've been asked to draft the written recommendation we circulate before the November meeting, and honestly I'm out of my depth.

I want you to act as a philosopher of history who's also spent time in museums, and please think carefully before writing — maybe brainstorm a couple of framings first, then give me the memo itself. The hard part is that both sides have real arguments, not just donor politics. The ordinary-day people are basically running the history-from-below playbook: ordinary lives are the actual substance of the past, elite papers survive at wildly disproportionate rates, so "significance" as traditionally measured partly just reflects who had the literacy and leisure to leave records. But the trustee's side has a comeback I can't dismiss: the day-in-1905 exhibit would be partly reconstruction and interpretation — we'd be guessing at typicality from scraps, and our single reconstructed "ordinary" person might be as unrepresentative as any great man, just cherry-picked for being relatable (the midwife's account book survived because her grandson kept it, which is already not nothing). Plus a bicentennial is arguably a civic event about the state as a polity — founding decisions, who got included and excluded from citizenship — not only about how people lived day to day.

So the deliverable is a decision memo the committee could act on: a concrete recommendation (whether to take the trustee's money at all, and what the actual gallery split should be, in rough percentages or square footage terms), the reasoning stated in terms the curators would recognize, and an honest treatment of the strongest point on whichever side loses — not a token paragraph. Please don't just split-the-difference as a cop-out; only recommend a split if you can give a principled reason why a split is right rather than just safe. If it helps, I don't care whether you name-drop the big-name historians and historians' debates or not, but I do want the committee members who've never read any of that stuff to be able to follow it. The memo should be short enough that people will actually read it before the meeting.
