Last month my hiking partner and I were dropped at an unmarked junction in unfamiliar terrain by a shuttle driver who was himself lost, and we spent the next hour arguing about a single sentence we'd heard. A ranger had driven past, radioed our description ahead, and told us: if we were on the Foothills side of the park or the Lakeside side, the odds were three to one we were on the Foothills side. Then he was gone. He never said how likely we were to be in the park at all rather than on some old logging network outside it, and he never mentioned the third drainage our map did show. Before he spoke, we had each put it at roughly a coin flip between Foothills and Lakeside, with a small residual chance of the drainage or the land outside the park.

My partner's view was that the ranger's sentence should move our confidence toward Foothills all the way to three to one, since that's the ratio he gave, and the outside possibilities are idle speculation we should just set aside. Mine was that the sentence tells us nothing usable unless we know how likely we are to be in one of those two areas at all — if the third possibilities are serious, the ranger's report seems to leave them untouched, and I couldn't see how a comparison of two options I might not even be between should drag my overall confidence anywhere. A friend we later called from the ridge took a third line: that the right move is somewhere in the middle, that we should lean toward Foothills but not nearly as hard as three to one, with the strength of the lean depending on how much probability we'd assign to being outside the two named areas altogether.

The disagreement mattered at the time, because the Foothills trail runs down to a road while the Lakeside trail dead-ends at a shelter, and we had about ninety minutes of daylight. Who, if any of us, was reasoning correctly about what the ranger's conditional report licenses? In particular, I'd like to understand whether evidence phrased only as "if A or B, then odds are thus on A" can shift one's credence in A at all when one isn't sure one is in the A-or-B case, and if it can, what the right shift is and what it depends on. If you think one of our three positions is right and the others mistaken, say which and why; if you think the question is genuinely underdetermined, explain what extra information would settle it and how the answer would change as a function of it.
