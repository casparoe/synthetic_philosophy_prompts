Anselm argues in Proslogion II that God, defined as the greatest conceivable being, exists in reality, and in Proslogion III that God cannot be conceived not to exist. Reconstruct both steps carefully, keeping track of what each establishes. Then: (1) explain the parody objection involving a perfect island, and say who pressed it and against whom; (2) explain Kant's objection in the Critique of Pure Reason, making clear why he holds that existence is not a real predicate and what his example of the money is supposed to show; (3) present Gödel's modal ontological argument in outline, including positive properties, the definition of God, and the route from possibility to necessary existence, and state which modal system the argument presupposes; (4) describe one serious objection that has been raised against Gödel's version, and assess whether Kant's objection threatens Gödel's version as well.

Here is the student's answer, reproduced without changes:

Anselm defines God as that than which nothing greater can be conceived. Even the fool who denies that God exists has this concept in his understanding, since he understands what he is denying. Now it is greater to exist in reality than to exist merely in the understanding. So if God existed only in the understanding, a greater being than God could be conceived, namely one that also existed in reality; since God is by definition that than which nothing greater can be conceived, this is a contradiction. Hence God exists in reality. In the next chapter Anselm strengthens the conclusion: something that cannot be conceived not to exist is greater than something that can, so God, being the greatest conceivable, not only exists but cannot be conceived not to exist. So chapter II gets us existence and chapter III gets us necessary existence.

Kant presses the parody objection: if Anselm's reasoning worked, we could prove the existence of a most perfect island, since an island than which no greater island can be conceived is defined exactly the way God is. Since we obviously cannot prove that such an island exists, the reasoning must be defective. Kant's deeper objection in the Critique of Pure Reason is that existence is not a real predicate. A real predicate is one that adds something to the concept of a thing, and existence adds nothing to a concept: a hundred real thalers contains not a coin more than a hundred imagined ones; it just means that the concept is instantiated. The ontological argument goes wrong by treating existence as a greatness-making property, so that it can be built into a concept and extracted again, but no existence claim ever follows from concepts alone. This is also what does in Descartes' Fifth Meditation version, since there existence is counted among the perfections of a supremely perfect being.

Gödel's modal version works with positive properties. The axioms say, roughly, that if a property entails only positive properties it is itself positive, and that for any property, either it or its negation is positive. God is defined as a being possessing every positive property. Gödel then argues that God's existence is possible (from the positivity axioms), that necessary existence is entailed by the God-like property, and that being God-like is the essence of any God-like being, from which it follows that God exists necessarily, and therefore actually. The argument can be formalized in S4: the crucial move, from the possibility of God's necessary existence to God's necessary existence, requires only collapsing iterated modal operators, which is exactly what S4 licenses. And since the argument is formalized, its validity is not in dispute; since its axioms merely unpack what positivity means, anyone who accepts the concept of a positive property is committed to God's existence.

The most serious objection to Gödel's version is modal collapse: the axioms entail that whatever is true is necessarily true, so the proof proves far more than its author intended and in effect abolishes contingency altogether. Kant's objection, by contrast, does not threaten Gödel's version, because Kant only denied that existence is a real predicate, whereas Gödel never treats existence as a predicate at all — he treats necessary existence as a positive property, which is a different claim. So Kant's objection applies to Anselm and Descartes but leaves Gödel untouched.
