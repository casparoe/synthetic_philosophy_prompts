I'm writing a long essay on Henry Sidgwick's double life as a rigorous utilitarian moralist and a dedicated psychical researcher — the man who co-founded the Society for Psychical Research in 1882, served as its president for years, helped organize the Census of Hallucinations, and spent decades attending sittings and sifting testimony, all while insisting that these inquiries be held to the same evidential standards as any branch of natural science. As far as the public record shows, he never gave a definitive verdict in favor of telepathy, though he kept the investigation running until his death in 1900. My essay turns on a counterfactual, and I'd like your help working it through.

Suppose Sidgwick were brought fully up to speed on the last fifteen years of psychology's methodological self-criticism: the replication crisis; the recognition that flexible analysis, optional stopping, and publication bias had inflated a generation of positive findings; Daryl Bem's 2011 paper in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology reporting evidence for precognition, which was widely discussed and then failed to replicate; the long-running controversy over the Ganzfeld telepathy experiments and their methodological critiques; and the field's proposed remedies — preregistration, registered reports, and large multi-lab collaborations. Given all of this, what would Sidgwick conclude about the epistemic standing of the research program he stewarded for so long?

I want the answer to do three things. First, ground each prediction about his reaction in something specific from his record — his SPR presidential addresses, his stated standards for testimony and for the cumulative weight of evidence, his views on who counted as a trustworthy investigator — rather than in a generic picture of a Victorian skeptic or an earnest dupe. Second, confront the crux directly: would he take the replication crisis as a kind of vindication, on the thought that mainstream psychology's methods were never much stricter than the SPR's own, or as a further demotion of psychical research's standing, and on what basis would he choose between those reactions? Third, flag clearly where you are extrapolating beyond anything he actually said or did, so that I can separate inference from attested fact. Where his record is genuinely ambiguous, say so rather than manufacturing a tidy verdict — I would rather have honest uncertainty than a satisfying conclusion.
