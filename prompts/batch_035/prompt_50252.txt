I edit a small satirical magazine whose readership overlaps heavily with the hard science fiction and philosophy of science communities — the sort of people who will happily spend an evening arguing about whether the Fermi paradox is an evidence problem or a conceptual one. For our upcoming issue on first contact, I'd like a parody of a terms-of-service agreement for a commercial tour operator that runs package holidays to Sol-3 under the assumption that the Zoo Hypothesis is basically correct: Earth is a nature preserve, humanity is the exhibit, and the silence of the sky is policy rather than absence. About 700–900 words, formatted as numbered clauses in deadpan legalese, with the standard ToS furniture — scope of service, prohibited conduct, liability waivers, an add-on excursion, an FAQ section, a dispute resolution clause — repurposed for the setting.

The humor has to come from getting the actual dialectic right, not from generic "aliens are quirky" jokes. Each clause should quietly take a position in the real debate. A few things I'd like to see worked in, in whatever form you find funniest: a non-interference clause whose justifications quietly reveal the operator's view of why the quarantine exists (a hint that it protects the exhibit from the visitors, or the visitors from something else, should be doing real work); a liability waiver that presupposes the Great Filter is behind humanity and treats extinction risks as "pre-mitigated," with the fine print revealing where the operator actually thinks the Filter sits; an upsell for a directed panspermia starter kit ("seed your own civilization") with a sharply limited warranty; a strict prohibition on responding to humanity's outgoing transmissions that treats SETI as poaching and grounds the ban in something darker than politeness — dark forest reasoning should be legible in the drafting style, a legal department that is visibly afraid of being observed, without the words "dark forest" appearing; and a refund schedule for tours cancelled due to exhibit extinction, with different payout tiers depending on whether the extinction was anthropogenic, natural, or "operator-assisted."

Two constraints. First, internal consistency: the operator believes the zoo arrangement is real and stable, so the document has to work as the actual policy of an organization embedded in that world — clauses can contradict marketing-speak in a revealing way, but not each other, unless the inconsistency is itself a deliberate joke you can defend. Second, layered readability: a reader who has never encountered the Fermi paradox should still find the piece legible as a parody of a tour operator's contract, while a specialist should feel that every clause tracks a genuine live position — for instance, whether a zoo arrangement requires some kind of coordinating body among civilizations, and what the existence of a commercial tour operator (with competitors?) implies about how crowded the galaxy actually is. Don't name or explain any of the underlying theories outright; the point is that the satire is fair to them precisely by never stating them.
