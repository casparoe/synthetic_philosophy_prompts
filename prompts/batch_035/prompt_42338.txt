I'm a journalist covering science and ideas for a mid-size magazine, and I've been sitting on a draft for our summer "big questions" issue on the whole Enlightenment-on-trial framing. The draft, roughly 1,400 words, goes something like this: it opens with the courtroom image — the prosecution being the Frankfurt School line from Adorno and Horkheimer's Dialectic of Enlightenment, where reason gets hollowed out into instrumental reason and ends up serving domination, plus the postcolonial historians who point out that "rights of man" coexisted quite happily with empire and slavery; the defense being the Pinker-style case that Enlightenment norms actually did drive abolition, public health, whatever. My move in the piece is that the trial is rigged because both sides need a single coherent defendant, and there wasn't one — and more interestingly, that the prosecution's own exhibit is a product of the defendant's workshop: the tools used to indict Enlightenment reason (universalism, critique, the demand that power justify itself) are themselves Enlightenment inheritances. I use the Haitian Revolution as my centerpiece: enslaved people and slaveholders both citing universal rights, which I claim shows the ideas weren't owned by either side. And my closing admission is that maybe I'm just doing the "the true Enlightenment has never been tried" dodge, the no-true-Scotsman shuffle.

My editor liked the frame but my factchecker got nervous about a few things, and honestly so am I. I'm not a specialist — I've read the Adorno and Horkheimer chapters once, years ago, and I know the secondary literature on whether Dialectic of Enlightenment is even a critique of the Enlightenment as such (as opposed to something narrower) is a live fight, but I don't fully know where that fight stands. Same with the Haiti material: I've seen historians push back hard on both the "Enlightenment caused abolition" story and the "it was all fig leaf for empire" story, and I don't want to quietly pick a winner and present it as what "historians now think."

So before I go another draft, could you give me detailed comments? Please structure the response as these four sections, in this order: "Solid ground" (about 150-200 words) — the claims in my outline that are safe to state flatly, as written; "Contested, not settled" (about 150-200 words) — the specific places where I'm flattening genuine disagreement among people who actually work on this, and what the live positions roughly are, since I'd rather flag the disagreement in the piece than fake a consensus; "Boring or borrowed" (about 100 words) — an honest call on whether my "the trial is rigged" move is actually fresh or whether I'm the ten thousandth writer to do the dialectical both-sands-and-neither bit, and if it's tired, what angle would actually earn a general reader's attention; and "The one change" (about 80 words) — if you could force exactly one revision before this goes to print, what it is. Be as blunt as you'd be to a friend, I have thick skin and a deadline.
