I write long-form features on neuroscience and the philosophy of mind for a general-interest magazine, and once a month I run a public "mind salon" — about twenty-five attendees, mostly readers with no philosophy background but a real appetite for it, ninety minutes in a library back room. Next month's session is on higher-order theories of consciousness, and I want the evening to turn on the central claim of that family of views rather than merely mention it: the idea that a mental state becomes conscious only when there is a further state directed at it — that what transforms a mere perception into an experience you are having is the meta-level — as against the first-order view that the perception is already fully conscious, and the higher-order state, if it occurs at all, merely reports on something that was there all along. I'd like an activity in which participants build something like that meta-level themselves and then watch it misbehave.

My rough sketch, which you should refine or replace as you see fit: a two- or three-stage perceptual task with barely discriminable stimuli — say, identifying which of two nearly identical patches was briefly flashed, or locating a digit in a masked display — where participants first make a forced choice, then rate their confidence in that choice, then rate how much they trust that confidence rating, each level recorded on separate slips so the ladder is visible. Then introduce conditions in which the levels come apart: trials where the first-order discrimination is well above chance while confidence is at chance (the blindsight-flavored dissociation), trials with no first-order task at all, and a stage in which feedback is corrupted — a confederate announces "correct" or "incorrect" at random — so that participants acquire a higher-order state about their own performance that is flatly false. What I'm hoping for is that the room ends up arguing, unprompted, about whether the meta-level made anything conscious or merely measured something that was already present.

Please supply the full rules, the materials (we have a projector, cards, paper slips, and no appetite for apps), a scoring scheme that involves no real money, and a timeline that fits ninety minutes. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the specific positions: Rosenthal's higher-order-thought view, Carruthers's dispositional variant, and first-order representationalists in the lineage of Dretske and Tye, along with the misrepresentation problem — when the corrupted feedback planted a false higher-order state, what should each theory say the participant's experience actually was? Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it, and be candid about which feature of the dispute no activity can reproduce. My own suspicion is that the constitutive question — whether the higher-order state confers consciousness or merely detects it — can't be staged at all, only asked, but I'd like your honest view on whether the game can at least make that question felt in the room rather than merely stated at the end. Keep the jargon out of the room until the debrief.
