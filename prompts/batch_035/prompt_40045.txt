The exam question was: "Parfit argues that personal identity is not what matters in survival. Explain the teletransporter thought experiment and its Branch-Line variant, distinguish psychological connectedness from psychological continuity, and explain how these notions support Parfit's claim about what matters."

Here's one student's answer:

"In the teletransporter case, a machine on Earth scans and records every detail of my brain and body and then destroys me, while a replicator on Mars builds an exact Replica of me, with all my memories, personality and intentions. Parfit accepts that this counts as survival: the Replica is me, and teletransportation is just an unusual way of travelling. In the Branch-Line variant, the scanner fails to destroy the original, who lives on for a few days before dying, while the Replica on Mars wakes up as planned. Since there is an unbroken chain of overlapping psychological links between me now and the Replica in both cases, I survive in the Branch-Line case too, because psychological continuity is preserved. Parfit holds that a person is nothing over and above a body and a series of interrelated mental events; this is his reductionism. But he also believes that when continuity holds there is a further fact of identity over and above the psychological facts, a deep fact that makes the later person literally me. Still, he says personal identity is not what matters: what matters is psychological continuity. In old age, direct psychological connectedness fades, but identity persists via continuity, and Parfit concludes that we have less reason to care about our future selves the more connectedness fades. So in both teletransporter cases we should be about as concerned for the Replica as for our ordinary future self."
