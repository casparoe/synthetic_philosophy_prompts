Every year I teach the philosophy of mind unit in an undergraduate course, and every year the discussion of the Turing test stalls in exactly the same place, with the same students making the same moves, and I've started to suspect the stall is the interesting fact. On one side are the students who take the behaviorist criterion at face value: if an interlocutor you interrogate at length, adversarially, and with no holds barred is indistinguishable from a thoughtful human, then nothing further remains to be known, and demanding some inner sparkle beyond the conversation is demanding something you never require of other people. On the other side are the students who insist that no quantity of conversational success could settle it, and their stock examples have stayed fixed for decades: the room of instructions that produces fluent Chinese without anyone or anything in it understanding Chinese, and the imagined machine of stupefying size that simply stores a lookup table mapping every possible conversational history to a sensible next line. Everyone knows the standard replies to these examples -- that the system as a whole understands, that the room should be given a camera and arms, that no table could survive unbounded interrogation -- and the replies get made, and then the seminar reconvenes the following year with the two camps exactly where they were. What puzzles me is that this debate has now outlived several technological milestones that were each supposed to resolve it: chess, natural-language fluency, systems that converse well enough to pass casual inspection, and the philosophical dispute has not budged an inch through any of them.

Here is my working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the two camps are not disagreeing about whether machines will pass some test, but about what kind of thing understanding is -- one side treats it as a status we attribute on the basis of behavioral evidence, the other as an inner fact about mechanism or causal organization that the evidence merely tracks. If that's right, the dispute may be partly verbal, a disagreement about the word "understanding" rather than about the world, or it may be that the behaviorist side is answering an epistemic question (what warrants the attribution?) while the skeptic side is answering a metaphysical one (what is it that's being attributed?). But I want a real diagnosis, not a survey of positions. Using what each side actually says, tell me what kind of disagreement this is -- verbal, substantive, or a case of two questions traveling under one name -- and whether some hidden empirical or formal question is quietly doing the work, perhaps about what is physically inside a system that behaves a given way. Then tell me honestly whether anything could count as progress: a conceptual distinction, a new class of behavioral evidence, a principled account of why the lookup-table example does or doesn't generalize, or an argument that the impasse shows the question was ill-posed from the start. Please answer in American English.
