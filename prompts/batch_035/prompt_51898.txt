I produce a long-form interview podcast on epistemology, and the guest for my next episode is a philosopher who defends the equal weight view of peer disagreement — the view that when you discover that someone you count as an epistemic peer disagrees with you, and you have no independent reason to think you're the one who's right, you should give their opinion exactly as much weight as your own, which typically means revising your credence at least halfway toward theirs. She has published on this for years and is practiced at deflecting objections with the line that the hard cases are "questions for the theory's further development, not objections to it."

Draft nine to eleven interview questions that make that deflection progressively harder to use. Each question must turn on the actual content of her position, and each must be paired with a follow-up keyed to the most likely reply. The set should cover, at minimum, the following pressure points, which I want asked in an order that builds:

1. The split-the-difference worry: whether equal weight has any determinate content once you move past simple two-option cases — what does it mean to split the difference when the possible answers don't lie on a scale, or when there are more than two live options and no compromise position between them?

2. The peerhood trap: whether the standard definition of a peer either makes the view trivial (if her past accuracy is relevant to whether she counts as my peer, then discovering that she disagrees is itself evidence about who's right, and the view has nothing left to say) or makes it so narrow that real disagreements almost never involve peers.

3. The obvious-error cases: cases where the disagreeing peer has just made a simple arithmetic slip or overlooked something on the very page in front of her — can the equal weight view explain why it seems clearly rational to stick with my original judgment there, or does it have to bite a bullet, and if it explains it, has it smuggled in first-order reasoning as a tiebreaker, which the view was supposed to forbid?

4. Disagreement about disagreement itself: since the equal weight view is rejected by able philosophers, does she, on her own lights, have to lower her confidence in it — and if she responds that only first-order substantive disagreements trigger equal weighting, what principled line separates the orders, given that higher-order evidence is still evidence?

5. The lived-experience cost: whether the view, consistently applied, requires someone in a contested speciality to hold almost no confident beliefs, and whether she accepts that or has a story about how credences can remain high in practice.

Also include one question that invites her to say something constructive rather than defensive — what she takes the equal weight view to have taught epistemology even if the specific formulation turns out wrong — placed at the end, after the pressure has been applied.

Constraints: no questions about her biography, her influences, or the state of the field in general; every question must be answerable only by someone who actually understands the dialectic. Keep the tone respectful but persistent, the way a good seminar moderator presses a visitor. Do not include any suggested answers of more than a clause or two; the deliverable is the questions and follow-ups, not her replies.
