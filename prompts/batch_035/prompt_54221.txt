I spent the better part of four decades as a natural scientist — first in biochemistry, later in public-health research — and I have come to philosophy only in retirement. My scientific training embedded in me the habits of what is often called sound science: quantify the risk, demand evidence, act on the central estimate. For most of my career I therefore regarded the precautionary principle as a slogan masquerading as a principle, a licence for the scientifically nervous to veto whatever displeased them. I want to test that scepticism against a case I watched unfold from the sidelines with professional interest: the British BSE affair. Analyse that episode through the lens of the precautionary principle, understood roughly as the view that where an activity carries a possibility of serious or irreversible harm, scientific uncertainty about its likelihood or magnitude should not by itself justify postponing measures to avert it.

Since you cannot look things up, let me set out the salient facts as I understand them, and please flag any of them you take to be mistaken rather than building on my errors. BSE was identified in British cattle in 1986 and was soon traced to feed supplements containing meat-and-bone meal rendered from animal carcasses. In 1988 a working party chaired by the zoologist Richard Southwood recommended control measures, a ban on the contaminated feed followed, and in November 1989 the government barred specified bovine offal — brain, spinal cord, spleen and the like — from the human food chain. Yet throughout this period ministers and officials repeatedly assured the public that British beef was safe, and the official scientific advice of the day judged the risk to humans to be remote. In March 1996 the government announced, abruptly, that a newly recognised variant of CJD in young people was probably linked to eating infected beef; the European Union then banned British beef exports, and a scheme was introduced under which cattle older than thirty months were slaughtered wholesale — over four million animals in all. The final human toll in the United Kingdom was around 178 deaths, a terrible figure, but far below the apocalyptic projections current at the moment of the 1996 announcement.

Here is what I would like, and it is more than a retelling. First, a disciplined mapping: who counts as the actor under the principle, what the live options were at each juncture — the feed ban, the offal ban, the assurances of safety, the mass slaughter — and whether the principle, so specified, actually recommended the measure taken at each point, or something stronger, or something weaker. Second, an honest account of where the principle falls silent: it must be silent somewhere, because it cannot by itself tell you how many cattle to kill, how much of a food supply to condemn, or how to weigh a small and uncertain probability of a slow horrific death against the certain destruction of an industry. Third, the case run in the other direction: does the eventual outcome — a toll far below what was feared — vindicate the 1996 precautions, or does it expose an unfalsifiability problem, such that both the alarmists and the complacent can claim vindication whatever the number turns out to be?

Finally, a hobbyhorse of mine. In my reading I have found it useful to distinguish precaution as a burden-of-proof rule — uncertainty about harm shifts the burden onto those who would continue the risky activity — from precaution as a decision rule — the principle dictates the protective measure itself. My working suspicion is that the BSE affair vindicates the first and embarrasses the second: shifting the burden early in 1988 looks defensible in hindsight, while the particular form and scale of the eventual response seem to have owed more to trade politics and panic than to any principle. Test that suspicion. Tell me whether the distinction survives contact with the case, or whether the affair shows the two roles cannot be pried apart. And if you think the whole episode is better understood as a failure of risk communication, or of the machinery of ministerial government, than as a test of precaution at all, then make that case rather than flattering my framing.

I should say what I do not want: a tour of competing formulations of the principle, a list of who has written what, or a policy brief. I want the case analysis, disciplined about the mapping, candid about the principle's silences, and willing to tell me that my burden-of-proof distinction is not doing the work I take it to do.
