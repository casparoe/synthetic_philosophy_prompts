I have been trying to work out, case by case, what exactly makes human extinction bad beyond the deaths it involves, and I would like your help extracting a principle from my verdicts — or being told plainly which verdicts have to go. Please write your response in American English.

Here are my verdicts:

1. An asteroid sterilizes Earth in the year 2200, instantly and painlessly. This is much worse than a pandemic that kills exactly the same number of people over a decade but leaves a viable population. The deaths are identical in number, so the difference in badness must be something other than the deaths.

2. Suppose I learn that humanity ends painlessly about a month after my own natural death — no one alive today survives to see it, but the ending is fixed and certain. I find this news disturbing in a way that learning merely that I will die next month is not, and I am inclined to think my life goes worse in the first scenario than the second. I take seriously the thought — the one Scheffler gestures at with his afterlife conjecture — that a good deal of extinction's badness falls on the living, who must carry on toward a future that will not be there, rather than on the dead or the never-born.

3. Yet I also judge that a slow, painless fading — the last generation simply does not reproduce, everyone dies old and contented, there is no catastrophe and no abrupt ending, and no one alive at any point knows for certain that it is over — is nearly as bad as the asteroid. In that scenario, no living person's projects are knowingly undermined in the way described in verdict 2. So whatever makes the asteroid worse than the pandemic seems to be present even when the mechanism from verdict 2 is absent.

4. I do not believe nonexistence is bad for those who never exist: a merely possible future person whom we never create has no complaint. But if extinction is worse than the sum of its deaths, and the extra badness falls on no one — not the dead, not the never-born, and in the fading scenario not even the undermined living — then it appears to be an impersonal fact about the value of worlds. And I am generally suspicious of impersonal value: I do not think a world is better merely for containing more happy people.

5. A future in which humanity is deliberately succeeded by greatly modified descendants — so changed that they would count as another species, but long-lived, curious, making art, exploring — seems to me far less of a loss than extinction, even though humanity in some biological sense has ended. Whatever the principle is, it should treat this as largely acceptable.

6. A future in which humanity survives indefinitely in permanent, contented stasis — every need met, no suffering, but no projects, no children, no discovery, and no difference between year ten thousand and year one million — seems to me about as bad as extinction, perhaps worse. This verdict worries me: it apparently forces the principle to say that survival with too little value is no better than ending, and I am not sure I want to say that about actual people living contented lives.

So here is what I am asking for. Is there a single principle that captures all six verdicts? I can see the outlines of candidates — one on which extinction's extra badness is its effect on the living (which saves verdict 2 but apparently not 3); one on which it is the impersonal loss of future value (which handles 1, 3, and 6 but conflicts with 4 and needs an account of why 5 is acceptable); perhaps a hybrid. Please state whatever principle or principles you favor precisely, with each clause tied to the verdict it earns, and apply the result to cases I have not listed — for example, whether it would be bad to create a final generation of people engineered to know they are last and to be content with it; whether a successor population of digital minds would count; and whether the extinction of a small, isolated tribe, with no effect on humanity's overall prospects, is bad in the same way or a different one. Finally, tell me honestly which of my verdicts your preferred principle cannot accommodate, and whether verdict 2 — the Scheffler-style one — is doing any real work in the final account or is a distraction I should drop.
