I'm a second-year physics and philosophy student, and our Philosophy of Physics module has just spent a fortnight on the applicability of mathematics. Our tutor keeps invoking Wigner's slogan — the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics in the natural sciences — to do real argumentative work. Last week he ran through his stock of examples: Dirac's equation written to tame the electron and then obligingly predicting the positron; Riemann developing his geometry decades before general relativity had any use for it; group theory sorting the particle zoo before the detectors confirmed the missing entries. He took these not as lucky accidents but as something demanding explanation, and drew a platonist moral: mathematics succeeds because it describes a realm of structure that the physical world somehow participates in.

I can't tell whether the slogan, once made precise, actually supports any of this, and I have three worries I can't resolve on my own. First, my flatmate says there's an obvious selection effect: physicists rummage through a vast attic of pure mathematics and quietly shelve the pieces that don't fit, so the slogan confuses the numerator with the fraction. Second, someone in the seminar mentioned a riposte essay about the unreasonable *ineffectiveness* of mathematics in biology, which, if the contrast is real, suggests the phenomenon is local to physics — though I can't see whether that locality would support platonism or undercut it. Third, I've since come across a later essay arguing that we deliberately pose only the questions mathematics is good at; but the positron case seems untouched by that, since Dirac wasn't shopping for a question.

Could you lay out the distinct precise claims the slogan could be making — from the near-trivial to the radical — and say of each whether it is plausible, whether it is really a substantive claim about the world rather than about our practices, and whether it would support my tutor's argument from unexplained success to platonism? I'd also like to know which reading Wigner himself needed for his talk of miracle to make sense, and whether the positron and Riemann cases are equally good evidence for whichever reading survives. Please answer in British English.
