My flatmate overheard me telling a mutual friend, with real heat, that texting while driving was selfish and reckless — this from someone who does it herself most mornings — and when I recounted the scene to a friend of mine, we argued about it for the better part of two hours. (1) She said my blame lacked standing: blame is not a bare assertion that an act was wrong, it is an address, a demand for justification issued from a moral position, and a hypocrite has forfeited the position — the way a bribed judge's rulings might still be legally correct while he has no business delivering them from the bench. (2) I answered that the blamer's own conduct leaves the content of the blame untouched: 'you shouldn't text and drive' is no less true in my mouth than in anyone's, and my hypocrisy is a separate wrong of mine, a defect in me rather than in the blame itself. (3) She replied that if blame were mere assertion, 'look who's talking' would be a pure distraction; the fact that it strikes us as a fitting riposte suggests blame carries the blamer's presumed authority along with it. (4) I said 'look who's talking' merely registers my additional fault without undoing the first one — two wrongs, cleanly separable — and noted that we constantly accept blame from hypocrites: 'I know I'm one to talk, but' seems to work fine. (5) She said softened blame works precisely because the speaker disclaims the authority, which concedes her point about ordinary blame. (6) I then argued her view collapses into 'only the blameless may blame,' which would silence everyone; she countered that standing is local — the habitual texter loses standing only about texting, not about lying or drunk driving — and I said that local/global line looks arbitrary, since the same underlying indifference to risk runs through both. Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: who is right about what, which moves are doing real work and which are noise, and what, if anything, are we both missing?
