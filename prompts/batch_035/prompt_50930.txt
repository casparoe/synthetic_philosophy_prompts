I'm a novelist, three books in, all of them written in close third person, and I've become mildly obsessive over the craft question of what a narrator cannot see. I'm now researching a novel set partly in a municipal records office, and the archival theorists I've been reading to get the setting right have unsettled me more than I expected. The picture they paint is something like this: an archive is not a neutral warehouse of the past but an artifact of whoever had the means and the motive to record; the decisions about what to acquire, what to appraise out, and how to catalogue it are themselves interpretive acts, so the finding aid is a kind of argument; and the silences come in different kinds — events no one with paper and standing thought worth recording, records deliberately destroyed, records that survive but are unfindable because of how they were arranged. There's also a further, stranger claim that the researcher's own questions manufacture additional silences, since what you don't think to ask in the catalogue never surfaces for you at all. I have my doubts about how far this stretches, but I can't quite dismiss it, because it feels uncomfortably like something I already believe about prose.

Here is what I want: translate that cluster of claims into the vocabulary I actually work in — point of view, focalization, the implied author, ellipsis, the unreliable narrator, backstory and lore that exists in my notes but never reaches the page, the paratext of chapter titles and section breaks. Do it claim by claim, as faithfully as you can. To seed the translation, I've found it useful to distinguish in my own drafting between the scene I cut in revision, the scene I drafted and chose to gesture past, and the scene I never conceived of at all — prospective omission versus retrospective omission — and I suspect the theorists are running several such distinctions together, though I may be projecting my craft onto their theory. Then give me the residue report I care most about: which of their claims map cleanly onto something novelists already have terms for, which translate only with distortion, and which have no expression in my vocabulary at all. In particular, I want you to confront the fact that every silence in a novel is authored — someone intended it, however dimly — whereas the archive people insist their silences are structural and distributed, produced by appraisal committees and colonial bureaucracies and cataloguing conventions rather than by any author. If that asymmetry is real, say so; if it's shallower than it looks, say why. And conclude with a verdict: does the residue point to something my craft vocabulary is missing, or to something vaguer in the theorists' claims than they admit?

Please write it as continuous prose rather than a list or table, and hold it to a hard maximum of 750 words — I would rather have the translation compressed and exact than exhaustive. Throughout, flag how confident you are in each mapping, and at the end single out the one claim in your residue report you are least sure of, with a sentence on what would change your mind about it. I'm not asking you to settle the archive debate; I'm asking you to tell me whether a novelist's way of thinking about what gets said and what doesn't is a genuine vantage point on it or just a flattering mirror.
