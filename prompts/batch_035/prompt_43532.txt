Like many democracies, mine is built around parties to a degree that goes beyond merely tolerating them: the state disburses public money to parties based on vote share, prints their names on the ballot next to candidates' own, in some districts literally nominates candidates through publicly regulated party primaries, and in others assigns parliamentary seats to closed party lists. Meanwhile, a candidate unaffiliated with a party must collect signatures to appear on the ballot while a party's nominee appears automatically. I have been rereading Simone Weil's wartime essay on the abolition of political parties, and the contrast is striking. Her argument, as I understand it, is roughly this: a party is a machine for stirring up collective passions, and collective thinking of that kind is inherently falsifying — the party member ends up holding opinions on every public question that he did not arrive at himself and cannot honestly reexamine, because party loyalty requires a fixed public stance on everything, including matters one has never considered. That is why she calls the party, in spirit, a variant of the Inquisition: agreement is not enough; the member must publicly affirm. So she proposes abolishing parties as electoral and political machines while leaving churches, unions, and similar groups free to organize internally — and she treats a single-party state as equally bad or worse. Here is my question. Our actual practice of state-favored, state-funded parties seems to presuppose almost the exact opposite view of political judgment: that party labels help voters by cheaply summarizing a candidate's commitments, and that the "responsible party government" model improves on a chaotic individualism. Does our institutional practice really presuppose that collective organizations can think well — and if Weil is right that collective party passions falsify judgment, our institutions would be financing the corruption of judgment itself. Or can the practice survive on a weaker presupposition, namely that voters judge better between two coherent teams than among hundreds of individuals, so that the presupposed view is about voter competence rather than about party members' inner freedom? And is the practice consistent in leaving unions and churches free while favoring parties — that looks like her own asymmetry, yet I suspect the underlying views differ. I would like you to reconstruct what our institutions presuppose, say whether the presupposition is compatible with Weil's diagnosis, and identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — i.e., whether several theories of political judgment would license it just as well.
