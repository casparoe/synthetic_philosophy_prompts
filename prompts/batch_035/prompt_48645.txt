I'm on a three-person panel that picks which freelance illustrator gets our studio's annual contract. The rules we announced to everyone were: we hire the candidate if a majority of us judges (a) she can handle the style we need and (b) she fits our budget. We each voted on the two criteria. Dilip and I said yes on style, Ada and I said yes on budget — so she passed both bars by majority. But when we went around the room saying hire/no-hire, Dilip voted no overall (he thinks the budget question was too close), Ada voted no overall (style doubts), and I voted yes. So 2 out of 3 of us wanted to reject her, even though by our own announced rule she cleared both criteria.

We deadlocked. Dilip says we're bound by the announced rule, so our overall preferences are irrelevant — the rule was the rule. Ada thinks that's insane: the panel exists to express what its members actually want, and a procedure that gives a result most of us reject is a procedure that misfired. I keep flip-flopping between them.

A friend who studied formal social choice told me there's a theorem that goes roughly like: for a setup like ours, any consistent way of turning individual views into a group view ends up either making one of us a de facto dictator or requiring unanimity before the group can decide anything. Did she make that up, or is that a real thing? And more to the point: is there a principled way out of our deadlock, or do we just have to pick which failure we hate least? For what its worth, we did announce the criteria rule in advance, and the illustrator heard us announce it — I don't know if that changes anything but it feels like it might.
