I have been a hospice volunteer for eleven years, and for the last three I have also run a monthly discussion group on death and dying at our public library — about a dozen adults, mostly retirees, a couple of nurses and a former EMT, no doctors. We usually talk about practical things: wills, funerals, what to say to someone who is dying. Lately, though, I keep arriving, no matter what the evening's topic is, at a question I cannot settle and cannot drop: whether the moment a person dies is something we discover or something we partly choose.

Two things put me onto it. The first was a family I supported through the decision to take their father off the ventilator. His heart stopped, the nurse said he was gone, and it struck me that with the machines on, his body had been doing a good deal of what living bodies do — warm, breathing with help, healing — and that what stopped was something specific, not everything at once. The second was the news stories you have probably seen about brain-dead pregnant women kept on machines for months so the baby could be delivered: dead enough for a death certificate in one sense, alive enough to grow a child in another. And somewhere I read that when organs are donated after the heart stops, rather than after the brain is declared dead, hospitals used to wait two minutes before the surgeons began, and later some waited five — and I have never been able to find anything in nature that makes it two rather than five.

Please design a ninety-minute activity for my group around this. I want them to run into the problem themselves, before anyone names it. My own idea, which you should correct or improve, is a staged case with roles — family members, an ICU nurse, a transplant coordinator, a chaplain, maybe a hospital administrator — built around a fictional patient on a ventilator with no brain activity, with the twist that the group must eventually set the number of minutes to wait after the heart stops before organs may be retrieved. I suspect each role will want a different number, for what turn out to be different reasons. Please give me rules, a timeline, simple and cheap materials, guidance on handling participants who actually know real hospital protocol, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the difference between brain death and cardiac death, and onto my real question of discovery versus decision. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about which part of the problem an activity like this simply cannot reproduce.
