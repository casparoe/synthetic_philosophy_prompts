I hold a version of the Great Filter position: the observed silence of the sky is best explained by the transition from dead matter to a colonizing civilization being extremely improbable, and the bulk of that improbability lies behind us — in our planet's past rather than in our future. I also hold a more specific refinement, which I have not seen argued much and which I want stress-tested: the filter is lumpy rather than smeared. That is, I believe the total improbability is concentrated in one or perhaps two transitions — my own candidates are abiogenesis and eukaryogenesis — rather than distributed thinly across many moderately unlikely steps, as it would be if the filter were the cumulative product of dozens of independent preadaptations each with a small individual probability. The coarse claim and the lumpy refinement can come apart, and I want to know where each of them is exposed.

Tell me what would count against this. Not a survey of Fermi paradox resolutions — I know them, and I hold one. What I want is the map of defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, given separately for the coarse claim and for the lumpy refinement. Some things you should address: (1) A second, independent genesis of life elsewhere in the solar system is usually cited as the classic falsifier, but is it fatal for the lumpy view only under certain conditions — does a second simple microbe actually bear on abiogenesis versus on the later transitions, and given the possibility of shared ancestry through meteorite exchange, what feature of such a discovery would disambiguate independent origin from panspermia? (2) What patterns in future biosignature statistics from exoplanet surveys would distinguish lumpy from smeared from filter-ahead? Concretely, if some fraction of temperate terrestrial planets turn out to have biospheres, at what fraction would you say the coarse view is in serious trouble, and which observations would specifically damage the claim that eukaryogenesis carries most of the weight? (3) Does the lumpy view make predictions about the timing of transitions in our own planet's history — about how long the gaps between steps should be, given the remaining habitable lifetime of the sun — and could the actual record of those timings count against it? (4) A clean technosignature is obviously fatal, but I want to know what a committed defender would concede short of that: an unambiguous artifact of independent manufacture, a systematically odd astronomical anomaly, a verified signal that decodes to nothing?

Finally, give me an honest assessment of whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability. I have noticed in myself a tendency to absorb every new finding: when extremophile discoveries made early habitability look easy, I quietly moved probability mass from abiogenesis to eukaryogenesis; when tentative biosignature claims on other worlds surface and then remain disputed, I find myself counting the dispute itself as confirming. At what point does the ability to redistribute weight among the lumps mean the position no longer risks anything? Is there a principled line between legitimate updating within a view and insulating it from refutation — and if there is, does my version of the view sit on the right side of it?
