My roommate is fully on team stochastic parrot — every chatbot is just fancy autocomplete, no understanding, end of story. I pushed back by pointing at how these models actually get evaluated: labs quiz them on benchmarks, and before they do they run what's called decontamination, meaning they check whether the test questions accidentally leaked into the training data, and if they did, they remove or rewrite those questions, on the theory that a model answering questions it has literally seen before proves nothing. My roommate was not impressed: "so you're grading the autocomplete on whether it can autocomplete stuff it hasn't seen yet — congrats, that's interpolation, not understanding." And honestly I don't have a great comeback. It got me wondering what conception of understanding the whole quizzing-and-decontaminating practice actually presupposes, because it seems to be something like: understanding is the capacity to handle genuinely novel cases, i.e. generalize beyond whatever got memorized. But that can't be the whole story. A very clever lookup-and-interpolate system could still ace novel questions, and going the other way, a human student who "gets" calculus after working through a bunch of solved examples still counts as understanding — prior exposure doesn't disqualify a person. So why exactly does prior exposure invalidate the test for a model but not for a student?

There's also a second tension in the same corner of the world that I want folded in. The same community that treats benchmark novelty as the gold standard also insists that just chatting with a model and being impressed doesn't show anything — people are easily fooled, conversational fluency is cheap, the Turing test was too low a bar. So casual conversation isn't enough, but structured quizzing is. Meanwhile some of these labs also do interpretability work, peering at the model's internals as if the real question about understanding is what's happening in there, not how it performs. So I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory (or theories) of understanding that the evaluation practice is committed to: what it strictly presupposes, what it merely fits nicely with, and whether its parts are even consistent with each other. And then tell me where the practice is underdetermined — could a hardcore behaviorist and someone who thinks understanding requires grounded inner states both accept the current setup for different reasons? Finally, what would the practice have to look like if we swapped in the parrot-skeptic's own view as the official one — would we just stop testing, or would the tests change into something else entirely, and if so into what?

For what it's worth, my instinct is that the decontamination move is doing more philosophical work than anyone running it has admitted — it seems to smuggle in a memory/understanding distinction that the parrot side says doesn't exist, since for them everything is memory of a statistical sort. But I'm not sure whether that instinct is right or whether decontamination can be defended on purely practical grounds (a model that saw the answer is a confound, whatever you think about understanding) without taking a side at all. Tell me if I'm onto something or just confusing epistemics with metaphysics, my roommate says it's the latter.
