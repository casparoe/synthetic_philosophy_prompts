I spent thirty-five years as a toxicologist at an agricultural research institute, and only since retiring, reading philosophy in the evenings, have I come to doubt my own long-standing account of something I did in 1981.

That year, a regulatory panel visited the institute to review a new fungicide before its approval. Our rodent study had shown an elevated tumor rate at the highest dose — a real effect, though the margins were not dramatic. Under the protocols of the time we were also running a comparison arm with an older, fully approved compound at an even higher dose, and that arm had fared slightly worse. When the panel's chairman asked me how the new compound had performed, I did not state a falsehood. I presented the complete data tables and remarked that the compound had performed favorably relative to the established comparator, and that we had observed nothing that would distinguish it from standard practice. Every clause was true. But I had chosen the comparator, chosen which comparisons to emphasize, and I knew perfectly well that no one on the panel would re-derive the absolute rates from the tables before the session ended. The compound was cleared.

For decades I told myself that what I did was categorically unlike lying: I asserted only truths, accurately presented, and allowed competent adults to reach their own conclusion. In the laboratory I would never have confused a measurement error with a calibration error; they were distinct faults with distinct remedies, and I have been tempted to treat the false and the merely misleading in the same way — as genuinely different wrongs, perhaps of differing severity. But I have also begun to wonder whether that taxonomy is just a device for letting a speaker feel cleaner than his record warrants. If the panel left the room believing something false, and I arranged for them to leave that way, it is not obvious to me that the truthfulness of my sentences settles anything.

The question has become practical again. A historian of science is writing an institutional history of the institute and has asked me, plainly, whether anyone ever lied to the regulators. I would be grateful for your help with three things. First, was my answer to the panel a lie, or does it fall into some other category of wrongdoing? Second, if the difference between what I did and lying is real, is it a difference that matters morally, or only a technical one? And third, setting aside 1981, what does intellectual honesty require of me in my answer to the historian — and is the honest answer to her necessarily the same kind of thing as the honest answer I owed the panel?
