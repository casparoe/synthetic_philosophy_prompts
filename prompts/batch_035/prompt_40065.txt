I'm writing a paper on Brave New World for an ethics class that just covered negative utilitarianism, and I keep thinking the novel is a better test case for tranquilism than for the usual freedom-vs-happiness debate people default to. By tranquilism I mean the view that only the absence of craving and suffering matters morally — not positive pleasure, not achievement or knowledge or connection, any of it. And the World State is basically the tranquilist project carried out: soma switches craving off without removing pleasure, conditioning prevents dissatisfaction before it can even form, and even death is engineered to be free of terror. If tranquilism is true, Mustapha Mond's civilization is close to optimal and John's demand for the right to be unhappy looks like a category error — mistaking the value of human flourishing for value, period. Huxley obviously wants us to side with John, though, and I'm not sure the book earns it. Helmholtz choosing exile and discomfort for the sake of writing poetry is the strongest counterexample-y moment, but a tranquilist can just call that noble delusion, and John's own final spiral honestly reads like a demonstration that unmanaged craving reliably terminates in suffering — which is a point for Mond, not John. So what I want is a reading that takes the novel as a serious argument against tranquilism rather than a gut-level recoil: where does it actually score against the view (Bernard's petty, soma-free envy is one candidate — craving that produces nothing anyone could value?), and where does Huxley cheat, i.e. smuggle in non-tranquilist premises to make the society look bad? And does the ending — which the book doesn't let us read as painless annihilation, the thing a consistent tranquilist might actually welcome — show the novel dodging the hardest version of the debate? I'd like something that stays fair to both the book and the philosophy, and says what the novel dramatizes here that the standard arguments don't. No need for quotes, I have the text.
