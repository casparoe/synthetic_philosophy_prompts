At a meeting of our metaphysics reading group this week, two members got stuck on Max Black's two-sphere universe — the imaginary world containing nothing but two qualitatively identical spheres, two miles apart and forever at rest, which Black proposed as a counterexample to the identity of indiscernibles. The disagreement ran through the following steps, roughly as recorded:

1. Maya said the example simply refutes the principle in its classical form: the spheres share every property — same size, same mass, same material, the same total pattern of relations — and yet there are two of them, not one. So numerical distinctness without qualitative difference is possible, which is exactly what the principle denies.

2. Theo replied that she was counting properties too narrowly: each sphere is two miles from the other, and neither is two miles from itself. The two stand in an irreflexive relation, and such a relation can hold only between numerically distinct things; so the spheres are discernible after all, and the principle survives in a relational version.

3. Maya called this circular: to apply the relation, you must already be able to tell that the "other" sphere is a different thing from this one. The irreflexive relation registers the fact that there are two; it does not ground it. So the relational version licenses just what it was supposed to explain.

4. Theo answered that the principle was never meant to manufacture individuality out of purely qualitative materials; it was meant to say when the qualitative facts entitle you to count two things rather than one. In this world they do — the distance facts, together with the irreflexivity of standing two miles off, entail that there are two — and that is all the work the principle was ever supposed to do.

5. Maya's last word was that this renders the principle empty: if the counting version is satisfied whenever the qualitative story happens to contain an irreflexive relation, then the principle no longer addresses the question Black actually raised — whether the total qualitative character of a world fixes how many objects it contains — and by her lights Black's example still shows that it does not.

I could not tell, as it happened, who got the better of the exchange. Please adjudicate it move by move: say who is right about what at each step, note whether Maya's final claim actually follows from what she has established by that point, and identify anything of substance that both of them missed.
