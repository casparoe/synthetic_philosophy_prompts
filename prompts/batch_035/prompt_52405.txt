**Draft: "The cow is not a chicken"**

The standard animal-ethics arithmetic says chicken is worse than beef per kilo of meat: one cow gives you a couple hundred kilos, one chicken gives you maybe a kilo and a half, so per meal you're responsible for roughly a hundred times fewer deaths if you eat beef. Broilers also live about six weeks, which intuitively means less total suffering accumulated per animal... except that a beef cow gets maybe a year and a half to two years, so per animal, the cow can suffer longer. Still, on deaths-per-meal, beef wins easily, and most people who make this argument stop there.

My point in this essay is that this stops way too early. Beef is mostly a machine for turning feed crops into a smaller amount of meat: by the usual feed conversion figures you need something like 4-5 kg of feed for a kg of chicken but 8-10+ for a kg of beef (dairy beef complicates this, more later). So per kilo of meat, beef demands several times more cropland, and cropland is where the hidden deaths are: animals shredded and poisoned in cultivation and harvest, and wild populations that would have lived on that land if it were left alone. If those deaths count, then the "fewer deaths" argument for beef is in trouble, and possibly reversed.

But here's where I think I have something genuinely new to add, a distinction I haven't seen anyone make: crop deaths are a *recurring* harm and habitat displacement is a *one-time* harm. Every planting season, the plow and the combine kill again. But converting wild land to pasture — which is what's behind a lot of beef, at least where cattle graze rather than eat feedlot grain — happens once, and after that the land supports both the cow and a reduced-but-nonzero suite of wild animals living alongside it. So on a long enough time horizon, grazing beef might involve a much smaller stream of deaths than chicken fed on annually-tilled feed. The chicken's death toll is front-loaded and visible (the bird itself) plus an ongoing invisible tax (its feed); the cow's is one animal plus a mostly one-off conversion.

Then the last section, which I'm least sure about. A total-view utilitarian could say that raising a cow who has a decent life and then killing it is *good for the cow*, in the only sense available — she wouldn't otherwise have existed. Wild animals displaced by soy fields mostly have lives that, on many estimates, are net-negative anyway (most offspring die young, etc.). So maybe beef and chicken don't even face the same moral ledger: one creates lives worth living and ends them, the other forecloses lives that were mostly going to be bad. I gesture at the obvious worry that this proves too much (it would justify basically any humane farming), and then kind of wave my hands and say that's a feature of total views, not a bug of my essay.
