At our department's weekly logic-adjacent reading group (I'm a PhD student in mathematics, working on operator algebras, so set theory is more of a serious hobby than my home turf), two of the other graduate students got into a long argument about the epistemic status of the axiom of choice, and neither would concede anything. Nina opened by saying the axiom is simply true: choosing one element from each member of a family of nonempty sets is something we do without hesitation when the family is finite, and it's unmotivated to believe that this becomes impossible or meaningless at infinity -- the infinite is supposed to extend the finite, not overturn it. She added that every working mathematician uses it constantly, mostly without noticing, and that the burden of proof is on the denier. Patrick replied that this is exactly the kind of reasoning the paradoxes should have cured us of: Banach-Tarski tells us that accepting choice commits us to decomposing a solid ball into finitely many pieces and reassembling them into two balls of the original size, which is absurd, and an axiom with absurd consequences is best regarded as a convenient convention rather than a truth.

Nina's second move was that the Banach-Tarski objection proves too much: the theorem is a genuine theorem, the decomposition is non-measurable and nothing physical, and "I find it weird" is not a counterargument to a proof; also, virtually every mainstream existence theorem in functional analysis -- maximal ideals, Hamel bases, Hahn-Banach in full generality -- depends on choice, and Patrick happily uses those. Patrick's second move was that this conflates conditional and unconditional mathematics: with choice those theorems are theorems of ZFC, but the question is whether they describe the sets that really exist, and he has heard there are models of ZF without choice in which every set of reals is Lebesgue measurable, which suggests the Banach-Tarski pathology is an artifact of the axiom rather than a feature of reality. Nina closed by saying that independence results cut both ways -- if choice can't be decided from ZF alone, then Patrick can't appeal to non-choice models as showing what sets are "really" like, any more than she can appeal to choice models; it's a stalemate about formalism, and in a stalemate you go with the axiom that makes mathematics work. Patrick's last word was that this is precisely why his position was never that choice is false but that the question of its truth is confused, and that Nina had quietly conceded the interesting part by retreating from "true" to "useful."

I mostly followed the exchange but couldn't referee it, and the group has asked me to write up the argument for next week. Could you adjudicate it move by move? In particular I'd like your verdicts on: whether the finite-to-infinite extrapolation argument for choice carries any weight, whether Banach-Tarski can function as evidence against an axiom rather than merely as a source of discomfort, whether Patrick's point about conditional versus unconditional theorems is a real distinction or a dodge, whether the appeal to models where every set of reals is measurable is legitimate given how such models are constructed, and whether the final stalemate claim -- that independence makes the truth question empty -- is itself a substantive philosophical position rather than a neutral observation. Please say who is right about which specific points, and identify anything substantive that both of them missed.
