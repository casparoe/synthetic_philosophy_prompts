I'm a retired physicist who spent most of my career on statistical modeling, and in retirement I've been reading philosophy and volunteering on a regional health technology assessment committee. A puzzle from that committee has been nagging at me, and I'd like a careful philosophical treatment of it. In cost-effectiveness analysis, agencies like NICE in the UK rank treatments by cost per quality-adjusted life year: health states are assigned utilities between 0 (death) and 1 (perfect health), typically elicited from survey respondents via the standard gamble or the time trade-off, and then a treatment's benefit is computed as the added years times the utility, summed across all patients it would help, so that a treatment giving 2,000 people each a 0.05 improvement counts the same as one giving 100 people a full unit. The agency also operates on an explicit principle that a QALY counts the same no matter who receives it -- young or old, sick or healthy -- though it has flirted with giving extra weight to the severely ill. Meanwhile, I remember enough decision theory to know that the standard gamble is a von Neumann–Morgenstern construction, and vNM utility is only defined up to affine transformation and within a single person's preferences. So the same theory that supplies the elicitation technique seems to forbid the arithmetic the results are then used in. And in the 1990s Oregon tried something similar to rank Medicaid services and had to abandon it amid public outrage, which suggests the underlying conception of welfare isn't even widely shared.

Please act as a professional philosopher of economics and value theory, think carefully, and begin with some brainstorming before committing to a line. What theory of utility and welfare does routine QALY-based prioritization actually presuppose? Is it committed to cardinal, interpersonally comparable utility -- a mental stuff that can be summed across heads -- or is there some more defensible reconstruction, say in terms of what an impartial observer would choose behind a veil of ignorance, or the weights a benevolent social planner would use? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with: could an ordinalist about utility, or a resolute preference-satisfaction theorist, still sign off on the summation if the numbers were generated differently, or does the whole apparatus collapse without a hedonic-cardinal foundation? Please also address whether the "equal value of every QALY" principle presupposes something further about distributive justice -- a kind of utilitarian equality that the severity weighting flirtations would violate -- and say what the practice would have to look like if we took the ordinalist critique seriously. I'm not looking for a critique of healthcare rationing as such; I want the implicit theory reconstructed, its rivals identified, and a judgment on whether the parts of the practice are consistent with one another.
