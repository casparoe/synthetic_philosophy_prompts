For the past three years I've sat on the conduct committee of a mid-sized community college — mostly faculty and staff plus two student members — and we hear something like three to six cases a week during term: plagiarism, purchased papers, small fraud schemes, intoxicated incidents in the dorms. I have no philosophy training; I just read on my own, and last year I got interested in free will after a podcast about Harry Frankfurt. Two things stuck with me. First, his thought that a person can be fully blameworthy even when they literally could not have done otherwise — his imagined case where someone would have been forced to act had they shown any sign of hesitation, but never showed any sign, so the forcing never actually happened. Second, his distinction between desires a person owns and desires a person is at war with — the willing addict versus the unwilling addict. Meanwhile, our committee's written standards are a tangle of "could have" language: mitigating circumstances, "the student had no real choice," "unbearable external pressure." My suspicion is that half the appeals we hear trade on the unexamined assumption that no-alternatives automatically means no-blame, and that Frankfurt's line of thinking shows that assumption is wrong — but I have no idea how to turn that into something five tired people can actually run in a forty-minute hearing.

What I'd like is a procedure the committee can follow case by case: an ordered set of questions, what a "yes" or "no" to each should do to our finding, where the procedure hands the decision back to human deliberation because the question is one of judgment rather than fact — especially the step of working out whether an action flowed from a desire the person endorsed or one they repudiated, which seems to me unavoidably a judgment call, and I want the procedure to say so rather than disguise it. It has to be usable by colleagues with zero philosophy background, so no jargon without a plain-language gloss, and it should say upfront where it will predictably misfire. Then run it on three cases like the ones we actually face: (a) a student who bought a paper online while working two jobs after her financial aid was cut mid-semester; (b) a student who submitted an AI-generated essay and, when asked about it, said quite calmly that given the grading scheme and the course load she would do the same again and feels nothing wrong with it; and (c) a student with a documented gambling addiction who let a friend run a small resale scheme through her meal card and has been in tears about it since.

I'd also like the procedure to come with one honest accounting of its costs: identify at least one of these cases where it excuses someone I'd personally want to hold responsible, or holds responsible someone I'd want to excuse, and tell me whether that discomfort is a defect to be patched or the whole point of taking Frankfurt seriously. And please be explicit about what the procedure cannot settle — I'm thinking especially of cases where we can't determine whether the student engineered the circumstance that removed their alternatives, and whether responsibility for setting up a no-choice situation should be handled by the same questions or by different ones entirely. If some piece of Frankfurt's framework doesn't survive translation into committee practice, say which piece and why, rather than pretending it all fits.
