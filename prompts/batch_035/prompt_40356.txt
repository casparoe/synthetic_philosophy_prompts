Last night my friend Mara and I spent three hours arguing about Taurek's coin-flip case — the one where you can rescue either a single person or a group of five, but not both — and I've come away with a half-formed idea I'd like to turn into a term-paper proposal (roughly 6,000 words, one semester). I'd like you to draft that proposal for me.

Please take on the role of a professional moral philosopher who has supervised many papers on aggregation, rescue cases, and fairness in risk-imposition, and who is practiced at converting a philosophical itch into a tractable project. Think carefully before you write, and feel free to open with some brief brainstorming — a few candidate framings and your reasons for choosing among them — before presenting the proposal itself.

To fix terms and tell you where the argument landed: Taurek famously said the numbers don't count and that flipping a coin between David and the five is permissible, since he refuses to judge the outcome "five saved" better than "one saved." His critics mostly attack head-on, arguing that the five is a better outcome, or that each of the five has a claim on me no weaker than David's, so David's claim is outweighed without any mysterious "aggregation." Mara, who is on Taurek's side, eventually abandoned the outcome-comparison language entirely and said something that struck me as either a dodge or the key to the whole thing: she said she isn't comparing outcomes at all, she is giving each of the six persons the same chance of rescue. Notice that the coin does this — under it, David's survival prospect is 1/2 and each of the five's is also 1/2 — whereas saving the five gives each of them a prospect of 1 and David 0. So maybe the real disagreement is not about whether numbers count but about where they are allowed to count: in the ex post evaluation of outcomes, or only in the ex ante claims of persons. My hypothesis is that reframing the debate along the ex ante/ex post line dissolves some standard objections to Taurek (for instance, the complaint that flipping a coin in the one-versus-one case is fine but reveals something monstrous in the one-versus-a-million case — on the ex ante view, the coin still gives every individual a 1/2 prospect there too) while generating new problems of its own (for instance, whether equal ex ante prospects are even a coherent ideal when the five live or die together, so their prospects are perfectly correlated; or whether a strict egalitarian about chances can explain why we may ever give the five a weighted lottery rather than a coin flip, or why we may save the five for certain once David consents to something).

The proposal should do the following: state the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; explain why the existing positions — straightforward aggregationism, Taurekian anti-aggregation, and the pairwise-claims view on which each person's claim counts but the group's somehow adds up — leave the question open; lay out the method, meaning what I would actually do chapter by chapter or month by month, including which cases and thought experiments would serve as tests; say what a positive result would look like and, just as important, what would count as the project failing; and give an honest assessment of whether this is genuinely doable in one semester or needs to be cut down. Please do not include citations or a bibliography; I will fill those in myself.
