I have followed the anthropic-reasoning wars across the rationalist blogosphere for well over a decade — the flare-ups on the forums, the recurring threads on Bostrom's *Anthropic Bias*, the semi-annual rediscovery of Gott's version by newcomers who think no one has thought of it before. In all that time, the two camps have mostly talked past each other: the doomsday affirmers charge their critics with ignoring observation selection effects, the critics reply that the affirmers are committing some amalgam of arrogance and equivocation, and almost nobody ever writes down, in wording the other side would actually sign, what is agreed, what is disputed, and what would change anyone's mind. What I want from you is the document an adversarial collaboration between these two camps should have produced years ago.

The two parties are:

(1) A defender of the Carter–Leslie doomsday argument, who accepts self-sampling reasoning over some reference class of observers and concludes that our relatively early birth rank is genuine Bayesian evidence that humanity's future contains far fewer observers than we would otherwise have predicted.

(2) A defender of the self-indication assumption (SIA), who holds that finding oneself to exist at all is evidence in favor of hypotheses posposing more observers, in exactly the quantity needed to cancel the doomsday shift, leaving our early birth rank with no doomsday import.

Draft their joint statement, with the following structure.

First, a numbered section of claims both camps can sign. I would expect it to include things like: that the Carter–Leslie inference is deductively valid conditional on self-sampling over a reference class that includes us — the dispute is about the inputs, not the calculus; that Gott's duration-based version is strictly weaker than the Carter–Leslie version and rests on far more fragile assumptions; that the argument, whichever way it comes out, has smaller practical implications for policy than either camp's popularizers have claimed; and that nothing in the argument depends on humanity being special, since the same logic applies to any reference class. But you should work out the full list yourself, and each claim must be non-vacuous — something a naive third party couldn't have signed without the collaboration.

Second, a disagreements table. Each row states one residual disagreement, with two columns: Camp 1's position and Camp 2's position, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording as an accurate statement of its view. No slanted formulations; no strawmen; no "Camp 2 merely asserts that..." constructions. If a disagreement is really a verbal dispute over the words "evidence" or "reference class," say so in the row rather than papering over it.

Third, a cruxes section. For each crux — the ones I'd guess at are the reference-class problem (whether choosing the reference class is a factual question or a modelling convention), the justifiability of SIA, and whether the argument's conclusion, if granted, would ever change what anyone should *do* — specify a concrete case or argument that both camps agree bears on that crux, and record each side's prediction about the case in advance, before any adjudication. To make this section do real work rather than recycle the classics, build it around these three cases:

(a) A "mild Presumptuous Philosopher" variant: two cosmological theories, empirically indistinguishable given current data, differ only in that one posits three trillion observers in the universe and the other two trillion. Not the usual trillion-to-one dramatics — a factor of 1.5. Each camp should record whether its position still requires an update here, and how large, and whether it accepts the update in this case while rejecting it in the standard dramatic version, or vice versa. This tests whether the SIA camp's commitment is to a principle or to a comfortable middle-sized conclusion.

(b) A sealed generation ship, launched centuries ago with a fixed complement and a fixed maximum observer-moment count determined by engineering constraints that no one aboard can alter. A shipboard inhabitant applies the Carter–Leslie reasoning to their birth rank among the ship's eventual observers. Both camps should record what happens: whether the doomsday logic gives different answers when the total is fixed by physics rather than by choices, and what this shows about whether the argument is really about *our* future or about observation-selection structure in general.

(c) The relocation question: whether SIA, once granted, merely defuses anthropic weirdness or relocates it — for instance, whether in cosmologies with very large or infinite observer counts, or with many extraterrestrial civilizations in the reference class, SIA generates its own counterintuitive conclusions that the SIA camp would then have to swallow. Each side should predict whether SIA's defusal of the doomsday argument is purchased at the price of other commitments elsewhere, and name one.

Fourth, a closing section on which parts of the original dispute the collaboration found to be verbal, which parts were genuine but currently undecidable, and what, if anything, both sides agree would actually move the debate — or a joint statement that nothing presently on the table would.

Throughout, keep the register of a jointly authored document rather than a debate transcript: third-person references to "the first camp" and "the second camp," agreed formulations in the shared sections, and side-specific formulations confined to the table and the predictions. The test of a good answer is that a knowledgeable member of either camp could read it and say: yes, that is what we hold, that is what they hold, and that is where we really differ.
