I help run a philosophy-of-biology reading group — eight to ten people, mostly longtime rationalist-adjacent folks who came in through LessWrong and have pre-existing opinions about teleology because of years of arguments about optimizers and selection — and we've spent the last month on the function literature: the selection-effect line (Wright, Millikan, Neander) versus the Cummins-style causal-role analysis. The group can recite the positions fine; what they can't do yet is feel how differently the two accounts carve the biological world, and how little the difference has to do with the textbook cases both sides agree on. I want an activity where the divergence shows up in what people argue about and what verdicts they're forced to commit to, not in anything I tell them at the end — they shouldn't hear the phrases "selected effect" or "causal role" as names of positions until the debrief.

Design a 90-minute session for this group. My rough sketch, which you should improve or replace: participants get a dossier on a fictional alien organism, with a set of carefully chosen traits — one clean heart-analog where both approaches should agree; one exaptation-like trait currently doing a job it plausibly wasn't selected for; one vestigial structure; one beneficial accident; and one trait whose evolutionary backstory I later "reveal" in a way that flips the selection-based verdict without changing anything about its present physiology. Half the group is mandated to assign functions strictly through selection history, the other half strictly through present contribution to system capacities — but neither mandate is framed as a philosophical stance; they should feel like two different jobs, say, an evolutionary historian reconstructing the lineage versus a systems engineer writing a maintenance manual for a biome that has to be kept running. Please write the actual trait dossiers, structure the reveals, and handle the awkward fact that real biologists never get selection history handed to them on a card.

Then a debrief that maps specific moments in the session onto the actual positions: where each team's verdicts tracked the Wright/Millikan story versus Cummins, what happened when a structure turned out to be damaged or malfunctioning (whose framework could even say that, and what did the other team end up saying instead), and whether the mid-session flip on the revealed history felt to participants like discovering a new fact about the organ or merely relabeling it. This crowd will also immediately push the analogy to machine learning — training objective versus emergent behavior, what a system was optimized for versus what it's now doing — so tell me whether that analogy is illuminating or a trap here, and how to use it in the debrief without letting it take over the room. Finally, be honest about what the activity structurally cannot reproduce — I'm thinking especially of the normativity of malfunction, and of the fact that real selection histories are inferences rather than disclosures — and give me your predictions for what will go wrong the first time I run it.
