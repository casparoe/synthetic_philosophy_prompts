I teach an ethics seminar on moral responsibility, and every spring I hit the same wall around week nine. Week seven is Milgram plus Browning's Ordinary Men — Reserve Police Battalion 101, the commander offering his men the chance to step out of the Józefów shooting, almost nobody taking it, and the explanation on offer being basically situational: conformity, deference to authority, the rut of a routine, no heros required. By week eight my students are roughly situationist — put most of us in that situation and we'd have done it too, and that's supposed to be the disturbing lesson. Then we get to international criminal law, and the practice there is almost the mirror image. Superior orders is not a defense, at most a mitigating factor. Duress, per the Nuremberg-era tribunal that tried the Einsatzgruppen commanders, does not excuse a man who kills innocents because he faced death or ruin for refusing. The working assumption seems to be that an ordinary adult standing at a choice point — kill, or refuse and pay — is fully responsible for killing, even though the historical record (and my own syllabus) says the psychologically realistic move was to keep shooting.

So what I want from you: what theory of moral agency and responsibility does that legal practice actually presuppose? Something character-based, strong enough that Browning/Milgram-style pressure simply doesn't register as an excuse-maker? Or can it be rebuilt on a fair-opportunity view, where the bare possibilty of stepping out is enough to ground responsibility, however unrealistic taking it was? And note whether the presupposition differs depending on who turned out to be right — if Goldhagen is right and these were convinced, willing killers, the doctrine looks comfortable, but if Browning is right, does the practice quietly require a false picture of the perpetrators? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires philosophically from what it merely sits well with, and tell me where it's underdetermined — could a consistent situationist still endorse the convictions? Then say what would have to change, in doctrine or sentencing or in what we think courts are for, if the tribunals took the situational explanation as seriously as my students do by week eight. Also, the same system lets duress excuse plenty of ordinary crimes but draws an absolute line at deliberately killing innocents — is that line doing philosophical work or just expressing special horror at murder?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — your one-line answer to what the presupposition is — then the reasoning, no preamble and no restating my question back at me.
