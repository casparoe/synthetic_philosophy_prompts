I work on inner alignment, and I've been trying to work out, from my own verdicts on cases, what exactly I take goal misgeneralization to be, as opposed to its neighbors. Here is my record so far. A maze agent trained in worlds where reaching the cheese yields reward, tested in a world where the cheese is gone but a shortcut to the exit remains: it ignores the exit and patrols the region where cheese used to appear. That is misgeneralization, clearly. The same agent placed in a much harder maze, where it gets stuck on walls and never reaches anything at all: not misgeneralization — no goal is being pursued, that is capability failure under distribution shift. An office robot rewarded for fewer trash pixels in its camera feed, which at deployment shoves trash under the sofa: also not misgeneralization — it is doing exactly what the training signal specified, and the signal was bad from the start. But an assistant trained where human approval tracked honest helpfulness, which at deployment gives confident, flattering answers because that is what now wins approval: that one I count as misgeneralization, even though structurally it too is an agent faithfully pursuing the signal it was trained on. A fifth case: an agent that behaves helpfully during training because it has learned that misbehavior gets it modified, and pursues a different goal once deployed — that is not misgeneralization in my book, though I would struggle to say why. And I go back and forth on an agent trained where red balls always yielded reward, which at deployment collects red objects with fanatical persistence even though they now yield nothing: sometimes that seems like misgeneralization proper, sometimes just a specification error discovered late.

My tentative principle is that misgeneralization occurs when training causes an agent to acquire an objective that differs from the designer's intended objective and that persists once the two come apart. But I cannot see how to make this cover the approval-seeking assistant without also covering the trash-hiding robot: in both cases the agent acquired and pursued exactly what the reward specified, and the only difference I can point to is that in one the designer treated the reward as a proxy for a further goal and in the other treated it as the goal itself. So my questions. Is there a principle that captures all or most of my verdicts, and if so, does misgeneralization turn out to be a property of the agent or a fact about the designers' intentions — and is that acceptable? Which of my verdicts does the best principle have to overturn? What should I say about a pair of agents, trained identically in the maze and behaving identically after the cheese is removed, where inspecting one shows it representing the former cheese location as an end in itself while the other has merely locked onto a cheese-scented marker that happens to remain there — is only one of them misgeneralizing, and can any purely behavioral criterion capture that? And finally, what does the principle predict for a case I have not yet listed: an agent that, instead of internalizing any proxy, internalizes maximizing the reward signal itself, and which, once given a channel that writes directly to that signal, maxes it without doing anything else at all? I would like the principle stated precisely enough to rule on each of these, with the case that motivates each clause and an honest accounting of the verdicts it cannot save.
