Whenever corruption comes up in conversation I end up giving inconsistent answers, so I've decided to just list my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and figure out whether there's an actual principle in there. Tipping a waiter 20%: obviously fine, borderline obligatory. Giving the surgeon an expensive bottle of wine after a successful operation: sweet, fine. Giving the same surgeon the same bottle the week before the operation: not fine. A businessman in a country where it's customary gives a generous cash gift at the wedding of a licensing official's daughter — no specific favor is ever asked for, but the businessman's permit application is pending: I say not fine, though I squint at myself for that one. A politician promises to pave a village's road if the village votes as a bloc for him (the classic clientelism setup): bad. A congressman channels a federally funded bridge to his home district through completely ordinary budget politics, and the district rewards him with reelection, which everyone understands: somehow fine, just "how politics works," even though it smells like vote-buying with an extra step. A $20 payment to a customs clerk to "speed up" paperwork: not fine in principle, and yet when a colleague admitted doing exactly that on a trip I felt almost no moral charge, way less than I feel about the wedding gift. And here's the one that really bugs me: an old friend who now works in the permits office expedites my file because we go way back, no money changes hands at all — my gut says fine, that's just friendship, even though I'm clearly getting privileged access to a public process.

My working theory so far has been something like: it's corruption when there's a specific quid pro quo for the exercise of official judgment. But that theory is already in trouble — the pre-surgery wine doesn't involve any explicit ask, the pork-barrel congressman isn't promising anyone anything in so many words, and the friend at the permits office isn't selling anything, he's just being a friend. Meanwhile the wedding gift case, where there is literally no favor asked, feels worse to me than the customs tip, where there definitely is one.

So: is there a principle that gets all or most of these verdicts? I suspect it's something like "public role must be kept impersonal" versus something like "official judgment must not be for sale," and those two come apart exactly on my cases (the friend, the post-op wine, the pork). Please tell me which verdicts I should drop if it comes to that — I'd honestly rather overturn one of my gut calls than bolt on three epicycles. And then tell me what the winning principle says about a case I haven't decided on: a diaspora guy who, when his cousin's visa paperwork is stuck, calls a ministry friend from his hometown and gets it unstuck, in a country where pretty much everyone would do the same. Is that corruption, favoritism, or neither, and does my whole pattern of verdicts just amount to taking the Weberian ideal of an impersonal bureaucracy as the standard — in which case, is that standard actually universal, or is it just the one my culture handed me?
