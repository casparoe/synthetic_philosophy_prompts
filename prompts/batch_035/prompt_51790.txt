I'm presenting to my department's moral philosophy seminar a paper defending a threshold version of negative utilitarianism — not the strict view that only suffering counts, but a lexical priority view on which (1) any amount of severe suffering outweighs any amount of positive experience, however large; (2) below the severity threshold, positive welfare trades off against mild suffering in roughly the ordinary utilitarian way; and (3) preventing severe suffering takes strict precedence over creating new happy lives. My core argument is that once you accept any person-affecting constraint on creating happy people, lexical priority for severe suffering is the least ad hoc way to honor the intuition that no one should be tortured for others' bliss, and that it is better to accept a partially ordered axiology than to swallow either the repugnant conclusion or the sadistic conclusion.

The audience is a seminar of about fifteen. Most are consequentialists or near-consequentialists, but the composition matters: one committed classical hedonistic utilitarian who takes the total view seriously and regards aggregation as a feature rather than a bug; one population ethicist who works directly on the repugnant conclusion literature and will know every move in advance; two Kantians who think all aggregative views miss the separateness of persons; and several graduate students sympathetic to suffering-focused ethics who nonetheless suspect the position is unstable under pressure. Everyone accepts that the moral status of future people is a live question, so I can't simply wave at the non-identity problem and move on.

Prepare my Q&A sheet accordingly: the seven or eight objections I should actually expect from this particular room, ordered by how much damage each would do to the paper, each phrased the way that kind of objector would actually put it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Make sure the sheet covers at least the following, since I can see them coming: the internal tension between my two clauses (the view appears to imply that adding severe suffering to a life already above the threshold costs nothing further, and that relieving one person just below the threshold always beats relieving a million people just above it); the reductio in which my view is forced to trade away astronomical amounts of mild suffering for a vanishingly small reduction in severe suffering; and the Kantian charge that my lexical threshold is just the separateness of persons smuggled back in through a side door, which would collapse my view into something non-consequentialist in all but name. Tell me which objection I should concede ground on early to buy credibility, and end with the question I am hoping nobody asks.
