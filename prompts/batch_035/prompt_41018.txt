I am grading a midterm examination for an upper-level undergraduate course entitled "Philosophy of Space and Time," and I would like a careful, independent evaluation of one student's answer before I assign it a grade.

The exam question read as follows:

"Newton's bucket experiment is often said to deal relationism about space a decisive blow. Reconstruct Newton's argument from the bucket as carefully as you can, making clear exactly which feature of the case does the argumentative work. Then present the strongest available relationist reply, whether Leibnizian or Machian, and assess whether it succeeds in blunting Newton's conclusion."

The student's answer reads as follows:

"Newton's bucket experiment appears in the Scholium at the beginning of the Principia. A bucket of water hangs from a twisted cord; when the cord is released, the bucket spins. At first the water remains flat while the bucket rotates around it; then friction gradually carries the water around with the bucket, and the water's surface becomes concave, climbing up the sides. Newton's point is that the curvature of the surface is a genuine dynamical effect indicating true rotation, and crucially, at the moment the surface is most curved, the water is nearly at rest relative to the bucket. So the deformation cannot be explained by any relative motion between water and bucket, and the rotation must be with respect to something else, namely absolute space. In this way the experiment gives us something like a direct perception of space itself acting on the water.

Leibniz replied that absolute space cannot exist. His identity of indiscernibles says that two things sharing all their properties are one thing, and since absolute space is perfectly homogeneous, a universe shifted three feet to the left would share all its properties with the actual universe and so would be the same universe. Moreover, God would need a sufficient reason to place the universe here rather than three feet over, and by the principle of sufficient reason there is no such reason; so there is no fact of the matter about where in absolute space the universe sits.

The strongest relationist reply came later from Mach: the water rotates not with respect to absolute space but with respect to the distant masses, the fixed stars. Rotation is perfectly real on this view, but it is rotation relative to other matter rather than to space itself. Mach's proposal was vindicated by Einstein's special theory of relativity, which showed that all motion, rotation included, is relative, and that no frame of reference is privileged over any other.

Finally, consider a universe containing only the bucket. The relationist must predict that the water's surface stays flat, whereas Newton's view predicts it curves. Since we can never run that experiment, relationism comes out unfalsifiable, and an unfalsifiable theory is a false theory. On balance, then, Newton wins the exchange."
