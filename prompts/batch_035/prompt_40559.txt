I'm preparing a seminar session on self-deception for advanced undergraduates, and rather than walking through the usual authors in sequence, I want a decision tree that sorts the positions on whether self-deception is even possible. The driving puzzle, as I'll present it, is that self-deception seems to require one and the same person to be both the deceiver (who must in some sense know the truth, since he's concealing it) and the deceived (who must not know it, since he's taken in). Every position on the topic is, at bottom, a way of denying one premise of that argument, so I'd like the tree built from the logical space first and the named positions located in it afterward.

The branch nodes should be questions a student could answer without knowing which position they lead to, each corresponding to a premise someone might deny. I'd expect nodes along the following lines, though you should reorganize as the logic requires: Is the self-deceived state genuine belief, or something else (an avowal one makes without believing, a pretense, an unexamined refusal to consider)? Does the agent have the true belief all along, or only at an earlier time, or never? Is there any intention to bring about the false state, and if so, is it a prior intention or the product of sub-intentional habits (selective attention, biased testing of hypotheses)? The leaves should then include: the deflationary view on which so-called self-deception is just ordinary motivationally biased belief-formation, with no paradox because no one is playing deceiver at all; the partitioning view on which the agent somehow holds both the true and the false belief, each insulated from the other; the view on which the agent never actually believes the comforting proposition but only avows it, in something like the Sartrean register of bad faith; and the outright skeptic who holds that self-deception proper never occurs — every real case is either ordinary deception by others, wishful thinking, or open pretense. Name the leaves after known defenders where they exist and mark unoccupied combinations of answers "unnamed," with one sentence diagnosing why nobody sits there (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored).

To keep the sorting diagnostic rather than verbal, each node should be tested against cases where the positions genuinely come apart, for instance: the partner who never once looks at the texts, versus the one who reads them and concludes they mean nothing, versus the one who declares "I know perfectly well what's going on" and proceeds as if he didn't. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate, and if deflationism and skeptical wishful-thinking collapse into the same leaf, say so explicitly rather than hiding it in the wording.

Finally, a request about the register of the answer: as you go, indicate how confident you are in each substantive claim, and at the end single out the point in the tree you are least sure of — I'd rather know where the structure is doing speculative work than receive it as settled cartography.
