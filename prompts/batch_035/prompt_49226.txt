I am a retired evolutionary biologist — some four decades spent in behavioural ecology and population genetics before I retired — and I have lately been reading philosophy of technology as a hobby, if one can call reading Langdon Winner and the social-construction literature a hobby. I have taken a particular interest in Winner's claim, from his 1980 essay on whether artifacts have politics, that technical things can carry political properties, together with the standard reply from the social construction of technology camp: that artifacts are what relevant social groups make of them, that their designs show interpretive flexibility, and that what looks like a politically charged object is really the residue of a process of closure among competing social groups.

Here is my request. As an evolutionary biologist, the natural way for me to understand Winner's thesis is in terms I already command: extended phenotypes, niche construction, exaptation, selection pressures, gene–culture coevolution, and the like. So I want you to restate the debate in that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, and then report to me the residue. Concretely: translate Winner's two kinds of case — the artifact deliberately arranged to settle a community's affairs, of which his discussion of Robert Moses's low bridges over the parkways is the canonical instance, and the large technical system that is compatible only with certain patterns of political relations, for which his account of nuclear power is the standard example — into the vocabulary of niche construction and the extended phenotype, treating a low bridge, if you will, as part of the extended phenotype of those who commissioned it and as an element of a constructed niche that differentially constrains the behaviour of others. Then translate the constructivist reply in the same way: interpretive flexibility as the claim that the artefact has no fitness effect of its own until a social group has fixed its meaning; closure as stabilising selection of a design; the relevant social groups as the populations on which the artefact's selective pressures operate. Where the translation is strained, say so; do not quietly repair it with biological assumptions and present the repair as translation.

After the translation, I want the residue report, which I regard as the real point of the exercise. Please tell me, first, which of Winner's claims survive the translation cleanly; second, which come across only with distortion, and what exactly is distorted; and third, which have no expression at all in my vocabulary. I have a hypothesis about the third category, which I offer as no more than a hypothesis: that what will not translate is precisely the political part — power in the sense of some agents systematically prevailing over others, the normative question of whether that prevailing is legitimate, and Winner's insistence that societies can choose and are responsible for what they choose. Selection in my vocabulary is blind; it has no notion of a choice that could have gone otherwise, or of a design that ought to be regretted. So my suspicion is that the residue consists entirely of things requiring that notion, and that the evolutionary translation will quietly convert Winner's political claim into a causal claim that is true but weaker and no longer about politics. If that is right, I would like to know whether the residue reflects a blindness in my vocabulary — something biology cannot say — or a softness in Winner's thesis, which perhaps was never as clear as its admirers suppose. And please write your answer in British English throughout; I was a postdoc in Edinburgh long enough for it to have stuck.
