I'm a science and ideas journalist working on a feature about those who believe humanity's far future could hold overwhelming value — trillions of potential lives, interstellar civilization, and so on — and about the critics who think this way of thinking has gotten out of hand. At a recent book event, the author of a forthcoming volume on digital futures gave me his summary of Nick Bostrom's simulation argument, which I noted down roughly as follows: "Bostrom showed that we're almost certainly living in a computer simulation. Once a civilization develops the computing power to simulate its ancestors, it would run huge numbers of these simulations, so simulated minds would vastly outnumber real ones, and by pure odds we should assume we're among them. And this has a practical upshot: simulations don't run forever — they get shut down, or their resources run out — so our civilization almost certainly has no long future. All this talk about safeguarding humanity's descendants for millions of years is aimed at a mirage."

The confidence of the first sentence didn't sit right with me — my recollection is that Bostrom's original paper is considerably more hedged than that — but I don't have the philosophical training to say precisely where this rendering goes astray, and I need to decide whether to quote the author's account in my piece, push back on it, or leave it out. Please take the summary apart piece by piece. What would a philosopher familiar with the argument accept as stated? Where does it conflate claims that the argument itself keeps distinct — for instance, between what is demonstrated outright and what follows only if certain empirical assumptions about future civilizations hold? Is anything simply false? Then evaluate the practical inference: granting whatever is correct in the summary, does the simulation argument support the conclusion that long-term thinking about humanity's future is misguided? Finally, a colleague suggested to me that the summary may have the stakes exactly backwards — that even a small probability of being in a simulation could, on the kind of expected-value reasoning these long-termists use, make the far future matter more rather than less. Is that a serious point, or a rhetorical flourish? Since I want to be fair to the author in print, please distinguish carefully between claims he could reasonably defend as a compressed popularization and claims that misreport the argument.
