hey, so I'm on the grants committee of a family foundation that gives out around $20M a year and has been quietly running on effective-altruist logic without ever saying the words. our track record the last few years: we funded mass deworming in east africa even after our evaluator flagged the income-effect evidence as shaky, since it was cheap, measurable, and RCT-backed; we rejected a proposal for long-shot work on improving how a government ministry makes decisions, explicitly on the grounds that its impact couldn't be quantified; we funded an earning-to-give fellowship that pays recent grads to take high-paying finance jobs and pledge 10%, reasoning that each fellow routes more money to effective charities than the fellowship costs; and we turned down general operating support for a well-run local food bank because local direct giving isn't "neglected."

now in front of us: a five-year unrestricted grant to a tiny org doing undercover investigations of factory farms. theory of change is that exposés slowly shift corporate practice and regulation, over decades -- no RCT, no lives-per-dollar figure, but if it works it redirects orders of magnitude more money than we could ever move directly, and the org argues our measurability obsession is exactly why nobody funds this stuff. we vote in two weeks and the family patriarch, who loves the deworming grants, is skeptical.

draft the memo I can circulate beforehand: a concrete recommendation (fund, reject, or fund with conditions, with a number and time frame), which of our own precedents it honors and which it quietly abandons, and a fair shake at the strongest argument on the losing side rather than a strawman. bonus points if you can tell me what principle we've actually been running all these years, because "fund whatever's measurable" and "fund whatever grows the total pool of effective giving" fit our past decisions differently and they split exactly on this one.
