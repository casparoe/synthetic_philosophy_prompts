I've been reading up on the old memetics thing and I keep noticing a weird pattern: the word "meme" won (it's in everyone's mouth, it's how people talk about internet virality) but the actual research program basically died. The Journal of Memetics folded in 2005, Susan Blackmore moved on to consciousness stuff, and meanwhile cultural evolution as a field kept going fine through other routes (Boyd and Richerson style population models, cultural phylogenetics of languages and artifacts, that literature is alive and well). So the fight over whether culture is Darwinian in the strict sense -- discrete units, high-fidelity copying, selection accumulating adaptations -- kind of just... petered out without anyone conceding.

Here's roughly how I understand the two camps, tell me if I'm off. The memetics side (Dawkins originally, Dennett as the cheerful defender, Blackmore) said the analogy is doing real work: if ideas reproduce with variation and differential survival, you get the logic of natural selection for free, and "meme" names the replicator. The critics, Sperber especially, said culture mostly isn't copied at all -- it's reconstructed by each learner from their own priors, so transmission is more like an epidemiological process with attractors (versions of a rumor or a recipe that everyone converges on regardless of what the previous person actually said) than like inheritance. And then there was the third-position crowd (Boyd, Richerson, and heirs) who basically said: who cares about strict replicators, you can model cultural change with population dynamics anyway and get testable models, which the memeticists never produced.

My reading group is stuck on whether this stalled because it was won, lost, or never really a substantive fight. One guy insists it was won quietly: the population-modelers got the explanatory goods, the "replicator" framing was just a metaphor that got you in the door, and internet memes don't rescue the theory because they're about attention, not inheritance. Another thinks it was a definitional dispute wearing a scientific costume -- the two camps meant different things by "copying" and "selection" the whole time, so their arguments never actually met. My own half-formed theory is that it's a case where the disagreement was really about what needs explaining: memeticists wanted to explain *why some content spreads* and their opponents thought that was a shallow question, the real explanandum being *why cultural content has the stable structure it does*, and you can't settle a fight about the explanandum with evidence.

So: diagnose this for me. Is the memetics dispute substantive, verbal, or two different research questions under one label? What did each side actually claim that the other denied? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here -- better formal models, digital trace data (like, actual transmission chains on social media where you can see the copying errors), a sharper concept of fidelity, or is the honest answer that the question dissolved and nobody should expect it to move? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of who wrote what.
