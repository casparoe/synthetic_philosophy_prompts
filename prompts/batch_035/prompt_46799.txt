I teach a final-year undergraduate module on the philosophy of mind, and next term I want to replace a rather tired seminar question on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness with a written assignment that forces genuine philosophical engagement rather than textbook regurgitation. Please write the assignment brief below as a prompt for your answer — no, rather, please carry out the task described in the assignment brief that follows.

Write a philosophical dialogue, of roughly 1,800 to 2,500 words, between two characters: a committed illusionist about phenomenal consciousness, and a defender of the reality of phenomenal properties who is nonetheless a naturalist — someone who thinks the hard problem is a real problem about the world, not a problem about our concepts. Give them names if you wish. They should be equally competent, equally charitable, and neither may be allowed to win by mere stubbornness, rhetorical flourish, or the other party's exhaustion. The illusionist must not be a vulgar behaviourist who denies mental life altogether, and the naturalist must not be a mysterian who shrugs at the explanatory gap; each should hold the strongest reasonable version of their view.

Structure the dialogue around a thought experiment I have constructed for the module, which I shall call the stabilised Necker cube protocol. It runs as follows. A subject wears a contact-lens-mounted apparatus (of the kind used in real retinal-stabilisation studies) so that the retinal image of a Necker cube is fixed at a constant position relative to the receptors — under ordinary conditions this tends to extinguish perceptual alternation, since the stimulus no longer moves across the retina. In our hypothetical version, however, the subject continues to report, in confidence and in earnest, that the cube is flipping between its two orientations, and their confidence in each report is high. But their forced-choice discrimination performance is at chance: when asked, at a randomly chosen moment, which orientation was perceptually dominant one second earlier, they cannot do better than guessing. Later, shown the timestamped record of their own button presses, the subjects sincerely revise their claims about what they experienced, insisting that the cube had been stable all along and that their reports of flipping were a kind of pressure they felt to say something.

Both characters in your dialogue must claim this case as evidence for their own position, and each must give the other a genuinely difficult reply. The illusionist will presumably want to say the case shows that what we call phenomenal character is a construction of introspective report, unmoored from discriminative access. The naturalist will presumably want to resist — perhaps by distinguishing between the content of experience and the subject's access to it, perhaps by arguing that the case is a case of post-hoc revision of judgement rather than of experience, perhaps some other line. Neither is allowed simply to stipulate what the subject's experience "really" was; the case must be treated as underdetermined in exactly the way the two positions disagree about.

At some point in the dialogue, the two characters should also address the meta-problem — the question of why the existence of phenomenal consciousness seems so obvious to so many thoughtful people — and disagree about whether a complete explanation of why we believe in phenomenal properties would be an explanation of the properties themselves or of nothing at all. Finally, the dialogue should end not with conversion but with both characters able to state, precisely and fairly, exactly which single consideration they take to be the strongest reason for refusing to move, and why the other's reply to it does not dissolve it.

A few formal requirements. Write in British English throughout (behaviour, colour, analyse, judgement, programme where appropriate). Avoid straw-manning either position; avoid historical name-dropping as a substitute for argument — references to actual philosophers are permitted sparingly, but each character's arguments should stand on their own formulation. Do not insert a narrator who adjudicates between the speakers. The prose should read as a real conversation between working philosophers: interruptions, concessions, reformulations, and at least one moment where a character genuinely strengthens the other's argument before answering it.

Please write the dialogue now.
