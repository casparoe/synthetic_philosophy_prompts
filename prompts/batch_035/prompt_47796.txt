A friend of mine in a philosophy PhD program tried to explain Jeffrey conditioning to me after I asked him what you're supposed to do when your evidence itself is uncertain. He said it's basically the fallback for when you can't do real Bayesian updating: you can't quite tell whether it's raining out, so you just nudge your credence in rain up a bit and rescale everything else proportionally. In his words, it's a hack, and a pretty empty one -- because nothing constrains how you nudge, any credence shift at all can get dressed up as an "experience," so the Bayesian can never actually be shown wrong and the theory loses all normative content. He also said there's some famous rat example where the rat keeps getting the same uncertain signal over and over and the updates compound until the rat ends up certain either way, which he says everyone agrees is irrational, so the rule can't model real learning at all. And he finished by saying Jeffrey himself basially conceded all this and moved on to other things.

He's usually pretty carefull and he knows his probability theory, so I'm inclined to trust him, but it felt a bit too tidy. Could you go through it claim by claim? Which parts would a defender of Jeffrey's view accept, which parts are subtly off (I have a vague memory that some rigidity assumption does real work here, but I don't know what), and does his bottom line -- that Jeffrey conditioning is a dead end patch with no content -- actually follow from the parts he got right? If some of his complaints apply to a version of the rule that its defenders don't actually endorse, I'd want to know which ones and why.
