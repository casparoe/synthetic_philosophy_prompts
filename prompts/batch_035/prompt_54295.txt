There's a whole family of practices where we deliberately blind the judge: orchestras hold auditions behind a screen, they even put carpet down on the stage so the committee can't hear heels click and guess the candidate is a woman, and nobody is allowed to chat with the performers between pieces. Essay graders cover the names. Wine judges get black glasses. Journals do double-blind review. I'm trying to figure out what theory of the mind this family of practices presupposes, and I keep going back and forth on how strong the implicit claim is.

On its face it presupposes that knowing things about a person changes how you evaluate them. Fine. But is it committing to actual cognitive penetration -- believing the violinist is a woman literally changes how the sound sounds to you, so the experience itself is off -- or just the weaker claim that you hear it the same and your downstream judgment gets skewed? It seems like the two readings call for different fixes. If it's only downstream bias, you could in principle just debias the judge, or use a rigid scoring rubric, and skip the screen entirely. The carpet only makes sense if the contamination is in the perceptual input itself, or at least can't be compartmentalized by an act of will. So maybe the practice already presupposes the strong reading? Or maybe it just doesn't care and is only presupposing something like "whatever the mechanism, you can't fix it by trying harder."

There's also a consistency question. Courts point in both directions at once. Eyewitnesses get to look straight at the defendant in court, and courts happily admit expert testimony about the cross-race effect, i.e. that perception itself discriminates faces worse across race, while still treating "I saw it with my own eyes" as paradigm evidence. So the law seems ok with perception being expectation-shaped in one breath and a neutral channel in the next. Does the blinding family of practices actually sit in tension with that, or is there a principled line (say: penetrate the perceiver's own priors vs. exploit a general feature of everyone's vision) that makes both coherent?

And one more wrinkle: does blind auditioning presuppose an aesthetic thesis, that the merit of a performance supervenes on the acoustic signal alone? Lots of people would say knowing a phrase quotes an earlier movement, or that the soloist is playing through an injury, is part of hearing the music at all, not bias to be screened out.

So basically: reconstruct the implicit theory of perception these practices need, tell me whether they presuppose penetration proper or whether impenetrability would do just as well, say whether the practice is consistent with how we treat eyewitnesses, and what would have to change in the practices themselves if we decided perception really was penetrable all the way down.
