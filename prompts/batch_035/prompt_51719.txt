My cousin, who is in seminary, and I have been arguing for months about Molinism — the view, going back to Luis de Molina, that God has "middle knowledge": knowledge, logically prior to any decree to create, of what every possible creature would freely do in every possible circumstance. On this picture God does not determine our choices; rather, surveying the counterfactuals, He decides which circumstances to actualize, and thereby steers history while leaving our wills free. My cousin is convinced this is the only account that reconciles strong providence with libertarian free will, and his credence that Molinism is true is about 0.6. Mine is about 0.15, mostly because of what I gather is called the grounding objection — I can't see what makes the counterfactuals of freedom true, or how God could know them without in some sense determining them. But I've noticed our arguments keep passing each other by, and I'd like a structured outside view.

Please give me your own credence that Molinism is true of the actual world — which folds in whether theism is true at all, so treat that as one of your terms — and then decompose the number. At a minimum I want a probability and a one-line justification for each of the following: (1) that theism is true; (2) that libertarian free will is actual, i.e., that at least some human choices are not determined by prior states; (3) that there are determinate truths of the form "S would freely do A in circumstances C" even when S is never in fact in C — that the "woulds" have truth values rather than being indeterminate; (4) that if such truths exist, they are pre-volitional, available to God prior to and independently of any divine decision, rather than made true by God's will (which would seem to collapse the whole thing into compatibilism); and (5) that middle knowledge is genuinely needed for the providence and theodicy work my cousin wants it to do, rather than simple foreknowledge or an open-theist picture doing nearly as well. Then show how you combine the numbers, tell me which term the final credence is most sensitive to, and say whether my cousin at 0.6 or I at 0.15 most likely locate our disagreement in the wrong place — i.e., which sub-question our disagreement most plausibly reduces to. If you think a sub-question is missing from my list (for instance, whether "would" counterfactuals are needed at all, as opposed to only "might" counterfactuals, which some defenders of the view retreat to), add it with your own number rather than ignoring it.

Please answer in American English, and don't hedge into "it depends on your theology" — give actual numbers, even if rough, and let the decomposition carry the argument.
