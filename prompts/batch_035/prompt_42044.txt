I'm taking a graduate seminar on philosophy of social science, and our unit on explanation by values has me genuinely stuck. The occasion is Weber's Protestant ethic thesis: roughly, the claim that certain Calvinist doctrines — predestination above all — produced such anxiety about salvation that believers threw themselves into disciplined, methodical labor and accumulation as a way of assuring themselves of their election, and that this religiously-formed disposition helped bring about modern capitalism where ordinary economic motives could not. The seminar keeps circling a deeper question, though: whether an explanation of this shape is a causal explanation at all, and I'd like a written essay that takes a stand rather than surveying positions.

Here is the core puzzle I want the essay to confront. When Weber says Calvinist values caused capitalist behavior, something must do the work of connecting the value to the action: the values have to select the behavior. Yet the natural candidates for that connection are themselves interpretive or normative — the believer treats worldly success as evidence of election because the doctrine means something to him — and it's unclear how a meaning becomes a cause. A regress also looms: if values caused the behavior, we ought to be able to say what gave the values their motivational force, and then what gave that its force, and so on. The essay should reconstruct this problem — sometimes called the problem of normative selectivity — on its own terms rather than name-dropping it, and it should weigh at least three complications. First, the objection that wherever religious affiliation is held instrumentally — congregational membership worn as a credential for respectability and creditworthiness, belief kept up because it was good for business — the explanation quietly shifts from values to interests, and the cultural element does no causal work. Second, the thought that a complete causal account of why people held the values in the first place might substitute for the value-explanation rather than support it. Third, the reverse worry: if values are treated as sui generis causes, we lose the ability to explain why the same doctrine appears to have fostered accumulation-oriented behavior in some communities and not in others.

Some guidance on the conclusion. The essay must not end by splitting the difference into "both culture and structure matter." Defend a definite verdict — for instance, that explanation by values is genuinely causal only under a specific condition, and say what that condition is; or that it is structural explanation in disguise; or that it is a real third kind of explanation that our concept of cause should be widened to accommodate — and then show what your verdict implies for the general question of whether culture can be a cause, using the Protestant ethic case as the test. Please argue the issue itself rather than giving a biography of Weber or a literature review of who said what when, and no bibliography or citations are needed.
