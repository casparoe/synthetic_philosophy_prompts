I teach a course on ethics and international security, and for the last few years my two TAs have had the same running fight every time the nuclear deterrence unit comes around. One of them is basically a Waltzian: nuclear weapons have made major war between armed states obsolete, the balance of terror is stable precisely because both sides can retaliate after being struck, and even messy new nuclear states tend to become more cautious, not less. The other is a Saganite pessimist: deterrence looks fine in a rational-actor model, but real arsenals are run by organizations with biases, parochial interests, and normal-accident failure modes, and the actual historical record is mostly a list of near misses we survived through luck -- false alarms in early warning systems, bombs lost in crashes, exercises that the other side could plausibly have misread as the real thing. They argue past each other constantly. The optimist treats every near miss as evidence the system worked, since after all nothing happened; the pessimist treats the very same events as evidence we're one bad day from catastrophe; and each accuses the other of begging the question with their read of the same history.

For our course reader I want the document they should have produced instead: the joint statement from an adversarial collaboration between the two of them. Give me the claims both could actually sign (about second-strike logic, about what near misses do and don't show, about proliferation, whatever), then the precise disagreements, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording. Then the cruxes. I suspect one crux is the luck question: what, if anything, could count as evidence that we survived by luck rather than by design, given that we only get to observe worlds where nothing went wrong? Another might be whether rational deterrence is a description of how states behave or an aspiration that a few states roughly meet and nobody reliably does. And there's a third thing lurking that they never quite name: whether the optimist's "stability" and the pessimist's "safety" even denote the same property, given the stability-instability paradox -- maybe very stable deterrence at the top makes limited conflicts at the bottom more likely, which each of them should have an opinion about.

For each crux, write down a concrete case, argument, or piece of evidence both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. They actually cooked one up over beers last semester that you could use: imagine an independent commission got full access to one nuclear state's command-and-control records for a 30-year period and scored every serious incident as absorbed by design (procedures, redundancy, fail-safe design caught it) versus absorbed by luck (the wrong person happened to be on shift, the phone line happened to be working, the other side happened to interpret the signal charitably). Ask what each TA would predict the audit would find, and whether either of them would concede anything if it came out the other way. If part of their dispute turns out to be verbal rather than substantive, say which part and why. Format: numbered agreed-claims section first, then a disagreements table. Don't try to referee who's right -- I want a document both of them could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact, even though I suspect after reading it that one of them is wrong.
