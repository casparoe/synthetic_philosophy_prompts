Here's the practice I'm puzzled by. I've been living in Germany for a few years and the way church membership works here is strange from an outsider's perspective. If you were baptized as a baby — Catholic or Protestant — your on the church's rolls, and the state deducts a church tax (roughly 9% of your income tax) straight from your paycheck and hands it over to your church, minus a small administrative cut for the trouble. The only way out is the formal "church exit": you go to a government office (a court or registry office, depending on the state), pay a small fee, and file a declaration that you're leaving. The state, not the church, processes your exit. The church then says that having filed this declaration you're no longer eligible for certain sacraments (the Catholics take this seriously; the Protestants are more relaxed about it). And meanwhile religion is taught in public schools as a normal graded subject, the big churches are recognized as corporations under public law, and the state routes vast amounts of welfare funding through church-run hospitals and charities.

So here's my question: what conception of religious membership does this whole arrangement presuppose? On one side, church membership is treated like a voluntary club affiliation — you can quit it at a government desk, like cancelling a subscription, and while you're in the club the state enforces its dues for it. But you never joined voluntarily in the first place, since it all starts with infant baptism. On the other side, the state treats these "clubs" as public institutions — a subject on the school curriculum, semi-public welfare providers, bearers of public legal status. Can a single coherent picture of what a church is do all this work at once, or does the system quietly switch between several incompatible pictures depending on which part of the machine you're looking at? I also keep going back and forth on one detail: the exit is filed with the state but has spiritual consequences according to the church's own rules. Whose declaration is it, legally, and whose is it spiritually, and does the system even care about the difference?
