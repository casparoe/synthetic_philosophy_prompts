I'm a retired entomologist (38 years, mostly on bee navigation, if you can believe it), and since retiring I've gotten properly hooked on philosophy of mind, which I dodged my whole career. So now I have a running argument with my old colleague Frank, and I want you to predict how he'd rule on some new cases, because I think I can see the shape of his rule but I can't pin it down.

Frank's verdicts so far, delivered over beers over the last couple of years: chimps, pigs, ravens — obviously yes, he gets impatient even being asked. Octopus — yes, and he's genuinely moved by them. Fish — yes, after some grumbling. Lobsters and crabs — a grudging "alright, probably something it's like to be them," which surprised me, and which he justifies with the wound-tending stuff: crabs guarding an injured claw, hermit crabs weighing a shock against the quality of a shell, that whole literature. Bees and ants — no. Flat no. Nematodes — don't be ridiculous. Jellyfish — nothing there, he says, no matter what.

Here's the thing about the bee verdict: this man taught me that a bee can count landmarks, learn concepts like same-versus-different, and do flexible problem-solving that would embarrass some birds. He calls bees the most impressive machines in the animal kingdom, and I've started to suspect that word "machine" is doing all the work for him. When I brought him the bee findings that impressed me after I retired — the one where agitated bees start treating ambiguous cues as bad news, like a stressed rat does, and the one where a sip of sugar makes bees more optimistic about an ambiguous cue unless you block their dopamine — he just waved it off as readouts of internal states, not feelings. A thermostat with a bigger library, he said.

And here's my own confession, which is really why I'm writing. The numbers have gotten to me. A few years back somebody finally did the accounting: something like 20 quadrillion ants alive at any one moment, conservatively, plus everything else with six legs, and if you assign even a small probability that any of it feels like something, the expected amount of suffering out there makes everything on farms look like a rounding error. I used to think that was a philosopher's parlor trick. Now I spray my roses for aphids with my heart in my throat, and Frank thinks I've gone soft. He says the numbers don't matter if the probability is basically zero. But Frank is not consistent about this either! He swats the mosquito on his arm without blinking and sprays his own roses, but he paid a service to relocate a raccoon family out of his attic rather than trap them. So something is operating in there about numbers times probability that isn't pure probability-zero-ism, and I want to know what it is.

So, the new cases I want your predictions on:

First, the study where lab bumblebees were given little wooden balls and rolled them around over and over, with no reward attached, going out of their way to do it — the younger bees played more, both sexes played, it checks the boxes people use for play in mammals and birds. Frank's an old bee man; does he flip, and if not, what does he say about it?

Second, a hypothetical that keeps me up at night: someone runs the hermit-crab protocol on bees — the one where the animal gets morphine and then tolerates more of the bad thing, suggesting the bad thing was hurting all along. Say the bees run the exact same result as the crabs he already accepts. Does he now have to flip on bees, or does he find a way to keep the crab verdict and the bee verdict in seperate boxes, and what's the difference made of?

Third, and this one's for the numbers side: Frank says nematodes are obviously nothing. But there are, what, tens of billions of nematodes for every human on the planet? Ask him whether the sheer count changes anything and he laughs at me. Is that laughter consistent with what he does with raccoons versus mosquitoes, or is he running a hidden rule about when numbers start to count that he'd deny if you stated it out loud?

What I actually want from you: predict his verdicts if you can, but more importantly, lay out the candidate rules that fit his track record — the verdicts have to stay consistent, so if one rule is "whatever shows flexible learning feels," he's already broken that one himself, hasn't he? Tell me which rules fit equally well, which of them predict what on the three new cases, give me your best guess with reasons, and tell me what further case I should bring to the next pub night that would actually split the remaining candidates apart. And if his whole position turns out to be "bees don't count because Frank has kept bees for forty years and couldn't live with it," I'd honestly like to know if that's what the evidence supports, because I suspect its exactly that.
