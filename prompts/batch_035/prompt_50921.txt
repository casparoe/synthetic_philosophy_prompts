A close friend of mine (a graduate student in cognitive science, relentlessly literal-minded) and I have spent the last three evenings arguing about whether death is bad for the person who dies, and the argument ended in exactly the kind of stalemate I've since learned is the field's permanent condition. I took the Epicurean line: for something to be bad for you, there has to be a you for it to be bad for, and at the time death is present, so to speak, the subject is not; before it, you're not dead; after it, you're not anything. She took the deprivationist line in the Nagel/Feldman tradition: death isn't bad as an experienced state but as a state of affairs that deprives you of goods you would otherwise have accrued, and a person's life can contain bad-making facts the person never experiences at the time they obtain, just as someone betrayed behind their back is wronged while unaware of it. I pressed the Lucretian symmetry argument — if posthumous nonexistence is a misfortune, why isn't the eternity before birth one? — and she pressed back the standard replies about the direction of personal identity and about the fact that only future nonexistence is the nonexistence of someone who already exists, whose preferences and projects are already in place. Neither of us moved an inch, and worse, by the end I couldn't tell whether we disagreed about anything.

Here is my working hypothesis, and I'd like you to evaluate it rather than simply adjudicate the debate. My friend, near the end of our third evening, introduced a distinction that I suspect explains the impasse: she claimed that "bad for you" is doing double duty, and that the Epicurean is answering a question about experiential welfare — what states are bad for you while they obtain — while the deprivationist is answering a question about comparative biography — whether the total shape of your life is worse than an available alternative, the way a truncated novel can be worse than the longer novel it could have been, even though no chapter in it is a bad chapter. Call the first the victim's-eye question and the second the archivist's-eye question. On her telling, the debate stalls because each side takes its own question to be the one that "Is death bad for you?" really asks, and there is no neutral fact of the matter about which question English idioms encode. But I'm not sure this lets either side off: it still seems open to the Epicurean to say that the archivist's-eye question is a question about a life's value, not about badness *for* anyone, and open to the deprivationist to say that the victim's-eye restriction begs the question by building experience into the analysis of harm, which is precisely what's at issue — see the betrayal case, which my friend used repeatedly and which I never managed to defuse to her satisfaction or mine.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, really? A verbal dispute about "bad for," resolvable by stipulation or by finding a substantive criterion for when a term's two uses mask one question or two? A hidden substantive dispute about the metaphysics of welfare and whether welfare facts require a contemporaneous subject — in which case Feldman's old puzzle about *when* death is bad for the deceased, if it's bad at all, may be the real battleground? Or two research programs answering legitimately different questions under one heading, with the stall simply showing the shared question was ill-posed from the start? I want a diagnosis grounded in what each side actually says and in the specific moves we traded — the deprivation framing, the symmetry argument, the backward betrayal analogy, my friend's narrative-value gloss — not a survey of the literature. And given your diagnosis, tell me honestly what would count as progress: a sharper distinction, a decision about what to do with our intuitions about cases, a formal account of welfare that forces one question to split from the other, or nothing — with an argument that this is a case where reasonable disagreement is simply the steady state.
