I'm writing a piece on the regulation of working time, and I keep colliding with an argument that is built entirely on the Econ 101 model of labor supply. The columnist's version runs as follows: leisure is a consumer good like any other, and the wage is its price — an extra hour at home costs you an hour's pay — so each worker simply picks the bundle of money and time that maximizes her utility. On that model, France's 35-hour week is a ration coupon: the state caps how little leisure you may buy, forbidding workers who would rather have the income from purchasing more labor. And Japan's karoshi — death by overwork — is what happens when the price of leisure is set absurdly high: people are priced out of rest, the way households are priced out of housing in expensive cities. The columnist concludes that both pathologies are price problems, not culture problems: let wages and hours flex, and the overwork and the underwork both clear — no Protestant-work-ethic sociology required. My Weberian friend says the whole thing is a category error, that hours are set by norms and bargaining power rather than by a menu of prices, but she cannot say precisely where the model breaks.

Audit the analogy. Start with the correspondence table: what plays the role of the good, the price, the budget constraint, the substitution and income effects, and the ration. Then lay out the breakdowns. Where does "leisure is a good bought at your wage" fail as a description of actual labor markets — consider salaried workers whose overtime is neither fully paid nor even recorded, which is largely how karoshi became a recognized category in the first place; or the French case, where the constraint was imposed by statute and collective bargaining rather than chosen from an individual menu; or the fact that for many workers the marginal hour of work is not sold at all, so there is no observable price for the model to clear. Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the columnist's conclusion — that price flexibility alone would resolve both overwork and underwork — from the ones that don't. Then assess the Weberian counterpoint: is treating the "price" of an hour at home as something set by a work ethic rather than by a market a repair of the model or a wholesale replacement of it? Finally, tell me whether a better analogy exists, and if so what it is.
