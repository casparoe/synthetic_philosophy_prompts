For most of my working life I was an atmospheric chemist; since retiring I have been reading political philosophy, and the question that has really taken hold of me is what democratic theory calls the boundary problem — the question of who belongs to the demos in the first place, which I gather most theory simply assumes away by treating "the people" as given. My professional instinct is to treat a boundary as something one draws for a reason, with the drawing judged by whether the neglected fluxes across the edge are small for the question at hand; I was therefore startled to find two camps in the literature arguing as though the boundary of the demos were a discovered fact rather than an operational choice. I now have a concrete occasion to test this instinct. I have been asked to serve as neutral rapporteur for a deliberative panel our electoral commission convened after a messy set of franchise disputes, and the panel has split into two entrenched camps: one holds that the demos should consist of everyone subjected to the coercive law of the political unit, the other that it should consist of everyone materially affected by its decisions. The disputes that triggered the panel: several tens of thousands of cross-border commuters who work and pay taxes in our capital region but vote elsewhere; a recent national referendum on a deep-water port expansion whose costs and benefits spill heavily onto a neighbouring state; a youth movement demanding standing for those who will live longest under the new climate law; and long-settled resident non-citizens asking for the local franchise. After two years the two camps have stopped listening to each other, and the commission wants, as a final deliverable, the document they should have produced.

Draft that document — the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a committed member of the subjection camp and a committed member of the affectedness camp. I want the claims both can sign, which I hope include at least these: that the boundary problem is genuine and not solved by pointing at existing states, whose franchises match no principle's extension exactly; that both principles are extensionally indeterminate in overlapping ways (what counts as subjection when much of the "coercion" is just the background operation of property and contract law; what counts as affectedness when everything is affected by everything in an interdependent economy); and that both face a bootstrapping worry, since whoever draws the boundary is already a demos. Then the disagreements, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording — particularly on the trigger cases above, on future generations (can trustees or representatives stand in for members-to-be, or is that category confusion?), and on non-resident foreigners affected by our trade and climate policy. Set out the cruxes: I suspect one is how much deep economic interdependence dissolves the practical difference between being subjected to a state's laws and being affected by its policies, and another is whether the subjection camp's coercion is distinctively political coercion or merely an especially intense form of affectedness. For each crux, identify a case, thought experiment, or argument both sides would agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance — including on cases where I expect the two camps to converge on the same verdict for opposite reasons, which is exactly where a joint statement is most in danger of papering over the disagreement.

Two further requests. First, say explicitly whether any part of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive — my chemist's suspicion is that the two camps may agree on nearly all concrete cases while using "membership" to do different work, one tracking a claim-right against coercive imposition and the other a stake in outcomes, and if so, name the residue that is not verbal. Second, on my operational-boundary observation: if some version of it can be honestly signed by both camps — as a claim about the epistemic and pragmatic status of boundary-setting rather than as a deflationary dismissal of both principles — include it among the agreed claims; but if either camp would read it as hostility, leave it out and say why. Format the output as a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table with one row per disputed question (the phrasing each side accepts, and the crux it rests on), then the crux section with each side's recorded predictions. Do not cite literature or give reading lists; I want the positions worked out on their merits, as the panellists themselves would have to do in the room.
