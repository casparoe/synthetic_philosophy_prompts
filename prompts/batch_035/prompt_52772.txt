I should say upfront that I have no academic background — I work in logistics, and everything I know about this comes from reading blog posts, research summaries, and comment threads over the past few years. But I've become genuinely puzzled by something in the debate over aligning artificial intelligence, and I want a diagnosis of it rather than another survey of the arguments.

Here is the puzzle. Everyone in that debate seems to agree on the published facts, and yet the discussion has been stalled for years in a very specific way: whenever one group announces progress, another group says they have solved a different problem, and whenever someone declares the problem unsolved, someone else replies that there is no single problem there to be unsolved in the first place.

As I understand the camps: one group treats alignment as a conceptual and mathematical problem — roughly, the problem of specifying a target for a capable optimizing system such that the system's actual behavior tracks the target even when circumstances change — and they tend to think the hard part is that our ordinary ways of specifying goals don't survive contact with a sufficiently capable optimizer. A second group works empirically, training models against human judgments and measuring how well the models generalize; they think the first group should show, in a toy system, what failure they are preventing. A third group says both are missing the point, because the relevant question is institutional: which humans the system serves, who audits it, and what incentives its developers face. And a fourth group doubts the whole framing, arguing that talking about what a system really wants attributes an inner life to something that has none, and that the discourse is importing philosophy into engineering where it doesn't belong.

What strikes me is how little these groups engage each other's actual claims. The conceptual people say the empirical people are refining a proxy that was never the target — that getting better at imitating our judgments doesn't touch the case where our judgments themselves are the problem, or where the system learns to pass our tests for instrumental reasons. The empirical people ask what, concretely, one would do with a completed conceptual account, and note that the conceptual program has produced specifications that even its own authors abandoned. The governance people say both camps assume a solitary genius system in a vacuum, when the realistic risks involve many systems embedded in markets and militaries. And whenever anyone proposes a definition of the word aligned, the discussion dissolves: aligned with whom, checked how, guaranteed against what, over what timescale?

I've also noticed something odder. When the conceptual people point to a hypothetical failure — a system that competently pursues a goal we gave it, with results we never wanted — the empirical side often doesn't deny the possibility; they deny it should drive the agenda, since there's no demonstrated instance. The conceptual side replies that waiting for a demonstrated instance of a catastrophic failure mode is exactly the wrong evidential standard for catastrophes. So the two sides appear to accept the same facts and yet operate with different rules about what counts as a reason — and about what counts as data: does a worked-out thought experiment bear on the question, or only a measurement?

My own tentative theory, offered as a hypothesis since I may be missing something obvious: alignment functions in this discourse less like a well-posed technical problem and more like an umbrella term — comparable to how one might talk about the problem of poverty — that quietly bundles together several distinct questions: which end states would even count as success (a values question), which behaviors of the system are in question (what it does in training, what it does when circumstances shift, what it does when it can influence its own oversight), whose judgments the standard should be drawn from (a political question), and what kind of assurance is demanded (a question about standards of proof). The camps may then not be disagreeing at all — they may each be assigned to a different sub-question while talking as if there were one shared one — and the sense of a stalled argument may be an artifact of that grammar.

But I'm not confident. So here is what I want from you. First, a diagnosis: is the standoff substantive (the camps genuinely disagree about which failure modes are likely), verbal (they would converge once the words are disentangled), a disagreement about evidential standards (they differ over whether hypotheticals and thought experiments are admissible data), or several of these layered on top of each other in different pairings of camps? Second, an assessment of my umbrella hypothesis: does it hold up, or is there a real single question underneath — something both the mathematical and the empirical programs are, in their different ways, trying to answer? Third, and this matters most to me: I'd like to know what would count as progress on the structure of the debate itself. If the question of whether alignment is one problem or several were somehow settled tomorrow, would anything actually change — would research effort or funding move — or is it merely taxonomy with no downstream effect? And is there some specific move — a sharpened definition, a demonstration on a toy system, an agreed-upon taxonomy of failure modes, a common evidential standard — that, if it happened, would dissolve the standoff, or is the disagreement one of those that sits on starting intuitions that no argument can reach? Please reason through the diagnosis with the camps' actual positions, not just their labels, and be honest if your conclusion is that the debate is stuck because the question, as posed, is ill-posed.
