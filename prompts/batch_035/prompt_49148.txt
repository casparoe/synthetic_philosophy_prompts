I've been trying to work out what I think a fair two-person bargain is, going by my gut on cases rather than by theory. Assume the two parties' utility gains over the no-deal outcome can be plotted as points, the available deals are closed under randomizing between them, and no deal is better for both than the no-deal outcome. Here are my verdicts; I'd like to know what principle, if any, captures them.

1. Two parties in exactly mirrored positions should split the gains down the middle. Obvious, but worth fixing.

2. Ann's best possible gain from this table is 100, Bob's is 20, and the frontier of available deals runs from (0, 20) through (60, 12) and (75, 10) to (100, 0). My verdict: the fair deal is (60, 12), where each walks away with three-fifths of what was on the table for them. A friend who has taken game theory says the standard rule is to maximize the product of the gains, which picks (75, 10) here, and that seems wrong to me — it rewards Ann for bringing a bigger appetite. What matters is how much of your own possible gain you secured, not how big the possible gain was.

3. But here is a verdict pulling the other way. Suppose we have settled the fair deal in some problem, and then an option that was never going to be chosen anyway — one leaving both sides worse off than the fair deal — is ruled out. It seems the fair deal should be exactly what it was. A loser's disappearance shouldn't change anything.

4. And one more: if circumstances improve for one side only — new deals become possible that raise that side's ceiling, while everything that was available to the other side, at every level, remains available — I don't think the side whose prospects improved should come away with less. Being offered more shouldn't cost you.

I'm told that 3 and 4, together with 1 and the demand that the fair deal waste no joint gains, cannot all be honored by any single rule. Is that right? If so, which of my verdicts has to go, and what principle captures the rest? State it precisely enough to compute, say whether it upholds my verdict in case 2 against my friend's product rule, and tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't decided: we are about to bring a third party into this negotiation — does the principle extend to three, or does something break?
