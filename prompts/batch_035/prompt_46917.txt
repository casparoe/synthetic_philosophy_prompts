I have been reading the rationalist blogosphere since the Overcoming Bias days, and I have followed every replay of the agreement-theorem wars: the threads where people tried to "Aumann" each other into consensus on AI timelines, the recurring spectacles of prominent community members who all accept Aumann's theorem and then go on disagreeing for a decade, and the periodic meta-arguments about whether an ideal Bayesian community would converge or merely sort itself into factions along prior lines. This fall I am teaching an upper-level seminar in formal epistemology to a mixed group of philosophy and computer science students, many of whom first met Aumann's theorem as teenagers in blog comment sections and can recite the standard summary — the theorem, a gesture at the Harsanyi doctrine, a shrug about priors being beyond rational criticism — in their sleep. I am writing the three-hour final exam, and I want the centerpiece question on the common prior assumption, worth 30 of 100 points and designed for about 75 minutes of a strong student's time. Please write the question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 30.

The question must be built around the following scenario, which I want printed in full on the exam. Two perfectly calibrated Bayesian forecasters, A and B, are hired to estimate the probability that a certain research lab ships a particular frontier system within eighteen months. Both begin with credence exactly 0.5 in shipment, and each knows the other does. Each then receives one private signal from the lab's internal tracker: A's signal moves her credence to 0.8, B's moves his to 0.2. They exchange exactly one round of messages, each stating only their own final credence; each message is received, understood, and known to be understood. So each one's posterior is common knowledge between them, each knows both of them are perfect Bayesians who started at 0.5 — and yet both credences stand, at 0.8 and 0.2, with neither party the least bit inclined to revise.

The question should have three parts. Part (a) should ask what the scenario proves about A and B, and here is the crux I want the question to force: the student must see that the pair "had the same prior" in the colloquial sense — both started at 0.5 on the very proposition in dispute — and yet they cannot have had a common prior in the technical sense, because common knowledge of posteriors plus a common prior is exactly what Aumann's argument rules out. The A answer must articulate that the common prior assumption is a constraint on the joint distribution over the event and the whole information structure, not on the marginal prior over the target event, and must say, at least informally, how the agents' beliefs could differ consistently: for instance, if they disagree about how the tracker's signals are generated, or about how their own and each other's signals correlate with the event and with one another. A student who concludes that the scenario shows the theorem's assumptions "fail in practice," or that one of the forecasters must be irrational, has not understood the assumption's content. Part (b) should demand an evaluation of the wager-style argument for common priors — that agents with differing priors should be willing to bet against each other, and persistent willingness to bet is somehow a mark against them — engaging at least the objections from adverse selection (the very fact that someone offers you a bet is information) and from risk aversion and stake limits. The strongest answers will also connect the agreement theorem to the no-trade results of Milgrom and Stokey (1982) and note the sense in which the former is a special case of the latter's logic. Part (c) should press the status question: is the common prior an empirical generalization about how minds happen to work, a normative ideal that any disagreement should be measured against, or a modeling convention the theorist imposes by choosing what to put in the state space — and does the Geanakoplos and Polemarchakis (1982) result that iterated announcement of posteriors converges (though with no finite bound) vindicate or undermine the folk practice my blog community has made of it?

The marking scheme should name the specific moves that separate an A from a B answer — in particular, I want the scheme to penalize the memorized moves that my students will reach for by reflex: reciting the theorem's statement, name-dropping the Harsanyi doctrine without content, or treating "differing priors" as a synonym for "irrationality." For each of the five or so must-hit points, allocate marks summing to 30 and add a one-line note on the way students typically botch it. The question should be fully self-contained: no credit should be available for reproducing citations, dates, or the literature, since I am testing whether they can work the machinery, not whether they remember the shelf it came from.
