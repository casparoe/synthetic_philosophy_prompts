My soc program has been re-litigating Weber's Protestant ethic thesis for three years now and the broader fight about whether culture can be a *cause* of anything is exactly as stalled as it was a century ago. The historical-comparative people say values and religious ethics genuinely cause economic behavior, via Weber's elective affinity between salvation anxiety and disciplined accumulation. The quant/economic sociology people, plus our one rational-choice theorist, say either culture collapses into institutions and incentives — so calling it culture is a placeholder for a mechanism you havent found — or it's unfalsifiable residue you appeal to when the numbers don't add up. The frustrating part is that both sides seem to agree on the actual cases: nobody denies Protestant regions of Europe developed differently, and everybody concedes you can't run the counterfactual. Every time someone operationalizes culture (trust scores from values surveys in growth regressions), the Weberians say the operationalization destroys the thing that mattered; every time the Weberians gesture at meaning and conduct of life, the quants ask for one falsifiable claim. My working hypothesis is that this isn't really an empirical disagreement at all but a disagreement about what a cause has to be — whether a cause must be something you could hold fixed in a counterfactual, or whether reasons and meanings are just a different kind of explanation that never competed with mechanisms in the first place. Diagnose what's actually going on: is it verbal, methodological, or bedrock commitments about individuals vs. social facts? Does the macro-micro issue (a value only causes anything through particular people's heads, so what work is 'culture' doing?) cut either way? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here — a mechanism, a natural experiment like the missionary-school ones, a conceptual distinction nobody has drawn — or should we just accept the question is ill-posed and everyone is answering something different?
