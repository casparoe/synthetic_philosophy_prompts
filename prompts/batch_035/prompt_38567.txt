I teach at a small liberal arts college and chair the commitee that decides which courses count for the philosophy major. Lately we keep getting hybrids — a "Philosophy of Data Science" course taught mostly by CS faculty, "Business Ethics" over in the management school, a "Law and Justice" seminar, a "Meaning of Life" course team-taught by religion and lit professors, and a formal logic course from the math department that's basically just proof theory. Half the committee wants a strict definition (taught by philosophy faculty, or centered on a canonical reading list), and the other half says philosophy is a family-resemblance concept so we should just eyeball each course — which so far means everyone votes for whatever they like. We meet twice a semester, we get the syllabus a week ahead, and students are waiting on these credits to graduate.

Draft a decision procedure the committee can actually run on a proposal: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it terminates in a no, and where it has to punt to a person because the question is really about what the major is for, not about facts. I'm the one who brought up family resemblance at the last meeting, so people assume I want the loose answer — be honest about where family resemblance gives no guidance at all, and where any checklist will smuggle a definition back in. Then run it on three of the courses above, including one where the procedure gives an answer I won't like.
