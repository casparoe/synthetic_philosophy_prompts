so i'm a software engineer, no philosophy background, but i fell down a rabbit hole. got into an argument with a coworker about whether math is discovered or invented, and someone linked me to this thing called Benacerraf's dilemma. the way i understand it: if you say math is about real abstract objects (platonism), it's a total mystery how we could know anything about them, since we never causally interact with the number 7 — we can't recieve signals from it any more than from a server we have no network route to. but if you drop the abstract objects, math and everyday talk about physical stuff end up with wildly different kinds of truth, which also seems wrong. and yet we somehow know more about 7 than about most servers i've worked with.

before i pick a side, i want the spec. write me the desiderata: what any adequate account of mathematical truth/knowledge has to deliver — the cases it has to get right (we know 2+2=4, we don't yet know whether Goldbach holds for every even number, mathematicians catch their own errors, people who never talk to each other converge on the same results), the distinctions it can't collapse (truth vs provability is the obvious one), what it owes us about why math is so uncannily applicable to physics, and how it has to treat mathematical vs empirical statements. benacerraf's whole thing seems to be a tension between wanting one uniform semantics for both kinds of talk and wanting epistemic access to what the talk is about, so tell me which of the requirements are in tension, whether some subset is jointly unsatisfiable, and if so which one you'd drop — that's the part i most want to see. also flag which requirement does the most work excluding candidates, and be honest about which items on the list are camp prejudices dressed up as neutral requirements.

then, briefly, how platonism, formalism (math as a symbol-pushing game), and like fictionalism (math is a useful fiction) do against the list. don't spend much space there, i care way more about the criteria.

one thing from my world i'd like factored in: we trust proofs way more than tests, and the proofs work without ever running anything. two teams using totally different tools — different proof assistants, different formalizations, or just pencil and paper — converge on the same theorems. that convergence is what keeps nudging me toward "discovered." i'd like the winning account to not explain it away as coincidence. but if that's actually a camp prejudice rather than a neutral requirement, tell me straight.
