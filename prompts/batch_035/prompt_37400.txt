Something about the standard Moloch narrative has been bugging me, and I want an argument that takes the worry seriously. I teach a course on collective action and coordination failures, and the race-to-the-bottom story we all tell — firms, students, nations trapped by competition into acting against their own values — always treats those values as fixed inputs. The firm would rather not pollute, the student would rather sleep, but the trap forces their hand. That seems like an unstated assumption, and a shaky one: in biological evolution, in markets, and in cultural transmission generally, it's the values themselves that get selected. An organization that internalizes growth as a terminal goal outcompetes one that grudgingly treats it as a means, and after enough iterations there may be nobody left inside the trap who resents being in the trap. Please argue that once you let values be endogenous to the competitive dynamic, the Moloch picture becomes more pessimistic, not less: the consoling thought that everyone would cooperate if only they could quietly disappears, because there's no stable population of would-be cooperators with antecedent preferences left to coordinate. You'll have to deal with the obvious comeback — that selection also favors commitment devices, punishment of defectors, and group-beneficial norms, which is after all how you get from amoebas to us — and explain why those escapes don't generalize here. And say where the analogy between biological selection and market or institutional competition actually breaks down, because if it breaks badly the endogeneity point might cut the other way entirely. Write it as continuous prose only, no headings or bullet points — like a long letter to a colleague who's sceptical but persuadable.
