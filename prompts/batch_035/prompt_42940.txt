i'm prepping a seminar session on whether history can be objective and i want a dialogue between leopold von ranke and hayden white to hand out. they can meet wherever works — my suggestion is a burning archive, both of them standing outside watching the same fire, arguing while the building goes up, but you do you.

the occasion for the argument: a cache has just surfaced with three mutually contradictory reports of the same event, say a bread riot in a catalan mill town in 1908 — one written up by the prefect's office at the time, one from a worker's memoir written decades later, and one by a foreign journalist who arrived the morning after and reconstructed the whole thing from bar gossip. they should fight over what a historian is supposed to do with this.

ranke's position: the riot itself is out there, past and fixed, and the historian's job is to get at what actually happened by weighing the three accounts against each other — the prefect's report has motive to minimize, the memoir has decades of mythmaking behind it, the journalist was basically making it up. the sources have a knowable relationship to the event, and patient criticism gets you closer to it.

white's position: the weighing itself is the historian's act. each report is already emplotted — the prefect writes farce or riot-as-miscegenation-of-order, the memoirist writes tragedy or epic, the journalist writes romance — and the historian who "sifts" them isn't discovering the riot but choosing which plot the evidence will be made to fit. three reports, three beginnings of three different riots, and the choice among them is not an empirical one. and this goes double for the historian who thinks they're just sifting.

both of them need to be smart and neither should win. specifically i want white to have a real answer when ranke points out that some construals flatly contradict the surviving evidence — the dead were buried somewhere, the grain store either burned or didn't — i.e., that the "plot" isn't free-floating. and ranke needs a response to emplotment that's more than stamping his foot about facts, maybe something about how the constraint of the sources, applied hard enough, disciplines the imagination in a way that makes the difference between getting it right and getting it wrong real even if it's never total.

end it unresolved but with them agreeing on something small and concrete — e.g., that the journalist's piece is garbage, or that the fire consuming the only copies of two of the three reports is the one fact tonight that neither of them can narrate away — while the actual disagreement stays fully intact. like maybe the last beat is them both watching the archive burn and drawing opposite conclusions from it.

maybe 900-1200 words. don't have them quote chapter and verse at each other or name-drop books, i don't want it to turn into a bibliography with dialogue tags — keep them arguing about the riot and the reports, not their own collected works. also fine if white occasionally talks like a man who's read too much literary criticism and ranke like a man who is extremely proud of not having.
