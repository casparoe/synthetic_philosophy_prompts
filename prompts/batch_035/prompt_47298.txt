I'm an analyst at a national infrastructure ministry. Before we commit to major projects — a rail line, a hospital IT overhaul, a flood barrier — I have to estimate what they will cost and how long they will take, and our bottom-up internal estimates are consistently too optimistic. Leadership has told me to use "outside views": compare each project to a class of similar completed projects and base the estimate on what happened to them. The trouble is that the answer changes depending on which class you pick — rail projects in our country, rail projects anywhere, megaprojects generally, or just the handful that resemble this one in the particulars — and each choice is defensible, so two analysts can run the same method and get different numbers. Please turn the reference class problem into a decision procedure I can hand to a junior analyst and expect them to run in a day: rules for constructing candidate classes, for deciding when a class is too small or too gerrymandered to trust (a class defined so narrowly that it excludes the failures, for instance), for weighting or combining classes that give conflicting answers — including whether to just take the broadest class and why or why not — and an explicit marker for the point at which the procedure runs out and judgment has to take over. Spell out what to do when only two or three comparable projects exist, since that case comes up constantly here. Then run the procedure on two cases: a new light-rail line in a mid-sized city, for which a large international class of comparable projects exists but mostly came in far worse than our internal estimate; and a one-of-a-kind automated port terminal, where only a handful of similar attempts exist worldwide and most of them failed. In the first case the procedure will give leadership a number they will not like — show that honestly. Flag which steps are genuinely mechanical and which are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and say plainly what the procedure cannot do. Keep the whole thing under 800 words.
