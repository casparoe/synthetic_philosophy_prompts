A friend of mine has spent years arguing her way to a position on the ethics of abortion, and I want to be able to predict her verdicts before she comes back from leave. Her track record, as she has stated it to me:

- Thomson's violinist: the ailing violinist may be unplugged, and this holds even granting him a full right to life.
- The people-seed case: if people drift in through a torn window screen and take root, the homeowner may remove them, even though she opened the window knowing that screens sometimes fail.
- An ordinary pregnancy resulting from contraception that failed despite being used responsibly: abortion is permissible.
- A pregnancy resulting from careless unprotected sex: abortion remains permissible, but she says it is somehow morally worse — not that the woman does wrong exactly, but that something is off.
- Abortion at thirty-two weeks of a fetus with a condition incompatible with life after birth: clearly permissible.
- Abortion at thirty-two weeks of a healthy fetus, for the sake of a career opportunity: not permissible. She was genuinely torn between calling it wrong and calling it indecent, but she insists it may not be done.
- Sperm, eggs, and early embryos have no right to life; a fetus one week before birth has a full one. She draws the line somewhere in the middle and admits she does not know where.

Two new cases are coming up in our reading group. The first: a woman and her partner tried to conceive, succeeded, and at eight weeks she changes her mind for no medical reason at all. The second: at twenty-six weeks, with no anomaly and no grave risk, a woman finds the remaining eight weeks of pregnancy a serious hardship, and her doctor offers her a choice between inducing early labor, after which the fetus would face an uncertain but real chance of survival, and a procedure that guarantees fetal demise before removal.

What rule or rules is my friend actually running — a pure bodily-autonomy rule on which no one is ever obligated to lend their body as life support, a responsibility-modified version on which knowingly incurring the need for aid changes what one owes, a future-like-ours account with a gradually strengthening right to life, or some hybrid? Tell me what each surviving rule predicts about the two new cases, including whether the choice between the two procedures in the second case turns on her distinguishing killing from letting die. Say which of her earlier verdicts do the most constraining work, give me your best guess at her verdicts with your reasons for preferring that rule over its rivals, and name the one further case, not among these, that would best split the remaining candidates. Finally, keep honest about the state of the debate: distinguish what is effectively settled among people who know this literature — for instance, that Thomson's argument is designed to proceed even granting the fetus a right to life — from what is genuinely contested, such as whether responsibility for creating a need for aid can defeat a right to refuse it, and do not present one side of those disputes as the consensus. Flag also where my summary of her views might be doing the work that a real theory would have to do.
