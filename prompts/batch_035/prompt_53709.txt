I've been chewing on something for a while and I want a way to test it. Every week I see claims like "Gen Z expects employers to care about their mental health" or "boomers refuse to adapt to new technology," and I've noticed that the arguments are always about whether the claims are true — never about whether the grouping itself tracks anything real. Is "Gen Z" like a species or a chemical element, something that carves the world at a joint whether or not we have a word for it? Or is it more like "people born in odd-numbered years," a line we drew for convenience and then started treating as a discovery? I don't have the philosophical vocabulary for this, so I want to force the question through a case where it actually matters.

Construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. The setting must be realistic — a public health agency, a school district, or a consumer research team, nothing science-fictional — and the scenario must be one where a decision-maker would act differently depending on whether the generation she's targeting is a real kind with real causal powers or a convenient label for an arbitrary slice of the population. Here is the trap I want the case to avoid: I've read enough to know that any pattern you observe in a cohort could just be a stage-of-life effect (people in their twenties have always behaved like this) or a period effect (everyone, young and old, changed together when the smartphone arrived). So the case has to be built so that a residue is left over — something that looks like a genuine cohort effect — and it is precisely on that residue that the two views of generations should issue different practical verdicts. The scenario itself should be one paragraph. After it, I want four things: a walk-through of what each view says the decision-maker should conclude and why; an identification of which single feature of the case is doing the work; an explanation of what happens to the divergence between the two views if that feature is removed; and an account of how a defender of the label view — someone who thinks generations are just bookkeeping — would attack the case and what she would tell the decision-maker to do instead. Two further constraints. First, the decision-maker must be working with the kind of incomplete, messy evidence real organizations actually have — no perfect longitudinal data, no godlike observer. Second, don't rig the case by stipulating a cohort effect that couldn't possibly be explained any other way; the divergence in verdicts has to be generated by the case, not assumed in it. To give you a sense of what the case is for: I want to find out whether my instinct that "Millennials are just a label" is a substantive thought or intellectual laziness, and a case where the two views recommend different actions would tell me which side has real work to do. If the two views turn out to be practically indistinguishable even in a case engineered to separate them, tell me that honestly and explain what that shows about the original question.
