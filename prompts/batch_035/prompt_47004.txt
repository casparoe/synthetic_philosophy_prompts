Keynes's 1921 Treatise on Probability held that not all probabilities can be given numbers, and that some pairs of probabilities cannot even be ordered against each other — an ancestor of today's imprecise probability frameworks. A funder can support three PhD students for four years of pure theory in this area: sets of probabilities, lower and upper previsions, the dilution problem, decision rules such as gamma-maximin, maximality, and E-admissibility, and the behaviors of sets of probabilities under conditioning. Which open questions should these students work on, and in what order? Defend the ordering: which questions are upstream of which, which are tractable with current mathematical tools, and which you would deprioritize because their answer would not change much downstream. Alongside the agenda, address Keynes himself: he distinguished the degree of a probability from what he called the weight of the argument behind it — roughly, how much relevant evidence one has, as opposed to how strongly it favors a conclusion. Modern imprecise probability is often motivated as capturing evidential poverty in a way single numbers cannot. Is that motivation actually delivered — do current frameworks genuinely represent weight of argument, or do they track a different phenomenon — and should answering that question rank near the top or bottom of the three-student agenda?
