Before my reading group argues about which theory of objective chance is right, I want us to have in hand the specification any such theory must meet, since adequacy here is relative to a job description. Fix the purpose as follows: a theory of chance is adequate if it can vindicate deference — if it explains why an agent who learns, of an event A, that its objective chance is x, and nothing else relevant, should set her credence in A to x — while still treating chance as a feature of the world rather than of our ignorance of it. Write the desiderata list, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and mark each as either non-negotiable or a camp preference smuggled in as a demand.

The cases the list must confront should include, at minimum: the crystal ball, where the agent gets evidence about tomorrow's outcome that is not itself a chance assignment, and the theory must say whether such evidence counts as admissible and why the deference principle appears to break when it does; undermining, where evidence about the global pattern of events seems to threaten the very chance facts that the pattern would ground; chances under determinism, as in a statistical-mechanical world where nothing at the fundamental level is chancy; the chance of an event that has already happened; the mixture case, where the agent knows the chance of A is either x or y without knowing which, so that the theory must say whether deference commutes with averaging rather than merely assuming it; and the possibility of imprecise objective probabilities, where the world itself fixes only an interval.

Then flag which pairs or triples of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — I take it the deepest result in this literature is that some trio of deference, supervenience of chance on the total history of occurrent fact, and a natural restriction on admissible evidence is inconsistent, and I would like to see that made precise — and say which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates. Finally, score the best-system analysis, single-case propensities, and hypothetical frequentism briefly against the list; the group will argue out the rest.
