I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and last month I sat in on a long argument between two AI-safety researchers I was profiling — one who works on provable guarantees for formal systems, one who works on evaluations of large models. It was the sharpest disagreement of my reporting trip, and I'd like to get it right before I characterize it in print. (1) The first researcher argued that alignment work should aim at worst-case guarantees: for existential risks, expected-value reasoning is a trap, because you cannot average across scenarios in one of which there is no one left to enjoy the average — so the only meaningful target is a system that is safe in every case, and anything short of that is a decision to accept some probability of catastrophe. (2) The second replied that this is incoherent as an actual standard: for any system more capable than its overseers, the worst case includes someone deliberately steering it into harm, so demanding worst-case safety either forbids everything or forces its advocates to quietly water the standard down — which, he claimed, is exactly what happens when industry "safety cases" begin as worst-case arguments and end up as arguments that the residual risk is tolerable, i.e., average-case reasoning in disguise. (3) She answered that this is an argument for honesty about the standard, not against the standard itself: aviation regulators achieved safety precisely by driving the worst case down through iteration and hard requirements, and no one would accept an airline's assurance that its planes are fine on average. (4) He countered that aviation works because its worst case is bounded — a crashed plane kills hundreds, not everyone, so near-misses can actually calibrate you — whereas with AI the unbounded worst case makes iteration useless as a teacher, and the worst-case frame misallocates effort besides: by severity alone rather than severity weighted by probability, it points researchers at the maximally capable misaligned system, which may be rare, rather than at the ordinary misuse of ordinary systems, which is happening now. Could you adjudicate this exchange move by move — for each of the four steps, whether it holds, and why — and then tell me who is more right overall and, importantly, what both of them are missing? I want a referee's judgment on the arguments as made, not a survey of the field.
