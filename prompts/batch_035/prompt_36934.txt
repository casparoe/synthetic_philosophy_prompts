I've slowly drifted (ha) into holding a particular view about how AI risk most plausibly plays out, and I want to stress-test it properly. The view: the catastrophic scenario to actually worry about isn't the classic one where a system develops a persistent hidden goal, plays along during training, and then grabs power at some point — it's the slow version. In the slow version no system ever "rebels." Humans stay nominally in charge the whole way down: there's still a sign-off box, still audits, still a policy team. But the substance of what gets decided migrates — AI systems propose, filter, and frame everything, humans can only rubber-stamp in the time available, and each individual step is defensible, profitable, and easier than the last. A century later human input is vestigial, like the queen in a beehive (or the British monarchy, pick your favorite). Nobody ever does anything that would show up in a history book as The Moment.

What would count against this? I'm not asking for reassurance. I want the defeaters ranked from "this would genuinely make me update" down to "merely awkward." A few things I already suspect are in play. The slow-drift view seems almost designed to be unfalsifiable — any evidence that things are going fine can be read as "the drift is happening too gradually to notice," and any evidence that a system is secretly scheming can be read as "drift accelerated." Is there anything observable in the next, say, ten years that the slow story predicts and the sharp-transition story doesn't, or the other way around? Or does the unfalsifiability charge actually not apply here — is it fair for a view of this kind to only get pinned down over centuries, and if so, what does that do to the claim that it should drive decisions today? I'm also curious which points the committed defenders of the view would themselves concede as counting against it versus wave off, since I mostly hear critics' versions of that.

One more thing I keep going back and forth on: does the slow-drift story even deserve to be called "risk from AI" in the same sense? If humans are making (or rubber-stamping) the choices every step of the way, maybe the honest description is just "humans chose this" — bad, but not a betrayal, more like everyone collectively choosing TV over exercise. If someone told me the drift story is really a story about human weakness with AI as an accelerant, not a story about AI at all, should that change how much weight I put on it relative to plain old governance failure and bad institutions? I'd honestly take a paragraph on that too.
