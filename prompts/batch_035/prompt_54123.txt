Quick setup in case: two players alternate moves, and on your turn you can either "take," which ends the game and gives you the bigger share of the pot as it currently stands, or "pass," which grows the pot before handing the choice to the other player. Finitely many moves total, and the pot at the end is way bigger than anything available early on.

The textbook answer is backward induction: at the last node whoever moves takes, so at the second-to-last node you take to beat them to it, and unrolling that all the way back, player 1 grabs the dinky pot on move one. Fine, I've accepted that for years. But then he springs this on me:

(1) At the last node, whoever moves takes — no controversy there.
(2) So if backward induction is right, a rational player 1 takes at node 1.
(3) Now suppose player 1 passes anyway. Player 2, sitting at node 2, now knows she's not facing a backward-induction player, since by (2) a backward-induction player would have taken.
(4) So everything player 2 believed about player 1 is out the window, and the only evidence she has left is the pass she just saw: player 1 is a passer. So she should expect passes at every future node too.
(5) Expecting that, she passes at node 2 (why kill the pot when the other player will keep feeding it and you can always take later, bigger?). And so on down the line — both players pass to the end and split the big pot.
(6) But then player 1, sitting at node 1, can see all of this coming: one pass and the whole chain of mutual passing is set off. So passing at node 1 is rational after all, and backward induction was wrong from the start.

He says this proves backward induction refutes itself — the reasoning only holds as long as nobody ever deviates, so the very first deviation destroys it. My instinct is that (4) is doing something sneaky, like seeing one pass doesn't actually tell you what she'll do at the last node, where taking is just obvious. But he swears (4) is fine and that I'm only doubting it because I'm clinging to (2). Which step should I actually be rejecting, and what does rejecting it cost me?
