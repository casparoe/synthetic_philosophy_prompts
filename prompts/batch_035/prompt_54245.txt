I serve on the committee that awards my university's Distinguished Teaching Medal, and this cycle we are deadlocked between two finalists. The first has an unremarkable teaching record — middling student evaluations, no curricular innovation, several colleagues describing his classroom as competent but charisma-free — but he is our most generous living donor, and our development office has let it be known, without ever saying so outright, that a public slight in the very semester the new science building's naming is being finalized could complicate a nine-figure pledge. The second finalist has an exemplary record but is early-career and could plausibly be honored in a later cycle without loss to anyone. Our charter says only that the medal recognizes excellence in teaching, while leaving the committee discretion to weigh the broader interests of the university.

Half the committee holds that donor considerations are simply corrupting: the only legitimate reasons for awarding the medal are considerations in virtue of which a candidate deserves it, and anything else is institutional bribery with better manners. The other half holds that the committee is an organ of the university rather than a passive register of desert, and that institutional consequences can be legitimate reasons for the committee's decision even though they are not reasons why the winner is an excellent teacher — much as, they say, the fact that believing something would comfort you can be a reason to want the belief without being a reason to hold it.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before our vote. It should recommend one concrete course of action (including whether a compromise such as deferring the medal with a public citation to the second finalist is coherent or just a way of hiding from the question), give the reasons a colleague could actually respond to, and engage the strongest version of the side it rejects — taking seriously the distinction between reasons that make a candidate deserving and reasons that can rightly move a committee whose action has consequences beyond recording desert. The memo should also state plainly which single consideration, if it came out differently, would flip the recommendation, and where its own reasoning is most likely to fail.
