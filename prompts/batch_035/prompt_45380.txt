My brother-in-law and I have been arguing about taxation for close to a decade, usually over holidays, and to stop going in circles I finally sat down and wrote out what I actually believe. Here is the list. (1) What you earn through voluntary exchanges — a wage, a fee, a sale — is yours in a strong sense: nobody else has a claim on it before you do, and the fact that other people need it, or could do good with it, does not by itself give them one. (2) Taking someone's property under threat of force is what theft is, and taxation is exactly that, so it needs a special justification that ordinary taking does not; the popular slogan to this effect strikes me as crude in tone but right in spirit. (3) I am nevertheless entirely comfortable with taxes that fund courts, policing, and national defense, because those things cannot plausibly be run on voluntary subscriptions — everyone would quietly free ride and the goods would collapse — so compulsion there seems legitimate to me. (4) I also think a decent society needs a safety net for people who genuinely cannot support themselves, and I would accept taxation for that purpose too, though I am less sure where the line falls than I am about the courts. (5) A majority vote cannot by itself turn taking into not-taking: if my neighbors voted to seize one particular person's house to fund something most of them wanted, that would still be theft, whatever the tally, so voting alone does not launder the act. (6) I never consented to any of this in any meaningful sense: I was born into this country, leaving is expensive, and there is nowhere on earth without taxes, so the claim that staying counts as agreement has always struck me as a dodge. (7) Despite all of the above, I would feel that I owed my taxes for the courts and the defense of the country even if I could evade them without any risk of penalty; I would think of myself as a freeloader, not merely as lucky.

My brother-in-law's view is that this list is flatly self-contradictory, though annoyingly he names a different culprit each time we talk: sometimes it is (2) against (3), sometimes (1) against (4), sometimes (6) against everything else. My own suspicion is that some of these pairs genuinely cannot live together and others only look like they clash, and that I have been unable to tell which is which because I have never forced myself to trace what each commitment actually commits me to beyond the sentence it is written in. It also worries me that a few of the items might be load-bearing for each other in ways I have not noticed — that, for example, my comfort with (3) might secretly depend on something in (5) or (6) being false, or that my felt obligation in (7) is doing more work than I realize.

So I would like an audit rather than a lecture. Which pairs of the seven are genuinely in tension, and which only appear to be once you look closely? For each real tension, please say how severe it is, what exactly would have to give, and what that revision would then cost me elsewhere on the list; then tell me which single change would resolve the most trouble at the lowest price. Please do not assume you know which commitment I would drop — I do not know myself, which is the whole reason I am asking. Open your reply with your bottom line in the first sentence, and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my list back to me.
