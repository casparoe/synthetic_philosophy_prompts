I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for well over a decade, since the days when the quantum mechanics sequence persuaded a good many of us that collapse was an embarrassment and Everett the plain truth of the equations. In that time I have watched the same argument play out dozens of times in comment sections: someone raises the Born rule as the one thing many-worlds cannot explain, someone else replies that decision theory derives it, and the thread degenerates into whether the decision-theoretic axioms are requirements of rationality or physics smuggled in through the back door, with nobody ever spelling out what would actually settle the matter. I now moderate a small forum where two long-standing members — one a convinced Everettian in the Deutsch and Wallace tradition who holds that the Born weights follow from the formalism together with the demands of rational agency, and one an equally convinced Everettian who accepts branching but insists that probability must be postulated in, since a deterministic theory in which every outcome occurs leaves nothing genuinely uncertain — have been arguing past each other for years. I have finally persuaded them to attempt an adversarial collaboration, and they have agreed, on the condition that someone produce a starting draft showing what such a document could look like. That draft is what I want from you.

Draft the joint statement they should be able to sign. It should set out the claims both accept: about the formalism itself, about the reality of branching, about the failings of naive branch counting as an account of the observed statistics, and about which parts of the measurement problem they regard as solved and which as merely deferred. It should then state their residual disagreement with precision, in wording each would accept as a fair statement of his own side. My sense is that the dispute turns on whether the decision-theoretic axioms — in particular something like branching indifference and diachronic consistency — are genuine norms of rationality that any agent must satisfy, or whether assuming them is tantamount to feeding the Born weights into the derivation at the premise stage; and on whether self-locating uncertainty is even available to an agent who knows she will have successors on every branch, or only arrives after the branching is effectively complete. Identify the cruxes: I suspect one is whether a toy agent who counts branches rather than weighting them by squared amplitude can be shown to violate some norm of rationality that both sides independently endorse, another whether the inexactness and decoherence-relativity of the branching structure itself undermines any derivation that claims to be a theorem, and a third what exactly it would take, by way of a derivation from weaker premises, for either side to concede. For each crux, describe the argument, thought experiment, or future result both would agree bears on it, and record each party's prediction in advance, so that if the prediction fails, the signatory is committed to moving. If any portion of the dispute strikes you as verbal — for instance, a disagreement that dissolves once probability is used in one sense rather than another — say which portion and why. Both parties have insisted that the document not be a litany of citations or name-dropping of living philosophers; it should stand on the arguments, and where it touches on a familiar programme in the literature it should characterise the position rather than appeal to the authority of its holders.

Please write the whole thing in British English, and, unusually for a document of this kind, in continuous prose only: no headings, numbered lists, bullet points, bold text, or tables. Structure it as a letter that both parties sign — agreed claims first, then the disagreements and the cruxes, then the recorded predictions — but let the structure be carried by the prose itself, the way one writes a long and careful email rather than a slide deck. Aim for roughly fifteen hundred to two thousand words.
