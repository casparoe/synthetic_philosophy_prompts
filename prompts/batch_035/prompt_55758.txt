Quick backstory before the ask: I'm a poli sci PhD student and in two weeks I'm presenting my dissertation chapter to a law-and-philosophy workshop, and the whole thing leans on tearing apart the Ulysses analogy for constitutionalism. The standard line is that constitutions are precommitment devices — the founders lashed us (and themselves) to the mast so later passions can't steer us onto the rocks. My chapter argues this framing is busted in two ways. First, Ulysses ties *himself*, but constitutional framers are dead by the time the binding bites — it's the dead hand, not self-binding, which everyone knows, but I want to say the disanalogy is fatal unless you patch it. Second, and this is the part I actually like: even in the original story Ulysses doesn't do the tying — the crew lashes him down and plugs their own ears. So the paradigm case of "self"-commitment is secretly other-commitment from the start, which I want to argue is a feature, not a bug. My patch for the dead hand problem is a rolling-commitment picture: each generation stays bound only because it declines to invoke the amendment procedure, so the constitution isn't a rope tied once, it's more like a rope that only stays a rope as long as everyone keeps holding their end. On this view entrenchment is continuously re-tied by non-use.

The room: mostly originalist constitutional law scholars who are, maybe surprisingly, fond of the dead hand — they think being governed by people long dead is fine or even the point — plus a couple of political theorists who think dragging behavioral econ into constitutional theory is just decoration for arguments you could make anyway, and one behavioral economist who's there because her husband is in the department and who I suspect will come at me from the direction I'm not expecting.

Draft me a Q&A prep sheet. I want the eight-ish objections I'll actually get from *this* room, not the generic ones, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased in that camp's vocabulary, with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Part of me already suspects the "declining to amend = recommitting" move is just tacit consent with a fresh coat of paint, so tell me how bad that is and whether I should preempt it or hope nobody connects the dots.
