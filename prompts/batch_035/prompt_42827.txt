I'm a PhD student in political theory and I'm writing a chapter section on Arendt and responsibility, and there's one thing I can't get straight. My working understanding is this: in Origins of Totalitarianism she describes Nazi evil as radical evil, evil so deep it can't be traced to any comprehensible motive — it supposedly destroys the very categories we use to judge. Then in Eichmann in Jerusalem she walks that back and says Eichmann's evil was banal instead. So if I have this right, her considered view is that the worst crimes get committed by people with no real motives at all, just thoughtless functionaries. But that's exactly what confuses me: if Eichmann had no motives and, on her telling, didn't even stop to think about what he was doing, on what grounds does she holds him responsible and endorse the death sentence? Responsibility usually seems to need at least intent or knowledge, so her position looks flat out inconsistent to me — or I'm missing some distinction she's drawing.

For context: I know from secondary reading that in her replies to her critics (the Scholem letters, the postscript to the book) she insisted she wasn't offering a theory of evil at all, just reporting what Eichmann looked like at the trial, and I vaguely remember her saying something like evil is never radical, only extreme, and only the good can be radical. But that seems to make my problem worse, not better — if it's just a fact about one man's courtroom demeanor, why did it kick off a decades-long debate about the nature of evil? And separately, I've heard people say the Sassen interviews from Argentina, published decades later, show Eichmann really was a committed ideological antisemite, which would suggest Arendt got played by his performance. I'm honestly not sure how much any of that should bear on reconstructing her actual position.

Anyway, I'd like to get this untangled before I write the section up.
