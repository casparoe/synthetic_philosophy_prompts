I'm taking a third-year course on free will and moral responsibility, and my study group has been going round in circles on Frankfurt cases all week. Everyone accepts that the classic neuroscientist versions work in the abstract, but they all feel a bit cheap: whenever the counterfactual intervener is a chip in someone's brain or a team of waiting surgeons, my tutor says we've smuggled in sci-fi machinery that a compatibilist shouldn't need, and that the real test of the argument is whether it survives with nothing exotic in play at all. So I want you to help me build something better than what my group managed.

Act as a professional philosopher specialising in free will and agency, and construct a Frankfurt-style case meeting all of the following constraints: (1) no neuroscientists, no brain chips, no demons, no prediction supercomputers — the counterfactual intervener must be an ordinary human (or small group of humans) using perfectly ordinary means, e.g. firing someone, lying to them, locking a door; (2) the intervener must act on some prior sign that appears before the agent has fully settled on what to do, but the agent must be genuinely unaware of being monitored, so nobody can say she acted out of fear of the intervener; (3) the agent must come out morally responsibile in the ordinary Frankfurt sense — she acts on her own reasons, wholeheartedly, and would cite those same reasons if asked afterwards. Realistic setting (an office, a family business, a courtroom, whatever works), and the scenario itself should be one paragraph.

Then do the verification work properly: show explicitly that the principle of alternative possibilities fails in the case, spelling out exactly what the agent could and couldn't have done at each moment. After that, spend real time on the flickers of freedom defence — the objection that the pre-action sign itself leaves the agent some tiny alternative, such as beginning to waver before the trigger fires. Think carefully about whether your case genuinely dodges that defence or merely hides it, and if it can't be fully dodged, say so honestly rather than papering over it. Finish by telling me which feature of the case is doing the real work, what a defender of the principle of alternative possibilities would say to attack it, and whether deleting the intervener entirely (keeping everything else fixed) would leave us with ordinary coercion or with something we wouldn't even notice. Please brainstorm two or three candidate scenarios before settling on the best one, explain why you picked it, and write the whole answer in British English.
