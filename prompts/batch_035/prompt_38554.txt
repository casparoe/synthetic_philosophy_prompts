I'm a senior analyst at a state consumer-protection agency, and a large share of the industry submissions crossing my desk turn on a single argument: that the purchase is the proof. Because consumers buy the product at that price, the submission runs, they value it at least that much; because they renew, they're satisfied; therefore the claimed harm is priced in, and intervention is paternalism over informed choice. We get this for extended warranties on electronics, auto-renewing subscriptions with post-renewal churn, high-APR short-term credit, add-on fees at checkout, and a fair number of others, maybe two dozen dockets a year. Each merits at most a day of analyst time before it either dies or becomes an investigation, and any finding has to be signed off by a senior economist who will not accept vibes. I've read enough LessWrong-adjacent argument over the years -- the perennial fights about whether akrasia means your revealed preference is to keep failing, whether stated and revealed preferences can just disagree -- to know that choice-as-evidence is a much subtler idea than the submissions make it, and I'd like the subtlety operationalized rather than admired.

Please write a procedure my team can actually run on a submission. I want the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and where the procedure exits with a defensible finding that the behavior is genuine evidence of consumer benefit versus a finding that it isn't. The distinctions that I suspect must survive the simplification: a choice among actually-available alternatives reveals more than a take-it-or-leave-it acceptance (Samuelson's machinery earns its keep when something affordable was declined, and licenses almost nothing when nothing else was on the table); a renewal is weaker evidence than a fresh purchase at the same price, because the counterfactual includes switching costs and inertia; a repeated choice under desperation, in the vein of Sen's critique, may reveal a conditional ranking -- the borrower prefers this loan to worse options -- without telling us anything about whether the transaction is good for them; and what a consumer prefers is a different question from what benefits them, which is precisely the step the submissions quietly smuggle through. The procedure should say what to do when we can't observe the counterfactual (we usually can't), should mark which questions masquerade as factual when they're really evaluative, and should be candid about the failure modes: when it will certify a market the interventionists on our staff want to open, and when it will decline to certify one the economists are already fond of.

Then run it on three cases, at least one of which should come out against my own instincts, since those run interventionist: (1) extended warranties whose actuarial value is strongly negative but which are repurchased by the same customers across successive products; (2) auto-renewing subscriptions where most revenue comes from users who never open the product again after renewal; and (3) short-term high-APR credit extended to repeat borrowers who, in survey after survey, say they understood the terms and would borrow again. Throughout, please keep two registers visibly separate: what is essentially settled among people who know the material -- what the revealed-preference results actually license given budget sets and observed choice, the fact that ordinal utilities don't aggregate across persons, the gap between demand existing and surplus being positive -- and what is genuinely contested, such as whether satisfied preferences constitute well-being at all, or how much weight to give choices made under constrained or adapted circumstances. I'd rather the procedure carry a visible flag at each live dispute than have one side of it presented to my team as the consensus.
