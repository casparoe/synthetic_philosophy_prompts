I've been keeping a notebook of my honest gut reactions to trade-offs between pleasure and pain, and I've come to suspect they add up to something like a lexical threshold view, but I can't get the details to hold together, and I'd like you to do the reflective equilibrium for me: tell me whether one principle can capture my verdicts, repair my tentative formulation if it's close, or tell me which verdicts have to go.

Here are the verdicts. (1) I'd take a day of moderate headache without complaint in exchange for getting to hear a piece of music I love as though for the first time again — small pains and modest pleasures seem to trade off straightforwardly. (2) But I would not accept ten years of the richest pleasures available to a human in exchange for living through one hour of unanesthetized severe burn pain; I don't mean the trade is bad value, I mean I won't make it at any rate, which feels different. (3) I think a life with well-managed chronic moderate pain that also contains love, absorption, and achievement is better than a painless but flat and bored life — so pleasure seems to have its own intrinsic weight, not just the status of pain's absence. (4) Yet I think a life containing equal measures of sublime joy and constant severe agony is worse than never existing at all, even though the ledger balances, and even though I'd rather live the chronic-pain-plus-riches life of (3) than not exist. (5) Within pain alone, I'm comfortable aggregating: a thousand strangers each suffering one paper cut is worse than one stranger suffering one paper cut, and somewhere between a thousand and a billion I think dispersed trivial suffering becomes genuinely comparable to a single grave pain — but no number of paper cuts, however many people suffer them, seems able to buy off an hour of the burn pain in (2), which is exactly the move my aggregation habit in (5) wants to make. (6) Finally, the relief when a bad migraine suddenly lifts feels to me like one of the best things in life, and I can't tell whether my verdicts treat that relief as a positive good on the pleasure side of the scale or merely as the cessation of an evil — and I notice I think it's fine to give someone a drug that removes pain's felt awfulness while leaving the bare sensation, but somehow worse to give one that leaves the awfulness and removes the sensation.

My tentative principle is this: below some intensity threshold, pleasures and pains lie on a single common scale and trade off normally; above it, the scale splits, and severe pains can be compared only with other severe pains, never with any quantity of pleasure or of lesser pain. Test this against the six verdicts. Tell me precisely what the threshold is a threshold of — intensity alone, or intensity and duration, and whether the number of sufferers matters on either side of it. Tell me whether relief of pain comes out as a positive good or a mere absence on the resulting view, and whether the migraine-lift case can be accommodated without smuggling pleasure back across the lexical divide. Then apply the principle to at least two cases I haven't listed — say, a dying patient offered one ecstatic final day at the price of a month of severe pain beforehand, and a world of painless but barely-conscious contentment compared against ours — and say which of my six verdicts, if any, the best version of the principle cannot save, and whether saving it is worth the complexity.

Please say how confident you are in the principle you end up with, and single out the point in your reconstruction of my views that you are least sure actually reflects what I think rather than what fits your theory conveniently.
