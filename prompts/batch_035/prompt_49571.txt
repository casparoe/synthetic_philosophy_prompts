I'm a medical resident rotating through the ICU, and our unit's practices have left me genuinely unsure what theory of the doing/allowing distinction, if any, the institution is operating with. Here is the bundle of norms as I've encountered them:

(1) We are taught explicitly that withholding a life-sustaining treatment and withdrawing one already underway are ethically equivalent — that there is no morally relevant difference between never starting a ventilator for a patient with a dire prognosis and switching one off next week. The ethics committee treats this equivalence as settled doctrine.

(2) Despite (1), administering a drug with the express intention of ending a patient's life is treated as categorically impermissible — not as something requiring unusually strong justification, but as killing, off the table regardless of consent, prognosis, or how the death would otherwise come about.

(3) Nurses titrate opioids and sedatives in dying patients to the point where everyone understands the dosing may foreseeably shorten life by some hours, and this counts as good palliative care rather than a hard case, so long as the doses track symptoms.

(4) A patient with decision-making capacity may refuse food and fluids, and staff are expected to honor that refusal and keep the patient comfortable while they die of dehydration — while the same staff may not bring about the death directly, even at that same patient's request.

(5) The equivalence in (1) is honored in the letter but not in the spirit: attendings go to great lengths to avoid starting anything they might later want to stop, precisely because withdrawing feels different to them, and published surveys of clinicians report exactly this asymmetry.

What I'd like to know is what philosophical commitments this bundle presupposes. Does the practice need the doing/allowing distinction to be morally significant or morally empty? The equivalence in (1) seems to say that turning off the ventilator is not a killing in the way the injection in (2) is; but the standard argument for that — that the ventilator is an ongoing intervention the patient may decline, so the resulting death comes from the underlying disease rather than from us — appears to apply equally to a lethal injection when, as in (4), the patient is asking for it. Is the practice relying throughout on the doctrine of double effect, on a distinction between what clinicians cause and what they merely permit, on something about the patient's rights rather than the structure of the act, or on nothing coherent at all? Please reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, distinguish which parts of the practice strictly require which commitments and which parts are compatible with several rival views, and identify where, if anywhere, the bundle is genuinely inconsistent rather than merely uncomfortable. I'm not asking for the history of these doctrines; I'm asking what the institutional logic is and whether it holds together.
