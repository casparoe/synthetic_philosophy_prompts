My dad had a hemorrhagic stroke back in March and hes been in this weird in-between state since -- not vegetative, "minimally conscious" is the term, though he was always more of a maximal guy if you knew him. My family is now politely fighting about whether he's suffering, whether to keep pushing aggressive rehab, whether mom should move him closer. In the middle of all this my brother discovered philosophy of mind and keeps deploying "illusionism" at me (he's very into the Dennett/Frankish stuff): the idea that phenomenal consciousness, the "what it's like" thing, is an introspective illusion, so I should stop asking whether there's some inner light on in dad's head and just look at the functional facts. Part of me finds that clarifying and part of me thinks it's suspiciously convenient, like a car salesman explaining the missing feature was a gimmick anyway.

So I've been trying to map what I actually believe by going with my gut on cases. Here's the list:

1. My dog yelps at the vet and then hides under the bed when we drive past that neighborhood: she feels pain. Obviously.
2. A fish on a hook: something is happening for the fish, I lean yes, but I'm less sure it hurts in the full way I hurt.
3. Blindsight patient says he sees nothing in his left field but guesses where the light was, way above chance. I think there is something it's like to be him, but nothing it is like to *see* that patch of wall. Whatever that means.
4. Pain asymbolia: woman says yes, that's pain, I can feel it right there, but she doesn't mind it, almost smiles. She still has pain, I say. Definately.
5. A dementia patient at the nursing home winces and pulls her arm away at the flu shot, but if you ask her right then she says no, no pain, and she isn't lying. I say she does feel pain.
6. Anesthesia: patient gets a paralytic plus a decent hypnotic, no memory afterwards, but heart rate and blood pressure spike when the surgeon cuts. My gut says no experience there -- or at least that the spike isn't what matters. But put this next to #5 and I cant articulate the difference.
7. Dreams: I'm sure I have experience while dreaming even though all my evidence is after-the-fact memory. Yet in #6 I refused to count after-the-fact indicators. These two refuse to be friends and I'd like to know which one blinks first.
8. Locked-in patient communicating with eye blinks: fully there, no question.
9. My phone's camera detects dad's face in a photo, a driverless car "sees" the crosswalk: no experience, and I'd say that even if the car were perfect.
10. Dad himself: sometimes tracks a balloon, and he smiled when mom read him the box scores. Neurologist says tracking can be reflexive and the smile can be subcortical. My gut says the smile meant something to him -- but I admit I can't give a reason for that which doesn't also apply to the phone camera (info about mom's voice in, smile behavior out).

I was playing with a tentative principle: wherever there's damage detection that's flexible in the right way -- learning, avoidance that updates, that kind of thing -- there's pain, full stop. Test it for me, because it feels both too strong (fish? insects? a robot with a decent controller?) and too weak (the dementia lady denies it, and dad's smile isn't pain-related at all).

So: what principle, or shortlist of principles, captures my verdicts? When I say the smile meant something to him, the illusionist reply that this is just me projecting feels like it's answering a different question than the one I asked, but I can't prove that.
