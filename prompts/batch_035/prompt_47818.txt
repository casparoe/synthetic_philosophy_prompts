I'm a retired materials scientist (35 years, mostly polymers) who got serious about philosophy after retiring, and lately I've been reading Quine, since the idea that philosophy is continuous with science is obviously going to appeal to someone like me more than the stuff my book club keeps assigning. I gather he wrote exactly one essay on ethics — called "On the Nature of Moral Values," I think it got reprinted in Theories and Things — and a philosophy podcast I listen to on my morning walks covered his take recently. The host summarized it roughly like this:

"Quine, being a thoroughgoing naturalist, held that ethics is simply a branch of psychology. Moral claims are empirical claims about what people do value and why, so once psychology and neuroscience are mature enough, moral questions can in principle be settled scientifically. When two people disagree about, say, capital punishment, that's really just a disagreement about psychological facts, and enough data about human nature would eventually dissolve the disagreement. The only residue Quine allowed for was taste: choosing between basic systems of values is, in the end, like preferring one cuisine to another. So ethics gets 'naturalized' in exactly the same way Quine naturalized epistemology, and this makes him a moral naturalist in the standard metaethical sense — moral facts are natural facts, discoverable by the same methods that discover facts about molecules."

I'll admit I found this pretty satisfying — as a scientist it's nice to think the moral hand-wringing could be brought under the scientific tent — but it seems to slide past the is/ought gap a bit too easily, and something tells me Quine was more careful than the summary makes him sound. Like, if ethics only has coherence to go on and not correspondence to observation (I think I read that somewhere), doesn't that matter here?

Can you go through the host's summary piece by piece and tell me which parts Quine would sign off on, which are conflations, and whether the "it's all taste in the end, like choosing a cuisine" bit is actually him or the host filling in the blanks? Also, specifically: does the last claim follow — is what Quine was doing with ethics the same thing metaethicists usually mean by "moral naturalism" — or is there a difference between naturalizing the study of ethics and saying moral facts are natural facts? I'd like to know whether to keep using this podcast as a source at all, definately want to calibrate how much I trust it.
