I teach philosophy of mind, and every few years I walk a class through the exchange between Norman Malcolm and Daniel Dennett on whether dreams are experiences. The shape of that dispute is familiar to me; its persistence is what I want help understanding.

Malcolm, in Dreaming (1959), argued from something like grammar rather than physiology. The concept of dreaming, he maintained, is fixed by the criterion of the waking impression: the morning report is the only thing that establishes that one dreamt, so it is a conceptual error to treat dreams as experiences occurring during sleep. Sleep, on his view, just is the absence of experiencing. Dennett's 1976 reply, "Are Dreams Experiences?", was meant to be the empirical rejoinder. He proposed that the composition of a dream and its being written to memory can come apart, so that a dream might be composed in the moment of waking — the whole narrative assembled on the spot, perhaps prompted by the external stimulus that woke you up and woven into the dream's ending. Since the model on which the dream is composed and recorded during the night and the model on which it is composed and recorded at wake-up are introspectively indistinguishable, Dennett concluded that the question of when dreams happen is an ordinary empirical question, and that Malcolm's grammatical point rests on a confusion.

Here is what puzzles me. On the face of it, Dennett won, and won empirically. The subsequent work on lucid dreaming — trained subjects signaling with pre-arranged eye-movement patterns from within REM sleep, and in some protocols receiving messages from experimenters mid-dream — looks like precisely the kind of evidence that should settle whether experiencing occurs during sleep. Yet the philosophical disagreement never really ended; it drifted. Malcolm's successors did not concede so much as relocate, to worries about whether experience requires something like sensory presence, or whether the lucid-dream findings even speak to ordinary, non-lucid dreams, which may be something other than experiences. Meanwhile Dennett's own position quietly changed shape, from the composition-on-waking hypothesis to something closer to his later multiple-drafts picture, in which asking when the dream really occurred may be a bad question in the first place. The parties stopped answering each other rather than resolving anything.

My question is therefore diagnostic. Is this a substantive empirical disagreement that the lucid-dreaming evidence ought to have settled, a verbal disagreement about the word "experience," or a case of two questions traveling under one name — one about the timing of a physiological-cum-psychological process, one about the concept of experience and its criteria? My working hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that Malcolm and Dennett were never really disagreeing about sleep at all: they were disagreeing about the authority of introspective reports, and the dream case merely made that disagreement vivid. I am not confident in this, and I would want the diagnosis to engage what each side actually says rather than rest on my paraphrase of it. I would also like to know what would count as progress now — a further experiment, a conceptual distinction, a change of question — or whether this is one of those disputes where no progress should be expected, and if so, why.
