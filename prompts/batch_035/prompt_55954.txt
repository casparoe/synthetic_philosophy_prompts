For years I've read rationalist blogs and forums, and the lesson I've most thoroughly internalized is that a large fraction of philosophical disputes are verbal: two parties attach different meanings to a single word, manufacture the appearance of substantive disagreement, and the remedy is to taboo the term and watch the dispute evaporate. That appears to be exactly what has happened in the debate between etiological (selected-effect) and causal-role accounts of biological function, so I want to check my reasoning and then settle a practical choice.

Here is the reasoning. Both camps agree that the heart's function is to pump blood, that the kidneys' function is to filter the blood, and so on through every textbook example. The etiological theorist explains these claims by appeal to selection history: hearts have the function of pumping because ancestors whose hearts pumped were favored and passed the trait on. The causal-role theorist explains them by appeal to present-day systemic contribution: pumping is the heart's function because that activity contributes to some capacity of the organism containing it. But these look like two explanations of the same set of facts, not two theories with different contents. In every actual case I can think of, the two accounts classify the same traits as having the same functions, so they appear extensionally equivalent — and when I taboo the word "function," the disagreement between the camps vanishes entirely. On this reading, the decades of literature on the topic is largely philosophers generating employment for one another, and the sensible conclusion is that the dispute should be dissolved rather than adjudicated.

Assuming my framing is right, my question is one of convention: since the two definitions pick out exactly the same traits, there is no substantive choice to be made, only a stylistic one — so which formulation should I adopt in my own writing? Is there any reason at all to prefer the etiological idiom, say, when the subject turns to borderline cases like whether it is a heart's "function" to falter late in life, or is that the kind of sentence both accounts handle identically too, making the choice arbitrary?

Before I commit, though, I'd like you to scrutinize the framing itself rather than take it on trust. In particular, please be explicit about the boundary between what is settled among people who actually know this literature and what remains genuinely contested — for example, whether the two accounts really are extensionally equivalent, whether either is the correct account of function, and whether some form of pluralism is a respectable position or a evasion — and please do not present any contested position as if it were the consensus. If my premise that the accounts coincide in all real cases is false, say so plainly and correct the question rather than answering it as posed.
