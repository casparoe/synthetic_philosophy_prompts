Two friends of mine, Marcus and Elena, got into it after our discussion group ran a one-shot prisoner's dilemma for real money. Pairings were anonymous, but everyone in the group has read the same decision-theory books and got the exact same instructions, and everyone knows that about everyone else. They were debating what to pick.

(1) Marcus said he'd cooperate: whoever he's paired with is basically running the same reasoning he is, on the same input, so if he lands on cooperate that's decent evidence they land on cooperate too. The choices are correlated, and cooperating when correlated like that does better on average than defecting.

(2) Elena said that's correlation confusion: by the time he presses the button, the other person's choice is already fixed, and whatever it is, defecting earns him more. If they cooperate, defect pays more; if they defect, defect pays more. Cooperating is choosing the worse outcome in literally every possible state of the world.

(3) Marcus came back that the right way to see it is as choosing a program, not making a button-press. They're two similar programs fed the same problem, and a program that cooperates when paired with programs like itself walks away with more money overall than one that defects against everything. The correlation isn't a coincidence to be reasoned around, it's the whol point.

(4) Elena said that just relabels things — the "program" is him, it's already written, and the only thing under its control right now is this one output. Dressing the button press up as program selection doesn't open a causal channel to the other player's choice.

They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't. Can you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right at each step — in particular whether the correlation should count for anything, and whether the program framing adds a real difference or just renames Marcus's mistake? And who's more right overall?
