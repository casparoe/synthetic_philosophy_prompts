Write a dialogue between an epistemicist and a supervaluationist about the sorites paradox. The setting: the two philosophers are observing a laboratory technician who, at their request, is removing grains of sand one at a time from a pile containing exactly 10,000 grains, and the interlocutors have agreed to signal the moment they believe the pile first ceases to count as a heap.

The dialogue should have real back-and-forth: each character should respond directly to the other's points, pressing objections rather than delivering prepared lectures. In particular:

- The epistemicist should maintain that there is a determinate, sharp cutoff — some exact number of grains at which the pile stops being a heap — while conceding that this cutoff is unknowable, and should offer a margin-for-error style explanation of why our inability to know the cutoff is exactly what we should expect rather than evidence against sharp boundaries.
- The supervaluationist should explain why the sentence "this is a heap" can be neither true nor false in the borderline region, defend the claim that a statement comes out supertrue only if it is true on all admissible precisifications, and argue that tolerance intuitions are preserved because removing a single grain can never change a supertrue "heap" verdict into a superfalse one.
- At some point, each character should deploy the problem of higher-order vagueness against the other: the supervaluationist should argue that "there is a heap" and "there is definitely a heap" cannot have identical extensions on pain of reintroducing sharp boundaries, while the epistemicist should press the charge that "admissible precisification" is itself a vague notion, so that the supertruth account simply relocates the sharp cutoff into the semantics rather than eliminating it.
- The characters should also disagree about what their standoff implies for the running experiment: whether the technician should report a number, report indeterminacy, or simply refuse to say.

Both characters should be equally competent and intellectually honest — neither should be a strawman, and each should concede genuinely forceful points made by the other. The dialogue may end without a winner, but it should not end with both characters simply agreeing to disagree in a way that papers over their substantive differences; make clear what, if anything, the exchange revealed about where the real crux between the two views lies.
