I am a retired ecologist — thirty-seven years in population dynamics, the last decade of which was spent largely on statistical methodology — and only since retiring have I begun reading philosophy of science and epistemology in earnest. A literature I keep encountering, on higher-order evidence, epistemic akrasia, and peer disagreement, strikes me as touching directly on something I lived through at the bench, but I cannot tell which parts of it are load-bearing and which are scholastic. I would value your judgment.

First, the situations that motivate my question, so that the prioritization has something to be relative to. Late in my career, a graduate student, as part of a broader audit of the lab's forecasting record, compared my preregistered effect-size predictions against the measured outcomes across roughly sixty studies. The pattern was unambiguous: I overestimated magnitudes by about a quarter on average, in a direction that flattered my theoretical commitments, and the overestimation was worst precisely in the subfields I knew best, where my confidence was presumably highest. I took this seriously, yet when the next round of predictions came due I found I could do nothing clean with it. Discounting every estimate by a flat factor felt like akrasia — overriding first-order reasoning that I could still inspect and that still looked impeccable to me, on the strength of a second-order statistical finding. Refusing to discount felt like ignoring evidence, which as an empiricist I am not entitled to do. And I could not find a principled middle, because the evidence bore on the very faculty that would have to do the averaging. A second episode: a junior collaborator once deferred to my judgment in a dispute over a shared dataset, evidently on the strength of my reputation — the same reputation the audit had quietly undermined. Whether I was obligated to disclose the audit to her, and what the disclosure would have rationally required of her, I still do not know.

Here is what I want from you. Write as a professional epistemologist who also advises working scientists, and think carefully rather than reflexively: before ranking anything, briefly brainstorm the candidate open questions you take this literature to contain, including ones I have not gestured at. Then rank, say, the three to five questions that matter most for the practice of a scientist in my situation, and defend the ordering explicitly. By "matter most" I mean for the practice, not for the seminar room: which questions, if settled, would actually change what a person in my position ought to do with the next prediction — and which of the literature's preoccupations are, in your view, overrated for this purpose or do not touch the practice at all. As provisional candidates I have in mind: whether higher-order evidence enters the same probabilistic calculus as first-order evidence, or instead operates at a second level governed by its own norms; whether conforming one's confidence to the audit while one's object-level reasoning still looks impeccable is epistemic akrasia or its opposite; what the rational response to the junior collaborator's deference would have been; and whether a cognitive faculty can rationally self-correct using evidence that indicts that very faculty, or whether such stepping outside is impossible in principle. Treat this list as provisional, not canonical. For each question you rank, say what makes it upstream or downstream of the others, whether it is tractable with the field's current tools or would need something genuinely new, and what would have to be true for you to move it up or down the list. Please keep the whole thing self-contained: no bibliographies, no citations, no reading recommendations — I want your reasoning, not a syllabus.
