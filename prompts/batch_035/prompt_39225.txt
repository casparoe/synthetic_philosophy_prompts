I'm writing a paper defending infinitism about epistemic justification — the view that a belief is justified only by a supporting reason, which is itself justified only by a further reason, and so on without terminus — and I need to stress-test it against the strongest available opponent. Please play that opponent: a committed foundationalist who holds that some beliefs (perceptual experiences, intuitions, self-evident truths) are justified non-inferentially, that the regress of reasons must terminate somewhere or else no belief is ever justified, and that this termination is the only alternative to arbitrariness. Make her sharp, not a caricature: she may concede small points when I earn them, but never the main one, and she should respond to what I actually say rather than to a version of it she finds easier. She takes a definite position in every reply — no surveying the options, no 'perhaps both views capture something'. Stay in character until I write 'stop'. Keep each reply to one or two paragraphs so it feels like a live exchange.

Here is my opening move. You foundationalists locate the arbitrariness problem in the infinite regress and solve it with a class of beliefs that need no reasons. I want to argue you've mislocated the problem. Consider what a putative foundation actually is on your own account: a belief that serves as a reason for others while lacking the reason that would justify it. Truncate any sufficiently long justificatory chain at an arbitrary point and you get exactly that structure. So my charge is that your termini are not a principled stopping place but a cutoff chosen by fatigue, and that the belief you stop at has, at that point, precisely nothing the infinitist's chains lack — except a next step that you've refused to take.

To make this concrete, I'll borrow a move from the mathematics of non-well-founded structures. Circles of justification — the coherentist's chains that loop back on themselves — are not a third kind of thing distinct from infinite chains: a circular chain is just an infinite chain with a repeating pattern, every link supported by the next, no more and no less than in a non-repeating descent. So coherentism collapses into infinitism, and the two serious positions in the debate are yours and mine. Your regress argument, I take it, is that justification is transitive along the chain of reasons and that nothing infinite can do the work a terminus does. But notice what we finite minds actually require: not that we survey the whole chain, only that a further reason be available on request, whenever we press — just as a general mathematical result can be valid though no one ever completes every instantiation of it. The finitude of our psychology is a fact about us, not about justification itself.

Your move: tell me why a foundation gives the beliefs resting on it anything more than unearned trust, and what you say to my claim that your termini are just arbitrarily truncated chains — given that you yourself insist every non-foundational belief demands a further reason, so I don't see how you can deny that the foundational ones demand one too.
