I'm a charge nurse on an acute admissions ward and we're getting more and more patients turning up with psychiatric advance directives — sometimes called self-binding contracts or Ulysses contracts — where they wrote down, while well, what they want done when they're not well. Refuse this drug, don't refuse that one, contact my sister before you consider ECT, don't believe anything my brother tells you about my wishes. Some are formal, witnessed documents, some are letters to themselves, one guy brought his printed as a small laminated card which was oddly moving. We have no consistent way of handling them. Some consultants basically honour them reflexively, some treat them as wallpaper, and the rest of us make it up per shift, which I don't think is fair on anyone including the patient.

What I want is a decision procedure my team can actually run at the point of admission, when we've got the person in front of us and maybe 20 minutes. It should be grounded in whatever the philosophy of precommitment actually says — the Ulysses and the sirens stuff, the question of whether the person who wrote the directive is the "real" patient or just an earlier snapshot, the worry that honouring these things just lets the well self boss around the ill self, and how constitutional thinkers handle the same problem when a founding generation binds later ones (there's something about how hard it should be to un-bind yourself that I suspect is relevant). But translated into steps we can follow, not an essay.

Specifically: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with "don't honour this", and where it deliberately hands off to human judgement because it's a values question not a facts one. It needs to handle the question of mid-episode revocation — patient arrives in crisis, tears up the directive, says they've changed their mind — and I want to be honest that I don't know if a procedure can settle that or whether it's the hard core of the whole thing. Also whether the strength of the directive should depend on how formally it was made, which feels wrong to me (a scrawled heartfelt letter vs a witnessed legal document) but maybe isn't.

Then run it on three cases:

1. Woman with schizoaffective disorder whose directive refuses olanzapine specifically after she gained 30kg on it, listing two acceptable alternatives — but she's arrived floridly psychotic and refusing all medication, not just the olanzapine.
2. Man with alcohol dependence who pre-authorised his wife to have him kept in for a week if he shows up drunk asking to go home, and he's now here, drunk, begging to go home and clearly meaning it.
3. A directive that looks like it was written mostly to please the patient's father, who drafted it and drove him to the appointment — the "signature" question.

Include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer I won't like — I'd want the wife's contract honoured, personally, and I suspect a decent procedure might not let me. And be clear about what the whole thing can't do: presumably it can't tell us whether honouring a past self's wishes against a present self's protests is care or coercion, only when we're doing it. Keep the philosophy real but assume the person running it has read none of it. Also please spell out the failure modes — I'd rather know in advance where this procedure misfires than find out at a serious incident review.

(fwiw we're in England, so we're working alongside the Mental Capacity Act, but I want the procedure to be about the ethics of it, the legal stuff we can look up seperately.)
