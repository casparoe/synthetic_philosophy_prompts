I'm a third-year philosophy student who has just finished a course on perception and epistemology, and over the holidays I read a popular psychology book whose chapter on perception left me uneasy. Its summary of the cognitive penetration debate, as best I can reconstruct it, went roughly like this: 'For decades psychologists assumed perception was a one-way street: light hits the retina, information flows upward, and cognition only gets involved afterwards, at the level of belief and judgement. That picture has now collapsed. We know that perception is thoroughly penetrated by belief, desire, and expectation. The classic demonstration came in the late 1940s, when Bruner and Postman showed that people literally could not see doctored playing cards: a red spade was seen as an ordinary black spade until the perceptual system was forced to catch up. Since then, the evidence has poured in: poor children see coins as larger than they are, hungry people see food as closer, and once you know a tomato is ripe, you see the ripeness in the colour itself. Any top-down influence on neural processing counts as cognitive penetration, and because visual processing is saturated with such influences — attention, expectation, prediction — the question is settled: all perception is theory-laden. The philosophical sting was drawn long ago: if beliefs shape what we see, then perception can no longer justify those very beliefs, since we would have to know our beliefs were true before trusting what we see, which makes perception useless as a source of knowledge altogether.'

Parts of this are compelling, but my lecturer spent two full weeks insisting that most top-down effects do not amount to cognitive penetration in the sense philosophers care about, and I suspect the chapter is trading on that ambiguity. I'd like a careful audit rather than a debunking, and I'd be grateful if you could structure your reply under four headings. First, a section called 'Verdict in brief' of about three sentences, stating how much of the passage survives scrutiny. Second, a section called 'What a perception scientist would accept' of roughly 150 words, covering the claims that are accurate or at worst harmless simplifications. Third, a section called 'The three trouble spots' of roughly 250 words, identifying each genuine misrepresentation in turn — for each one, saying both what the literature actually holds and whether it is a subtle distortion or a straightforward falsehood. Fourth, a section called 'Does the epistemological conclusion survive?' of roughly 150 words, on whether the epistemological payoff announced at the end follows even from the accurate parts of the summary, or whether it depends on one of the distortions. One thing I would especially like addressed along the way: whether the chapter's stipulation that any top-down influence on neural processing counts as penetration is a harmless simplification or in fact the load-bearing error, since it seems to do the work of getting from plausible findings to the sweeping conclusion that the question is settled.
