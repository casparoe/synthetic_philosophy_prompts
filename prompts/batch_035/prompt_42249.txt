I'm a PhD student in econ, mostly doing decision theory / epistemic game theory stuff, and I'm TAing an intro strategic thinking course this term where the professor wants pop-culture hooks for each lecture. So last week a few of us did a movie night for the prisoners' dilemma lecture and watched the ferry scene in The Dark Knight, and ever since I've been unable to stop chewing on it, because everyone casually describes that scene as "the prisoner's dilemma where humanity passes the test," and once you watch it with Hofstadter's superrationality in mind it's way messier than that.

Quick sketch of the lens so we're on the same page: superrationality, from Hofstadter's old Scientific American column, is the idea that if you know the other player is reasoning like you, you shouldn't treat their move as independent of yours — you're effectively choosing the pair of moves, not your move given theirs — so in a one-shot PD the superrational agent cooperates, and it's not tit-for-tat, not repetition, not communication, just the symmetry of the reasoning. The ferry scene fits almost too well on paper: two ferries, each holding the detonator for the other, destroy the other boat before midnight or the Joker blows both at midnight. But everytime I try to call it a superrationalist triumph something fights back. The civilians take an actual vote, and if I'm remembering right the vote goes for blowing up the prisoners — they just can't find anyone willing to press the button. And the prisoner who chucks the detonator out the window doesn't look like he's running Hofstadter's reasoning at all; he looks like he's refusing to run any reasoning. Meanwhile the Joker is the one running the experiment and betting hard on defection — he's basically a guy with a very confident prior about which decision theory ordinary people use. Is he just wrong, or is the movie saying something about where cooperation actually comes from that superrationality can't capture? And is it even a clean PD, given the deadline, the asymmetric populations (one boat full of convicts, and the civilians know it), and the fact that neither side has much reason to think the other boat is a co-reasoner rather than a bunch of strangers or criminals?

Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no intro or conclusion, no headings. Roughly I'd want: whether the scene's game is actually a PD or something stranger; what the vote does to the superrational reading versus what the refusal to press the button does; whether the cooperation the film shows comes from somewhere superrationality mislocates; where exactly the Joker's model of the players goes wrong; and one thing the scene dramatizes about all this that the decision theory literature doesn't. If you think the superrational reading just fails for this scene, say so in one of the points instead of rescuing it — I'd rather know.
