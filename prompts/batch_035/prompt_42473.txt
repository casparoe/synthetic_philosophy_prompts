After thirty years of drafts, my series detective has been revised so many times that not a single sentence, trait, or mannerism from the first manuscript survives; the publisher now proposes that another author continue the series after me. My writing group has split into two camps that have argued for a year: the continuity camp holds that the character remains the same character throughout, in the way the Ship of Theseus remains the same ship, provided the changes were gradual and each revision was recognizably a revision of its predecessor; the conventionalist camp holds that sameness of a fictional character, like sameness of a ship, is not a hidden fact we discover but a convention we settle, so that the question of whether the ghostwritten detective is "really" my character has no answer independent of what publishers, readers, and we decide. Draft the joint statement these two camps could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration: the numbered claims both accept about fictional identity and the Ship of Theseus parallel; the precise points of residual disagreement, each phrased so its own side would endorse the wording; the cruxes — I suspect one is whether gradual replacement preserves identity only when some fixed element, an origin or "keel" like the unpublished first draft, persists, and another is whether reader uptake can constitute rather than merely track sameness; and, for each crux, a concrete case or test both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Conclude by saying whether any part of their dispute is merely verbal, and if so, which part and why. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table.
