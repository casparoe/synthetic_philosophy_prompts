A disagreement broke out at the end of a philosophy of biology reading group I sit in on, and both parties have asked me to write up the exchange because I'm the one with no stake in it. The topic was Dennett's notion of free-floating rationales — the idea that there can be reasons in nature without any reasoner who grasps them, as when we say the reason a fox is camouflaged is that camouflage concealed its camouflaged ancestors from predators, even though no fox, and no selection process, knows or understands anything. What follows is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it, move by move.

First, Mara said the whole notion rests on a pun. A rationale, she argued, is the kind of thing that can be stated, weighed, or acted for; it presupposes a mind to have it. The camouflage story is a true historical claim about differential reproduction, and calling it a reason is metaphorical shorthand that Dennett has dressed up as ontology. Nothing floats free, she said; it's cause all the way down, and 'reason' is just our label for certain causes we find illuminating.

Second, Julius replied that the explanatory form itself defeats her. When asked why a cuckoo chick ejects its nestmates from the nest, we answer by citing the benefit of doing so — that benefit is what the behavior is for, and the explanation has the shape of a reason-giving explanation, not a mere chronicle of events. If reasons strictly require reasoners, then Darwinian biology cannot use one of its central explanatory forms, which is absurd; so some reasons have no reasoner.

Third, Mara said this gets the metaphysics backwards. The explanation is fine, but it reduces without remainder to an etiological fact: ancestors with the trait out-reproduced ancestors without it, and that is the whole story. Nothing in that fact is 'for' anything — even the phrase 'selected for' is breeders' intentional vocabulary smuggled back in, so the apparent reason-giving is interpretation laid on top of history by us, not something history contains.

Fourth, Julius answered that bare history underdetermines function, and that this is where something genuinely gets added. A heart makes thumping sounds, and those sounds may incidentally benefit the organism in some scenario, yet the heart is for pumping blood, not for making noise; the difference lies in which effects explain the organ's persistence through selection. Selection is precisely what converts fortuitous benefit into genuine point, and that point is the free-floating rationale — it is added to history, not projected by us.

Fifth, Mara raised what she called the promiscuity problem. If rationales need no mind to anchor them, what stops every selected side-effect from having one? A trait that was favored as part of a package deal acquires an equally good free-floating rationale for each of its incidental effects, and since there is no one whose purpose could adjudicate which effect is the point, 'the' rationale is just whatever our current explanatory interests single out. Free-floating reasons, she concluded, are reasons on paper only.

Sixth, Julius replied that the rationale is fixed by what selection actually favored, not by our interests, and that his view can happily admit that in some cases the rationale is indeterminate — tied selection, package deals — without concluding that reasons don't exist, just as vagueness about heaps doesn't abolish heaps. He then extended the point: a machine-learning controller evolved by search contains wiring that no human can explain, yet there is a fact of the matter about why the wiring works, a rationale no one has ever grasped — which, he said, shows such reasons were never ours to begin with. Mara's final word was that this conflates an engineering fact about what the wiring does with a reason why it does it, and that the difference is exactly the mind she keeps insisting on.

I could not referee this. Please adjudicate the six moves one at a time, saying who is right about which specific point and why, not merely who comes out ahead overall, and say what, if anything, both of them are missing. Throughout, please separate what is genuinely settled among informed people from what remains contested — for example, do not present any single account of biological function or of the status of reasons as the consensus view if specialists are in fact divided on it. Please write the whole adjudication in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way one would write a letter.
