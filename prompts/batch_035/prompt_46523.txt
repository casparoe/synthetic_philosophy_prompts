My department runs a little annual workshop on anthropic reasoning, and for the last two years the same two people have dominated it: one of them, a grumpy Bayesian who thinks the doomsday argument (Carter and Leslie style) is basically sound -- your birth rank among all humans is evidence about how long the species lasts, and learning you're roughly the 100th billion human should shift your credence toward fewer total humans than you'd otherwise expect -- and the other, who is just as committed to the self-indication assumption: the fact that you exist at all is more likely in worlds with more observers, and she thinks this exactly cancels the doomsday update, leaving you where you started. They've gone in circles about reference classes, about Gott's delta-t version versus the birth-rank version, about whether "observer" should be "human," "mammal," or "anything that can wonder about its birth rank," and honestly some of it sounds like they're using the same words for different questions. We've finally bullied them into an adversarial collaboration and they've asked me to draft the joint statement, but I'm too close to it. That's your job.

Write the document both of them could actually put their names to. I want a numbered section of claims they agree on -- including things like the formal statement of the birth-rank update, the fact that the reference class problem is real, and whatever they both concede about the strengths of the other side. Then a disagreements table where each residual disagreement is stated twice, once in wording the doomsayer would sign and once in wording the SIA defender would sign, so neither side is stuck endorsing the other's framing of their own view. Then the cruxes: I suspect one is whether the self-indication assumption is a legitimate way to handle the evidence of one's own existence or just double-counting it, and another is how much the reference-class relativity actually matters versus being a merely verbal issue -- if you conclude part of their dispute is verbal, say which part and why. For each crux, give the concrete case or argument both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Two cases they've already agreed to use: Bostrom's presumptuous philosopher thought experiment, and a coin-flip variant one of them cooked up last spring where a fair coin was flipped before humanity began, heads meaning 200 billion total humans ever and tails meaning only 2 billion -- they disagree in advance about what credence on heads a rational agent should have after learning nothing but its own birth rank, so put both predictions in the table.

Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table, then the cruxes. Keep the tone neutral enough that neither party would call it slanted, but don't sand off the disagreement either -- if the doomsayer really thinks the presumptuous philosopher is a reductio and the SIA person really thinks it's just how confirmation works, the statement should say so in each one's own words.
