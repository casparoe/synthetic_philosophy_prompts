I'm a retired atmospheric physicist -- thirty odd years building climate models, so trust me, I know what sensitive dependence on initial conditions looks like up close. Now I've got time on my hands and I'm reading ethics, and I keep hitting this thing people call cluelessness: the claim that for most acts you can't work out, even in principle, whether the total consequences are net good or bad, because everything ripples into everything else. Before I go read what actual philosophers have proposed, I want the spec first. Write the desiderata: what any adequate account of how to act under cluelessness has to deliver. Which cases it must get right -- a donation whose effects propagate through markets and other people's responses, an act that looks obviously good but whose knock-on effects could swamp the direct benefit, the situation where you know the sign of the near-term effect but nothing at all about the total. Which distinctions it must respect -- between can't-calculate and no-fact-of-the-matter, between what was reasonable ex ante and what turned out to be right, plus any others you think matter. And what it cant contradict of ordinary decision theory, since as a scientist I'd have assumed expected utility maximization right up until I met this problem. Give the case behind each requirement, say which you'd treat as non-negotiable and which are more like taste, and -- this is the part I care about most -- tell me straight if you suspect some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable. Then just briefly: how expected utility maximization, satisficing, and a rough "act on the foreseeable and let the rest go" view do against your list. Keep the weight on the criteria, the scoring can be rough.
