Analyze the 2002–3 Iraq WMD intelligence failure as a case study for pragmatic encroachment and epistemic contextualism, running the analysis in both directions: what the theories say about the episode, and what the episode does to the theories.

Here are the salient facts as I understand them; if I have any wrong, say so briefly, but the analysis should not turn on contested details. In October 2002 the US intelligence community issued a National Intelligence Estimate judging that Iraq possessed chemical and biological weapons and was reconstituting its nuclear program, resting largely on satellite imagery, defector testimony (including reporting later shown to come from a single unreliable source), and intercepted communications. In February 2003 the Secretary of State presented the public case at the UN Security Council. The invasion followed, and the subsequent inspections concluded that the stockpiles did not exist and the programs had been abandoned years earlier.

I want a disciplined mapping before any verdicts. Fix explicitly, for each moment you discuss: who is the candidate knower or the subject of the knowledge attribution; what the total evidence actually available to that person was (not what was later learned); and whose stakes were in play. That last one is the crux, and I want you to stay honest about it. In the textbook cases these theories are built on, the attributor, the actor, and the person bearing the costs of error are the same individual at a bank teller's window. Here they came apart: the analysts making the confident judgments bore professional stakes; the officials making the public assertions bore political stakes; the decision to act on the judgment sent other people entirely to bear the physical stakes. So my first question is this: on the interest-relative view of knowledge, whose practical situation sets the evidential bar? If it is the attributor's own situation, the theory predicts that officials facing enormous political stakes in the aftermath of 9/11 should have demanded more evidence before counting Iraq's programs as known — and if instead the high stakes were treated as a reason to lower the bar for acting, that looks like the theory predicting the opposite of what it says in the low-stakes cases. Take a stand on which reading the view actually supports, and say whether the episode reveals that pragmatic encroachment was built for individual epistemology and simply has no account of deferred and displaced stakes, or whether it can be extended without ad hoc machinery.

Second, contextualism. On the familiar contextualist picture, the standards for "knows" shift with features of the conversational context: practical interests at stake, error- salience, how much scrutiny is being applied. In the internal documents the analysts hedged and caveated; in the public presentation the same evidence was presented as settled. A contextualist can describe this as ordinary standard-shifting between a scrutiny-heavy context and an ordinary one — the same sentence true in the one context and false in the other. But notice what is odd here: the standard did not drift with the conversation; it appears to have been selected by the party with an interest in where it sat, and the shift ran in the direction of the shifter's advantage. So my second question: does contextualism diagnose this episode, or merely redescribe it? Can it distinguish ordinary context-driven standard variation from strategically engineered variation, and if it cannot, is that a problem for the semantic thesis itself, or just a reminder that semantics was never meant to be a theory of manipulation? Be careful not to answer this by smuggling in a normative verdict about the war; the question is about the theory, not the politics.

Third, and most important: say where the theories are silent rather than forcing them to speak. My working suspicion is that the deepest causes here were institutional — the politicization of analysis, the career incentives to tell superiors what they wanted to hear, the feedback loop in which the demand for confidence generated the supply of it — and that neither pragmatic encroachment nor contextualism has anything to say about any of that, because both are theories of the standards governing a single attributor, not of the systems that produce and launder the attributions. If you agree, show me exactly where the mapping fails, rather than gesturing. If you disagree, show me what the theories actually predict that came true.

Close by running the analysis the other way: state whether the episode confirms each theory, constitutes an anomaly it can absorb, or presents a kind of case it never anticipated — and, if the last, whether the needed extension is a modest widening or a change in the subject matter.
