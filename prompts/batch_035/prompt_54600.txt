I'm in a low-key reading group that's been working through Einstein's popular essays, mostly the religion-and-science ones from his later life. We just did the 1930 New York Times piece where he sorts religion into stages — fear-based religion first, then the social/moral kind with an anthropomorphic father-God — and finally what he calls cosmic religion: no personal God, no church, no doctrine, just awe at the lawful harmony of the universe, which he claims is the real root of all genuine science. One of our members kept insisting this isn't religion at all and that Einstein is just helping himself to the word, which got quite heated. Meanwhile I'd also read the bit in The World As I See It where he denies free will outright — my copy is in storage so I'm paraphrasing form memory, not quoting, but as I recall it he says he doesn't believe in freedom of the will, that Schopenhauer's line about how a man can do what he wills but cannot will what he wills accompanied him through his whole life, and that this awareness of unfreedom reconciles him to other people's behaviour even when it's genuinely hurtful, and stops him taking himself and others too seriously as independent agents.

What I'd like help with is working out how these two things fit together, because I can't tell if there's a real connection or if I'm manufacturing one. A few angles, but feel free to reorganise. (1) What actually is the cosmic religiosity supposed to be — an emotion, a judgement, a Spinozist intellectual love of God in all but name? And is the awe specifically directed at the fact that events are determined, or just at their orderliness? If the harmony he venerates is deterministic, that harmony includes the fully determined cruelties people inflict on each other, and I can't tell whether revering that differs from mere resignation. (2) He says the awareness of unfreedom reconciles him to others — but reconciliation sounds like a normative, almost therapeutic achievement, and I can't see how determinism by itself yields anything you're supposed to feel about it. Is he quietly importing a value that his physics can't ground, or is that complaint confused? (3) Relatedly, if everything including Einstein's own serenity is determined, is his recommendation of that attitude even coherent, or does he need a standing exception for himself as an arguer? We also argued about where his determinism came from in the first place — the block universe of his own physics, or just Schopenhauer and Spinoza, given he apparently thought quantum randomness was no rescue for free will either (dice aren't authorship, roughly).

Please answer in British English. Two requests on how you go about it: no reading lists or name-dropping of secondary literature as a substitute for the reasoning — this is for a discussion evening, not a bibliography — and if you think I've misread him, or that the religion essays and the free will material genuinely have nothing to do with each other and the whole question is a artifact of me reading them in the same week, just say that plainly rather than politely constructing a synthesis. I'd rather be told the premise is off than get a tidy answer.
