Got into a huge argument with my friend Dana last night (like three hours, my throat hurts) about whether heritability numbers mean anything for policy, and afterwards I realized the thing we were actually fighting about is a move I see constantly and I don't have a word for it. So: does this have a name?

The move is: someone cites a high heritability estimate for some trait, and then jumps straight to "so it's basically fixed, there's nothing to be done about it." Dana did it with IQ — she said something like "it's 50-80% heritable, so preschool programs and all that are a waste of money." It's not just her; I've seen people do it with weight, with mental illness, with athletic ability. And the jump is just wrong, right? Heritability is a statement about how much of the *variation* in a specific population living in a specific range of environments tracks genes. It says nothing about what would happen if you changed the environment, and it says nothing at all about any individual.

The cases that make this vivid to me: height is about as heritable as traits get in rich countries, yet the Dutch gained like 20cm over a few generations — the genes didn't change, the food did. Myopia shot up in Singapore and China within one generation, again highly heritable within each cohort, environment moved. And the killer example: PKU is 100% genetic — you either have the mutations or you don't — and it's almost perfectly treated by diet. Fully genetic, fully treatable, zero contradiction, because heritability isn't a claim about treatability at all.

So my question is several-fold. Is there an established name for this specific inference error — "high heritability therefore unchangeable/pointless to intervene"? The nearest things I know of are genetic determinism, but that seems to name a broader view about genes causing traits rather than this specific statistical mixup, and the naturalistic fallacy, which is about deriving ought from is and seems like a different animal (Dana wasn't saying genetic is *good*, she was saying genetic is *fixed*). There's also maybe some connection to the "fixed mindset" stuff in pop psychology but that's about individuals' beliefs about themselves, not about misreading a population statistic. I've also noticed the move has a mirror twin, which I fell into myself during our argument at one point: hearing "it changed a lot over generations" and concluding "so it must not really be genetic." As if changeability and heritability were on one slider instead of being answers to two different questions entirely.

If there's no established term, I'd honestly be happy with a good coined one plus a real definition, because I want to nail down the edges too: does the fallacy only occur when someone makes the leap *explicitly* from a heritability number, or does it cover the vaguer version where someone just says "it's in the genes" and leaves it at that? Is it still the same error when the trait really is hard to change for other reasons, or does that make the conclusion accidentally right while the reasoning stays wrong? And is there a population-level version of the mistake — like interpreting cross-country differences using within-country heritability estimates — that deserves to be counted as the same fallacy or is its own thing?

Please don't just give me a label and walk away. I want to know what the phenomenon exactly is, what it's not, and where it stops. Dana and I are meeting for coffee next week and I want to be armed.
