My closest friend and I spent most of Saturday evening arguing about the procreation asymmetry and, by the end, mostly restating our own intuitions at each other. My position was roughly the one Parfit put on the table in Reasons and Persons: it is wrong to bring into existence a person whose life will go badly for them, wrong for that person's sake, yet it is not the case that we have any duty, or even a reason of the same kind, to bring into existence a person whose life will go well. Hers was a straightforward total view: if the presence of a miserable life makes an outcome worse, the presence of a happy one makes it better, and by enough to matter when populations are large. She pressed what she called the incoherence objection -- if creating a miserable person is bad for that person, creating a happy person should be good for that person, and my asymmetry can't explain the asymmetry -- while I pressed the non-identity problem, pointing to the case of the girl who conceives at fourteen and gives her child a life that is poorer than her other children would have had, but still clearly worth living, and asking her theory to explain why so many of our confident judgements about whom we may create evaporate when the child could not have existed otherwise. She is prepared to swallow the conclusion that a very large population of lives barely worth living can be better than a smaller, happier one, and I find that a serious cost of her view; she finds my refusal to count creating a happy person as any kind of gain a serious cost of mine.

What I would like from you is the document the two of us should have produced instead of shouting: a joint statement, as from an adversarial collaboration between a committed defender of the asymmetry and a committed totalist. Please give me (1) a numbered section of claims both could sign, stated in wording neither would reject, including claims about how the asymmetry and the total view are to be formulated, about the non-identity problem, and about what a life worth living is; (2) a disagreements table setting out each residual disagreement, each side's position phrased the way that side would phrase it; (3) the cruxes -- the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of us -- with, for each crux, a case, thought experiment, or argument both would accept as bearing on it, and each side's prediction written down in advance. If you think part of our dispute was merely verbal -- say, over whether the word "benefit" applies to someone who would not otherwise have existed -- please say which part and why.
