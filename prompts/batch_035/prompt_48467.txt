Using this contrast, explain what he takes sovereign power and disciplinary power to be, and what role Bentham's Panopticon plays in his account of discipline. "Foucault's contrast is between two ways power acts on the body. Sovereign power is spectacular and episodic: the king retaliates against the criminal's body in public, in proportion to the offense, and the point is to display the sovereign's force to everyone watching. Disciplinary power, which Foucault says emerged in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries in prisons, schools, factories, and barracks, is instead continuous and detailed — it targets what he calls the soul, and it works through training, timetables, and the measurement of individuals against norms. The Panopticon is Bentham's prison design where a central tower allows guards to watch every prisoner at all times, and the prisoners know they are being watched constantly, which is why they behave. Foucault thinks this design captures the logic of modern power because it renders surveillance cheap and automatic. On power-knowledge, Foucault basically endorses the old Baconian slogan that knowledge is power: the more you know about people, the more control you have over them, and vice versa. It is in this book, too, that Foucault develops his critique of the repressive hypothesis — the idea that power works by silencing and prohibiting, which he rejects in favor of seeing power as productive of subjects and of knowledge. As for replacement, yes: Foucault's whole argument is that discipline replaced sovereign power. The scaffold disappeared because discipline made it obsolete; once people are normalized from childhood, spectacular punishment is no longer needed, and sovereign power simply dies out in modern societies."
