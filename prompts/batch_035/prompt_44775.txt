I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm preparing a long retrospective feature pegged to the anniversary of the end-of-history moment: Francis Fukuyama's 1989 essay in The National Interest, expanded into his 1992 book, arguing that liberal democracy and market economics mark the terminal point of humanity's ideological evolution. The centerpiece of my piece is a confrontation I find genuinely fascinating and under-examined: Karl Popper. In The Poverty of Historicism — serialized in Economica in 1944–45, then published as a book in 1957 — Popper argued that large-scale historical prophecy is impossible in principle, chiefly because we cannot predict the future growth of knowledge, and that belief in laws of historical development fuels utopian rather than piecemeal social engineering. And in The Open Society and Its Enemies he named Hegel as one of the arch-historicists. Fukuyama, of course, built his thesis on Hegel, by way of Kojève. So the 1989–92 episode is almost a staged duel between a Popperian prohibition and a revival of exactly the tradition Popper spent two books attacking.

I'd like a careful analysis of the episode through Popper's framework, disciplined about the mapping. First: does Fukuyama's thesis actually qualify as historicism in Popper's technical sense? Popper distinguished unconditional prophecy from conditional scientific prediction, and Fukuyama's claim came with a mechanism — the directional logic of modern natural science, plus the Hegelian struggle for recognition — while also allowing for reversals and a long timetable. Decide honestly whether that is a Popperian law of history or a conditional prediction Popper should permit, and say where the distinction blurs. Second, Popper's self-fulfilling and self-defeating prophecy problem — the Oedipus effect: policy elites in the 1990s plainly believed the thesis or something like it, and it arguably shaped decisions on democracy promotion, European integration, and engagement with China. Should we treat the subsequent trajectory of the 2000s and 2010s — 9/11, the rise of an authoritarian China, democratic backsliding in consolidated democracies — as a failed prophecy, or as an illustration of Popper's point that belief in prophecies is itself a historical force that can refute the prophecy? Third, Popper's argument from the unpredictability of future knowledge seems to bite hard here: a defense of the thesis that in the 1990s leaned on technology as a vector of convergence has to confront technologies that nobody then anticipated, and Fukuyama himself later worried that biotechnology could reopen history by changing human nature itself. Finally, run the analysis in the other direction: what does the persistence and partial vindication of the end-of-history thesis — more democracies than in 1989, yet no shortage of war — do to Popper? Was his blanket prohibition on directional claims too strong if some directional, mechanism-based claims about social evolution turn out to be defensible? And is there a tension in Popper's own position, given his confident attachment to the open society as the form of social life that endures? Where Popper's framework is simply silent about this episode — say, about the normative and psychological appeal of the thesis, which plainly filled a need for meaning after the Cold War — say so plainly rather than forcing the theory to speak. What I most want is an account of which features of the episode Popper explains well, which he misdescribes, and which reveal that the poverty-of-historicism argument was never aimed at a thesis like Fukuyama's at all.
