As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I have spent the past few years writing about meditation research, hedonic adaptation, and the psychology of well-being, and somewhere along the way I drifted into sympathy with a position I have since seen called tranquilism: the view that what ultimately matters in a life is the absence of suffering and of craving for things one lacks, with positive states such as pleasure, joy, and euphoria having no intrinsic value — they count only insofar as their presence or absence changes how much one suffers or craves. Before I commit to this in print, I want to find out what I actually think, and for that I need help working from the ground up.

You are a professional philosopher working in value theory and the ethics of well-being. Please think carefully and take your time. Before answering, brainstorm candidate principles that might fit the material below — do not simply survey the standard positions and stop there.

Here are my gut verdicts on a set of cases:

1. The adaptation studies I've covered found that lottery winners, a year on, are barely happier than accident victims a year on. My verdict: the winner is not better off in the way that matters; if anything his desires recalibrated upward and he's slightly worse off.
2. A life of permanently painless, craving-free flatness versus a life of mild chronic wanting punctuated by great joys: the flat life is the better one.
3. A jazz musician absorbed in improvisation — energetic, fulfilled, wanting nothing while playing, not merely tranquil: I want to say this life is better than the flat one. That seems to give an energized state intrinsic value, which tranquilism denies.
4. A hypomanic person — euphoric, sleepless, bursting with plans: clearly not good for him, and I'd want to say that even though he reports feeling wonderful. Tranquilism seems to get this right only if euphoria secretly involves craving.
5. Someone who spends a month happily anticipating a holiday: the anticipation itself seems intrinsically good to me, even though it is the very state of wanting something one lacks. This is the one I keep going back and forth on.
6. A pill that guarantees lifelong, physically comfortable, perfectly contented craving-free numbness versus one's ordinary ups and downs: I resist recommending the pill, but I cannot tell whether that resistance is an insight or a bias toward the dramatic.

Is there a single principle that captures all of these verdicts, or close to it? If several candidates are live, sketch each and say which you prefer and why. For the principle you settle on, state it precisely, motivate each of its clauses with the case that demands it, and tell me what it predicts about cases I have not listed — for instance, whether a world of contented, craving-free beings whose experience is largely empty would be as good as a world of substantially fulfilled and flourishing ones, and what it implies about wanting things for others, such as one's children, where the craving is not one's own. Finally, be honest about which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and whether the cost of saving every verdict is a complexity that ruins the view's original appeal. I would rather overturn one of my gut reactions than end up defending something too qualified to be recognizable as tranquilism at all.
