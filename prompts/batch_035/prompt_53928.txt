I'm a novelist, three books out, and the one I'm finishing now is a fictionalised life of a Victorian industrialist, so the whole "great man with a hideous shadow" question is basically my day job. My agent Dana has extremely firm opinions about judging historical figures, and across years of book-launch dinners she's handed down a pretty consistent run of verdicts. I've started to think I can predict her, my partner says I definately can't, and I want to know before the next one.

Her record so far. Jefferson: she comes down hard. The "man of his time" defence gets nowhere with her — abolitionists were walking around in his lifetime and he knew it, she points out, since his own first draft of the Declaration went after the slave trade until the other delegates struck it out. Churchill: full pass. The racial views were horrible and she doesn't minimise the Bengal famine stuff, but she says those views were standard for his class and time, and the wartime leadership is what we celebrate him for, so it stands. Columbus: condemned, and specifically not excused by his era, because his own contemporaries were horrified enough that he ended up shipped back to Spain in chains. And the one that surprised me: MLK's dissertation plagiarism, which she waves off entirely — an ugly personal failing with nothing to do with what we honour him for, doesn't subtract from the ledger.

Now a magazine wants to run a roundtable on judging the dead and I've somehow been asked to moderate, and I already know these three are coming up. Aristotle, whose defence of natural slavery is stitched right into the Politics — the same body of work we admire him for — and as far as anyone can tell basically nobody in his world was saying otherwise. Wagner, whose antisemitism was loud, written down in essays, and known to everyone, though the operas themselves are the celebrated thing. And a living novelist, midlist and much admired by both of us, who it turns out has been genuinely cruel to the women in his life for decades — no era excuse available to him whatsoever, though none of it shows up on the page.

What will Dana say about each of those three? Honestly I care less about the answers than about the rule she's actually running — whether it's era-relative blame (excused only if nobody in her position could plausibly have known better), some kind of centrality test (does the evil contradict the very thing we celebrate them for?), or a plain contributions ledger where the personal stuff is weighed against the achievement. As far as I can tell at least two of those fit her four verdicts equally well, and my partner and I have each backed a different one. Give me your best guess on each case with your reasons, say which rule you'd put your money on, and — since I'll have the drinks table to myself with her afterwards — tell me what single further case, if I sprang it on her, would most cleanly tell the rules apart. And please write the whole answer in British English; she'll read over my shoulder and she's a terror about Americanisms.
