Quick context, then the question. I'm a psych grad student, and my dissertation has gotten tangled up in a debate that, as far as I can tell, has been running since the eighties without either side moving an inch: cognitive penetration of perception. The stalemate goes roughly like this. One camp holds that what you know, expect, or believe can literally alter what you perceptually experience -- your cognition reaching down into early vision and changing what things look like. The other camp, in the tradition of Fodor's modularity and Pylyshyn's old argument, says what looks like penetration is nearly always something else sneaking in: shifts of attention, changes in where you look, or post-perceptual judgment. The observation that a muddy patch looks redder when you're told it's a picture of a sunset doesn't show that seeing changed, only that guessing or deciding did. And the newer critics in this second camp (the Firestone and Scholl style objections, like the El Greco fallacy point) have set a bar the first camp keeps failing to clear in their eyes.

Here's why it's suddenly my problem. My study trained people on object categories where an ambiguous in-between shade almost always appeared as part of one object type and never the other, then tested how that same patch looks in a neutral context. I get a real effect: the point of subjective equality shifts a couple of degrees of hue depending on the training history. Half my committee calls this a textbook top-down effect on perception proper. The other half says it's a criterion shift, not a sensitivity change, and told me to run signal detection analyses. I did, and then they started arguing with each other about whether d-prime is even a clean measure here, since a criterion that's applied before some stage of perceptual processing can move d-prime too. Nobody moved. I left the meeting with nothing decided and my defense date looming.

So what I actually want from you is a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not a verdict on my experiment. What kind of dispute is this, really? My working hypothesis, offered as just a hypothesis: both sides largely agree about the behavioral and neural facts -- top-down signals demonstrably reach early visual cortex, expectation measurably changes discrimination performance in lots of paradigms -- and the fight is over how to carve up the causal stream into "perception" versus "cognition" versus "judgment," which makes it partly verbal, a border dispute that no experiment could settle because every behavioral measure is compatible with "the percept changed" and "the criterion changed" given the right auxiliary assumptions about where the criterion sits. But if that's true, I don't understand why very smart people have fought about it for forty years with so much heat -- purely verbal disputes usually get diagnosed and dissolved faster than this. So maybe there's a hidden substantive question doing the work: something about whether the perception/cognition border is a natural kind boundary or a pragmatic one, or about what modular architecture vision actually has, or even about whether perceptual content is the kind of thing that can be rationally influenced at all (which I gather connects to whether perception is a proper epistemic input rather than just another belief-forming process).

A few specific things I'd want you to take a stance on. Is the "no pure measures" problem -- the worry that every task mixes perceptual and post-perceptual contributions -- a genuine methodological obstacle that future clever designs could overcome, or a permanent feature that guarantees the debate can't be settled empirically? Are the two sides perhaps answering different questions under one label, one about the architecture of visual processing and one about epistemology, so that they're not even in conflict? And if you think progress is possible, what shape would it take: a new paradigm, a conceptual distinction, a decision to reframe the question (e.g., dropping the binary penetrable/impenetrable framing for a graded, stage-by-stage account of where in the stream information flows)? If you think no progress should be expected, tell me why the question keeps generating papers anyway instead of dying.

One more thing, and I mean it: please keep straight what's actually settled versus what's contested among people who know this literature. E.g., don't just assert that strong modularity of early vision is dead, or that the signal-detection critique is decisive, or that the whole debate was settled by some adaptation study -- I've had committee members on both sides present each of those as consensus, and at least some of them can't all be right. Where the field itself is split, say so, and mark clearly which parts of your diagnosis are the mainstream reading and which are your own reconstruction. If some framing you're using is itself a contested move in the debate, flag it rather than smuggling it in.
