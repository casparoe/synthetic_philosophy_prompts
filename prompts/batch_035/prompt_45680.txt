I sit on the medical policy committee of a mid-sized regional health insurer, and at our next meeting we must decide whether to cover a structured sixteen-session psychotherapy protocol for a condition our behavioral-health vendor diagnoses as prolonged grief disorder, a category now included in current diagnostic manuals. The decision turns on a threshold question our benefit design makes unavoidable: the plan covers treatment of "medically necessary mental health conditions," and the exclusion list names "bereavement-related distress that does not meet criteria for a mental health condition." Two of my colleagues have circulated rival memoranda, and the committee is split.

The first, an internist, holds that disease judgments should be value-free descriptions of statistically subnormal biological functioning. On that view, the intense yearning, preoccupation, and disorganization that follow a death are an expectable response to loss rather than a dysfunction; our role is to pay for medicine, not to underwrite the conversion of ordinary human suffering into billable illness, and each successive widening of the diagnostic manuals makes the problem worse.

The second, a psychiatrist, holds that judgments of harm are constitutive of the disease concept: the condition in question is severe, disabling, distinguishable from ordinary grief in both duration and functional toll, responsive to a specific treatment, and no value-free line could separate normal suffering from disorder anyway, because any such line must smuggle in assumptions about what a human life should be like. He adds that if we refuse coverage on the ground that grief is "not really medical," subscribers who cannot afford therapy out of pocket will go untreated, which is itself a harm we are choosing.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the meeting. It should (1) state the general criterion the committee should use when a proposed category's status as a disease or disorder is itself what is disputed, engaging both memoranda fairly rather than caricaturing either; (2) recommend a concrete course of action — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with conditions specific enough that the committee could apply them and the vendor could comply; (3) treat the strongest consideration on the losing side honestly, including the medicalization worry, rather than dismissing it; and (4) address whether a condition can properly be covered for reasons — treatment efficacy, access, parity with conditions we already cover — that do not require the committee to settle the underlying dispute between value-free and value-laden accounts of disease, or whether that would be evading the question our benefit design forces us to answer.

Please write the memo in American English, in a formal register suitable for circulation to a committee that includes clinicians, a lawyer, and a benefits administrator.
