I'm a retired chemist who fell down the philosophy rabbit hole a couple years ago, and I've gotten obsesed with the Peirce/James split on truth. Peirce, as I read him, says truth is what inquiry would converge on in the long run — an asymptote, like the curves I drew on blackboards for decades, never actually reached but perfectly well defined. James says truth is more like a successful experiment: a belief is true insofar as it works, gets verified, and squares with the rest of one's beliefs. Before I read another book on this, I want the spec first. Write the desiderata a pragmatist theory of truth has to meet: the cases it must get right, the distinctions it can't collapse. At minimum cover: the lucky-believer case (a belief held for terrible reasons that nonetheless keeps making good predictions — is it true?); Peirce's unsettled questions (questions inquiry just abandons, where he bites the bullet and says there may be no fact of the matter — a theory owes us something there); the collective-error case (what if inquiry converged on something false — is that even possible, and if a theory says no, why not); and the situated-inquirer case, where a belief works for one person's purposes but not another's. For each requirement, mark whether it's non-negotiable or just a preference of one camp, and which side it squeezes. And don't hand me a menu — take a stance on which requirements you'd actually hold fixed, and give me your committed verdict on whether any two of them can't be jointly satisfied (I have a hunch the collective-error one and Peirce's convergence idea are in tension, but I want your considered view, not a survey of what's been said). Format: a table with columns requirement | the case behind it | non-negotiable or camp preference | which side it squeezes, then a short closing paragraph with the verdict.
