In a seminar on political ethics we were discussing whether a president can permissibly authorize a targeted killing that will foreseeably kill innocent bystanders, and a classmate waved the whole worry away with the old line that you can't make an omelette without breaking eggs. It struck me that this proverb, and the family of remarks around it, is doing a lot of work while remaining completely vague, and I'd like to know exactly what I should concede to it and what I can resist.

Act as a professional philosopher specializing in political philosophy and the ethics of public office. Before writing anything final, brainstorm the readings thoroughly, then present a systematic analysis with the following structure. First, lay out the distinct precise claims the omelette proverb could be making when it is invoked in a political context, as an explicit menu of readings rather than a single interpretation. I want you to distinguish at least the following dimensions, plus any others you find worth separating: (a) readings about the moral status of the harm — that the harm is a mere means, or a foreseen side effect, or constitutive of the goal in a way that makes talk of 'costs' misleading; (b) readings about the kind of necessity involved — that no political success is achievable without such harms, versus that every practically available alternative carries equal or worse harms, versus merely that harms are statistically typical in politics; (c) readings about what follows — that the harm is permissible, that it is wrong but excusable, that it is wrong and remains a genuine wrong but one that a political agent must nonetheless sometimes commit (the dirty-hands reading in the strict sense), or that agents who commit such harms are in no way morally worse off and bear no residue at all; and (d) descriptive versus justificatory uses — whether the proverb is an empirical prediction about political life or a normative license for it, and whether it functions in argument as a conclusion, a burden-shifting premise, or a way of reclassifying the question so that moral criticism appears naive.

Second, for each reading, say whether it is plausibly true, trivially true, substantive and contested, or simply false, and explain what the strongest objection to it would be. Pay particular attention to whether any of the defensible readings actually get my classmate his conclusion about the targeted killing: I suspect that most plausible versions of the proverb excuse nothing and merely restate that politics involves hard choices, while the versions that would justify the killing depend on empirical claims about alternatives that no proverb can establish on its own.

Third, connect the best readings to the philosophical debate about dirty hands in politics: on which readings does the proverb amount to the claim that political office is a role whose occupants face genuine moral conflict, and on which does it instead amount to a consequentialist denial that there is any conflict there at all — and why does that difference matter for whether the agent should feel guilt, express regret publicly, or seek some form of penance? Conclude with a short verdict paragraph I could bring back to seminar: what the proverb most plausibly means, which of its uses are intellectually dishonest, and what the classmate would have to argue for separately if he wants to justify rather than merely shrug at the deaths of bystanders. Be careful and charitable throughout; where a reading is weak, say precisely why rather than dismissing it.
