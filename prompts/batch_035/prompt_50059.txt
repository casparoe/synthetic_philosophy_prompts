Been reading the rationalist blogs since the Overcoming Bias days, and there's one argument I've never seen actually get resolved: when we say a policy makes people "better off" or treats people "equally," what is the thing being equalized? Money? Happiness? Ability to do stuff? Everyone agrees on equal moral worth and then the rubber hits the road. I've watched people in EA circles fight about whether being born deaf counts as a deficit (the QALY people say yes, the Deaf comunity says no, we're a culture not a damage report), and whether the guy who developed a taste for champagne is owed more income than the guy who's happy with beer. Also somewhere in the 1930s an economist declared interpersonal utility comparisons meaningless and everyone just kept making them anyway, which makes me trust nobody. It's all vibes. So before I go read the actual distributive justice literature, I want the spec first, not a tour of isms.

Write me the list of requirements that any candidate metric of equality — the thing you'd literally equalize across persons — has to meet, with the concrete case behind each one (I care way more about the cases than the labels). Some things I'd expect to show up somewhere in the list, add or cut as you judge:

- it has to be operationalizable enough that an actual agency could measure it approximately, or else explicitly own up to being only a standard for comparing institutions in the abstract
- it has to take a position on expensive tastes and on people who convert money into wellbeing at wildly different rates (disability, chronic illness, being someone who needs quiet to function)
- it has to handle adaptive preferences — the person whose wants shrank to fit their circumstances — without either patronizing them or letting oppression set the baseline
- it has to say whether choice matters: is the guy who deliberately chose the low-income surf-bum life owed redistribution, and why is that case different from the person whose "choice" happened under bad options
- it can't be trivially gameable, and if strictly equalizing the metric predictably destroys it (leveling everything down to nothing), the theory should have something to say about that

Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's preferences dressed up as demands. And the part I most want: tell me whether some subset of these can't be satisfied together. I have a suspicion the operationalizability requirement is at war with basically everything else on the list, and I want to know if that's a real tension or me pattern-matching to "well, everything's a tradeoff."

At the end, briefly run plain welfare, plain resources, and capabilities against the list — short scorecard is fine, the criteria are the part I actually want.
