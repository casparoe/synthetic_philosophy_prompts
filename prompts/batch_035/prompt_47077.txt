I edit a small philosophy blog, and for an upcoming series on causation I want to build an interactive "which view of causation by omission do you actually hold?" quiz — the sort of thing readers complete in five minutes and then discover they were never in the camp they thought they were. The background debate is whether absences, omissions, and non-occurrences can be genuine causes: the gardener who never waters the plants, the doctor who never administers the antidote, the dog that didn't bark. The leaves of the quiz should be four positions: (a) the generalist extension of the counterfactual framework David Lewis set out, on which the plants died *because* the gardener didn't water them, the non-event counting as a cause in the usual dependence structure; (b) the no-absence view, on which causation relates events, a failure to act isn't an event, and so strictly speaking nothing is ever caused by an omission — our ordinary talk of neglect killing the flowers is shorthand for something else (claims about what would have happened, or prevention claims, or responsibility attributions); (c) the norm-sensitive view, on which an omission causes only relative to a duty, expectation, or role that singles it out — the gardener's not watering caused the death only because gardeners are supposed to water, and the same non-event in a normless world is no cause at all; and (d) the contrastivist view, on which "was the omission the cause?" is an incomplete question, since causes are always causes *rather than something else*, relative to a contrast, and different contrasts make the same omission both cause and non-cause.

Please write eight to ten questions. Each must be a concrete scenario with two or three answer options phrased entirely in plain language about what happened — no labels, no jargon, nothing that tips the reader off to which camp an answer feeds. The point is to sort readers by their reactions to cases, so no question may just ask "do you think omissions can be causes?". The set of cases must include: a norm case in which the same non-event occurs once with and once without an accompanying duty (a parent and a passing stranger both standing by while a child drowns, with the reader asked whether one inaction caused the death and the other didn't, or whether the two are on a par); a redundancy case in which two neighbors were each in a position to water the plants and neither did, so the outcome depends on neither non-event individually; a collective case in which the omission belongs to everyone at once (nobody calls the fire brigade); a double-prevention-flavored case (a goalkeeper who doesn't move, or a bodyguard who fails to step in); and at least one case aimed at the boundary between omissions and mere absences, e.g. whether my not having sneezed at a particular moment, or the rain's not falling, caused anything on the occasions we'd ordinarily mention. At least one option somewhere should let a reader say, in effect, "nothing was caused here; things just happened that way."

Then provide a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto the four leaves, and — this is the part I care most about — tell me honestly where the instrument fails. Flag any pair of leaves the quiz cannot in fact separate: I worry, for instance, that the norm-sensitive and contrastivist positions issue identical verdicts on every one of my mandated cases, since the cases I've chosen all happen to be norm-loaded, and if so I want you to say whether some further case could split them or whether they aren't separable by cases at all. Note any combination of answers that no live position occupies, and say whether that's because the combination is incoherent or merely unexplored. And if anything in my setup is off — if the fourfold division misdescribes the logical space, or one of my mandated cases is ill-posed, or a case I think diagnostic doesn't actually cut between the positions the way I assume — please say so plainly and adjust, rather than building quietly around my mistake.
