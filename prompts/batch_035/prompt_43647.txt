I'm a PhD student in philosophy writing the dissertation chapter I've been circling for a year, on higher-order evidence, and before I send a draft to my committee I want an outside audit of whether the package of views I've drifted into actually coheres. These views were acquired piecemeal — some from trying to defend a conciliationist line about disagreement, some from cases involving drugs and sleep deprivation — so they were never adopted as a system, and I honestly can't tell where, or whether, the pressure points are. Here is the set. (1) I'm an evidentialist about rational credence: at any time, what you're permitted to believe is fixed entirely by your evidence, in the familiar conditionalization-based way. (2) I'm a conciliationist about peer disagreement: when you and someone you regard as an epistemic peer, judging from the same shared evidence, land on different credences, each of you is required to move significantly toward the other — in the symmetric cases, roughly to split the difference. (3) I hold that higher-order evidence works by undercutting rather than by being counterevidence. My stalking horse is the calibration pill: a subject wakes with a vivid visual experience of a red speckle on the wall, then learns she may earlier have taken a pill that, in about ninety percent of subjects, causes vivid hallucinated red speckles while leaving everything else intact; she has no memory of taking it and no independent way to check. I want to say the pill information is not evidence about the wall at all — it doesn't make it less likely that the speckle is there — rather, it removes what she's entitled to do with the experience. (4) I'm firmly anti-akrasia: it is never rational to believe p while believing that your belief that p is unjustified, and I have never found an alleged counterexample convincing. (5) My actual verdict in the calibration pill case is that the subject's credence in the speckle should remain quite high, around .8 — the experience is just that strong, and the pill only tells her about herself, not about the wall. (6) I deny bootstrapping: you cannot acquire evidence for p from the fact that you believe p, plus the known general reliability of your beliefs on that topic; checking your own track record by recalling your past views and noticing they were right gives you no new support for what you currently believe. (7) I endorse the principle that evidence of evidence is evidence — if you have evidence that there is evidence for p, you thereby have some evidence for p — and I confess this principle carries a lot of the weight in my argument for conciliationism, since the peer's dissent seems to me to be evidence that I have mishandled my evidence rather than evidence against the proposition itself. For flavor: the issue isn't academic for me, since I recently graded a stack of anonymized seminar papers while badly sleep-deprived and only afterwards learned what sleep deprivation does to exactly that kind of evaluative judgment; the grades are recorded, the papers are gone, and I genuinely don't know whether to stay confident in them, which is perhaps why the undercutting view appeals.

What I want from you is an audit, not a survey or a lecture on the literature: identify which pairs or triples among these seven commitments are genuinely in tension, rank those tensions by severity, and for each say what the cheapest available repair is and what that repair costs me elsewhere on the list. Please also flag any tension that only appears to be one and dissolves on inspection, so that I don't discard a commitment I can actually keep — I'd rather you discriminate carefully than flag everything as equally problematic. And since my committee will interrogate whatever I write, please say as you go how confident you are in each claim you make, and single out the one point in your own audit that you are least sure of.
