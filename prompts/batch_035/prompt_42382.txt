I'm teaching a small intro ethics seminar (14 students, one 75-minute session) and next week we hit the doctrine of double effect and the trolley literature. I don't want to lecture at them about means vs. side effects and then show them the standard cases, because they'll just nod along. Instead I want to run an adversarial game where the distinction shows up as something they have to invent to save themselves. Here's the rough idea I had, and I'd like you to design it properly.

Split the class into two teams. Team A are "case writers": each round, they secretly write a pair of short scenarios (2-3 sentences each) that they predict Team B will judge inconsistently -- e.g., saying "permissible" to one and "impermissible" to the other. Team B members vote individually on both scenarios, and Team A scores a point if the majority verdicts come out inconsistent with how Team B voted on any earlier pair. No real money, but I want some token/score scheme that rewards Team B for consistency across the whole game, not just per-round accuracy, and rewards Team A for genuinely tripping them up rather than for writing maximally weird cases. Roles should swap halfway. The scenarios should be constrained somehow (maybe a fixed menu of ingredients like "a runaway trolley OR a hospital OR a lever") so it doesn't just become a sci-fi arms race.

Then I need a real debrief: how to map the moments where Team B, in their scramble to stay consistent, converges on something like the means/side-effect distinction (or the intention/foresight distinction, or "would the plan still work if the harmed person were absent?" style tests) onto the actual doctrine of double effect and its known pressure points. I'm expecting the loop-track style cases and the transplant/surgeon contrast to come up naturally -- build in how to handle those. Also tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (I'm guessing: case pairs that differ in five ways at once so nothing is isolated, and Team B inventing some ugly gerrymandered principle that "works" but nobody would defend), and what feature of the real philosophical debate the game just cannot reproduce no matter how I run it.
