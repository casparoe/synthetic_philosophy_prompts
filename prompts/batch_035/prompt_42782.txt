I want you to take the 2016 Colombian peace plebiscite and run it through the epistocracy vs democracy debate. Here are the facts as I understand them, correct me if I've got any wrong: in October 2016 Colombians voted on whether to ratify the Santos government's peace deal with the FARC, and the No side won by under 60,000 votes -- 50.2 to 49.8 -- on a turnout of just 37% of registered voters, so soemthing like 18% of the electorate actually rejected it. Nobody really disputes that most Colombians wanted the war over; the No campaign, led by Uribe, was mostly about the terms being too soft -- no real prison time for FARC leaders, guaranteed congressional seats for them, that kind of thing. Five days later Santos won the Nobel Peace Prize. His government then negotiated changes with the FARC, signed a revised deal in late November, and -- this is the part I care about -- instead of going back to the voters they took it to Congress, which ratified it. No second referendum. The people said no, the representatives said yes, and the representatives won.

Now the analysis. What does each side in the epistocracy debate say about this? If I'm remembering Estlund's terms right, his epistemic proceduralism holds that a democratic procedure is legitimate even with ignorant voters, so long as the procedure is qualifiedly acceptable -- no reasonable person could reject it -- and doesn't sink past the breakdown point, where it does no better than chance. So: was a 37%-turnout plebiscite that every poll predicted would go the other way already flirting with that breakdown point? And Brennan's competence principle -- high-stakes political decisions need a competent decider -- would seem to say the plebiscite was illegitimate from the start, since an electorate that mostly hadn't read a 300-page deal wasn't competent on it. And then what about Congress ratifying the thing? On Mill's plural-voting logic, the more competent body getting the final word on some questions is exactly the design -- is what happened here just soft epistocracy through a different channel, and does it get to look legitimate because the plebiscite had already failed, or does it look like a bypass precisely because the demos had spoken, even a 37% demos?

Then the part I actually want: be honest about what the episode does to the theories, not just what the theories do to the episode. Two things nag me. First, the No voters weren't obviously ignorant -- they had substantive objections about transitional justice, so the epistocrat's favorite premise (the people didn't know what they were voting for) may just misdescribe this case. Second, both theories are set up to answer who should decide this kind of thing, and what actually happened was that the plebiscite got treated as a diagnosis rather than a verdict -- the decision was rerouted after it failed. Is there anything on either side of the debate that predicts or forbids that, or does the episode show the whole framing misses that real systems are mixed, and the interesting question is which channel you route a decision through once it fails somewhere? Don't just tell me the Congress route was pragmatic statecraft. Tell me whether this case is a data point for the epistocrats, for the democrats, or honestly for neither.
