As a PhD student in linguistics working at the edge of psycholinguistics, I sat in on our lab's meeting last week, and two of the other graduate students got into a disagreement about a pilot study we are designing on Greek and English color categories. I stayed out of it, but the exchange had several genuine moves, and I would like a point-by-point adjudication of who is right about what.

The background: Greek has two basic terms covering the region that English covers with one — a term for light blues and a term for dark blues. Earlier work has found that Greek speakers are faster at discriminating color chips that straddle that lexical boundary than chips within one side of it, while English speakers show no corresponding advantage for the same physical stimuli. The pilot we are designing adds a concurrent-task manipulation: participants make same–different judgments on pairs of blue chips while rehearsing either a string of digits (verbal load) or a spatial layout (nonverbal load). The idea is that if the Greek cross-boundary advantage survives under spatial load but disappears under verbal load, the effect runs through language; if it survives both, it is presumably operating at a nonlinguistic level.

The exchange went as follows.

1. Mara, the psychology student, said the pilot could settle the strong-versus-weak question. If verbal load kills the advantage, that shows the effect lives in a verbal code — speakers are covertly naming the chips — which is only the weak claim: language shapes performance on tasks with a verbal component, not perception or thought as such.

2. Tomas, the typology student, said that was backwards. An effect mediated by a verbal code is still a linguistic-relativity effect, just not the strongest one, and whether language influences thought cannot be settled by stipulating that only nonlinguistic tasks count. Moreover, in ordinary life there is rarely a moment with no covert verbal activity in it, so the weak version, far from being trivial, covers most actual cognition.

3. Mara replied that on Tomas's position the hypothesis cannot lose: if an effect shows up under some conditions, it counts as confirmation, and if it fails to show up, the load was too light or the task insensitive. She challenged him to name, in advance, a pattern of results that would count against the linguistic-relativity claim.

4. Tomas accepted the challenge. He predicted that the cross-boundary advantage should be confined to tasks where a verbal code is a plausible mediator — same–different judgments, short-delay memory, free sorting — and absent where it is not, for instance in rapid parallel visual search for an odd chip among simultaneously displayed ones. If the Greek advantage turned up in rapid search as well, he said he would treat that as evidence against his own mediation story and count it, reluctantly, against the weak claim.

5. Mara was not satisfied. She said the same data would still get read differently by the two camps: a rapid-search advantage would be hailed by the stronger Whorfians as perception itself being reshaped, and its absence would be claimed by the weaker ones as exactly their view, so Tomas's risky prediction is only risky given a task taxonomy that everyone accepts — and which taxonomy to accept is precisely what is in dispute. The underlying disagreement, she concluded, is about what is meant by language shaping thought, and no single experiment can settle a verbal dispute.

6. Tomas answered that if she is right, the conclusion is not that the field is vacuous but that the demand for a single decisive verdict is outdated. If the claim is indexed to task and timescale — that language shapes performance of kind X as measured by task Y under conditions Z — then it is ordinary cognitive science, with ordinary piecemeal evidence, and Mara's insistence on one experiment that settles everything is the part of the framing that should go.

Please adjudicate each of the six moves: say who is right about what in each step, whether Mara's charge in move 3 is fair given what Tomas actually does in move 4, whether her point in move 5 about divergent readings survives Tomas's reply in move 6, and, finally, what, if anything, both of them are missing — in particular, whether the pilot as designed can bear the weight either of them wants to put on it, and what result patterns would genuinely discriminate between their positions rather than merely being absorbed by whichever camp predicted them.
