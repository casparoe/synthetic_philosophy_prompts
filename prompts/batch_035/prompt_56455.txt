I serve on the clinical ethics committee of a residential memory-care facility, and we need a written policy on what staff may tell residents with advanced dementia. The recurring cases: a resident who asks every evening whether her son has come to pick her up (he died four years ago), and a resident who becomes agitated trying to leave for a job he retired from decades ago. Current practice is inconsistent — some staff redirect, some tell the blunt truth, some say what the resident wants to hear. One group of nurses has proposed a formal policy permitting "therapeutic fibbing" under defined conditions; another group, several of whom cite Augustine's absolute prohibition on lying, wants deception forbidden outright, with redirection or truth-telling as the only options.

Draft the written opinion I'll circulate ahead of next month's meeting. It should recommend one concrete policy — forbid, permit, or permit with specified conditions — with reasoning both sides could actually engage. Please take the Augustinian position seriously rather than treating it as archaic. In particular, address: whether Augustine's definition of a lie (a statement made against one's own mind with intent to deceive) even covers a caregiver who, in the moment, genuinely enters the resident's frame of reference rather than intending to deceive anyone; whether his view that the wrongness of lying lies in the will to deceive itself, not in the harm done, can survive the fact that these falsehoods appear to relieve distress; and whether his distinction between lying and merely withholding or redirecting offers the anti-fibbing side a workable alternative, or merely relocates the deception from words to tone and gesture. Make clear where the strongest consideration against your recommendation lies, and why it doesn't prevail.
