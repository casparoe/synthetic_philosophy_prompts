Question 4 (25 points). In finite normal-form games, a pure strategy can be dominated not only by another pure strategy but by a mixed strategy over the player's other pure strategies. (a) Define strict dominance by a possibly mixed strategy, and explain why admitting mixed dominators makes iterated elimination more powerful. (b) Does the order in which dominated strategies are eliminated affect the set of strategies that survives? Treat iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies and of weakly dominated strategies separately. (c) What must the players know about one another for the iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies to be justified as ruling out strategies that will not be played? Be precise about how much knowledge each round of elimination requires. (d) State, as precisely as you can, the logical relationships among the strategies that survive iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies, the rationalizable strategies, and the strategies played with positive probability in some Nash equilibrium.

The student's answer reads as follows. (a) A pure strategy is strictly dominated when some other strategy, whether a pure strategy or a mixed strategy over the player's remaining pure strategies, yields a strictly higher payoff against every combination of the opponents' strategies. Mixed dominators make the procedure more powerful because a strategy can lose to no single alternative and still lose to a randomization: if two strategies pay 2 and 0 against the opponents' first profile and 0 and 2 against the second, neither pure strategy dominates a third strategy paying one half against each profile, since each pure alternative is worse somewhere, yet the fifty-fifty mixture over them pays 1 against both profiles and strictly dominates it. (b) The surviving set is independent of the order of elimination. For strict dominance the reason is that the payoff inequalities hold strictly against every combination of the opponents' strategies, so they continue to hold, and to hold strictly, after any of the opponents' strategies are deleted; a strictly dominated strategy stays dominated as the game shrinks, so every deletion order removes exactly the same strategies in the end. The same reasoning carries over to weak dominance: weak dominance, like strict dominance, is preserved under deletion of the opponents' strategies, so no matter which weakly dominated strategy is removed first, the procedure converges on the same surviving set. One does have to be careful with weak dominance for a separate reason: eliminating a weakly dominated strategy can delete Nash equilibria, so it is safest to restrict the procedure to strict dominance. (c) The procedure requires that the players are rational, in the sense of never playing a strictly dominated strategy, that each knows the game, and that each knows the other players are rational. This is enough for any number of rounds. Since every player knows that the others never play dominated strategies, each can delete the opponents' dominated strategies from the game, and since everyone knows that everyone can do this, each can also delete what becomes dominated in the reduced game, and so on down. One level of knowing that the others are rational supports the whole iteration. (d) The strategies that survive iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies are exactly the rationalizable strategies: common knowledge of rationality pins down precisely the strategies the procedure leaves in place. As for Nash equilibrium, no strategy played with positive probability in a Nash equilibrium is strictly dominated, so the equilibrium strategies survive elimination; and conversely, since the procedure removes only strategies that cannot be played in equilibrium, the surviving set is exactly the union of the supports of the Nash equilibria. So in a dominance-solvable game the survivor is the unique equilibrium, and in general the three sets coincide.
