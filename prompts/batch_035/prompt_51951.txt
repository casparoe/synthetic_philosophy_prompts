My reading group spent last month on Wigner's old essay about the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics, and it broke into a running argument between two members that never really got settled. Dev, who's doing physics, kept stressing the cases where the math came first: Riemann's geometry was sitting on the shelf for half a century before Einstein needed it for general relativity, and group theory was originally developed for thinking about polynomial equations, got dismissed by physicists as useless abstraction when it first showed up in quantum theory, and then turned out to be exactly the right language for atomic spectra and crystals. His point: nobody built these to fit nature, they were pursued as pure puzzles, and they fit anyway, often to more decimal places than anyone designing a tool would dare aim for. If tools built blind for one job keep turning out to be the right tools for a totally different job, he says, that's a fact about the world, not a coincidence you can explain away.

Lucia, who's more of a math person, wasn't having it. Her first move was selection bias: we remember the hits and bury the misses — for every group theory there's a junkyard of formalisms that matched nothing, and the hits get enshrined in textbooks so they end up looking like the norm. Her second move was that the overlap isn't mysterious anyway: mathematicians and physicists share their taste, both liking symmetry and simplicity, so they filter toward the same structures — same filter, similar output, no miracle. Dev pushed back that this would explain why some math is useful but not why the fits run so deep and so unexpected; nobody selected Riemannian geometry to fit gravitation. Lucia then made a different kind of point: the word improbable is doing all the work, and it's undefined — improbable relative to what? Nobody has a base rate for how often abstract math should turn out to be the right language, and only a tiny sliver of math and a tiny sliver of physics have ever actually been in contact, so a few lucky hits inside that sliver, then enshrined, can look like a pattern even if its noise. Dev answered with recurrence: the same structures keep showing up in totally unrelated places — group theory in crystals, in particle physics, in spectroscopy; Gaussians wherever independent errors pile up. One hit is luck, recurrence across domains starts to look like something about the world. Lucia's last word was that the formalism is elastic enough that the fit gets partly manufactured in retrospect: physicists used the Dirac delta for decades before distribution theory existed to make it rigorous, so math absorbing whatever physics needs and then calling it a ready-made fit is at least as common as math lying on the shelf waiting.

They more or less dropped it after someone quoted a quip — that the only thing more unreasonable than the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics in physics is the unreasonable ineffectiveness of mathematics in biology — and nobody could even remember where it came from. Can you go through their whole exchange move by move and say who's right at each step, not just who comes out ahead overall? And is there anything both of them were missing that the quip might be pointing at?
