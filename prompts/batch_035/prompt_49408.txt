I am a novelist, and my current book follows a string quartet across a decade: four musicians who rehearse, quarrel, tour, and slowly decide — member by member, and somehow also as a body — to commission and champion a piece that none of them individually loves. My editor, who read philosophy before she went into publishing, keeps pushing a stronger narrative conceit on me: the quartet, she says, is a single organism with four limbs, one mind thinking in one voice, and so the novel should include chapters narrated by the quartet itself, in a collective "we," and should let that "we" be the true subject of credit and blame — when the ensemble rushes the tempo, the ensemble did it, the way a body twitches. Musicians talk this way too ("we played as one"), and I gather that some philosophers of joint action have at least flirted with the thought that a group can literally intend, decide, and be responsible. But before I commit a novel's architecture to the picture, I want the analogy audited rather than merely invoked.

Please lay out, as explicitly as you can, what maps onto what in the comparison between a group of agents acting together and a single agent: what plays the role of the individual's intention, her belief, her decision, her resolve, her conscience. Draw on the main positions in the literature on collective intentionality as you know it — the Searle-style claim that "we-intend" is primitive and not built out of "I intend" plus beliefs about what you intend; Bratman's picture of shared intention as interlocking individual plans, mutually responsive and renegotiated over time; Gilbert's notion of a joint commitment that creates rights and obligations between participants in something like the way promising does. Then show me where the organism analogy breaks. Some candidate fractures I half-see from my reading: the quartet has no central executive (no conductor — coordination happens by eye contact and breathing), so nothing plays the brain; the members can jointly commit to an interpretation of the score while privately dissenting, and the group can settle on a tempo no individual member wanted, which a body arguably cannot do; the quartet can survive the replacement of every one of its members, which cuts both ways for the organic picture; and when the premiere fails, only individuals can feel shame, though perhaps the quartet can still be blamed. Tell me which of the disanalogies actually matter for my use — narrating the group, apportioning credit and blame — and which are merely picturesque. And tell me whether my editor's conclusion survives: can I narrate the quartet as one consciousness in good philosophical conscience, or does the organism analogy quietly assume the very thing it was supposed to establish? If it cannot carry that weight, propose a better analogy a novelist could structure a chapter around — a duet, a conversation, a handshake, a marriage of convenience — and say what it would get right that the organism gets wrong.
