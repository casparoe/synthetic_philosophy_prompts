After years on our condominium board I recently agreed to chair our building's new restorative-conflict process, where neighbor disputes (noise, shared renovations, the odd parking war) are handled through facilitated circles instead of escalation to bylaw enforcement. Our facilitator, who trained in South Africa, closes nearly every session with the maxim "a person is a person through other persons." Last week she invoked it against me: I proposed that two feuding neighbors exchange written apologies before any joint meeting, since their hostility was such that a face-to-face session risked re-igniting it, and she replied that routing the apology through paper "treats each of them as if he were finished on his own," whereas the whole point is that they must become present to one another. I half-agreed and half-bristled, but I could not say precisely what was at stake, because I do not know what the maxim actually claims. It seems to slide between at least these possibilities: a near-truism that our characters are socially formed; a psychological claim that reconciliation requires mutual recognition; a stronger claim that persons are in some sense constituted by their ongoing relations, so that an unattended relation leaves the parties themselves diminished; and a normative claim about what we owe to those we have wronged. Please lay these readings out as a menu, with a verdict for each on whether it is plausible, whether it is defensible at all, and — crucially — whether it does the specific work her objection needed, namely showing that a mediated written apology is inferior to a live encounter even when the live encounter carries a real risk of failure. Tell me which reading she is most likely relying on, and whether it can carry that weight. I would like something I can bring to our next facilitators' meeting.
