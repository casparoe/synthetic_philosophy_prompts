I run a graduate seminar on formal epistemology — twelve students, one two-hour session — and the next unit is on imprecise probabilities: the proposal that a rational agent's doxastic state is not a single probability function but a set of them, with credences in an event given by an interval rather than a point. The readings have already covered the Ellsberg-style urns, where people prefer betting on an urn of known composition to one of unknown composition, and the standard move of explaining this by treating the agent as holding a set of probability functions over the unknown urn. I want an in-class activity, not a lecture or discussion section: something with rules, roles, and real choices, in which participants hold and act on sets of probabilities rather than point credences, so that the philosophical content shows up in what they do rather than in what I say.

The activity should do at least three things, and the difficulty is arranging it so the participants encounter them rather than being told about them. First, they should feel that ambiguity is not just risk with a bigger variance — that acting on a set of probability functions is a genuinely different predicament from acting on any single one of them. Second, the activity should stage a divergence between the decision rules one might adopt over a set: choosing the option that maximizes expected utility relative to the worst probability function in the set, choosing an option that is admissible — optimal relative to at least one function in the set — and choosing an option that is not strictly dominated across the whole set. I want a moment where these rules mandate different choices, participants have been assigned to them as roles, and the group must watch the consequences of the divergence rather than merely hear that the rules disagree. Third, there should be a round in which everyone receives the same public evidence, updates every probability function in their set accordingly, and then discovers that for some event their interval has grown wider rather than narrower — the phenomenon sometimes called dilation — so that learning has made them less committal, and they should feel the strangeness of that before it is named.

Practical constraints: no real money — use tokens redeemable for something modest, or a leaderboard with stakes the students care about; materials should be cards, urns or bags with colored balls, and worksheets simple enough to run in ten minutes of setup; the technical vocabulary must not appear until the debrief, where you will map specific moments in the activity onto the formal distinctions. I also want two honest notes: what will predictably go wrong on the first run (groups confusing the set-of-probabilities state with mere ignorance about which single probability is right, arithmetic failures, roles collapsing), and which feature of the philosophical debate the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself — for instance, whether the set is meant as a psychological description of an agent or as a normative ideal, or the question of whether dilation is a defect of the framework rather than a consequence of it. Please give the complete design: materials, rules, payoff scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline for the two hours, the debrief, and the two notes.
