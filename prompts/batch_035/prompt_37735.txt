I'd like an analysis of the monstrous moonshine episode as a test case for Wigner's thesis about the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics in the natural sciences, with the case running in both directions.

Take the following facts as given. In 1978, John McKay noticed that 196884, the leading coefficient in the Fourier expansion of the normalized modular J-function, is exactly 196883 + 1, where 196883 is the dimension of the Monster group's smallest nontrivial irreducible complex representation. The Monster is the largest of the sporadic finite simple groups, and modular functions like J belong to a branch of complex analysis cultivated since the nineteenth century; the two subjects had no known point of contact. Further coefficients of J kept decomposing as sums of Monster representation dimensions with small non-negative coefficients. Conway and Norton dubbed the phenomenon "monstrous moonshine" and conjectured that it issued from a graded, infinite-dimensional representation of the Monster whose graded traces were specific distinguished modular functions. The conjecture was proved in 1992 by Richard Borcherds, working from a vertex operator algebra constructed by Frenkel, Lepowsky, and Meurman; crucially, the proof imported machinery that had no prior life in pure mathematics, including the no-ghost theorem from string theory. Borcherds received the Fields Medal for this work in 1998.

Now run the case through Wigner. His essay puzzles over why mathematics developed with no regard for physics keeps turning out, uncannily, to fit physics. Moonshine looks superficially like the same miracle, but the direction of fit is scrambled. The J-function is pure nineteenth-century mathematics, the Monster is pure twentieth-century group theory, and the bridge between them was built from structures borrowed from a physical theory that has never been tested against experiment. So my questions, in order of importance.

First, is moonshine even an instance of the phenomenon Wigner described, or does it show that his puzzle was aimed at the wrong target? His examples run from mathematics into natural science; here the traffic partly runs the other way, from physics-flavored constructions back into pure mathematics, and one might argue that this either vindicates a deeper version of his thesis, or that the very thing Wigner found mysterious -- no causal or purposive connection explaining the fit -- is absent here for a boring reason: string theory and the relevant algebra may share a common mathematical ancestry rather than meeting by coincidence. Second, does it matter to the case that string theory is empirically unconfirmed? If what we have is mathematics-derived-from-unconfirmed-physics being effective at organizing other mathematics, is anything unreasonable left over, or does the puzzlement survive in a different form? Third, does the episode discriminate among the standard responses to Wigner -- the selection-effect story, on which we notice the fits and forget the misses; realist explanations, on which both structures trace back to the same underlying reality; and deflationary explanations, on which we simply develop the mathematics our favorite problems demand -- in a way Wigner's own examples cannot? I suspect the absence of an experimental feedback loop here is doing important work, since the usual story about co-evolution between physical theory and mathematical apparatus has no obvious analogue when the organizing principle comes from a theory with no data.

Be disciplined about the mapping: say explicitly what corresponds to "mathematics," what to "natural science," and what to "effectiveness" in this case, since each correspondence is contestable. And where the Wigner framework simply has nothing to say, I want that stated plainly rather than papered over. End with a verdict: does moonshine confirm Wigner, constitute an anomaly, or reveal that his question was a different question in disguise?
