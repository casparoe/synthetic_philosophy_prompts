I recently read a long review essay, in a general-audience science magazine, of Hofstadter and Sander's book Surfaces and Essences, and I have been uneasy about it ever since, because the review made the book sound both impressive and wrong. The burden of the review is carried by one summary paragraph, which went like this:

"Hofstadter and Sander's central claim is that analogy is not one rhetorical device among many but the very mechanism of thought. The book's evidence is mostly experimental: in a series of controlled studies, the authors show that whenever we categorize — deciding whether a whale is more like a fish or a mammal, or whether an unfamiliar kitchen object goes with the cups or the bowls — we are silently making analogies, and they show the same process at work in children's overextensions, when a toddler calls every four-legged animal 'doggie.' Their account is essentially an application of Gentner's structure-mapping theory: a good analogy abstracts away from surface features entirely and keeps only the system of relations, which is why novices, who are stuck at the surface, reason so poorly. The deeper implication they draw is that the mind contains no innate categories at all — it is a general-purpose analogical engine that builds every concept out of the first analogies a child notices. The practical upshot, which the authors state frankly, is that analogy is the root of prejudice and rigid thinking, so education should train people to set their analogies aside and judge each case on its own terms."

I found this persuasive at first reading, partly because I know Gentner's name from a psychology course and the blank-slate framing sounded like exactly what a book arguing that categories are fluid would have to say, and partly because the closing conclusion feels like common sense about prejudice. But something nags at me: I have read some of Hofstadter's older work, and he has always celebrated the freewheeling conceptual slippage that analogies produce, which sits oddly with a call to set our analogies aside. I don't own the book and can't easily check it, so I would rather have an audit than a hunch.

Please go through the review's claims one at a time and tell me which ones Hofstadter and Sander would accept as fair, which are defensible simplifications of something they do hold, and which they would dispute, with a brief account of what they actually claim in each disputed case. I especially want to understand four things. First, what kind of evidence the book actually relies on — is it a report of controlled experiments, or something else, and does the answer change how much weight the thesis can bear? Second, whether the book really adopts Gentner's structure-mapping theory or instead takes its own stand on the role of surface features — and given the book's title, whether the reviewer has the relationship between surfaces and essences more or less backwards. Third, whether the authors really commit to a mind with no starting categories, or whether their story of concepts growing through analogy presupposes some initial anchors to analogize from in the first place. Fourth, whether that closing practical conclusion — rationality as the suppression of analogy — is something the book draws itself, a standard objection the reviewer has smuggled in as the authors' own conclusion, or a misreading of what the authors say about becoming conscious of the analogies we inevitably make. Finally, tell me whether the review's overall verdict survives the corrections, and sketch what a fair one-paragraph summary of the book's central thesis and method would look like instead.
