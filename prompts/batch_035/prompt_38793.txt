I am enrolled in a graduate seminar on the philosophy of psychiatry, and for our final assignment we have to draft the kind of document that an adversarial collaboration would produce, rather than another paper that merely restates the two sides. I have chosen the dispute between a committed neo-Kraepelinian nosologist and a Hackingian social-constructivist about psychiatric classification, and I would like you to write the joint statement they should be able to sign after a year of good-faith argument.

By the neo-Kraepelinian I mean someone who holds that DSM-style categories are provisional hypotheses about underlying dysfunctions — syndromes awaiting validators — and that the aim of nosology is to identify natural disease entities, however slow the progress. By the Hackingian I mean someone influenced by Ian Hacking's work on "interactive kinds" and looping effects: the thought that classifying people changes them, because classifications filter into self-understanding, clinical practice, institutions, and the marketplace of diagnoses, so that psychiatric kinds are not merely tracked but partly made. It would be fine to invent names or brief character sketches for the two collaborators, but please keep them recognizably within these camps.

Please produce the following:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. These should cover at least: what both accept about the epistemic functions of a diagnostic manual; that diagnostic categories shape the behavior of clinicians, patients, and institutions, not just record it; that the historical record contains cases where a category's meaning drifted (you might use the vicissitudes of autism criteria across successive DSMs, or the controversies around the bereavement exclusion for major depression); that subjective suffering is real and that neither side is skeptical of it; and that both reject the naive view that classification is a mere labeling exercise with no grip on the world.

2. A disagreements table. Each row should state one residual disagreement, phrased twice — once in wording the nosologist would accept, once in wording the constructivist would accept — so that neither entry is slanted toward the other camp.

3. The cruxes. I suspect one crux is whether looping effects merely contaminate the measurement of a stable underlying condition, or whether they partly constitute the condition, and I suspect a second is whether the analogy between psychiatric kinds and "indifferent" biological kinds (the kind that do not care how we sort them) does any useful work. Please identify whether you agree these are the cruxes, and add any others you find.

4. For each crux, a concrete case, historical episode, or line of argument that both parties would accept as bearing on it, with each party's prediction written down in advance — for example, what each would predict about diagnostic substitution, about what happens to prevalence when criteria tighten or loosen, or about how self-advocacy communities reshape symptom reporting. Please record what each side would concede if their prediction failed.

5. A verdict on which parts of the dispute are substantive and which are verbal — for instance, whether the two camps simply mean different things by "real" or "valid" when they argue about whether psychiatric kinds are real, and, if some of the dispute is verbal, say exactly which part and why.

One further request. In our seminar we have been toying with a distinction that I have not found in the literature, and I would like the collaborators to evaluate it rather than presuppose it: the distinction between measurement-driven looping, in which classification alters people's behavior mainly through institutional channels (reimbursement, accommodations, legal capacity, research funding), and identity-driven looping, in which classification alters people mainly by furnishing templates for self-understanding. The collaborators should assess whether this distinction is coherent, whether it cuts across their dispute in a way that could dissolve part of it, and whether each of them can accept it without conceding anything substantive. If the distinction turns out to favor one camp, the joint statement should say so rather than papering over it.

Please keep the register formal, as befits a document both parties would put their names to, and do not attempt to adjudicate who is right — the deliverable is the jointly signed text with the residual disagreement made exact, not a verdict. Avoid bibliographies and reading lists; where you reference a work or an episode, do so only insofar as its content is needed for the argument.
