I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and for years now most of my reporting on perception has kept one foot in the psychophysics lab: just-noticeable differences, psychometric functions, the CIE standard observer, instrument drift, calibration against standards. That's the vocabulary I'm actually fluent in — I can talk comfortably about discrimination thresholds, error bars, the difference between an instrument's bias and its noise, and the fact that any measuring device gets classified from the outside, by how it behaves across a battery of tests. I mention this because I've become fascinated by an argument that, to my knowledge, exists mostly in talks and an unpublished manuscript rather than in print — Alexander Chase and Benjamin Sanborn's attempt to "quine" qualia, in Dennett's sense of showing the posits to be ill-grounded and dispensable. As I understand them, roughly: our claims about how things look to us are like classifications made by a scientific theory; perceptual systems can make errors relative to a standard, but the standard itself is another classification, equally revisable; so there may be no determinate fact of the matter about the exact grain or identity of experience — the supposed qualia that were supposed to ground all this turn out to be posits doing no work we can specify, rather like positing an unobservable whose behavior is forever invisible behind the test results.

What I'd like you to do is restate their position, as faithfully as you can, entirely in my vocabulary: discrimination data, calibrations, drift corrections, the standard observer, measurement error versus miscalibration, the distinction between what an instrument reports and what an engineer can verify about it. Treat the perceiver as I would treat any instrument I have to write about — characterized by its discriminations and its reported groupings, never opened up. And then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually need: which parts of Chase and Sanborn's argument translate cleanly into that language, which parts you had to distort or finesse to make them fit, and whether anything in the argument has no expression at all in measurement terms — no amount of behavioral data and calibration talk can say it.

The reason this matters to me is a suspicion I can't resolve from inside my own framework. When I report on a colorimeter, I never need to say anything beyond accuracy and drift; yet when I write about a person, editors and readers insist that something is left over — the look itself, which no discrimination battery fixes. So my final question is this, and please take a stand rather than splitting the difference: does the residue show that my measurement vocabulary is missing something a science journalist covering the mind needs to acquire, or does it show that the qualia talk was less clear than it looked all along, and that what seems unsayable is really just a refusal to say it in the only terms that ever fixed anything? I'd rather know which of those you believe and why than get a survey of the options.
