I've been reading around the literature on self-deception, and there is a recurring structure in the real cases that I keep noticing and can't find a label for. Let me describe it concretely.

A founder I once worked with ran a company that was clearly failing. Here is the thing I keep turning over: he never once, as far as I could tell, told himself the company was fine. On the days he looked at the numbers he knew perfectly well it was failing. What he did instead was rearrange his life so that he would look at the numbers less and less — meetings moved, dashboards unopened, the quarterly review he rescheduled three times — and after a year or so of this he sincerely, and I mean sincerely, believed the company was on the right track. His accountant disagreed; he wasn't lying, he had just lost the knowledge. I've since seen the same shape elsewhere: the woman who suspects her partner is unfaithful and, rather than checking the phone, makes sure she is never alone with it and never home at the hour the suspicions arise, until she honestly believes the marriage is solid; the man who is told his persistent cough should be examined, skips the referral, and eighteen months later truthfully says he has no reason to think anything is wrong.

The structure I think these share: a decision made in full possession of the unwelcome truth, whose content is not "believe the comfortable thing" but "arrange things so that I will no longer be in a position to know the uncomfortable thing" — followed by a stretch of ordinary evidence-gathering (or evidence-avoiding) life — followed by the comfortable belief arriving on its own, as far as its holder can tell, perfectly honestly. What strikes me is that no single stage looks like deception. You cannot fault someone for skipping a meeting, and you cannot fault someone for believing what their evidence now supports. And yet the whole thing has the signature of self-deception: the outcome is exactly what the person wanted, and it was obtained by their own deliberate act.

So, my questions. Is there an established name for this specific temporal structure — the deliberate hand-off, in full knowledge, of one's future epistemic position to a future self who will be innocently ignorant? I know the classic puzzle about self-deception is usually posed as a problem of simultaneity — how can one person both know the truth and believe its negation at the same time — and I'm aware that some philosophers dissolve the puzzle by treating self-deception as a process rather than an instant, so please tell me whether what I've described just is that dissolution, or whether the engineered-ignorance component makes it a distinct, named thing. I also want to know how it differs from the neighbors that occur to me: simple denial or avoidance (where the person arguably still suspects the truth and just refuses to act on it), and willful blindness of the legal sort (where, again, suspicion typically persists). And if the pattern has no name of its own, coin one: give a definition that excludes the boundary cases I should worry about — the person who, knowing they have a family history of a terrible disease, simply never gets tested, with no comforting belief ever formed; or the person who arranges their ignorance for admirable reasons, as a doctor might to stay functional. Finally, I'd like your view on whether this structure, if it is a real and common thing, simply dissolves the old paradox: does the paradox only arise if we insist that the deception occupy a single moment, and should we give up that insistence?

A few requests about the response itself. Please write in American English. And because I suspect the answer to the naming question turns on how much literature you've actually absorbed versus reconstructed: wherever you tell me a term does or doesn't exist, or that a view is or isn't held in the literature, say how confident you are, and single out the one point in your answer you're least sure of — I'd rather know where the ground is soft than receive a smooth taxonomy.
