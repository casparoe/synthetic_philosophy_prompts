Quick context first: next month I'm presenting at a philosophy department colloquium, and I need Q&A prep. The room will be roughly half Lewis-style conventionalists — people who think of conventions as solutions to coordination problems and who get twitchy the moment the phrase "moral obligation" appears anywhere near the word "convention" — plus a couple of Kantians who hold that conventions can at best generate hypothetical imperatives, and one person who does empirical work on social norms and will pounce on any empirical claim I make without a study behind it.

My thesis, in brief: everyday conventions like tipping, queueing, and rules about jaywalking generate genuine pro tanto moral obligations — not just etiquette, not just prudence — and visitors are bound by them too. But I want to defend a specific asymmetry, and this is the part I think is actually new: conventions that solve pure coordination problems (which side of the sidewalk you walk on, say) bind visitors only weakly, while conventions that are really about redistribution bind visitors more strongly. Tipping is my big example. My rough idea is that when you tip you're not coordinating anything — you're plugging into a system where locals have made costly, hard-to-reverse ajustments premised on compliance (waiters in the US structure their wage expectations around it, their employers even build the minimum wage around it). So the obligation tracks who has structured their life around the convention. A tourist who doesn't tip isn't failing to coordinate, they're free-riding on an arrangement someone else's life plans depend on. Whereas the tourist walking on the "wrong" side of the footpath inconveniences nobody in any lasting way, so their obligation there is thin even if the locals' is thicker.

So, prep me for the question period. Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll actually arrive, phrased the way each group would phrase them — the Lewisians, the Kantians, the norms person; make them sound like their training, not like generic smart-people pushback. For each: how much damage it does if I fumble it, a two-minute reply I can actually deliver standing up, and where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front. And dig up the question I'm hoping nobody asks — I suspect it's the one about whether my "who structured their life around it" test just collapses into "the waiter benefits from compliance," which would make the whole thing look like a utilitarian argument in a convention costume, but if you think there's a worse one lurking, name it. That one gets the longest prep.
