I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of mathematics, and my department's logic reading group has spent the semester on Skolem's paradox. We've gotten to the point where everyone can recite the mechanics: the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem yields a countable model of first-order ZFC, and inside that model the power set of the naturals is uncountable anyway, because the external bijection with the naturals simply isn't among the model's sets. What we can't agree on is what follows. One of the faculty in the group, who works on the history of set theory, has taken to saying that the theorem relativizes cardinality the way special relativity relativized simultaneity: just as there is no frame-independent fact about whether two distant events are simultaneous, there is no model-independent fact about whether a collection is countable. He is fond of adding that Skolem himself seems to have drawn exactly this relativist moral, whereas Zermelo apparently thought the whole line of reasoning was a sleight of hand — and my professor takes Skolem's side, using the Einstein analogy to argue that our ordinary set-theoretic notions are parochial, artifacts of wherever we happen to be standing.

I have a growing suspicion that the analogy, not the theorem, is doing the persuasive work, but I can't get the objection into focus. When I press him, the correspondences seem to line up nicely at first: the model's internal universe plays the role of an inertial frame, the model-theorist's external vantage plays the role of another frame, and the missing bijection plays the role of the relativity of simultaneity itself. But then things come apart. In special relativity the frames are symmetric — no frame is privileged, and the Lorentz transformations relate any frame to any other, so each description is recoverable from the other. In the Skolem case the situation looks asymmetric in two ways: the external vantage is not itself another model of the theory, but the theorist's standpoint outside all of them; and the external description seems strictly more informative, containing the bijection the internal description lacks. It's as if one "frame" could see the contents of the other but not vice versa, which is not how relativity works. A classmate of mine counters that this misses the Putnam-style point — that any attempt to describe the external vantage from within is just more theory, more model-relative talk — but I notice that this move would need to be defended on its own, since nothing in the physics analogy obviously generates it.

Here's what I'd like from you: a proper audit of the analogy. First, the explicit correspondence table — what plays the role of the inertial frame, the transformation, the invariant quantity like the spacetime interval, the physical parameter (velocity) that shifts you between frames. Then the disanalogies, sorted by whether they actually matter for the relativist conclusion my professor wants to draw (that there's no fact of the matter about the "real" cardinality of the continuum, or about absolute set-theoretic notions generally) as opposed to being harmless simplifications. I'd particularly like your view on the symmetry issue: does the fact that the internal/external relation is asymmetric, with the external standpoint strictly more informative, break the analogy in a load-bearing way, or is the Putnam "just more theory" response enough to restore something like frame-parity? Finally, if the Einstein analogy is salvageable only in a heavily qualified form, is there a better one — perhaps something involving nonstandard models of arithmetic, or a cartographic metaphor where the model is a low-resolution map that cannot represent its own omissions — and does the better analogy support relativism or undermine it?
