Next month I'm presenting a paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on political reconciliation and transitional justice, and I want a Q&A prep sheet prepared for the audience I'll actually be facing. My thesis is what I call the commissive theory of state apology: a state's apology for a historical wrong is not an expressive act that reports institutional regret, but a commissive act on all fours with a promise. On my view its felicity conditions include (a) that the apologizing agent can be identified as the same corporate agent over time, (b) that the officials offering it hold something like a mandate from the current polity, and (c) that it carries a determinate, budgeted remedial commitment. The provocative upshot I lean on: if the remedial program is later abandoned or quietly defunded, the apology is not weakened but nullified — a botched speech act — so the historical record should be retrospectively reclassified, with the awkward implication that the descendants were, all along, being owed an apology while receiving a nullity.

The audience is not a general philosophy crowd. It's roughly a third speech-act theorists with strong Austinian scruples who will want to know whether a collective can perform a commissive at all or whether officials are merely ventriloquizing an entity that cannot stand behind its word; a third transitional-justice scholars who think I've made apology hostage to legislative budget cycles and who will defend the independent ritual and symbolic value of a bare expression of regret for descendant communities; and a couple of political realists who consider sincerity conditions on states a category error, plus one nationalist-minded member of the workshop who will object that the mandate condition turns apology into a permanent referendum on national self-accusation. Please give me seven to nine objections ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased verbatim in the vocabulary of the segment that will raise it — not in my vocabulary — with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is soft. Do not include objections that would only come from allies, and do not pad with generic objections about "the passage of time" that nobody at this workshop would bother making.

Deliver the prep sheet as a JSON object with exactly these fields: "objections", an array of objects each with the fields "as_they_will_put_it" (a verbatim question or interjection in that segment's voice), "raised_by" (the segment), "damage" (an integer 1-5), "two_minute_reply" (plain text, no jargon I'd have to explain on my feet), and "soft_spot" (where the reply is weakest); then "concede_up_front" (what I should grant in the opening minutes), "question_hoping_nobody_asks" (the one that could sink the paper — my bet is it concerns whether my retroactive nullification thesis implies that the wrong of non-apology persisted the entire time, so treat regress worries about promises-needing-backing-commitments seriously), "reply_if_it_comes" (a salvage attempt, honestly labeled as such if it's only a salvage), and "the_one_that_must_land" (the single reply that, if it fails, the whole session goes badly — and a sentence on why it's that one).
