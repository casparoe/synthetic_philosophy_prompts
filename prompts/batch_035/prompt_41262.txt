My friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about Dennett's idea that the self is a center of narrative gravity — a posit of the brain's own storytelling, no more a concrete thing than a center of mass is — and I came away unsettled not about his view but about whether my own package of views survives contact with itself. Here is roughly where I stand. First, I accept the narrative-gravity picture: the self is an abstraction an organism posits in order to keep its autobiographical narrative coherent, not an inner object that exists independently of the telling. Second, I am broadly Parfitian about what matters: personal identity is not what matters, psychological continuity is, and there is no further fact about survival over and above physical and psychological continuity, whatever its cause. Third, and against the deflationary current of the first two, I take authorship seriously in a robust way: when I deliberate, it is genuinely I who settle the matter, and I am blameworthy for what I do because I am the one who authored the deed, not merely the point at which causes intersected. Fourth, I believe there is a real and important difference between a person whose self-narrative is largely accurate and one who systematically confabulates, even though I deny that the self is anything over and above its narrative. Fifth, I think other people's narratives about me partly constitute who I am, while also holding that first-person authority is basic: no third party, however well-informed, speaks more authoritatively about my values than I do. I would like an audit of these five commitments rather than a lecture on any of them. Give me exactly five numbered findings, ordered from most to least serious, each a sentence or two naming the commitments at issue, stating either what the tension is and what would have to give, or why the apparent tension dissolves on inspection; where a finding requires revision, say what the cheapest fix costs. No introduction or conclusion.
