In two weeks I defend my thesis chapter before our departmental colloquium, and I want a question-period prep sheet rather than more drafting help. The chapter defends what I call rational irrationality: the thesis that an agent can be fully rational in acquiring and maintaining a disposition that, at the moment of execution, it would be irrational to act on, and that in Parfit's hitchhiker the agent who genuinely pays is the practically rational one. As I set the case up: a stranded traveler will die unless a passing driver rescues him; the driver can predict the traveler's future behavior with confidence and will rescue him only if she is confident of being paid later; once the traveler is safe, the payment buys him nothing he still wants, and defecting is causally optimal. My claim is that the momentary optimizer dies, and that dying for the sake of never being disposed to dominate is not a triumph of rationality but a failure of it.

My argument, briefly, so your objections can target it. First, rationality fundamentally attaches to the selection of dispositions, not to acts: given that others can read those dispositions imperfectly, the rational disposition to select is something like Gauthier's constrained maximization, and the hitchhiker who pays is simply executing it. Second, I distinguish two kinds of commitment, and I take this to be the chapter's novelty: insurance-style commitment, where you purchase or build an external device that later forces your hand, and character-style commitment, where you become the sort of person who would pay, with no external apparatus at all. The hitchhiker case shows the two come apart, because the driver detects character, not contracts; so no hybrid view that reduces rational commitment to prudently installed external constraints can capture what the successful hitchhiker has. Third, I argue that calling the paying agent's act irrational commits a category error: an act flowing from a rationally selected disposition is not independently assessable as irrational, because there is no momentary act-level standpoint from which the agent's reasons are fully specified.

The audience: a department dominated by causal decision theorists, one committed evidentialist, two economists from the adjacent political economy program who will attend because my discussant invited them, and a Kantian moral philosopher who disapproves of the whole rational-choice framing. The economists care about commitment devices, renegotiation-proofness, and whether anything in my story survives the availability of even crude external enforcement.
