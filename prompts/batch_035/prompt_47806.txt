I am a professor of philosophy who has been appointed to a university committee charged with deciding how our institution should commemorate figures whose legacies are now contested: which names to keep on buildings and awards, which to remove, and how to frame the ones we keep. A colleague on the committee keeps invoking Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation, on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, and while I know the essay well, I want a structured account of what it actually implies for a deliberative body like ours rather than the usual cherry-picked aphorisms. Please describe the most important implications of Nietzsche's distinction between monumental, antiquarian, and critical history — together with his notion of plastic power, the standard he uses for how much past a person or culture can absorb and still remain healthy — for how a commemoration committee should reason about its decisions. In particular: what each of the three modes of engaging the past would recommend in a situation like ours; where the three recommendations come into conflict and how Nietzsche himself suggests prioritizing among them; what the framework implies about the criteria we should use, and, just as important, which questions it leaves entirely unaddressed (for instance, questions of distributive fairness among the constituencies affected by our decisions); and whether the essay actually supports my colleague's apparent view that remembrance should be governed chiefly by the life-needs of the present rather than by fidelity to the historical record, or whether that reading stretches the text. Please confine yourself to what follows from the essay and from Nietzsche's broader thought as you know it; no bibliography or citations are necessary.
