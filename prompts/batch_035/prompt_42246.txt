In three weeks I present at a closed, day-long workshop on transitional justice, and what I need is a Q&A preparation sheet, not a defense of my view. My thesis: the South African-style model of conditional individual amnesty — amnesty available only to an applicant who appears in person before a quasi-judicial panel, makes full public disclosure of the specific acts he committed, and can be refused if the panel finds the disclosure incomplete or the act disproportionate to any political objective — is not, as it is usually treated, a regrettable second-best adopted because prosecution was politically impossible. I hold that it delivers a distinctive good of its own: a public, victim-facing record of who did what to whom, established out of the perpetrator's own mouth under a legal duty of candor, which adversarial criminal trials — with their plea bargains, sealed evidence, and proof beyond reasonable doubt — deliver poorly even when they succeed. I further hold that in negotiated transitions, "full prosecution" is usually a fiction: either the docket dies quietly of attrition, or the attempt endangers the transition itself. I concede from the outset that blanket self-amnesties — decree laws passed by outgoing regimes to shield themselves, the kind the Inter-American Court held without legal effect in its 2001 Barrios Altos judgment against Peru — are a different animal, and I do not defend them.

The audience is about twenty people, and I want the objections tailored to them, not to a generic seminar. Most are international criminal lawyers — prosecutors and legal officers with ICTY and ICC backgrounds — for whom the anti-impunity norm and the duty to prosecute grave crimes are bedrock, and several of whom regard the South African amnesty process as a stain dressed up as a moral achievement. There will also be two or three representatives of victims' associations from Latin America, who will hear any defense of amnesty as a defense of their tormentors, and a couple of political scientists who study why peace processes collapse and who could cut either way.

Please prepare the sheet as follows: list the eight or so objections I will actually face from this audience, ordered by how much damage each would do if badly answered; phrase each the way that segment of the audience would phrase it (the prosecutors in their idiom of gravity, complementarity, and impunity; the victims' representatives in theirs; the political scientists in the language of commitment problems and credible guarantees); give me a two-minute reply to each; and state plainly where each reply is soft, rather than presenting every answer as a winner. Take a definite position throughout on which objections are fatal if flubbed and which are merely uncomfortable, and where an objection has genuinely more force than my position comfortably absorbs, say so and tell me what to concede on the spot. Finally, include the one question I am hoping nobody asks, and a note on how I should handle it if it comes anyway.
