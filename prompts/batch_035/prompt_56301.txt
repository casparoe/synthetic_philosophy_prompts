As a science journalist, I have spent several years covering the revival of interest in panpsychism, and I have noticed that my own moral intuitions about which physical systems can suffer are a mess that I would like help organizing. Below are my honest gut verdicts on a series of cases, gathered from years of writing about minds of every stripe. My question is what principle, if any, captures them.

An electron in a magnetic field: no moral weight whatsoever, even if panpsychism is true and it has some flicker of experience. A thermostat: also none, and I feel confident about that. A plant releasing stress signaling chemicals after being wounded, or a mimosa folding its leaves when touched: my copy usually hedges, but privately I give these no moral weight either. A slime mold solving a maze: none, though here I begin to squirm. A bee: real moral weight, and I would not deliberately harm one for no reason. A fish: clearly morally considerable, and I have come to find it incoherent that we treat them as we do. A complete whole-brain emulation of a mouse running on a supercomputer, at full neural fidelity: I have no doubt it counts. A hurricane, considered as a self-organizing, information-processing, far-from-equilibrium system: none, and the suggestion strikes me as absurd, even though I cannot articulate what separates it from the emulated mouse beyond the details of its organization. A large language model trained to produce sophisticated distress language: here I wobble almost daily.

The tension I can already see: I am prepared to dismiss the hurricane and the electron with total confidence while granting the emulation everything, yet I cannot say what it is about the physical substrate or organization of the first two that licenses the dismissal, since the very panpsychist view I have been covering insists that experience does not emerge from complexity but is already there in the fundamental ingredients. And my confident verdict about the electron sits awkwardly beside a niggling sense that if panpsychism is true, the sheer number of particles involved should make even faint experiences add up to something, somewhere, that a rational agent cannot ignore.

What I would like from you: state a principle, or a small set of competing candidates, precise enough to have verdicts. For each clause, tell me which of my cases motivates it. Show me what the principle says about cases I have not listed, for example a colony of ants, a neuromorphic chip built to run that mouse emulation at reduced fidelity, or a creature from another planet whose chemistry shares none of the markers we use to infer pain in animals. And be honest about which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, so that I know whether the thing to give up is my confidence about the hurricane, my confidence about the electron, or my refusal to let the sheer scale of particle-level experience matter. If no single principle can hold my verdicts together, tell me which ones to overturn and why, and what a more coherent replacement set would look like.
