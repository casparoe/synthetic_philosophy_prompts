Last night my friend and I spent three hours arguing about the Hart–Fuller debate over dinner and got precisely nowhere, and this week I am interviewing a legal philosopher who defends Fuller's side: the claim that fidelity to law's internal principles — the ones Fuller illustrated through his allegory of the bumbling King Rex, who fails in eight distinct ways to make law at all — constitutes an inner morality of law. Draft eight to ten interview questions for him that go beyond having him restate the position. At minimum: one question pressing Hart's objection that principles of good rule-craft are like the poisoner's skill in his craft — a virtue of technique, not a moral virtue, since the eight desiderata can be satisfied by regimes pursuing substantive evil — with a follow-up keyed to the standard Fullerian reply that evil regimes find these principles self-defeating in practice; one on the grudge informer cases, asking whether Fuller's account actually delivers the verdict he wanted or only explains why the postwar German courts had to strain to maintain the fiction of legal continuity; and one on the point my friend and I could not settle — whether an algorithmic administrative system, say a risk-score program that silently determines eligibility for public benefits, can satisfy the desiderata at all, given that continual model updates resemble Fuller's vice of too-frequent change in the rules and that an opaque score seems to break congruence between announced rule and official action. For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the answer the guest is most likely to give, and avoid generic questions about how he came to the field.
