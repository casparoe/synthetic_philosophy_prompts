I'm a PhD student working on philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and for the past two terms I've been in a reading group that has slowly worked its way through the teleosemantics literature -- Millikan's selected-effects account of proper function, Papineau's version, Neander's, and the various ways they try to get determinate content out of biological purposes. What strikes me, and what I'd like your help thinking through, is that this debate seems to have crystallized in the late eighties and early nineties and then essentially frozen. The objections are all on the table: the disjunction problem (why does the frog represent "small dark moving thing" rather than "fly or BB or passing shadow"), the indeterminacy worries about which function is the relevant one when an organ or behavior has multiple selection histories, the swampman-style cases where a creature with no evolutionary history seems to have beliefs anyway, and the more general charge that "selected effect" and "biological function" are themselves contested notions doing suspicious work. Defenders respond that the indeterminacy is benign, that folk psychology was never that determinate either, or that the relevant notion of function is well-defined enough for the job. And then nothing moves. Everyone I talk to seems to have picked a side around year one of graduate school.

My working hypothesis, which I offer as a hypothesis rather than a conclusion, is that the stall isn't due to any of the object-level arguments being harder than expected. Instead I suspect the two camps are operating with different standards for what would count as success: the critics implicitly demand an account that secures something like the determinacy and normativity we attribute in ordinary belief-desire psychology -- they treat the fact that our everyday attributions seem sharp as data -- while the teleosemanticists have quietly revised the explanandum, treating the loose, gerrymandered, holistic character of actual biological function as a feature that a naturalistic semantics should reproduce rather than a defect. On that reading it's a disagreement about what the datum is, and maybe about whether intentionality is even a natural kind, rather than a straightforward dispute about which theory is true. But I can also see a rival diagnosis: that there's a hidden empirical question about how brains actually implement content-bearing states, and the philosophy has stalled because the neuroscience hasn't yet come in either way.

I'd like you to diagnose the disagreement rather than adjudicate it: tell me whether it's substantive, verbal, or a case of the two sides answering different questions under one heading; whether the impasse reflects an ill-posed question or merely a hard one; and, given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress -- a new distinction, a formal result, empirical findings about neural representation, or simply a change in what philosophers ask for. Please engage with what the two sides actually say rather than giving me a survey of the positions.
