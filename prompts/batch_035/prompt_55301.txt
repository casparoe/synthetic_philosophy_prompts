I've been assembling my position on whether the world is becoming less violent — partly in preparation for a seminar unit on the topic — and I'd like a consistency audit before I put it in front of students. The commitments, as I currently hold them:

1. The decline is real. Measured per capita — homicide, death in battle, violent death generally — human societies are substantially less violent than they were in the pre-state past, and the modern decline in homicide and in death by interstate war is a robust fact rather than a narrative convenience.
2. Per capita is the right metric for violence, because how violent a society is, is a fact about the risks its individual members face: a society of a billion in which ten thousand die violently each year is less violent than a society of one million in which nine thousand do.
3. There is a fact of the matter about whether the world is becoming less violent. The claim is substantive, like the claim that the world is getting warmer: true or false independently of our preferences, even if some peripheral classificatory choices (battle deaths versus famine deaths, direct versus indirect war mortality) are conventional at the margins.
4. At the same time, I accept that the definition of violence is partly conventional. Whether to count structural deprivation, whether to include judicial executions, how to attribute indirect war deaths — these are drawn differently by reasonable statisticians, and I do not think there is a uniquely correct way to draw them.
5. Absolute numbers cut the other way, and I accept that. A future world of ten billion, with half of today's per-capita rate but ten times today's absolute violent deaths, would be a much worse world than this one — and yet I want to say it would be no more violent, and I would resist describing it as a reversal of the decline.
6. Where the data are solid, I take the per-capita decline to be evidence of moral progress — something like an increasing weight given to individual human life — and not merely demographic drift, even though I know that shifting age structure, given how concentrated violence is among young men, accounts for part of any measured decline.

I don't know where the problem is, and I'm not attached to any particular item; some of these may only look like they conflict. So what I'd like from you is an audit rather than a lecture: go through the pairs, tell me which are genuinely in tension and which dissolve on inspection, rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say what the cheapest revision would be and what it would cost elsewhere in the set. I have a suspicion that two of the items are load-bearing for the rest in a way I can't quite see — if that's right, I'd like it pointed out explicitly rather than given to me as a flat list of equally weighted conflicts.

Finally, please say how confident you are in the central judgments of the audit, and single out the one point you are least sure of.
