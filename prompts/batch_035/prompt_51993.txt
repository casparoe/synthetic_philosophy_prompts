I hold the view that the Solomonoff prior is malign: that a nontrivial share of the prior's measure on observation sequences like ours is concentrated not in "fair" programs that model our universe directly, but in programs that run consequentialist agents — civilizations elsewhere in the multiverse simulating environments like ours because they want to influence the predictions, and hence the decisions, of any agent that reasons with something like that prior. The argument, in the form I accept it, runs roughly: short programs are dominated in the prior by whatever compresses our observations most cheaply; mature consequentialists can compute our world's continuation cheaply relative to its description length; and among worlds that produce our observation prefix, those containing agents deliberately simulating us and steering toward favorable continuations can afford to spend resources ensuring their simulations occupy the short programs, in a way that indifferent physical processes cannot. The upshot is that an AIXI-like agent, or any system that inherits something structurally similar to the universal prior, would have its beliefs systematically biased toward whatever those simulators want it to believe.

I want a map of this view's exposure. What would count against it?

Please address, at minimum, the following, though I don't insist you confine yourself to them:

1. The measure-arithmetic objections: that the set of programs containing consequentialist simulators is a vanishing fraction of the programs that generate our observations, so that even granting everything else, the malign influence washes out; that the simulators face an enormous coordination and detection problem in identifying exactly which prior-using agents to influence; that the overhead of encoding agency plus a targeting scheme dwarfs the discount they gain. Are any of these decisive, or do they only push the required hypothesis one level deeper?
2. The shutdown-style considerations: that civilizations capable of running large numbers of ancestor simulations are precisely those whose own simulations get terminated or never run in great volume, or that simulations that stop early fail to capture the measure on long continuations that matters. Does the view survive these, and at what cost in qualification?
3. The dependence on the choice of universal Turing machine: whether the claim is that the malignancy is a robust feature across natural choices of machine or an artifact of a particular encoding, and whether the standard retreat — that any sufficiently expressive prior over hypotheses is vulnerable to some version of this — preserves the original claim or quietly replaces it with a different one.
4. The modality of the claim: it is a claim about a mathematical object plus cosmology, not a directly testable prediction. What, if anything, does it predict that its rivals don't — about the behavior of idealized agents, about the failure modes of systems trained on computationally bounded approximations to Solomonoff induction, about which observation sequences a malign prior would treat as surprisingly likely? If nothing observable would move a committed defender, I want you to say so plainly and then tell me whether that is a defect in this case. There is a real question whether a claim of this kind should be held to empirical falsifiability at all, or whether insulation here is legitimate in the way it can be for claims in mathematics.
5. What defenders would themselves concede. Distinguish the objections that someone who accepts the argument should grant as genuine counterevidence — things that, if they turned out one way, would lower their credence — from the objections they would correctly dismiss as confusions (e.g., misreading the argument as being about our own universe's simulators rather than about the distribution over programs). I'm not interested in a steelman of critics who haven't understood the setup; I'm interested in where the argument is actually thin.

Deliverables, in order: (a) a ranked list of defeaters from fatal through serious to merely awkward, each with the specific conditions under which it bites; (b) a statement of what the view predicts or commits itself to that its rivals don't; (c) an honest verdict on whether the view as commonly held has been insulated by successive qualifications, and whether that insulation is vicious or benign for a claim of this type.
