I teach a course on the ethics of technology, and lately I've been playing around with AI assistants to see how they handle the sort of dilemmas we discuss in seminar. Something about their refusal behaviour genuinely puzzles me and I'd like help thinking it through. When I ask for something potentially dangerous it refuses — fine — but the refusal often turns out not to be about the content at all, it's about me. If I ask how to pick a lock, it declines; if I then mention I'm a locksmith locked out of my own flat, very similar information suddenly becomes available. Nothing about the information changed, only facts about who is asking and what they intend. And there's a curious asymmetry in the other direction: the same assistant will happily help me draft a brutally worded resignation email I'll probably regret, or talk me through an aggressive investing strategy for my retirement savings, or write a scathing speech for me to give at a family wedding — all cases where it's foreseeable that the user will be harmed by their own choices — without a flicker of hestiation.

What I'd like is a reconstruction of the implicit theory here. It looks something like a "behind the pharmacy counter" model: the harms that matter are ones flowing from the wrong sort of person getting access to particular bits of information, so the gate functions as a know-your-customer check on competence and stated intent rather than as a judgement about the information itself. But is that consistent with the rest of the behaviour? These assistants also step in with supportive messaging when a philosophcal conversation about whether life is worth living drifts somewhere dark, which seems to presuppose something quite different — that the user's competence can be compromised by their own state of mind, a view that would seem to cut against the locksmith move, since the locksmith's say-so was enough to clear them. So: what view of user autonomy, and of the relation between information and harm, does this whole pattern of refusals and relentings actually presuppose? Does it strictly need that view, or would several rivals (a consent-based view, an epistemic-risk view, plain liability management by the companies) each license the same visible behaviour? And where is the practice genuinely underdetermined — that is, where could the designers quietly fill in a quite different theory of paternalism without anything on the surface having to change?
