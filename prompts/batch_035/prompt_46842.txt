I'm a PhD student in political theory working on the family, and there's a one-liner I keep running into that I can't get a handle on: marriage exists for the sake of children. Last week a discussant at a workshop used it to argue that the state has no business recognizing same-sex marriages: if the institution's point is procreation, then unions that cannot procreate fall outside its purpose, so extending marriage to them is a category mistake rather than an expansion of it. When I raised infertile opposite-sex couples, he replied that the institution only needs to be structured around the kind of relationship that is procreative in type, not around any couple that actually conceives — the way a knife doesn't stop being a cutting instrument just because some knives are blunt. I'd like a menu of the distinct precise claims the slogan could stand for: a historical claim about the institution's actual origins, a conceptual claim about what the word means, a functional claim about what the legal institution is for, a normative claim about what the state ought to privilege — plus any other readings you can identify. For each, tell me whether it is plausible, and, the part I care about, whether it actually supports the conclusion that same-sex couples should be excluded from marriage, or merely the weaker conclusion that they are not the paradigm case. Finally, tell me where his type/token repair fits in: which of the readings it rescues, and whether it does any real work beyond blocking the infertility counterexample — or whether it quietly changes the claim mid-argument. Lay it out as a list I can take back to the workshop.
