I'm a history PhD student and the theory chapter of my thesis is supposed to justify my approach to explaining why the Soviet Union collapsed, but before I commit to anything I want the spec a causal explanation of a historical event actually has to meet. I'm less interested in the USSR case itself than in the general requirements, so use whatever cases make each requirement vivid (the outbreak of WWI works, so does the Reformation, whatever else fits).

Write me the desiderata list. For context, here's what I think the hard parts are:

- overdetermination. In the July 1914 crisis it's plausible that even without Sarajevo, the alliance structure, the naval race and the mobilization timetables would have produced a general European war within a decade anyway — and also plausible that the alliance structure alone wasn't enough and it took the assassination. Any theory I use has to say something about cases where multiple putative causes are each close to sufficient on their own, and about the "close to" doing alot of the work there.
- absences. Half of what historians cite as causes are non-events: the intelligence wasnt shared, the warning went unread, the emperor didnt send for the minister. A spec that can't count omissions as causes can't cover the actual practice of my discipline.
- the "why then" question vs. the "why at all" question. The French Revolution might have been coming for a century, so is an explanation of 1789 supposed to explain that it happened, or that it happened when it happened? The two answers point at different causes and I need the spec to keep them seperate instead of mashing them together.
- what I've been calling crowding-out. At some point adding another long-term cause stops adding information. I'd want the spec to say something principled about when a cause has earned its place, since "list everything since 1815" is not an explanation.

Behind each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and be explicit about which are non-negotiable and which are just what one school of historians happens to like — I suspect the demand to rank causes in order of importance is the second kind, and I'd like to hear whether you agree. And the thing I most want: tell me if some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable. I have a suspicion you can't simultaneously handle overdetermination properly, honour the but-for intuition wherever it does apply, and keep single dated events as the things being explained, but I can't prove it. If some requirement quietly knocks out the standard approaches historians actually use — narrative plus implicit but-for reasoning, or the "deep causes vs. proximate causes" two-layer picture everyone reaches for — say which one does the knocking out.
