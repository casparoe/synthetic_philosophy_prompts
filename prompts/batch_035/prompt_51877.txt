I'm a software engineer, and while I have no formal philosophy background, I've spent enough years building dashboards and metrics to have developed a stubborn conviction that no measurement is philosophically innocent: every scoring rule smuggles in a theory of the thing being scored. The case I can't stop chewing on is the World Happiness Report and the national rankings built on it. As I understand the mechanics, the headline ranking comes from a single survey item (the Cantril ladder): roughly a thousand respondents per country are asked to picture a ladder with steps numbered 0 to 10, where 10 is the best possible life for them and 0 the worst, and to say where their current life sits; each country's score is the average of these answers pooled over about three years, and countries are then sorted. The same surveys also ask about positive and negative emotions experienced the previous day, but the ranking itself is driven by the ladder question, not the emotion questions. My question is what theory of happiness this whole apparatus presupposes. Does it presuppose that happiness is the sort of thing that can be averaged over a population and compared across cultures — that people's private 0-to-10 answers are cardinal, interpersonally comparable, and meaningfully aggregated into a single number? Does it presuppose that what matters is a person's reflective self-assessment of their life as a whole rather than how they actually felt yesterday? And is the practice internally consistent about what it's measuring, given that it ranks on the ladder question while presenting the emotion data as complementary "happiness" evidence?

The second half of my question concerns what happens when the ranking becomes a target, because this is where my professional instincts kick in. Anyone who has worked with metrics knows the failure mode: the moment a measure becomes a KPI, teams optimize the measure rather than the thing it was supposed to proxy — teaching to the test, overfitting to the benchmark, gaming the dashboard. Some governments have already taken the ranking at least semi-seriously as a policy target; the UAE appointed a Minister of State for Happiness in 2016, and New Zealand ran a Wellbeing Budget in 2019. If a government genuinely tried to top the ladder ranking, what would that practice presuppose about the purpose of the state — for instance, that raising citizens' subjective self-reports is the proper end of policy, rather than improving the objective conditions of their lives or their day-to-day emotional experience? And is there a deeper incoherence lurking: a ladder score is partly a function of what people expect from life and how they compare themselves to others, so a government "optimizing" it might succeed through expectations management and narrative rather than through making anyone better off, which looks like Goodhart's law operating at the level of an entire country.

What I'd like from you: reconstruct the implicit theory (or theories) of wellbeing that the ranking practice rests on, distinguishing what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with; say where the parts of the practice presuppose different and possibly inconsistent things (the ladder versus the emotion questions, the cross-country comparison versus cultural differences in scale use); and tell me what would actually have to change if the ranking were redesigned around a different underlying theory of happiness — say, one on which happiness is moment-to-moment feeling rather than a life evaluation, or one on which it isn't interpersonally comparable at all. Finally, given all that, tell me whether the question "should a country try to top the rankings?" is well-posed, or whether the implicit philosophy of the practice makes it confused.
