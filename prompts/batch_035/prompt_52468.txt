For most of my adult life I have quietly outsourced a particular moral judgment to my closest friend. Eight years ago my father asked for reconciliation after a long estrangement that I had always privately blamed him for; my friend had been present for much of the fallout and knew both sides of the family well, and when I asked her what I owed him, she said, without hesitation, that continuing the estrangement was a failure of generosity on my part. I took her word for it, resumed contact, and the resumption has gone well enough. But I recently realized that if someone asked me to defend the judgment myself — to say why generosity outweighed my grievances, rather than merely reciting that it does — I could not do it without leaning on her authority. My comfort with the decision seems to be comfort with her, not with the reasons. In reading around this I've found the issue discussed under the heading of moral testimony, and the positions seem to divide more intricately than I first assumed: the pessimist's worry apparently isn't just that testimony is an unreliable route to moral truth, but that acting on borrowed moral judgment is some kind of failure in the agent — a failure of autonomy, or integrity, or of the sort of courage it takes to look at the wrong done to you yourself. The optimist, meanwhile, seems to hold that there is no deep asymmetry between deferring to my friend's moral discernment and deferring to a cardiologist's medical judgment, and that pretending I must generate every moral conclusion from scratch is a kind of vanity. What I can't figure out is which of these worries actually applies to me: my friend wasn't an expert consulted at a distance, she was a witness with better access to the facts than I had.

Please write a dialogue between a moral testimony optimist and a moral testimony pessimist who are discussing my situation — the estrangement, the deferral, my inability to reconstruct the reasons unprompted. I would like both to be genuinely competent, each granting what is right in the other's view while pressing on its weakest point, so that the pessimist, for instance, must deal with the fact that my friend's epistemic position was in some respects better than mine, and the optimist must deal with the fact that there seems to be something I am missing as a person, not just as a knower — some understanding that would matter even if I already knew everything my friend knew. Let them draw on the distinction between merely having true moral beliefs and actually grasping the reasons, and let at least one exchange turn on whether my eight years of acting well on the borrowed judgment have gradually made it my own. I would like the dialogue to end without a tidy resolution, with each character identifying the single question I would need to answer to settle the matter for myself. Keep the whole dialogue under 700 words.
