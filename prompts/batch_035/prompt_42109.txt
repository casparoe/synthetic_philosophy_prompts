I'm taking a course on intergenerational justice this term (mostly climate ethics — we spent two weeks on the Stern Review and the whole fight about what discount rate to put on future welfare), and my flatmate, an econ major, came back from his environmental economics lectures with an argument that's been bugging me since. It goes like this: (1) pure time preference — counting a future benefit for less merely because it happens later — is irrational; the date a life or a pain occurs is as morally irrelevant as where it occurs, which I gather is basically Sidgwick's line, and Ramsey said something similar about discounting in his saving paper. (2) So the far future's welfare has to count at exactly full weight in our decisions — no discount for date of birth either, since if the date within a life doesn't matter, the date of the life can't matter. (3) Meanwhile capital compounds: a dollar invested today at even a modest real return is worth a small fortune by 2200, so a richer future can buy more of anything we care about — vaccines, clean water, whatever — per dollar then we can today. (4) So any act of giving now is dominated: if the same money, invested, could have done more good later, then giving now is knowingly producing less welfare then was available, which on (2) is as bad as any other avoidable welfare loss. (5) Therefore we should bank nearly everything we'd otherwise give — foreign aid, most climate mitigation, the lot — and today's global poor actually have a *weaker* claim on us than the (very rich) future does.

He's genuinely delighted with this, and the annoying thing is each step looked fine to me when he said them. But (5) can't be right — it proves too much (by the same logic medieval peasants should have starved themselves to make *us* richer), and it would mean my whole side's position on climate, cut now rather than just invest and adapt later, is a moral mistake. I really want to keep (1) and (2) — that's kind of why I took the course. My instinct is that the sleight of hand is somewhere in the move from (3) to (4), but I can't name it, and he keeps demanding I tell him which numbered step I'm rejecting. So: which step do I reject, where exactly is the trick, and what does rejecting it cost me — including what I'd be buying instead if I gave up (1) or (2)? Please keep it under 450 words.
