I keep circling the same unresolved question in the literature on choice under ambiguity, and I've come to suspect the problem is that nobody has agreed on what a theory in this area is actually being asked to do — so arguments about whether ambiguity-averse behavior is rational talk past each other. Before I engage any more particular models, I want the specification drawn up. Please write out the desiderata: what any adequate theory of rational choice under ambiguity has to deliver, with the motivating case behind each requirement, a judgment about which requirements are non-negotiable and which are prejudices of particular camps, and — the part I most want — an honest assessment of whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, in which case the list itself is the result.

So we are working from the same material, here is the data and the candidate theories I have in mind, described rather than cited. The canonical observations come from Ellsberg's urn problems. In the two-urn version, a person is offered a bet on drawing a red ball either from an urn known to contain red and black in equal proportions or from an urn with the same two colors in completely unknown proportions; most people are indifferent between the colors within each urn but prefer betting on either color from the known urn, which violates the sure-thing principle, since the unknown urn's composition is a symmetrically unknown mixture. In the three-color version, an urn holds ninety balls: thirty are red, and the remaining sixty are black or yellow in unknown proportion. People typically prefer betting on red over black, and prefer betting on black-or-yellow over red-or-yellow — the first preference implies they think red balls number more than thirty-equivalent, the second that they think fewer than thirty, a straightforward contradiction under any single, additive probability assignment. There is also a second, less-discussed datum: experimental work on what its authors call comparative ignorance suggests that ambiguity aversion is weak or absent when a person evaluates a single ambiguous prospect in isolation, and appears mainly in side-by-side comparison with a more familiar risk. I take no stance on whether that finding supports or undermines the requirement below; I want your view. The two candidate theories I'll ask you to score at the end, briefly, are: first, maxmin expected utility, on which an agent holds a set of probability distributions and evaluates each act by its worst expected utility across the set; and second, the smooth ambiguity model, on which the agent holds a subjective second-order distribution over the possible probability distributions and applies a concave transformation to the expected utilities computed under each, so that ambiguity attitude enters as a curvature parameter analogous to ordinary risk aversion.

The requirements I can see being demanded of a theory in this area, at minimum, are these; consider each, amend or add as you see fit, and mark each as non-negotiable or as a camp preference:

1. Taking the data: the theory must treat the Ellsberg reversals as something to be explained rather than diagnosed as error — or else say explicitly and with reasons why diagnosing them as error is the right move, in which case the theory owes us an account of why the pattern is so robust and so intuitively compelling to intelligent subjects.

2. The expected-utility limit: when probabilities are objective and agreed, the theory must collapse to ordinary expected utility, so that it is a theory of ambiguity on top of, not instead of, a theory of risk.

3. A formal floor: preferences remain complete and transitive, and the theory must say what represents them, so that it remains a theory of rational preference rather than a catalogue of phenomena.

4. Locating the attitude: the theory must declare whether ambiguity aversion lives in the agent's beliefs (as a set of priors or a non-additive charge on events) or in her evaluation of outcomes (as a transformation applied at the level of expected utilities), and must identify an observable situation in which the two placements come apart, so that the choice of placement is not purely decorative.

5. Behavior over time: the theory must say what its agent does across multiple stages — whether she is dynamically consistent, and whether she is consequentialist in the sense that her choices at a later node depend only on what remains possible from there. My understanding is that there are results showing that dynamic consistency and consequentialism, taken together without restriction, squeeze ambiguity attitudes so hard that the agent is driven back toward expected-utility behavior, and that the standard escape is to constrain the set of priors as information arrives so that it retains a certain rectangular structure. Do not treat that as settled: tell me whether the tension is as sharp as I've described, what each candidate theory gives up to handle it, and whether this requirement should be marked non-negotiable at all.

6. Information: it should never be the case, other things equal, that the agent would prefer to remain ignorant — receiving more information should not make her worse off by her own lights. I am told this can fail for some updating rules under ambiguity; if so, say whether that failure is a cost of the theory, a benign artifact, or a genuine refutation of the requirement.

7. Exploitation and inactivity: the theory must not expose the agent to a sure-loss across bets she accepts, and it must not predict pathology — a maxmin agent with an unconstrained set of priors can refuse every trade offered, which is empirically false and, arguably, rationally embarrassing. The theory owes us some account of why ambiguity-averse agents still participate in markets, or an argument that the requirement is illegitimate as stated.

For the joint-satisfiability question, I want more than a list of pairwise tensions: tell me whether there is a core subset — say, 1, 2, 3, and 5 — that no extant style of theory satisfies at once, and if so, which requirement you would drop and what accepting its negation would look like as a theory. Then close with a short scoring of maxmin expected utility and the smooth ambiguity model against your final list, noting which single requirement does the most work in separating candidates. I want the reasoning in the requirements, not in the scorecard, so keep that final section brief. No bibliography is wanted; describe what you need inline, as I have done above.
