After a career in analytical chemistry, I have spent my retirement reading epistemology, and I have arrived at a view I will defend in public for the first time next month: that accepting a testimonial report is not an inference from observed reliability, but a form of non-inferential uptake — closer to reading a well-calibrated instrument than to extrapolating from a track record. My working analogy comes from the laboratory: we routinely recorded instrument readings we had not independently checked that day, because the trust was underwritten by the instrument's place in a whole practice of calibration chains, maintenance, and inter-laboratory comparison — chains that themselves bottom out in values we simply took on trust, since no one re-derives the reference standards. My audience is a university epistemology workshop: mostly philosophers of a broadly reductionist bent who hold that testimonial justification is inductive, plus one social epistemologist and a visiting philosopher of science. Please prepare me a Q&A sheet for the session: the objections I am likely to face, in roughly the order they will arrive and phrased as this audience would phrase them, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft; indicate which objections have genuine force and what I should concede up front; and include the one question I am quietly hoping nobody asks.
