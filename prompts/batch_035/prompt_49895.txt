I'm on the analytics team at a mid-sized software company, and for the past year a single sentence has been doing a surprising amount of work around here: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Our director first deployed it to kill a proposal to tie team bonuses to a customer-satisfaction score — she said making it a target would "ruin the signal," and that was that. One of my colleagues took the lesson in a different direction: he argued we should keep our internal dashboards private and refuse to publish team-level league tables, on the theory that a metric only corrupts once people know it's being watched, so the cure is obscurity rather than abandonment. And at a recent offsite, someone invoked the very same line in an almost Heisenbergian mood, claiming that the act of measuring an organization inevitably changes the organization, so that no metric survives contact with management regardless of incentives. Most recently the director used it to wave off a question about whether we should invest in refining the satisfaction score at all, on the ground that any version of it will be gamed the moment anyone cares about it.

The line is usually credited to the economist Charles Goodhart, though as I understand it his original point was considerably narrower — something about statistical regularities in monetary aggregates breaking down once the authorities leaned on them for purposes of control. That is a long way from bonus schemes, dashboards, and league tables, and I suspect the sentence has drifted far from whatever it originally meant. So what I'd like is a careful disambiguation: what distinct, precise claims could this one-liner actually be making? I can think of several candidates — that agents deliberately game proxies once those proxies are incentivized; that even honest agents, optimizing hard on a proxy, drift onto the dimensions where the proxy and the underlying goal have come apart; that selecting on the extreme of a noisy measure regresses toward the mean, so the top performers on any metric are disproportionately lucky rather than good even with no behavioral change at all; that publishing a number is itself the intervention, and an unpublicized metric stays clean; and the sweeping thesis that measurement as such is self-undermining.

Please lay these out as a menu of readings, ordered from trivial to contentious to outright false, and — this is the part I most need — for each reading, say whether it would actually have supported the use the slogan was put to in each of the three episodes above: killing the bonus scheme, keeping the metrics secret, and declining to refine the score at all. It would also help if you could tell me which of the readings are claims about the behavior of measured people and which are claims about the statistics of measurement itself, since the two feel very different and tend to get run together around here. Finally, the old parable about the Soviet nail factory — rewarded for tonnage, it produced a few colossal nails; rewarded for count, it produced useless pins — keeps getting invoked in these discussions as if it settled everything. If you could say which of your readings that story actually illustrates, and whether it is a fair stand-in for the slogan at all, I would be grateful: I want to come to the next metrics review with something more precise than a proverb.
