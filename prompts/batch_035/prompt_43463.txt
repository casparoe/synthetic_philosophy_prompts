So I'm reading this popular book on the history of thinking about the self (not going to name it, it's one of those big sweeping "history of consciousness" things), and it has a section on Avicenna's flying man thought experiment. The way the book tells it: Avicenna, the Persian philosopher writing around 1000 CE, asks you to imagine a human created all at once, fully formed but with no sensory experience whatsoever -- blind, floating in the air so nothing touches him, limbs spread apart so they don't even brush against each other. And Avicenna says this person would still be aware of himself, would still affirm his own existence, even though he has no body-awareness at all. From this the book concludes that Avicenna proved the soul is an immaterial substance, separate from the body and capable of surviving its death, and that this makes the flying man basically a dress rehearsal for Descartes' cogito -- Avicenna got to "I think therefore I am" 600 years early, he just didn't get the credit. The book also says the argument was designed as a direct refutation of Aristotle's claim that the soul is just the form of the body.

I have a feeling some of this is glossing really hard, but I honestly don't know enough about Avicenna to tell where. Like, the floating blind guy with no sensory input part sounds right? And "he would still know he exists" sounds like what I've heard. But the jump to "proved the soul is an immaterial substance that survives death" feels like it's doing a lot of work, and the Descartes comparison seems like the kind of thing a pop author would say for drama.

Can you go through it and tell me which parts a scholar of medieval Islamic philosophy would sign off on, which parts they'd wince at, and which parts are just wrong? I'm especially curious whether the man in the thought experiment is supposed to be reasoning his way to "I exist" the way Descartes does, or whether that's a misread -- and whether the "survives death" conclusion actually follows from the thought experiment itself or if Avicenna needs to bring in other machinery to get there. Also the claim about it being aimed at Aristotle specifically -- is that a fair description of who or what the argument is supposed to be attacking?
