"Naturalists about disease claim to define health and disease in purely descriptive, value-free terms, while normativists hold that some evaluative element is ineliminable. Consider two cases:

(i) ASH-LUNG: Two hundred years ago, an isolated island population was blanketed by volcanic ash, and the ash is still in the soil and water. Every single living member of the population develops mild pulmonary fibrosis in early childhood, leaving them with roughly 80% of the lung capacity that members of their species typically have elsewhere in the world. Within the island population, prevalence is 100%; off the island, almost nobody has it. Islanders notice no day-to-day impairment, though they fatigue a bit faster on long climbs.

(ii) SPRINT-FOOT: A congenital anatomical variant of the foot, found in about 1 in 10,000 people worldwide. It is entirely painless and causes no problems in ordinary life, but biomechanically it reduces top sprinting speed by around 4%.

Explain how Boorse's biostatistical theory and Wakefield's harmful dysfunction analysis would each classify these two cases, making clear both where the theories agree and where they diverge. Then assess: do these cases show that naturalism is false, that it needs patching, or that a naturalist can simply bite a bullet? Defend your answer."

And here's the student's answer:

"Boorse's biostatistical theory (BST) defines disease as a condition in which some biological part functions statistically subnormally, relative to the reference class of healthy members of the same species, age, and sex. On this view the function of a trait is whatever it was selected for by evolution, so a part is diseased when it performs that selected contribution worse than the statistical norm. Wakefield's harmful dysfunction analysis is basically BST plus a value judgment: a disorder is a harmful dysfunction. Since dysfunction for Wakefield is the same statistical subnormality as for Boorse, the two accounts agree on the extension of disease whenever we all agree about harm — the disagreement is only about whether values enter the picture.

Ash-lung: since everyone on the island has the condition, it is statistically normal within that population, so BST must say there is no disease there. This is a serious cost for naturalism, since we would clearly want to call ash-lung a disease — the lungs are damaged, capacity is reduced, people fatigue faster. Wakefield gets the right answer: the lungs are failing to do what they were selected for, which is a dysfunction, and the harm clause is satisfied, so it's a disorder. Since the naturalist theory gets this case wrong while the hybrid theory gets it right, the case shows that naturalism is false and that we need an evaluative component.

Sprint-foot: here things reverse. For Boorse, whether it's a disease depends on whether top sprinting speed counts as a biological function of the foot. If we take locomotor performance to be a function of the foot, then sprint-foot is a statistically subnormal part-function and hence a disease on BST, even though the person is perfectly fine. That seems like the wrong result, and it is exactly why Wakefield added the harm clause: the foot performs all of its selected functions adequately, there's no harm, so there's no disorder. So the two cases together show the BST overpathologizes harmless statistical outliers while underpathologizing genuinely harmful conditions, and Wakefield's analysis handles both. This is the pattern we'd expect if normativism were correct."
