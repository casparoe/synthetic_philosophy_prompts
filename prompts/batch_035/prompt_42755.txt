I'm a criminal defense attorney who's gone back to graduate school part-time in philosophy of law, and my department's free will reading group has asked each of us to write up a "philosophical profile" — a list of our actual commitments on free will and moral responsibility — so the others can interrogate it. Before I submit mine, I want a rigorous consistency check, and I'd rather get it from someone who won't be polite about it. Please take on the role of a professional philosopher specializing in agency, free will, and moral responsibility, and audit the following package rather than explain the basics to me — I've read the standard literature. Here is the set of views I hold:

(1) Determinism is very likely true, and libertarian free will — whether agent-causal or event-causal — is not merely false but incoherent; adding quantum randomness to action would make it random, not free. (2) Compatibilism is right: moral responsibility is compatible with determinism, and the freedom that matters is acting from a reasons-responsive mechanism of one's own. (3) I find Frankfurt-style cases persuasive: an agent who couldn't have done otherwise, because a neuroscientist would have intervened had she been about to refrain, can still be blameworthy, so alternate possibilities aren't required for blame. (4) I nonetheless endorse the principle that ought implies can. (5) I'm a basic-desert retributivist about punishment: some wrongdoers deserve to suffer for what they've done, intrinsically, and not merely as an instrument of deterrence or rehabilitation — I've watched too many sentencing hearings to be comfortable with anything purely forward-looking. (6) I have a firm intuition that a thoroughly manipulated agent — one whose desires and values were covertly implanted and are acted on "freely" in every compatibilist-respecting sense — is not responsible, and I believe it's a serious obligation of the compatibilist to say what the morally relevant difference is between that agent and an ordinary determined one. (7) I think the constitutive-luck objection — that no one controls the genes and upbringing that made them who they are — can be answered by compatibilists, and I'm suspicious of philosophers who deploy it to reject ordinary blame of ordinary wrongdoers. (8) I think the reactive attitudes, especially resentment and gratitude, are justified and more or less indispensable to human life, whatever the metaphysics turns out to be.

Please begin by brainstorming internally about which pairs of these commitments are genuinely in tension, which merely appear to be, and then present your findings as an ordered audit: rank the real tensions by severity, explain for each what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me given the rest of the set (for instance, what my retributivism in (5) does or doesn't survive if (6) and (7) get resolved one way rather than the other), and explicitly dissolve at least one tension you judge to be merely apparent, saying how it dissolves. Don't lecture me on what each view is; assume I know that. And don't try to guess which commitment I'd be most willing to abandon — I genuinely don't know, which is why I'm asking. Be specific about which of my eight commitments is doing the damage in each tension, rather than gesturing at "the standard debate."
