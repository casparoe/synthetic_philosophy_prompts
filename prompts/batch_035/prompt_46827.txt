I write literary fiction, and like most novelists I spend my working life obsessed with how words attach themselves to the world — to what "elm" and "beech" do in a paragraph, to why a character who calls a sofa a "settee" becomes a different person, to what happens to a reader's mental lexicon when I quietly revise a character's backstory in chapter nine. I have been told that the semantic externalism debate — Putnam's Twin Earth scenario with its XYZ, Twin-Oscar who is molecule-for-molecule identical to Oscar yet whose word "water" means something else, Burge's patient who misuses "arthritis," the division of linguistic labor by which my word "gold" inherits its content from chemists I will never meet — is supposed to be a debate about exactly my obsession. So I want a translation, done honestly.

Restate the core externalist positions and arguments in the vocabulary I actually use: worldbuilding, the difference between what a narrator says and what is true in the world of the book, character diction and free indirect discourse, retconning and canon, the reader's lexicon versus the author's, the way a fictional town's place-names acquire referents over three hundred pages. In particular, I want the Twin Earth case done as a worldbuilding problem — two novels that are word-for-word identical except that in one, the clear liquid in the wells is H2O, and I never tell the reader which — and I want the slow-switching argument done as what happens to a character's word "water" if I transplant her, mid-narrative, into the other book without telling her.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Which parts of the debate translate cleanly into my toolkit, which parts only translate if you distort them — and note honestly where my vocabulary smuggles in assumptions the philosophers would reject, since in fiction there is no fact of the matter about a term's referent until I write one. And what cannot be said in my vocabulary at all: I suspect "meaning just ain't in the head" is, for me, trivially true — nothing in a novel is in anyone's head except the reader's — and I want to know whether that triviality shows that the externalism debate is about something my toolkit genuinely cannot express, or whether the debate, once translated, is less substantive than philosophers take it to be. If it is the former, tell me what my vocabulary is missing; if the latter, tell me what the philosophers are really arguing about that I have flattened.
