I'm a software engineer with about fifteen years of experience in systems programming — compilers, virtualization, distributed systems — and no formal philosophy background. I've been reading about which computations could be conscious, and whether moral status attaches to the program or the hardware running it. I keep having the feeling that this is a problem I almost understand in my own terms, but I can't tell whether that feeling is insight or overconfidence. Please act as a professional philosopher of mind who is also genuinely fluent in systems software, and help me find out.

Translate the following debate into my working vocabulary — instruction set architectures versus microarchitectures, emulation versus simulation, virtual machines and hypervisors, behavioral equivalence classes, lookup tables, compilation versus interpretation, the distinction between a program as static text and a running process — as faithfully as you can. The pieces I want translated are: (a) functionalism and multiple realizability, the claim that what matters is a system's functional organization rather than its physical substrate; (b) the thought experiment in which the population of a large country implements a brain's state transitions by passing messages, each person following simple rules, and the worry that this system has no experiences even though it is functionally isomorphic to a brain; (c) the gradual-replacement argument, associated with Chalmers, that a system whose components are swapped one by one for functionally equivalent ones, with behavior unchanged at each step, must either retain its experiences or see them fade while nothing functional changes, and what that is supposed to show about substrate independence; (d) Searle-style objections that syntax is never sufficient for semantics, and that simulating a thing is not doing the thing, in the way that a program simulating digestion digests nothing; and (e) the question I actually care about: whether moral status attaches to the running process rather than the static artifact, and what that implies about attributing sentience to a system emulating a mind, or to a giant lookup table that realizes the same input-output function.

Think carefully before you commit to the translations — sketch a few candidate renderings for each piece and keep the most faithful rather than the most flattering to my vocabulary. Keep the translation honest: do not smuggle in engineering assumptions (that observable behavior is all there is, that emulation and simulation are cleanly distinguished in practice, that anything computable is implementable) and present them as the philosophers' claims. And give me the residue report: for each piece, say what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion, and what has no expression in my vocabulary at all, and tell me whether that residue points to something my vocabulary is missing or to something less clear in the philosophers' claims than it looked.

Present your entire answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, one each for (a) through (e), with the residue analysis folded into the relevant item rather than saved for the end. Each item should be one or two sentences of dense but plain prose. No introduction, no conclusion, and no text outside the list.
