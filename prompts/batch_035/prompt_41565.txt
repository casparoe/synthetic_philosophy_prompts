I'd like your help identifying a concept, and I'd rather have careful carving than a quick label, so please consider the nearest candidates before concluding anything.

Here is the pattern. In discussions of minds, science, or society, someone invokes Gödel's incompleteness theorems for a sweeping conclusion: that no system can understand itself, that any theory rich enough to describe the world must leave some truths unestablished, that science can never finish its self-description, that a legal system must contain gaps it cannot close. When pressed, the arguer retreats to the precise statement — any consistent, effectively axiomatized theory strong enough for arithmetic is incomplete — and, having defended that, goes on using the sweeping conclusion as though it had been established. What makes it interesting is that the retreat usually isn't a knowing bait-and-switch; the arguer is often sincere. The slide happens through words like "system", "prove", and "complete", which mean something exact in the theorem and something much looser in the conclusion, so the theorem appears to license a claim it says nothing about. I've noticed the same figure with other results: Heisenberg's uncertainty principle invoked to show that knowledge in general is limited, the second law of thermodynamics invoked to show that social order must decay, the halting problem invoked to show that software can never be fully verified.

I'm aware there's a general literature complaining about loose invocations of Gödel. But I'm after a name for the specific rhetorical structure: the sincere round trip between a true technical statement and a sweeping claim that the statement does not support, mediated by a word that is precise on one side of the trip and vague on the other. The nearest concepts I know are equivocation and motte-and-bailey, but neither quite fits: motte-and-bailey usually involves retreating from a claim one knows is indefensible, whereas here the sweeping claim isn't clearly false, merely unsupported; and it isn't plain equivocation, because the arguer isn't ping-ponging between two fixed meanings of one word so much as borrowing the theorem's authority across the word's vagueness.

So, as an expert in philosophy of logic and the rhetoric of formal results: does this figure have an established name? If yes, define it precisely and say how it differs from equivocation, motte-and-bailey, and mere loose analogy. If not, say so plainly, coin a term, and give a definition that fixes its edges — does it cover using the second incompleteness theorem to argue that minds are not machines, and at what point does an instance stop being this phenomenon and become an ordinary, harmless analogy? A bare name will not satisfy me; I want the boundaries drawn.
