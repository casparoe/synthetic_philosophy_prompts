I teach a graduate seminar on explanation in the social sciences, and next week we reach Weber's Protestant ethic thesis. My students keep talking past each other in a familiar way: the Weberians gesture at "culture as a cause" without saying what the causal claim amounts to, and the reductionists — rational-choice types and materialists alike — reply that values are either rationalizations of interest or shadows of material conditions. I want a constructed case that forces the issue rather than merely restating it.

Build a thought experiment to the following specifications:

1. Two communities, matched on everything material — geography, technology, market access, political institutions, even the distributions of wealth and skill — whose economies diverge within two generations. The divergence must be traceable to a doctrinal difference, and the doctrine must do its work off-label: nothing in it may directly command diligence, thrift, or accumulation. It should mandate something apparently orthogonal — a practice concerning time, or record-keeping, or the examination of conscience — from which methodical economic conduct emerges only through a chain of inferences the faithful themselves draw.

2. At least one pivotal act must run against the material self-interest of the actors, in a way they explicitly cite the doctrine to justify — so that a pure rationalization story has to strain.

3. The case must make two standard reductionist replies visible but answerable within the fiction: a selection story (those who joined the strict faction were already the disciplined sort) and an endogeneity story (the doctrine was elaborated after the conduct, to sanctify it). Build in the evidence that answers each — for instance, records of who converted and when — but be honest about where that evidence thins.

4. No real religions and no real history: a fictional setting, so that we are testing the logic of cultural explanation, not anyone's historiography.

After the scenario, do the demonstration. Give the Weberian causal reading and say exactly what the value is doing in it — is it functioning as a cause, or as a constitutive rule of a practice, and does the answer matter? Then give the strongest reductionist reading — you choose whether the rational-choice or the materialist version is more formidable here — and say which feature of the case blocks it. Finally, tell me which of the four specifications is doing the most work, what happens to the case if I remove that one, and where a clever reductionist would still press the attack. If you find that the case can only succeed by cheating — by stipulating the verdict rather than generating it from the details — say so, and say precisely where.
