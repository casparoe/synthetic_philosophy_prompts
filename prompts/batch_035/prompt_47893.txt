I'm writing a term paper for a course that pairs the philosophy of education with the early history of AI, and my topic is the closing section of Turing's 1950 Mind paper, where he abruptly changes strategy: "Instead of trying to produce a programme to simulate the adult mind, why not rather try to produce one which simulates the child's? If this were then subjected to an appropriate course of education one would obtain the adult brain." What I find most striking is not the child-machine proposal itself but two supporting commitments he makes almost in passing: that the child machine's initial mechanism may be simple and general enough to be programmed outright, and that its teacher operates blind — "An important feature of a learning machine is that its teacher will often be very largely ignorant of quite what is going on inside" — so that rewards and punishments, the ordinary furniture of a schoolroom, are all the teacher has to work with. My thesis is that the education analogy is doing real argumentative work here, not merely decorating an engineering proposal, and to make that case I need a thought experiment that gives the analogy something to do.

Here is what I want you to build. Two design teams get the same target competence, the same budget, and the same information; the only difference is route. Team A engineers the adult competence directly, spelling out the rules and procedures the finished system needs. Team B builds Turing's simple initial machine and then educates it the way he describes: no access to its internals, only reward and punishment administered over a long course of varied exercises. The scenario must be decided by a single observable behavioral outcome — some test or perturbation that neither team anticipated — on which the two routes make opposite predictions, and the design must be such that Turing's route is predicted to win, but only because of one of his specific commitments (the simplicity and generality of the initial mechanism, or the epistemic humility of the teacher, or the variety of the educational environment — you pick), not because the case simply stipulates that the educated machine "understands" and the programmed one doesn't. Constraints: the scenario itself is one paragraph; it must be plausible circa 1950, so no post-Turing vocabulary (no neural nets, no gradient descent, no deep learning — punched cards, instruction tables, and reward signals described in Turing-era terms); no superintelligence; and every difference between the machines must show up in behavior a strictly behaviorist judge could score. After the scenario, walk through what each team predicts and why, hold fixed the variables (budget, information, time) so the route is the only thing that varies, and tell me honestly which single feature of the design is doing the work — what happens to the verdict if you remove that feature, and whether the case collapses into a tie or reverses in Team A's favor.

Since this is meant to be the hinge of my paper, I need a committed construction, not a menu: choose one design and commit to it, state plainly which feature you built the verdict on, and take a definite position on the strongest objection a defender of direct programming would raise — namely, that your chosen test (whatever transfer or flexibility measure you picked) was bound to favor the educated machine, so the case begs the question against engineering rather than showing that Turing's route earns anything. I'd rather have a clean answer that takes that objection seriously and argues one side of it than a survey of possible scenarios, and I'd rather hear that a feature is doing all the work and the case is therefore weaker than I'd hoped than a construction that quietly fudges it. If you think Turing's own commitments make the two routes converge in the scenario as specified, say so and revise the scenario in the minimal way needed to drive them apart, explaining the revision.
