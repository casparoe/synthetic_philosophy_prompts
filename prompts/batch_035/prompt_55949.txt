I've been writing up my AI-risk views for a workshop where everyone has to lay out their actual position and get it picked apart by the others. Before I embarrass myself there I want a consistency audit. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) The orthogonality thesis — how capable a system is and what it terminally wants are basically independent. Nothing about being really smart forces you toward any particular goal; a superintelligence can be built around basically anything.

(2) Strong instrumental convergence — for almost any terminal goal, a sufficiently capable system ends up pursuing self-preservation, resource acquisition, and not letting its goal get rewritten. This is the backbone of why I think capable AI is dangerous regardless of what it's "for."

(3) The systems we actually build — gradient descent over tons of data — produce heaps of context-triggered heuristics rather than one clean global utility function, and I don't think scaling obviously converts that into a coherent expected-utility maximizer. A big pile of very good heuristics can stay a big pile of very good heuristics; the "it must become a unified optimizer" story always sounded more like a philosophical intuition than an engineering claim to me.

(4) Moral realism — there are moral facts that don't depend on what anyone, human or machine, happens to want.

(5) Moral facts are knowable in roughly the way mathematical facts are, and an entity much better than us at figuring out what's true would get the moral facts right the same way it would get the math right. Honestly this is part of why I'm less freaked out about the whole thing than most people at these workshops.

Go through the pairs and tell me which are in real tension, which only look like it, and rank them by severity rather than treating everything as equally damning. For each real one, say what would have to give and what that costs. Then actually commit: if I have to drop or rewrite exactly one of the five, which one, and what does the package look like after? Don't lay out the menu and leave me to choose — you choose, and defend it. If you genuinely think the whole set hangs together you can say so, but you'd better have a real story, not just vibes. End with a line like \giveup{...} naming the commitment that has to go (by number or a short label), or \giveup{none} if you're committing to the consistent-package reading.
