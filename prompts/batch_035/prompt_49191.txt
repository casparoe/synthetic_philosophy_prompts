I spent forty years in isotope geochemistry before retiring, and I have only lately taken up philosophy, so I confess I have fallen back on old habits: when I noticed that my oldest colleague — call him R., a bench chemist I still see at our annual reunion dinners — hands down rulings on who is entitled to blame whom, I began keeping a lab notebook of his verdicts. His record so far, in chronological order: our former department chair, who publicly denounced grant-padding only after padding his own for a decade, without ever acknowledging it, had no standing to blame anyone for it; R. condemns a former postdoc of ours for data dredging, though he dredged data himself once in the eighties, confessed it publicly, and has campaigned against the practice ever since — and he insists his own standing is intact; a cousin of his who plagiarized once long ago, has never told a soul, genuinely regrets it, and would never do it again has, by R.'s lights, full standing to condemn another cousin's plagiarism; and an acquaintance who still gossips, openly admits it, and figures that since everyone does it the norm cannot really bind him has no standing to join in blaming a vicious gossiper. At next month's dinner the case will be a mutual friend who firmly and sincerely blames her former business partner for screaming at subordinates, while she herself has snapped at her own assistants twice this past year — she is ashamed of it, has apologized to each of them, is in therapy, and fully believes the conduct wrong; she simply has not yet succeeded in stamping it out. I would like you to predict R.'s ruling, reconstruct the rule or rules his record is actually consistent with (including, if you can, what his record has already ruled out — the crude view that any past instance of the same fault costs one standing, for instance, seems to have been eliminated by the data), say what each surviving rule predicts here, give me your best guess together with the grounds for preferring it, and propose the one further case that would most cleanly separate the surviving rules.
