I'm in a political philosophy course on theories of distributive justice, and for our final section meeting we've been asked to circulate a short position memo listing our considered commitments on the "equality of what?" question, so that our classmates can challenge them. Before I send mine out, I'd like a consistency audit of the package, because I have a feeling something in it doesn't hang together, but I genuinely can't tell where. Here is what I've written down. (1) I'm convinced, mainly by the disability examples we discussed, that the right metric of advantage is capabilities — what people are actually able to do and be — rather than either resources or subjective welfare. Two people with identical resource bundles can lead radically different lives if one faces physical or social barriers the other doesn't, and any metric that doesn't register that is measuring the wrong thing. (2) At the same time, I accept something Rawlsian: whatever metric we use has to be specifiable in political terms, in a way every citizen could endorse without signing on to any comprehensive doctrine of the good life — I don't want the state ranking ways of living as more or less valuable. (3) I'm also drawn to responsibility-sensitivity, in the luck-egalitarian spirit: disadvantages that trace to a person's own deliberate choices shouldn't attract compensation, while those traceable to circumstance should. (4) I think the demands of justice are ultimately threshold-based rather than strictly egalitarian: what matters is that everyone is brought above some decent capability threshold, and inequalities above that threshold are not unjust in themselves. (5) On the rival metrics: I hold that subjective welfare is decisively ruled out, both because of adaptive preferences (people can be satisfied with genuinely deprived lives) and because of expensive tastes (people can develop costly preferences that inflate what they need). (6) And, as a concrete policy commitment, I think the state should fully fund treatment for smoking-related illness, with no surcharges or cost-sharing for smokers, on the grounds that health is a basic capability and access to it can't be made conditional on how people got sick.

What I'd like from you is an audit rather than a survey. Which pairs of these commitments are in genuine tension, which only look like they are, and how severe is each real conflict? For each genuine one, please tell me what would have to give and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set. I'd particularly like the tensions ranked by severity rather than presented as equally serious, and at least a sentence on whether the apparent conflicts dissolve on closer inspection — I suspect some of them do, but I don't trust myself to say which. I'm not asking you to pick which commitment I should abandon; I'd rather understand exactly what each of them commits me to before deciding that myself.
