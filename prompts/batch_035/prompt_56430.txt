Analyze the controversy over pediatric cochlear implantation in the 1990s through the lens of the gradual-replacement debate in the philosophy of mind. The salient facts: a cochlear implant is an electrode array that stimulates the auditory nerve directly, substituting for the transduction the inner ear performs; implanted early in a congenitally deaf child, it typically permits the development of spoken language; and Deaf communities and their organizations opposed implanting children, on the grounds that deafness is a culture and a form of life rather than a deficit, and that the decision — normally made by hearing parents — presupposed that a deaf life is a lesser one. I want the mapping done carefully rather than gestured at: what exactly is being replaced here, and at which level of description — signal transduction, a whole sensory modality, or a form of life; who occupies the role of the subject whose functions are being substituted, and who has standing to decide; and where the implant sits on the replacement ladder, given that it is external, partial, and — crucially — restores a function that never existed rather than preserving one that did. Then give me an honest account of where the gradual-replacement framework illuminates the controversy and where it breaks: the standard argument for uploading's possibility assumes some level of abstraction at which function is preserved, and I want to know whether, in this episode, choosing that level already takes a side in the dispute over what deafness is — and whether that choice is one the framework itself can justify, or one it simply inherits.
