I've been around the rationalist blogosphere since roughly the Sequences era, and for as long as I can remember the community has had a running flirtation with the Kelly criterion — old arguments about whether a rational agent should bet Kelly, whether expected-value maximization is even coherent for a bounded agent, and more recently the geometric rationality line of thought, which tries to rebuild chunks of decision theory on maximizing geometric rather than arithmetic expectations. Here's what has been nagging at me, and I'd like a careful, rigorous comparison rather than the usual cheerleading.

As best I can tell, the case for Kelly-style behavior rests on two arguments that everyone mixes together but that have visibly different premises:

The first argument — I'll call it the ergodicity argument — is the one popularized by the ergodicity economics crowd. It starts from the observation that repeated multiplicative gambles drive a wedge between the ensemble average of your wealth and the time average. If you bet your entire bankroll each round on a favorable coin, the arithmetic expectation of your wealth after many rounds is enormous, but it is carried entirely by vanishingly improbable jackpot trajectories; the typical trajectory is ruin. Since you live along one trajectory rather than being smeared across the ensemble, arithmetic expectation is simply the wrong functional, and maximizing expected log wealth is what actually captures the growth a single persistent agent experiences. The slogan is something like: expectation averages over copies of you in different branches, but only one of them is you.

The second argument — call it the bargaining argument — comes from the geometric rationality sequence on LessWrong. There, expected utility maximization is reinterpreted as a form of Nash bargaining: an agent facing uncertainty is modeled as a collection of copies of itself, one per possible outcome, who must strike a cooperative bargain over what the pre-commitment should be. Geometric aggregation — maximizing the product or, equivalently, the expected logarithm — falls out as the bargain, with connections drawn to Kelly betting, Thompson sampling, and even Bayesian updating. On this view, the arithmetic maximizer is an agent who lets a single branch's welfare swamp everyone else's, while the Kelly bettor is the fair bargainer.

Both arguments deliver something like the Kelly fraction, but they seem to get there from opposite directions. The ergodicity argument treats the problem as one of correctly describing a single agent extended through time; the bargaining argument treats it as one of many momentarily-coexisting selves who need a fair collective decision rule. What I want to know is whether there is any daylight between them, and if so, exactly where. Concretely, please address at least the following:

1. Does either argument actually derive the Kelly rule, or do they both merely make it post hoc palatable? Where does each one smuggle in a concavity assumption (e.g., log utility) that a classic expected-utility theorist would just say you were entitled to all along?

2. Standard-utility responses. A dyed-in-the-wool VNM theorist says: just assign utility logarithmic in wealth, and ordinary expected-utility maximization recovers Kelly — no ergodicity, no bargaining, no reform of decision theory needed. Does this response gut argument 1, argument 2, both, or neither? If geometric rationality is supposed to apply to all features of the world and not just wealth, does it evade the response in a way the ergodicity argument can't?

3. Divergence cases. Where would the two arguments give different advice or different verdicts? Think about: a genuinely one-shot gamble with no repetition; an agent with outside income (a job, or the ability to borrow) whose wealth process is partly additive; adding two gambles jointly rather than evaluating them separately; agents whose utilities are naturally bounded (lifespan, probability of total extinction); situations where the number of "copies" is literal (many actual parallel agents sharing a bankroll) rather than metaphorical.

4. Time-bunching and branch-counting. The ergodicity argument says the trajectory is what matters and the ensemble is a fiction; the bargaining argument says the branches are populated by real-enough selves that fairness applies. But Kelly-style behavior can also be derived from a symmetric case in which infinitely many copies really do exist and you don't know your index. Which of the two arguments, if either, survives the observation that the other's ontology can be made precise without the one feature it claimed was essential?

Please write this up as a formal comparison: define your terms, keep the math light but exact where it matters, and defend a clear thesis about which argument is doing real foundational work — or whether they are, at bottom, the same argument wearing different ontologies. I'm interested in where they come apart, not in a survey of who believes what, so don't worry about citations or reading lists; the arguments speak for themselves.

End your response with a single line of the form \answer{X}, where X is exactly one of: "ergodicity" (the time-average argument does the fundamental work), "bargaining" (the multi-self bargaining argument does), "equivalent" (they are notational variants of one argument), or "neither" (both reduce to ordinary expected utility with a concave utility function, and neither yields a genuine reform of decision theory). This line should be consistent with the thesis you defend in the body.
