I'm a PhD student in legal philosophy working on the legitimacy of algorithmic decision systems, and I'm preparing a seminar unit that stages a hypothetical exchange. Lon Fuller died in 1978, long before platforms began enforcing content rules through opaque classifiers or governments began using automated systems to flag benefits fraud. But his "internal morality of law" was deliberately general: in his 1958 exchange with H. L. A. Hart and in The Morality of Law, he argued that any system of social ordering through rules must satisfy eight desiderata to count as law at all — generality, publicity, prospectivity, clarity, non-contradiction, the possibility of actually complying with the rules, stability over time, and congruence between the rules as announced and the rules as applied by officials. He also thought a system's failure on these dimensions could be so total that it forfeits its claim to fidelity: his examples of Nazi informer statutes, retroactive laws, and secret terror were meant to show legality is not morally inert.

Suppose Fuller were shown a present-day algorithmic governance arrangement: platform rules on "hate speech" or "authenticity" enforced by machine-learning classifiers, takedown notices that cite no specific violated rule, enforcement criteria that shift whenever the model is retrained, decisions produced by processes even the platform's own staff cannot fully explain, and a private appeals process that reverses the outcome without ever articulating a reason. What would Fuller conclude about whether such a system qualifies as law under his own criteria, and about whether it can earn the fidelity he thought legality deserves? Beyond applying his checklist, I want you to reason in Fuller's own terms: he insisted the internal morality of law is not a neutral instrument but a mode of reciprocity between rule-maker and subject, so think about whether opacity and silent model updates sever precisely that reciprocity. Please take his position seriously rather than refuting it, but also flag honestly where you are extrapolating beyond what he actually wrote, and where a Hart-style positivist would push back on treating platform governance as a question of legality at all. Please state how confident you are in each of your major claims, and single out the point you are least sure of. Please answer in American English.
