I sit on the grants committee of a mid-sized development philanthropy, and at our June meeting we must commit our full remaining allocation for the year — roughly $30 million — to one of two programs in a single low-income country. So that the memo does not turn on contested facts about a real place, let me describe the country rather than name it: it is landlocked, tropical, and malarial; it has a history of extractive colonial settlement followed by post-independence authoritarian rule; and it now has an elected government that, for the first time, has formally invited external partners into governance reform. Program A would fund that governance work: judicial training, an anti-corruption commission with protected staffing, procurement transparency, and a constitutional review process. Program B would fund what its designers call changing the country's givens: a paved freight corridor to the nearest port, malaria and river-blindness control in the corridor's catchment, and rural electrification along its length.

The committee is split along lines that map onto the deep-causes debate. The institutionalist faction holds that institutions are the fundamental cause of prosperity and everything else is downstream or palliative: you cannot expect a road to raise incomes when the customs service taxes it informally at every checkpoint. They lean on the natural experiments — above all the Korean peninsula, two populations with shared history, culture, and geography, diverging catastrophically under different institutional regimes — and on the instrumental-variable tradition that uses early European settler mortality as an instrument for institutional form, arguing that the extractive institutions planted where Europeans could not settle persist to the present and explain the income gap. The geographers reply that the institutionalists' own instruments are geography in disguise: settler mortality varied with disease environment, so the deep cause may be the pathogen ecology, in which case the corridor and the malaria program target the actual root and Program A rearranges the branches. A third trustee, whom I find annoyingly persuasive, holds that both factions overclaim: the identification strategies in this literature exploit historical accidents that no longer vary, the cross-country correlations are hostage to a handful of influential cases, and nothing in them licenses choosing between two forward-looking grants in one country, since we cannot rerun a nation's history with the other program in place.

That same trustee has proposed a way out: that we stop trying to pick the true cause and instead decide by portfolio logic — treat the $30 million as a bet under deep uncertainty, fund whichever program is more reversible and more diagnostic (i.e., teaches us something about the other one), and explicitly stop describing our choice as tracking what history has already proved. The chair has asked me to circulate a written opinion before Thursday, and I want it to do four things. First, recommend a concrete course — Program A, Program B, a specified split with a mechanism for revisiting it, or something structurally different, including the option of declining both if that is honestly the best case. Second, give reasons the losing faction can recognize as responsive to their strongest argument, not a restatement of the other side. Third, contain an honest section on the limits of causal inference from history: what the Korean natural experiment and the settler-mortality instruments can actually establish, what they cannot say about a marginal grant in a single already-poor country, and how a decision-maker should act when the available evidence underdetermines the choice. Fourth, evaluate the trustee's portfolio proposal on its merits: whether it is a principled stance for a philanthropy under deep uncertainty, or a sophisticated way of not deciding, and if the latter, what a better version of it would look like. Please keep the memo to a length the committee will actually read, and write it so that a trustee with no econometrics can follow the causal-inference section without the section being dumbed down.
