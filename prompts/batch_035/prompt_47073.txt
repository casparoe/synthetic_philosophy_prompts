Here is my situation. I'm a high school junior, and our school has two policies that I only recently noticed pull in opposite directions. First, our Academic Integrity Policy treats identical submitted work as presumed plagiarism: if two students hand in the same essay, the default assumption is that someone copied, and coincidence is treated as an extraordinary claim requiring proof. Second, our science curriculum — and basically every science textbook I've seen — teaches that calculus was arrived at independently by Newton and Leibniz, and that natural selection was arrived at independently by Darwin and Wallace, and treats this as an inspiring fact about how science works rather than as something suspicious. My chemistry teacher put it something like this: if Newton had died young, we would still have calculus today, just named after someone else.

So here is what I want to understand. What view of ideas and where they come from does each practice presuppose? The plagiarism rule seems to assume that the same idea occurring to two people is nearly impossible — that ideas are individual possessions with owners. The textbook celebration of simultaneous discovery seems to assume the opposite: that discoveries are, at least sometimes, inevitable, products of a shared intellectual moment rather than of individual genius, so that priority is partly luck — being born at the right time, receiving the right letter, publishing first. And there is a third practice layered on top: prize-giving. Science competitions, the Nobel Prize, and our own school science fair all award the first person and give nothing to second, even when the second person got there independently and honestly.

Please reconstruct the implicit theories behind these practices. Does the plagiarism rule really presuppose that ideas are ownable in a way that makes coincidence nearly impossible, or is there some other view — about incentives or evidence rather than the nature of ideas themselves — that fits it better? Does the priority-obsessed prize system presuppose that discoveries are not inevitable, and would prizes still make sense if everyone fully believed they were? Say whether the practices are consistent with one another, where they are philosophically underdetermined such that several rival theories would license them, and what would actually have to change in a school — in grading, in awards, in how we name theorems and species and buildings — if we took the multiple-discovery view seriously. Finally, does it matter who made a discovery? Would Darwin's claim to credit survive learning that Wallace, or someone else, would inevitably have published the same theory a few years later?
