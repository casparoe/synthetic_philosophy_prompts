I'm drafting an essay on whether white lies are wrong, and I'd like your comments on the notes below. Specifically, I want your judgment on three things: (a) whether my central move succeeds or smuggles in an unargued premise; (b) whether the cross-cultural material earns its place in the argument or is just decoration; and (c) what the strongest objection is that I haven't considered.

Thesis: The white-lie debate is usually run as a clash between benevolence (spare someone's feelings, in a broadly Mill-style way) and sincerity (lying is wrong in itself, in a broadly Kant-style way). Both camps evaluate the individual utterance. I want to argue this is the wrong unit of analysis: white lies are moves in a signaling game, and their moral status is a property of the norm or equilibrium, not of the token utterance.

Notes:

1. Conventionalized indirection. When I end a conversation with "we should get coffee sometime," nobody is deceived; both parties price the register in, the way nobody takes a poker player's patter as testimony. So the Kantian objection misfires whenever it is common knowledge that the hearer is applying a discount filter. The philosophically interesting cases are precisely those where it's uncertain which register is in force.

2. Where the filter fails. Consider complimenting a friend's genuinely bad work. If she cannot tell whether I'm in honest mode or kind mode, my utterance degrades the information value of all my future praise. The primary victim of the white lie is therefore not its immediate hearer but my future self, trying to give honest feedback in what I call a "polity of discounting." White lies as a commons problem: in a high-trust norm regime, honest speakers free-ride, but each kind lie slightly erodes the shared resource of words that can be taken at face value.

3. Cross-cultural material. Dutch directness is supposed to show that sincerity is viable at scale. But the Dutch norm works because it is a shared expectation; the same utterance transplanted into a Japanese high-context setting is not more honest, just uninterpretable — it can't be decoded as either sincerity or kindness because the code has no slot for it. So the live variable isn't honesty versus politeness but the cost of maintaining common knowledge about which register one is in. The real question isn't "are white lies wrong?" but "who should bear the cost of disambiguation, and when is that cost worth paying?"

My own worries: I suspect section 2 quietly assumes a rule-consequentialism about norms that I never argue for; and I fear the Kantian will say the equilibrium framing simply dodges the question of whether the individual act is a lie. I'm also unsure whether the "polity of discounting" idea does any real work, or whether it just restates section 1 in grander language.
