I'm a retired condensed matter physicist — thirty-seven years in a lab, mostly messing about with semiconductors — and since retiring I've fell hard for philosophy of science, of all things. My walking partner Dorothy, who taught high school chemistry for decades and can out-argue anyone, has gotten in the habit of issuing rulings on little thought-experiment worlds I cook up for her about laws of nature, and I've been keeping notes. Her verdicts so far: (1) if there were a world whose entire history, every last event, was identical to ours, it couldn't have different laws — she was extremely firm on that; (2) in a world where the whole career of every particle fits some monstrous equation with hundreds of terms, nothing simpler comes remotely close, and the fit is perfect, that ugly equation is the law of that world — nature doesn't owe us elegance, she says; (3) a world containing just ten particles that wander around with no pattern anyone could find has no laws at all, or at least none worth the name — and when I pressed her on whether there might be laws there that are just hopeless to state, she said maybe, but there'd be nothing to point to; and (4) in a world that runs exactly like ours up to next Tuesday, at which point every particle freezes dead mid-flight and stays frozen forever, the laws right up to Tuesday were still our laws — she called the freeze itself a one-off miracle, not a change in the laws of that world.

Here's the new one I haven't sprung on her yet, and I want your read before I do. Take a world just like ours except that at midnight tonight it simply ends — no frozen tableau, no last tableau at all, just no events after midnight, ever. In that world, were the laws in force this afternoon our laws? And a second part, which I suspect is the sharper question: in that world, if you'd asked someone this morning what the laws of nature are, could the correct answer have depended on how much more world there was going to be afterward?

What I want from you isn't just a yes or a no. First, lay out the candidate rules Dorothy might be running — I can see at least two that fit her four verdicts just as well as each other, possibly more — and say what each one predicts for the ending world and for my second question, since I'd bet they come apart there. Then give me your best guess at what she'll actually say, with your reasons for backing that rule over its rivals. And finally, design me one more thought-experiment world that would cleanly split whatever candidate rules are still tied after that, because I'd like to settle this while she's still amused enough to keep humoring me.
