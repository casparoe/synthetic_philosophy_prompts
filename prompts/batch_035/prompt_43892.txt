So I've been reading LessWrong and the surrounding blogosphere since like 2011, and the free will debates there have mostly been the standard "libertarianism is incoherent, compatibilism is obvious, next question" thing, which I basically bought for a decade. But then a friend and I both read the Dennett-Caruso debate book (Just Deserts: Debating Free Will, the back-and-forth one they did together) and it totally detonated our reading group, because it turns out the compatibilist position you pick has huge consequences for what you think punishment is *for*, and my two friends came out of it in opposite corners and have been fighting about it ever since. Last weekend it got properly heated and I transcribed the gist because I genuinely can't tell who's winning.

The exchange went roughly like this. (1) Maya, who came out a Caruso-style skeptic, said just deserts is a fantasy: nobody is the ultimate author of their character, so nobody *deserves* to suffer for what they did, period — prisons should be about protection and rehabilitation, full stop, and any suffering inflicted is either a regrettable means or just cruelty dressed up. (2) Jonas, the Dennett partisan, said that's demanding a magic kind of desert nobody sensible ever needed: on his view desert is just the everyday fact that competent, reasons-responsive agents who weren't coerced can be held responsible, and giving them worse treatment than bystanders is fair *because* they did it, not merely because it deters. (3) Maya said that's sleight of hand — reasons-responsiveness explains why someone is a fit target for influence and blame-as-communication, but it never explains why suffering is *owed* to them, it only explains why it's useful or expressive, so he's smuggled in retributivism under a compatibilist label. (4) Jonas fired back that if desert needs "basic desert" grounded in ultimate self-creation or it doesn't exist, then the same argument applies to basically everything we value — gratitude, achievement, even the special wrong of betraying a friend — and Maya presumably doesn't want to deflate all of that, so the free will skeptic is only being a skeptic where it's emotionally cheap, i.e. about punishing criminals. (5) Maya's last move was that gratitude and the rest are non-moral comparisons, they don't license *inflicting harm on purpose*, which is a categorically different thing that needs a justification she claims the compatibilist never actually produces.

They've asked me to referee and I honestly bounced off it — I can feel that move 5 is doing a lot of work (is intentionally harming someone really categorically different from owing them worse treatment?) but I can't say who's actually landed blows where. Please go through the five moves and rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with your reasons, and then tell me who wins overall and, importantly, whether there's some option both of them are missing entirely — e.g. whether blame and punishment might come apart, or whether Jonas's everyday desert can justify non-consensual hard treatment at all without borrowing from Maya's toolbox. I'd especially like to know if move 4, the "deflate everything or nothing" argument, is as strong as it sounds, because it felt a bit like an appeal to consequences for valuing things.
