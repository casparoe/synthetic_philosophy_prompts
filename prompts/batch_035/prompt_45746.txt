I joined my university's Equal Access and Hardship Committee this term. The fund it administers is small and discretionary, its stated purpose "to remove financial barriers to full participation in university life", and it is spent case by case with no written criteria. On Monday I must draft our recommendation on a new application, and I would rather be consistent with the committee's own record than with my private instincts. That record, from this session:

1. We funded a full sign-language interpreter rota for a deaf student and, over one member's objection that this went beyond access to teaching, added a contribution toward evening sign-language classes for her flatmates, on the ground that isolation outside seminars was itself a barrier.
2. We refused a hardship grant for counselling after a student's painful breakup, though she described severe distress; the minute records that ordinary grief, however sharp, is "something everyone must carry without compensation".
3. We funded travel support for a student hit by a rail fare rise at the level of the cheapest season ticket, and only that level, when she had asked for help toward running a car.
4. We refused resit tutoring for a student who had skipped lectures all term to tour with his band, while funding identical tutoring for a student whose failure followed a long hospital admission.
5. We funded a wheelchair user's share of an adapted kitchen in her shared flat, beyond the accessible rooms the university could provide free, because eating with her flatmates mattered.

The new application: a final-year student with inflammatory arthritis lives on a route served by the university's subsidized accessible bus. She asks us to cover the difference between the bus fare and a taxi — about £30 a week — saying that the bus's crowding and jolting leave her exhausted and in pain by the time she arrives, and that she has begun skipping classes. Nothing in her file disputes any of this.

Tell me, definitively, what we should decide. Set out the rules that could fit our five decisions — I can see at least three — what each predicts about the taxi application, and which precedent is the closest analog. Then commit: give one recommendation with your reasons, and state plainly what principle we would be adopting by deciding that way rather than its rivals. Finally, name the application that, if it ever reached us, would most cleanly separate the candidate rules.
