My aesthetics textbook dissolves the paradox of fiction with a make-believe analogy: a work of fiction is a prop in a game of make-believe, the way a doll is a prop when a kid pretends it's her baby. The doll plus the game make it fictional that there's a baby there; the novel plus my participation make it fictional that there's a girl on a train. So when I'm terrified for the girl, what I've got is quasi-fear, a state internal to the game rather than real fear -- hence no irrationality in caring about someone I know doesn't exist, and the paradox dissolves. My roommate, who does philosophy of emotion, says the doll is doing all the work in this argument and the comparison is actually pretty shaky -- her example is that the kid makes the game up as she goes, whereas the novel's content was fixed by an actual author long before I ever picked it up. Can you lay out the correspondence table: what plays the role of the prop, the game, the rules that say what's to be imagined, who's playing, who's authoring. Then the places the mapping breaks -- not just her point, I want the rest of them too, things like the difference between playing a game and spectating at one, or the fact that doll games don't have anything like genres and narrative conventions telling you in advance what to imagine. Then tell me which of the disanalogies actually matter for the no-genuine-emotion conclusion and which are just noise, and whether the conclussion survives once you throw out the parts of the analogy that don't hold -- or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole way. And if the doll is just the wrong prop, what would a better one be?
