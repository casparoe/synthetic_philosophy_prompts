The dispute is Kavka's toxin puzzle, which has been running since the 1980s and, as far as I can tell, has not moved in decades. The setup: a billionaire offers you one million dollars if, at midnight tonight, you intend to drink a toxin tomorrow afternoon; the toxin will make you painfully ill for a day but threatens no lasting harm; the money is paid at midnight, before you drink; and drinking has no consequences beyond the illness itself. Once you have the money, drinking serves no purpose whatsoever.

The camps, as I understand them, are roughly three. First, an orthodox instrumentalist line: a rational agent cannot form the intention, because the agent knows in advance that at the time of drinking there will be no reason left to drink, and an intention you know you will have conclusive reason to abandon is not something you can rationally or even sincerely form; Bratman-style planning theorists give this a structural gloss, arguing that intentions are not directly formable at will and that their persistence norms explain the impasse. Second, the constrained-maximization or resolute-choice tradition associated with Gauthier and McClennen, which holds that it can be rational to form and carry through such an intention, because rationality attaches to whole plans, dispositions, or policies rather than to each later act considered in isolation. Third, a revisionist cluster, including work in the Mele tradition, which suspects the puzzle turns on whether intentions are under direct voluntary control or on an overly demanding assumption about the reflective insight agents have into their own future rationality.

My reading group has cycled through this for a year, everyone understands everyone else's position, and nobody has moved an inch. My working hypothesis is that the dispute is at bottom about the relata of rationality — whether 'rational' primarily evaluates acts at the moment of performance, whole plans or dispositions, or persons and their characters — and that the puzzle is a pressure point where these three conceptions come apart while the word stays the same. But I am not confident: it might instead be a genuine substantive disagreement about whether intentions are directly voluntary, or about the coherence norms that govern them, or two groups answering different questions under one heading, with one group treating the case as a test for a theory of intention and the other as a test for a theory of rational choice.
