Last month my family relocated from Massachusetts to Wisconsin, and the move has made a set of positions I've quietly held for years suddenly practical: canvassers at the door, a vote that people now insist "actually matters," and my partner's demand that we settle our household food policy before our daughter is old enough to ask why we eat what we eat. Before these positions harden into anything I defend out loud, I want an audit of whether they can all be true at once.

Here is the set. (1) I'm an act consequentialist, and I take expected value to be the right criterion for what to do when effects are uncertain. (2) I believe markets are full of thresholds — production runs, rounding in orders, aggregation at the distributor — such that a single one of my purchases is almost never the straw that breaks the camel's back; the probability that my buying or not buying a given chicken or carton of eggs changes what gets produced is vanishingly small, and I've concluded the expected effect of my individual consumption on animal suffering is effectively zero. (3) On the strength of that, I think paying a premium for welfare-labeled eggs does nothing for chickens, and that the right response to these harms is to redirect the money one would otherwise spend on such premiums to effective animal charities, where it buys a known quantity of good rather than a lottery ticket on a rounding threshold. (4) I have nonetheless been vegetarian for twelve years and intend to raise my daughter vegetarian; when pressed, I say this is just who I am, not a calculation. (5) I have never missed an election — including local primaries — and I lose some respect for people who stay home on the grounds that one vote never changes anything. (6) I have no patience for expressive justifications: doing something causally pointless in order to feel like the sort of person who does it strikes me as self-regarding theater, whether it's recycling one extra bottle, skipping a flight to send a message, or wearing a pin. (7) I fly several times a year for work and buy carbon offsets, and I think that is perfectly fine.

Which of these commitments are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and what is the cheapest revision that would restore consistency? Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, and for each one say what would have to give and what that concession would cost the rest of the set. I'm especially interested in whether my attitude toward voting can be made to cohere with my attitude toward consumption, but don't let that steer you away from worse problems elsewhere in the set.

Begin your reply with the bottom line in the first sentence — your overall verdict on whether the set hangs together — and follow immediately with the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
