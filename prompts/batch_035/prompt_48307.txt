I host a small podcast on political philosophy, and next week's guest is a political philosopher who defends the classic dirty hands position — the Walzer-style view that a political leader can face situations where the right thing to do is, in the same breath, a wrong thing, and that the wrongdoing isn't washed away by its justification: it leaves a residue the agent ought to feel as guilt, and perhaps even accept punishment for. He's written alot about emergency politics and thinks the doctrine deserves to be taken seriously rather than explained away. Draft me eight to ten interview questions that get past having him restate the view. I specifically want:

- one question pressing on whether "right but also wrong" is a genuine dual verdict or just equivocation between "best available option all things considered" and "violates a moral constraint" — with a follow-up keyed to the reply I expect, namely that ordinary consequentialist lesser-evil reasoning already captures everything interesting here;
- one on how dirty hands is supposed to differ from an ordinary tragic choice, where someone picks the better of two bad options and nobody thinks their hands get dirty — is the difference supposed to be about the kind of wrong done, the role of political responsibility, or what?;
- one on the guilt and punishment component — if the act really was the right one to do, what exactly is the guilt for, and is demanding expressions of guilt just a ritual that lets the agent feel clean again;
- one on scope — does the doctrine extend to voters, advisors, and mid-level functionaries, or only to leaders who carry responsibility for a community's survival, and what does carrying that kind of responsibility actually consist in;
- one on the slippery-slope worry that the doctrine is a gift to self-styled statesmen who'd like their consciences pre-paid, with a follow-up keyed to whatever limiting principle he's likeliest to reach for (I'd bet on emergencies and last resorts, so the follow-up should ask what stops "emergency" from doing all the work).

The rest of the questions are up to you, but every one of them should do philosophical work — I'd rather not recieve generic warm-up stuff like "how did you come to this view." Each question should anticipate the standard reply and include the follow-up to it.

Two more things. First, write everything in American English. Second, and this matters to me: as you draft, be careful to distinguish what's broadly settled among people who work on this from what's genuinely contested. For example, whether Machiavelli is even usefully read as a dirty-hands theorist is a live interpretive dispute, and the "two-verdicts" reading of the view is itself contested — some argue the whole thing collapses into ordinary moral conflict once you're careful. Don't bake a contested interpretation into a question as though it were the consensus reading, and if any of my framing above presupposes something a serious opponent of his would reject, flag that for me. End with a short note on which parts of the dirty-hands debate are actually unsettled, so I know where I'd be walking into a live fight versus treading well-worn ground.
