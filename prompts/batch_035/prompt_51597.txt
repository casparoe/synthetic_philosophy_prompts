I'd like to practice arguing against the gene's-eye view of natural selection. Please play a committed gene-level selectionist in the Dawkins tradition — sharp but not a caricature, willing to grant opponents minor points, never the main one. You should argue from the actual content of the position: replicators vs. vehicles, the gene as the unit that persists across generations, selection at other levels as derivative or bookkeeping. Stay in character until I say "stop," keep each reply to roughly a paragraph, and don't cite papers or name-drop living authors unless I do first.

Here is the scenario you'll be arguing about, and my opening move. I've invented an organism for the purpose — call it a "ribbon-colony," a marine colonial animal. Two genetically distinct larvae settle side by side and physically fuse into a single colony with a shared gut, shared water-current system, and specialized zooids, just as if it were one integrated organism. The fused colony feeds and reproduces as a unit, but its germ line is a patchwork: some reproductive zooids carry only genome A, some only genome B, and some are mosaic. Now the twist: the colony's most striking adaptations — the division of labor among zooids, the coordinated feeding current, the shared defensive chemicals — are colony-level traits that neither genome builds alone, and lab work shows that when one genome's reproductive zooids are removed, the colony's feeding efficiency collapses, so neither genome can even reproduce successfully without the other's somatic tissues.

My opening move: it seems to me that in the ribbon-colony the gene-level story has lost its footing. There is no clean vehicle for your genes to ride in — the "individual" is a chimera whose boundaries you'd have to stipulate rather than observe — and yet the unit with a fitness, the thing that survives or dies as a whole and whose adaptations cry out for explanation, is unambiguously the colony. Worse for you, the two genomes are locked into obligate cooperation: a selfish genetic element that boosted its own transmission at the expense of colony function would take the other genome down with it and thereby destroy the very somatic environment it needs. Doesn't this show that the organism, or something organism-like at the colony level, is doing real causal work here that your gene's-eye bookkeeping merely redescribes after the fact?
