I've been living in the US on a green card for like 15 years and finally decided to naturalize, mostly for practical reasons -- voting, not having to sweat my status every time I change jobs, my kids are Americans anyway. But now the oath ceremony is coming up and something about it bugs me. The oath has you renounce your old allegiances and pledge to bear arms for the country if it comes to that, and honestly I feel weird promising special loyalty to 330 million strangers just becuase they happen to live on the same landmass as me. My wife thinks I'm massively overthinking it -- she says loving your country is like loving your family, nobody calls you a bad person for caring more about your own kids than random kids. My brother-in-law goes further and says I'd be a straight-up freeloader if I took the passport with zero sense of loyalty: I've enjoyed the roads, the schools, the fact that other people's taxes funded the army that kept this place safe while I sat out whatever burdens came with belonging to it. Then there's my dad back home, who says patriotism of any kind is exactly the thing that gets young men killed in other people's wars, so the less anyone has of it the better. And my coworker, who gives most of her paycheck to charities abroad, tells me that caring more about compatriots than about people who are worse off and easier to help is just map bias dressed up as a virtue. So now I've got four people in my ear and no clear view of my own. Is there an actual position here that's more than either "patriotism is great" or "patriotism is a disease"? Like, is there a version of being a good citizen I could honestly swear to -- and if so, what does it let me get away with, and where does it stop? Or should I just treat the ceremony as paperwork, say the words, and not worry about what they mean? I'd genuinely like to hear how the different sides of this old argument would size up my specific situation, and which side you think handles my brother-in-law's freeloader point best.
