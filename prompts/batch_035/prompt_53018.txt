I'm writing a seminar paper on the value problem (the Meno problem, why knowledge beats true belief) and I keep bumping up against the road-to-Larissa move — the thought that the guy who *knows* the road to Larissa will reliably get there while the guy with the lucky true belief might wander off. Fine as far as it goes, but it makes knowledge's value hostage to instrumental usefulness, and we all know where that leads (the swamping problem and friends).

So here's what I actually want: a thought experiment where the person with the *mere true belief* does strictly better, practically speaking, than the person with knowledge. The reverse of the Larissa direction. Constraints:

- Everyday scenario — no demons, no clairvoyance, no fake barns, nothing a normal person couldn't plausibly find themselves in.
- The true believer's advantage has to flow from whatever it is that keeps her belief from counting as knowledge in the first place — e.g. the speed or cheapness of an unearned hunch versus the time cost of earning a safe belief through inquiry — and not from some bolted-on psychological trait like boldness or overconfidence. If the advantage is really coming from "she just happens to be the decisive type," the case is cheating and I will notice.
- Both people should hold the same true belief about the same question and both should act on it; the only difference between them is epistemic position.

After the scenario, walk me through:

1. What the practical/instrumental answer to the Meno problem has to say about a case like this, and whether it can just bite the bullet (maybe by saying the utility was never in the belief-state itself but in the surrounding inquiry costs?).
2. Whether the achievement/credit answer — knowledge as epistemic success attributable to cognitive ability, Sosa's archer picture — fares any better. The case looks engineered to punish credit, so tell me whether that's a genuine problem for the view or just an artifact of my constraints.
3. Which feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to the verdict if you remove it — e.g. if verification becomes instant and free, does the whole thing collapse back into the ordinary situation where knowledge wins?
4. And honestly — do you think a case meeting all these constraints can even be built, or is the impossibility itself the interesting result? I half suspect the only way true belief ever wins is by importing the *costs of earning* knowledge into the ledger, and if that's right, the Larissa fan might have a comeback I should take seriously before I build a paper on this.

For what it's worth, I'm inclined to think the interesting version of the case is one where inquiry costs do all the work, but I want to see whether there's a cleaner construction I'm missing before commiting to that line.
