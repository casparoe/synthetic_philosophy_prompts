Two years ago I bought a framed charcoal landscape at an estate sale, attributed to a well-known early-twentieth-century artist. I've since had it examined by a specialist at an auction house, who tells me it's almost certainly not by that artist: most likely a deliberate forgery made decades ago, executed well enough that the paper, ink, and signature all pass inspection. Nothing about the object hanging on my wall has changed — same marks, same sheet, same image I've enjoyed looking at every day — and yet I find I enjoy it less. I'm trying to work out whether that reaction tracks something real or is just snobbery by another name.

As a software engineer, I keep reaching for analogies, and they keep disagreeing with each other. Two files with identical contents but different histories are, for almost every practical purpose, the same file — if the bytes match, the history is a curiosity. Yet my colleagues and I care intensely about a project's commit history: a fork of a repository, even one that is byte-identical to the original, is somehow not the project, and none of us would describe it that way. And the people who buy NFTs seem to think provenance is the entire product, while nearly everyone else finds that absurd. I can't tell which of these intuitions, if any, applies to my drawing.

So my question is this. When a forgery is discovered, does anything of value actually leave the object — some real property the original has and the copy lacks — or does the object remain exactly as valuable as it was the day before the specialist called, with only its market price and my social standing taking the hit? Two related points I'd like your view on: does it matter whether the piece was made as a deliberate deception rather than an honest copy, and does it matter that it succeeded for decades at fooling people who looked carefully? I'd also welcome your advice on what to do with the drawing now — keep it, display it, quietly retire it — though I care more about getting the underlying question right than about the interior decorating.

Please take a definite position. I've read enough to know that thoughtful people have defended both answers, so a survey of the options won't help me. Commit to one, tell me where the version-control analogy helps and exactly where it breaks down, and be explicit about the cost of your view — which of my intuitions it asks me to throw overboard, and why that's a price worth paying.
