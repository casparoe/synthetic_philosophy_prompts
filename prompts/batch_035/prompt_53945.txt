I teach high school physics (public school, mostly 10th graders, ~28 per class, wide ability spread), and I've been trying to get smarter about lesson design. At a PD day last month one of the facilitators went off script about predictive processing and the free energy principle — the idea that the brain is basically a prediction machine and learning is what happens when predictions get beaten by reality, so teaching is really about managing prediction error. It sounded like it might actually cash out into something usable, unlike most of what we get in PD.

But there's a tension I keep hitting and can't resolve myself. If brains want to minimize prediction error, the "optimal" lesson would be maximally predictable, which is obviously backwards — a dark room would be the best classroom. So the theory must have some way of distinguishing error you should attend to from noise you should ignore, and also some story about why learners actively seek out uncertainty in the first place rather than avoiding it. I gather the first bit involves something called precision weighting and the second involves expected free energy, but I don't trust my pop-science-level understanding of either. Complicating matters: my department chair is a hardline "desirable difficulties" person — retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving, make 'em struggle a bit before telling them — and I honestly can't tell whether predictive processing *adjudicates* between her view and a more "keep students comfortable and scaffolded" view, or whether it just redescribes the same classroom moves in fancier language.

So here's what I want: a procedure I can actually run in about fifteen minutes at my kitchen table while planning a lesson. Something like an ordered checklist — which predictions to elicit from students before revealing anything, how big a gap between prediction and outcome is useful vs. just confusing, when to shrink the gap by scaffolding and when to let it stand, what to do with the kid whose prediction was already right (no error, no learning?). For each step, say what counts as a good enough answer and where the procedure has to stop and hand it back to my judgment because it's a values call or the theory runs out. Then run it on two real lessons of mine: (1) introducing Newton's third law, where I have students predict which vehicle feels the bigger force when a loaded dump truck rear-ends a compact car (almost everyone says the car, and the wrongness is productive); and (2) my famous first-day-of-the-unit demo where I fire a ping pong ball through three soda cans using a vacuum cleaner and PVC pipe — the kids love it, it's the one thing they remember in June, and I have a bad feeling your procedure is going to tell me something about it that I don't want to hear. Be honest about which parts of the procedure are real predictive-processing content and which parts are just good pedagogy wearing a lab coat, and tell me where the procedure is most likely to misfire on a real Tuesday with real sophomores.
