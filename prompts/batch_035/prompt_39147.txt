My roommate is a stats grad student and she thinks she's refuted the idea of believing anything, and honestly she has me half convinced. Her setup: she writes risk reports where she makes about 10,000 separate claims, each one she'd put at 99.99% confidence or better, and she also writes a preface saying "in any report this long at least one figure will be wrong" — and she says she believes the preface just as firmly as any claim in the body. Then she runs this argument on me:

(1) If your evidence puts p above 0.999, believing p outright is rational — refusing to believe things you're that sure of is an epistemic vice. (2) Rational belief is closed under conjunction: if it's rational to believe p and rational to believe q, it's rational to believe p-and-q, and this iterates. (3) So it's rational for her to believe the conjunction of all 10,000 claims in her report. (4) It's also rational for her to believe the preface — that at least one claim in the report is false — since she knows her own error rates better than anyone. (5) Those two beliefs contradict, and rational belief can't be contradictory. (6) So (1) is false: no matter how high the probability, it never licenses outright belief. (7) Now take anything you believe — that your keys are where you left them, that your friend will show up friday, that the sun will rise. She says each of these is just a claim in some giant report you're implicitly writing, each with some nonzero error rate, and you know it. (8) So rationality forbids all empirical belief, and since you can't act on bare credences without ever treating anything as flat-out true, she concludes that outright belief is a convenient fiction we should stop philosophizing about, and maybe that rationality forbids acting too.

I obviously can't accept (8). But I can't find the bad step either. I know the lottery version of this is famous — fair million-ticket lottery, rationally believe each ticket will lose, rationally believe someone will win — and I know the standard moves are "drop closure" or "drop the threshold." But her preface version feels nastier, because her claims aren't dependent on each other the way lottery tickets are (the tickets only all have high probability because exactly one wins; her claims are just separate facts she's separately sure of), so the usual trick of blaming the dependence structure doesn't obviously work. If it matters, I'd much rather keep (1) than (2), and I want to know exactly what keeping (1) costs — does it mean my roommate rationally believes something she also rationally believes is false about her own report, and is that actually so bad? Also, between (6) and (8) something smells like a slide — she goes from "no fixed threshold licenses belief" to "no empirical belief is rational," and I can't pin down whether that's even the same claim. Which step do you reject, and what do I pay for rejecting it?
