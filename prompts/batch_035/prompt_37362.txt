This term I am teaching an advanced undergraduate seminar on pain and the mind–body problem, and I have to write the final exam. The unit the students struggled with most concerned pain asymbolia: patients — typically after damage to or disruption of limbic-cortical circuitry, with the syndrome described in the literature under that name — who report that they feel pain, localize it, rate its intensity as normal or high, and yet show no aversion to it. They do not withdraw from the stimulus, they may smile or shrug through procedures others would find unbearable, and they request analgesics only casually, if at all. The textbook moral drawn from this is a two-component picture of pain: a sensory-discriminative component that registers where and how intense the stimulus is, and an affective-motivational component that makes the sensation matter to the subject, with the asymbolic patients lacking or disconnected from the second. The further moral usually drawn is that the badness of pain lives in the affect, not in the raw phenomenal feel — asymbolics have the feel without the badness, so the feel by itself cannot be what makes pain bad.

I want one essay question, answerable in about ninety minutes, that cannot be satisfied by reciting the case reports and the two-component slogan. The question should force students to engage with at least the following pressure points. First, the inference from lack of aversion to lack of badness: a student who has grasped the issue should ask whether the asymbolic patient's calm might be a revaluation of the pain rather than evidence of its harmlessness — a kind of Stoic accommodation — and should consider whether first-person reports are data about badness or partly constitutive of it. Second, the modal structure of the claim: the case at most shows that the phenomenal character is not sufficient for badness; the question must make students see the gap between that and the stronger constitutive thesis that the badness of pain consists in its affective character. Third, a converse probe: whether an experience that is not pain at all (say, intense dread) could be bad in the same way, which tests whether the affective story generalizes or quietly assumes that only pains need explaining.

Please give me the exam question as it would appear on the paper, a model answer of roughly 600 to 700 words, and a marking scheme totalling 20 marks built around five must-hit points, with the marks allocated to each and a one-line note on how students typically go wrong on each — for instance conflating a sufficient-condition reading with a constitutive one, or treating the patient's indifference as straightforward proof rather than as evidence standing in need of interpretation. It would also help if you flagged, in a sentence or two after the marking scheme, the single best follow-up question I could use in the oral exam for a borderline student who wrote a competent but purely descriptive answer.
