I'm a novelist, five chapters into a book whose narrator is a former Buddhist monk who's lost his faith and can't stop arguing theology with everyone who will hold still. His whole arc is about a man who spent thirty years meditating on the nonexistence of the self and now, having left the monastery, can't figure out who exactly is supposed to be having the crisis. So obviously the anatta doctrine is the thing he chews on all book long. The problem is that I got his big argument into shape with a philosopher friend of mine over too much wine, and its uncomfortably clean — I suspect it proves too much, and I can't find the seam, and my narrator now sounds smarter than I do. Here's the argument as he delivers it in chapter seven: (1) The Buddha's core insight is anatta — there is no enduring, independent self behind experience, just a stream of aggregates (body, feeling, perception, volitions, consciousness) arising and passing away. (2) What exists in this moment is numerically distinct from what exists the next moment; the aggregates now are not the aggregates a moment ago. (3) If nothing persists, then the one who performs an action and the one who later reaps its karmic fruit are two different events, not one being. (4) But karma, on which the whole teaching rests, is precisely the claim that what a being does bears on what that same being later experiences — and if the doer and the experiencer are numerically distinct, karma rewards and punishes no one. (5) Likewise rebirth: rebirth without a self is just one stream ending and another starting, which is ordinary birth and death, not rebirth. (6) And if nothing persists even from moment to moment, there is no one who suffers at all, hence no one to liberate — nirvana solves a problem no one has. (7) So the no-self doctrine devours the very things it was supposed to make sense of, and the Buddha's teaching is self-undermining.

My friend swears the attack belongs at step 3, muttered something about me conflating identity with mere continuity, and then ordered another bottle, which didn't help. Someone else told me there's an old dialogue where a king gets told a chariot isn't to be found in its parts, and that this is supposed to settle it, but I haven't actually gotten to reading it and I don't want to fake it for the narrator. So: where exactly does the argument go wrong, and what do I have to give up to break it? I'd rather not gut premise 1 if I can avoid it — the entire novel depends on my narrator believing it, and frankly so do I, most mornings before coffee. If the honest answer is that some step has to go and its going to cost him something, I want to know what it costs, because that trade-off might be the actual ending of the book.
