I'm an amateur birdwatcher who keeps a backyard logbook, and a recent discussion with friends from my local birding club has left me genuinely confused about how to treat my own records. We were talking about Hempel's raven paradox: since "all ravens are black" is logically equivalent to "all non-black things are non-ravens," any white teapot I observe seems, by the same evidential logic, to confirm that all ravens are black. My friends insisted this is a philosopher's curiosity with no bearing on real observation, but then one of them raised a toy example from the confirmation-theory literature that unsettled me even more: suppose an ornithologist knows the region contains either a hundred ravens, every one of them black, or else a thousand ravens of which exactly one is black; if she then spots a black raven chosen at random, that sighting actually supports the hundred-raven hypothesis, under which nearly all the remaining, unseen ravens are not black — so seeing a black raven can make it *less* probable that all ravens are black.

My own situation seems to have the same structure of worries, plus one more. My logbook is hopelessly nonrandom: I record corvids whenever I happen to be outdoors near raven habitat, but I only ever "sample" the class of non-black things from my kitchen and desk, where ravens are not exactly common. So I can't tell whether my records confirm anything, confirm it only trivially, or would even have the direction of support backwards in some bounded-population scenario like the one above. I'd like to understand whether any of this matters outside the seminar room.
