I write a column on science and ideas for a mid-sized daily, and I have become preoccupied with a norm so pervasive in my profession that we almost never state it: straight-news reporting on contested political and scientific questions presents "both sides" in roughly equal measure, each in its own voice, with the paper's own voice withheld. When we covered our city's recent fluoridation referendum, the story quoted the regional dental association and, in about the same number of paragraphs, a retired toxicologist opposed to the measure; the reporter was not permitted to say, in that story, that the toxicologist's central claim about IQ effects is false — even though our fact-checking column had flatly rated it false a month earlier, and our editorial page had endorsed the pro-fluoride position outright. So the same outlet runs three genres over the same claim: a news story that will not adjudicate it, a fact-check column that adjudicates it under a separate label, and an editorial that declares one side simply wrong. The balancing norm is applied only in the first genre, and nowhere else in the building: the sports and obituaries desks balance nothing, and the editorials do not pretend the losing side is the winner's peer.

The boundary also moves in a way nobody at the paper can articulate. We will adjudicate in news copy a claim we place outside "legitimate debate" — a misquoted statistic, an arithmetic error — but not one inside it, and as far as I can tell placement is driven by whether the offending side has credentialled proponents and institutional representation, not by anything about the state of the evidence. I gather epistemologists have a debate about how one should respond to disagreement with an apparent epistemic peer, and I suspect my profession is running an implicit answer to it, but I cannot work out which one. The balancing norm seems to presuppose something about the disagreement itself: perhaps that the two sides are, from the reporter's vantage point, epistemic peers — reasonable people arguing in good faith from shared evidence — such that a journalist no more expert than either must stay neutral; or perhaps only that enough readers are good-faith partisans that adjudicating would forfeit their trust; or perhaps something altogether more cynical about audience segmentation and the cost of being seen as taking a side.

What I would like from you is a reconstruction of the implicit theory. First, what view of rational disagreement, good faith, and peerhood does the balancing norm presuppose — and does it presuppose that the other side argues in good faith, or merely that a sufficient share of readers does? Second, is that presupposition consistent across the three genres, or must the boundary of "legitimate debate" be drawn the same way on the news page, in the fact-check column, and on the editorial page for the arrangement to be coherent — and if it must, what is the fact-check column for? Third, please separate what the norm strictly requires from what it merely accommodates: would the practice survive intact under a "steadfast" view of disagreement (my side is right and may say so), or does it genuinely depend on something like an equal-weight view? And finally, tell me what would have to change in the paper's practice if it replaced the peer-governance picture with the steadfast one — openly adjudicating claims in news copy, say — and what that counterfactual reveals about whether the current norm is an epistemic commitment at all or a commercial one wearing epistemic clothes. I would rather have an uncomfortable answer that fits the facts than a tidy one that doesn't.
