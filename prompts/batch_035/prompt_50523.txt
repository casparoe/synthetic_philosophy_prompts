For the past two years I have been running a small prediction game with my PhD supervisor, who works on moral epistemology with me and has unusually firm intuitions about moral deference: when it is appropriate to take someone else's word on a moral question, and when there is something defective in doing so. I feed her vignettes (drawn partly from the literature — the novice who reads Peter Singer on the ethics of eating meat and gives it up purely on his authority is the obvious one — and partly invented by me), she rules on them, and I try to predict the ruling in advance. I am winning less often than I would like, and I have started to suspect that she is not running a single principle at all, or that if she is, I have not found it. Before our next session I would like a second opinion, so I am setting out her track record and asking for predictions.

Her rulings so far:

1. A patient with cancer takes her consultant's word, without weighing the evidence herself, on which of two courses of treatment better fits the priorities she has stated to him. Unproblematic, she says.
2. The vegetarian novice — a person who has never thought about the ethics of eating meat reads a Singer-style article and gives up meat purely on the author's authority, unable to reconstruct even the broad shape of the reasoning. Objectionable, she says.
3. A woman in a deteriorating marriage takes her closest friend's word that she must leave, though she cannot articulate why herself. Unproblematic — her judgement, my supervisor says, is clouded by proximity, not absent.
4. A man with no settled views polls a large sample of strangers about capital punishment and adopts whatever the majority says. Objectionable.
5. A young woman raised in a community where every significant decision is referred to the elders does exactly that, including whether accepting a particular university place would be selfish — the elders having known her all her life and having her interests at heart. Objectionable, my supervisor says, and interestingly she does not soften this one.
6. A chemistry doctoral student asks her philosopher friend, over dinner, whether she should decline an invitation to a conference hosted by a repressive government, and simply acts on the answer without further reflection. Unproblematic.

My own candidate explanations are these: she is running the Hills-style view that what deference cannot supply is moral understanding, so deference is fine just when the deferrer already has, or is positioned to acquire, enough understanding in the neighbourhood (call this the understanding view); or she is running the view that the defect is abdication of moral agency — the problem with the novice and the man with the poll is that they have handed over their consciences, whereas taking a loan of judgement on a particular question is fine (the abdication view); or she is running the Hopkins-style pessimist line that it is one thing to defer about what to do and another to defer about what to believe (the practical/doxastic view). There may be a personal-relationship view, on which what matters is whether the testifier knows the deferrer and stands behind the advice, but that looks embarrassed by case 4 versus case 5, and I would like to know whether anything in the record rescues it.

Here are the three vignettes for our next session, and the predictions I need from you:

α. A woman who has never given animal ethics a moment's thought but who is, generally, morally thoughtful, reads the Singer-style article, follows about half of one strand of the argument, and becomes vegetarian — partly on her own half-grasp, partly on the author's word.
β. A man of entirely ordinary moral competence, quite capable of reasoning through his own choices, suffers a crisis of confidence and adopts a standing practice of referring every significant decision to a philosophy professor he trusts, then following the advice. His competence is intact; he has simply stopped exercising it.
γ. A hospital ethics committee of strangers, reasoning carefully and well, delivers a considered verdict about whether continued treatment would benefit the asker's mother. The asker has full relevant understanding of the issues but takes the committee's word rather than the reasons, and acts accordingly.

What will she say about each? I am less interested in the three verdicts themselves than in the principle she is implicitly running, the rival principles that fit her record just as well, and which of the three vignettes does the most work in separating them. If you think her record genuinely underdetermines the answers, say which of the three best reveals that, and design one further vignette — not merely a variation on these — that would cleanly settle which principle she is running. Please write your answer in British English, and feel free to name positions in the moral testimony literature where you think she has simply internalised one of them.
