I've come round to a fairly specific version of the view that the left/right distinction is real: not that there's an essence of leftness, but that political opinion in a population has a dominant single dimension. When you look at how attitudes hang together — redistribution, immigration, punishment, tradition, markets — one axis explains far more of the covariation than any second axis, and that axis is what "left-right" tracks. Part of my evidence is the folk observation that knowing someone's view on, say, abortion predicts their view on trade policy better than chance, and part is stuff like legislative roll-call scaling, where a one-dimensional spatial model fits congressional voting shockingly well — like, eerily well. So my question: what would count against this? I want concrete falsifiers, not vibes. What survey results, what patterns in newly enfranchised or newly polled electorates, what realignments would show that the single dominant dimension is an artifact — of issue bundles fixed by parties, or of the questions pollsters happen to ask, or of Western democracies in a particular era — rather than something out there in the population? Sort the defeaters by how decisive they'd be, say which ones a committed unidimensionalist could absorb with a principled move versus only with a fudge, and be honest with me about whether the view can survive anything — there's an obvious danger that "dominant" gets quietly redefined after every surprise until the claim is definately unfalsifiable, and I want to know if that's already happening. Also tell me what this view predicts that a rival "left/right is just a party label that travels" view doesn't, so I can tell them apart empirically. After the discussion, give me a table with these columns: defeater; what we'd actually observe; how decisive it would be (fatal / serious / merely awkward); and whether a unidimensionalist could absorb it in a principled way or only with a fudge — one row per defeater, ranked from most to least decisive.
