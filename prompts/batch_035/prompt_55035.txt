In 1996 the physicist Alan Sokal published an article in the cultural-studies journal Social Text arguing, among other things, that physical reality is at bottom a social and linguistic construct and that the natural sciences can claim no privileged epistemic standing over the narratives of marginalized communities; three weeks later he revealed the piece as deliberate parody, salted with nonsense, to show the editors could not distinguish scholarship from fluent, ideologically congenial gibberish. Analyze this episode through the lens of Rorty's antirepresentationalism. Rorty denied that truth is correspondence to a mind-independent reality, held that knowledge is not representation but coping with the world, and argued that the authority of science rests on solidarity among inquirers rather than on a rational method unavailable to other vocabularies. Map the episode carefully: by Rorty's lights, is there even something Social Text failed at, given his rejection of the picture of beliefs as answerable to the world? Which features of the affair does his framework illuminate — for instance, how a pragmatist about truth can still fault the editors for violating their own in-group standards of solidarity — and which does it miss or misdescribe, such as the sting of Sokal's charge, or whether deliberate deception is even a coherent failing on a view with no representational target? I am less interested in what Rorty actually said about Sokal than in what his philosophy, taken seriously, commits him to; conclude by saying whether the episode, so analyzed, confirms antirepresentationalism, embarrasses it, or exposes a case it never anticipated.
