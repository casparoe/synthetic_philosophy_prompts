I have spent over a decade in the rationalist blogosphere — first as a lurker, later as a frequent commenter and then a volunteer moderator on a community forum. Our moderation policy contains two rules that have always struck me as individually obviously correct and collectively a little suspicious, and after finally sitting down with Grice's essay "Logic and Conversation" I suspect they make a philosophically loaded pair. I want your help figuring out what our practice presupposes.

Here is the practice, concretely.

Rule A (misleading truths are punishable). We warn and ban users for statements that are literally true but engineered to mislead. This covers cherry-picked statistics; true claims juxtaposed so that readers naturally draw a false conclusion; headlines that are technically accurate but wildly slanted; the phenomenon, familiar to anyone who has read the classic essays in our corner of the internet pointing out that the press almost never states an outright falsehood and instead misleads by careful selection and arrangement of true facts; dogwhistles, whose surface content is innocuous but which carry a coded meaning legible to a subgroup; and the omission trick that Grice himself made famous with his hypothetical reference letter, in which a writer praising a job candidate only for his excellent command of English and his punctual attendance thereby communicates that the candidate is mediocre, without asserting a single false thing. Our community treats all of this as deception. The stock defense — but every individual statement was true! — gets no traction with us.

Rule B (inferences are the reader's responsibility). When a user draws some absurd or offensive conclusion from another user's plainly true and plainly intended statement, we refuse to punish the original speaker. The official line is that a speaker cannot be held accountable for every wild thing somebody might infer from her words; readers own their own reasoning.

Both rules are applied constantly, and both feel right. But notice that they are both about the same boundary: how far a speaker's communicative responsibility extends beyond the literal content of her words. Rule A holds speakers responsible for something beyond what they literally said. Rule B refuses to hold speakers responsible for something beyond what they literally said. Something must be distinguishing the cases, and I cannot tell whether it is a principled distinction or just vibes.

So here is my question: what view of speaker meaning, and of communication-based responsibility, does this pair of moderation rules presuppose? I'd like you to do the following:

1. Reconstruct the implicit theory. Under what account does the practice only make sense? The obvious candidate is Grice's: speakers are responsible for what they intentionally implicate — for what they intend the audience to work out on the assumption that the speaker is being cooperative in the Gricean way. But an audience-centered view — speakers are responsible for what a reasonable or typical audience would in fact take away — would also predict roughly our rules. Lay out the candidates and say which one, if any, our practice actually embodies.

2. Test the edges where the candidates come apart. Consider at least these: the sincere speaker who couldn't reasonably foresee that her audience will reliably draw a damaging implicature; the cynical dogwhistler who engineers an inference while keeping plausible deniability precisely because the inference is driven by the audience's own background assumptions rather than by anything she asserted; and the speaker who exploits a convenient implicature while sincerely hoping most people won't draw it. Show me which rule wins in each case under each theory, and whether that matches what our moderators actually do.

3. Say what the practice strictly presupposes versus what it merely fits well with. Is there some third variable — predictability of the inference, community common ground, whether the speaker had an obligation to check before posting — that could do the real work, leaving the deep theory of meaning underdetermined? Is the pair of rules consistent, or is one of them quietly parasitic on the other?

4. Tell me what would have to change if we swapped in a rival theory. If we adopted a purely intentionalist standard, what dogwhistles and slanted-true-but-misleading posts would we have to let through? If we adopted a purely audience-responsiveness standard, what sincere and careful posters would we start punishing? Would the resulting moderation regime even be workable, or does the equilibrium we have reflect a compromise that no single theory of speaker meaning would license on its own?

Please be rigorous and concrete; use real-seeming examples of the kinds of posts moderators actually see rather than abstract schemas wherever you can, and don't shy away from concluding that our practice is philosophically incoherent if that's where the argument leads. A crisp, essay-style answer with clear sections is much preferred over bullet-point fragments.
