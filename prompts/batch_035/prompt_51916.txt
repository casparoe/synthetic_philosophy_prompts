For a graduate seminar I am teaching on the measurement problem, I am preparing a unit on probability in the Everett interpretation, and I would like a dialogue that gets beneath the usual slogans ("all outcomes happen, so nothing is uncertain" versus "rational agents bet by the Born rule, problem solved"). Please write it for me.

The two characters:

1. **Deirdre**, an Everettian working in the decision-theoretic tradition of Deutsch and Wallace. Her view is that probability in the many-worlds interpretation is not about predicting which outcome occurs, since all occur, but about how much a rational agent should care about each of her future selves. On her picture, the Born weights drop out of structural axioms of rational preference — additivity, continuity, and a dominance principle — together with the requirement that preferences be indifferent to mere re-descriptions of the same branching structure.

2. **Bernard**, a critic. He is not the naive branch-counter of introductory lectures; he concedes that "number of branches" is ill-defined, since how finely the worlds decohere is a matter of degree and of the apparatus's details. His sharpened objection is circularity: the decision-theoretic axioms, and in particular the axiom that forbids caring about branch multiplicity, are not self-evident requirements of rationality but are chosen precisely because they yield the Born weights. The derivation, he claims, is a retrodiction dressed as a derivation.

To force the discussion out of familiar ruts, have them argue over a thought experiment they call the **asymmetric splitter**: a device that can be run in either of two modes on the same input state. In Mode A it produces two outcomes with Born weights of 2/3 and 1/3. In Mode B it produces a thousand outcomes: one of weight 1/2, and 999 decohered, subjectively distinguishable outcomes whose weights sum to 1/2. An agent is offered a payoff scheme attached to the outcomes. Deirdre should use the device to argue that if branch multiplicity had any legitimate role in rational preference, then trivial engineering choices about how coarsely the apparatus decoheres would rationally matter, which is absurd — and that the invariance under such re-descriptions is exactly what her axioms capture, just as a classical agent's preference between lotteries should not depend on whether a coin flip is implemented once or by a thousand-fold redundant circuit. Bernard should reply that the analogy is loaded: in the classical case there is a fact of the matter about what happens, whereas here the "redundancy" is the very thing whose evaluative significance is in dispute, and that Deirdre's appeal to absurdity only works if one has already accepted that amplitude measure, and not multiplicity, is the natural measure of how much a future self "counts."

Requirements for the dialogue:

- Both characters are to be equally competent. Neither wins by luck or by the other's blunder; Bernard should not be permitted to forget his own concession about the ill-definedness of branch count, and Deirdre should not be permitted to simply assert her axioms as obvious.
- The exchange must involve genuine back-and-forth: each character should engage with the other's most recent argument, reformulate it in their own terms, and answer it, rather than delivering set-piece monologues on alternating turns.
- At least one point in the dialogue, each character should make a real concession that the other presses to advantage.
- Let the dialogue end not with a decisive victory but with the two of them correctly locating the single axiom about which their disagreement ultimately turns, and articulating clearly why each finds it reasonable or question-begging.

Please aim for roughly 1,000 to 1,400 words, in a register appropriate to two philosophically trained physicists arguing over coffee between seminar sessions — technically serious, but conversational rather than lecture-like.
