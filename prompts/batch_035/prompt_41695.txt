I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've walked myself into an argument I can neither accept nor refute. My background is in distributed systems, where I spend my days accepting that there is no global "now": every process has its own clock, every snapshot is negotiated, and the ordering of events is whatever some protocol can get the participants to agree on. That is exactly the situation special relativity describes, and somewhere in the analogy an argument for the block universe took shape. It goes like this.

(1) There is no privileged inertial frame: if an event is definite — settled, a fact of the matter — then it is definite full stop, not merely definite-relative-to-some-frame.
(2) For any observer, everything simultaneous with them in their own frame is definite; that is simply what an observer's "now" is.
(3) Definiteness transfers between observers: if an event is definite for O1, and O1 and O2 are colocated (so O1 is unambiguously definite for O2), the event is definite for O2.
(4) By the relativity of simultaneity, for any event e however far in my future, there is an inertial observer passing me right now whose hyperplane of simultaneity contains e; Penrose's two pedestrians whose tiny difference in walking pace gives them wildly different "nows" at Andromeda make this vivid.
(5) So by (3), e is definite for me, now.
(6) e was arbitrary, so every event is definite: the future is as settled as the past, and the block universe follows.

The conclusion strikes me as too strong, for two reasons. First, nothing in the argument touches dynamics: (6) would follow from the geometry of spacetime alone, which seems to settle the question of whether any quantum event anywhere could be genuinely open a priori — no experiment, no physics of chance, just kinematics. Second, when I port the same reasoning back to my own field — treat each replica's local state as "the state," add the premise that any state a replica would have after any message is a state of the system, and transfer between replicas — I conclude that a distributed database already contains all of its future states, which is obviously false, and I can see exactly which premise is doing the damage there. That makes me suspect a premise in (1)–(3) is doing the same damage, but I can't see which. I can also see that the argument is a ratchet: a pedestrian walking the other way has a "now" on which some distant event has not yet occurred, but once a faster-moving observer has made it definite, no one can make it indefinite again. Where does that direction come from, and should its existence make me more suspicious?

Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked innocent to me, and what rejecting it costs. For what it's worth, I would very much like to keep (1): giving up on the equivalence of frames is a price I won't pay, and if the only escape routes require it, I want to know whether there is a principled way out that keeps (1) — or whether I have to accept that "the state of the world right now" was never a frame-invariant notion in the first place, and learn to live with that.
