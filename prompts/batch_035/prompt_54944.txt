I'm a junior in high school and the chair of my school's Student Activities Committee, which decides by vote how to split a fixed discretionary budget among club proposals each semester. Our bylaws require that a proposal pass three criteria — demonstrated need, a workable plan, and broad benefit to the school — and for each proposal we take a yes/no vote on each criterion, followed by a final yes/no vote on whether to fund it. Last semester I noticed something that has bothered me since: one proposal was approved by a majority on every single criterion, yet a majority of the very same members voted against funding it — and when I checked the roll, the majority on each criterion was made up of different members. A teacher I described this to said it resembles what philosophers call the discursive dilemma or the doctrinal paradox, and that it raises a genuine question about whether a committee has a "view" of its own in the way its individual members do.

I would like a decision procedure the committee can actually run at the table during meetings, within roughly ten minutes per proposal. It should specify: the order in which to take and record the votes; how to detect, before the final vote is taken, that we are heading toward an inconsistent result; what to do once we detect it — for instance, whether and how to reopen the criterion votes, whether to require members to state their reasons aloud, or whether to simply accept the conclusion-based vote and drop the pretense that the criteria govern; and at what points the procedure must stop and hand the decision to me as chair, because the question is one of judgment or fairness rather than of aggregation. Please be honest about the places where the procedure cannot settle anything, and where I would in effect be issuing a discretionary ruling dressed up as a rule.

After laying out the procedure, apply it to three cases from this semester's docket. First, a robotics club requesting travel funds for a regional competition, where the members who find the plan workable all doubt the need, and the members who find the need real all doubt the plan. Second, a proposal from a well-liked community-service club for an event that most of us privately support but which fails the broad-benefit criterion in the votes — a case where the procedure may deliver an answer I will not enjoy announcing. Third, a proposal every member individually wants funded, but on which a majority rejects one criterion, so that strict application of our bylaws produces the opposite of what any of us intend.

Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do: whether it can tell us anything about which voting rule is fair rather than merely consistent, and whether it can distinguish a true aggregation problem from a member voting tactically — that is, voting down a criterion she actually believes in, in order to sink a proposal she dislikes on other grounds. If it cannot do either, say so directly rather than papering over it.
