I'm a novelist, and the chapter I'm writing follows a woman through a single ordinary morning: she backs out of her driveway while checking her phone and glances up at the crosswalk half a second late. Whether a child steps into that crosswalk during the half-second is the only difference between two versions of her morning, and the chapter deliberately never says which version is the real one. I'd like a sonnet to close the chapter. The octave should render the morning in which the crosswalk is empty; the volta should be nothing but the luck itself — not a choice and not a discovery, just the coin landing; and the sestet should be the same morning with the child in it, the same actions, the same half-second, so that the poem reads as one life read twice. Let the sestet carry what Bernard Williams called agent-regret — regret that attaches to what one did rather than to what merely happened near one — but keep the words "blame" and "tragedy" out of the poem entirely; the facts should do that work on their own. A reader who knows the moral luck literature should recognize the problem from the form alone, and a reader who doesn't should still find it a good poem about a terrible morning.
