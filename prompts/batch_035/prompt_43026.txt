Our departmental reading group has spent the past two years on the platonism–nominalism dispute in the philosophy of mathematics, and I have come to suspect that we are not so much debating as performing a well-rehearsed stalemate. The moves are all familiar. The nominalist presses the epistemic worry descended from Benacerraf: if abstract objects are causally inert and outside space and time, it becomes obscure how our mathematical beliefs could track them, and the platonist's appeal to a quasi-perceptual or a priori grasp of numbers starts to look like a name for the very thing that needed explaining. The platonist replies with indispensability: mathematics is woven irreducibly into our best scientific theories, and by our own lights we are committed to the entities those theories quantify over. We then watched Hartry Field's Science Without Numbers get taken apart — the nominalization program for Newtonian gravitation, the conservativeness claim, the complaints that it cannot be extended to quantum mechanics or that it quietly keeps abstract machinery in the metalanguage — and the group split exactly as it had before, with each side reading the same text as either a proof of concept or a cautionary tale. Since then: nothing has moved. Not through the Benacerraf dilemma, not through indispensability, not through the various paraphrase strategies, fictionalism, structuralism, or the "easy road" deflationary attempts to keep mathematics true while making its objects harmless. Each of these repositions the combatants, and the combatants oblige by re-forming the same two lines.

Here is my working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and not a conclusion: the apparent dispute about whether numbers exist is no longer really a first-order disagreement about mathematical objects at all, but a meta-ontological one about what the question is asking and what would count as an answer. The platonists in my group seem to be answering a semantic and objectivity question — what makes mathematical statements true, and what preserves the difference between mathematics and a game of make-believe — and they treat nominalist paraphrases as changing the subject. The nominalists seem to be answering an ontological-explanatory question — what does reality have to contain for our total theory of the world to be correct — and they treat the platonist's truth-making talk as inflation without explanatory payoff. If that is right, the fifty-year standoff is not a sign that the question is hard but that there are two questions being asked under one heading, and each side's strongest arguments target the other side's weakest commitment.

Is this diagnosis right, or is it a comforting way of avoiding a verdict? Diagnose the structure of the disagreement using what the two sides actually say: is it verbal, a difference over standards of explanation, a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work, or a genuine substantive dispute that is merely difficult? If it is genuinely substantive, explain where the real clash lies and why the standard arguments have failed to settle it. And whichever diagnosis you give, tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a settled meta-ontology, a change in the question itself — or argue that no progress should be expected, and say why that expectation itself would or would not be a form of conceding the field to one side.
