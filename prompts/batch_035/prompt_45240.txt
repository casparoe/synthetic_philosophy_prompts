I'm putting together a term paper for a political philosophy course on nationalism and cosmopolitanism, and I keep running into a cluster of practices that seem to presuppose a specific theory of what compatriots owe each other — one that nobody ever states outright. Conscription is the clearest case: the state claims the power to compel citizens to kill and to risk death for the defense of compatriots, which is surely the most demanding special obligation anyone recognizes anywhere in moral and legal life. Yet the very same states treat aid to distant strangers as supererogatory — a matter of charity, national generosity, or at most a thin humanitarian floor. A citizen can be drafted to die for strangers-by-proximity in a border town, but no one is ever drafted, at comparable risk and cost, to rescue famine victims abroad. There are also two subsidiary wrinkles. Many countries exact from naturalizing citizens an oath that includes a promise to defend the country militally (typically with a modified oath available for religious pacifists), while the native-born never swear any such oath — so the very same obligation is treated as consent-based when it comes to immigrants and as consent-independent when it comes to the born. And, historically, draft boards have granted conscientious-objector status to universal pacifists, including ones who would accept arduous civilian service, while refusing it to selective objectors — people who fought no objection to war in general but believed one particular war was unjust.

I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory of special obligations that makes this pattern intelligible. Which of the standard candidates does it actually fit: consent, reciprocity or fairness within a cooperative scheme, associative duties of membership, or something about shared institutions that Rawls-style views gesture at when they scope the difference principle to a closed domestic society? Does the pattern presuppose that cosmopolitanism is false, or is it consistent with a cosmopolitanism that grants the state latitude to assign domestic-defense duties to its members while denying it license to ignore the global poor? Do the parts cohere — or does the differential treatment of the naturalized and the native-born, or of pacifists and selective objectors, presuppose commitments the rest of the practice quietly contradicts? Please distinguish what these practices strictly presuppose from what they merely sit comfortably with, and tell me where the pattern is philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories would license it. Finally, say what would actually have to change if the presupposed view were replaced by a straightforward cosmopolitan one — would conscription survive, would the oath have to go, would a cosmopolitan-conscientious-objector exemption become mandatory?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. As you go, say how confident you are in your major claims, and single out the point you are least sure of.
