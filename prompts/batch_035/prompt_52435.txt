I sit on the programming committee of a civic lecture association — a nonprofit that runs a monthly public lecture series in a mid-sized city, funded partly by member dues and partly by two corporate sponsors. Six weeks ago we invited a historian of technology to deliver a lecture on the politics of automation, a topic squarely within the theme of this year's series. Last week, a local journalist surfaced a fifteen-year-old blog in which the invitee, under a pseudonym, wrote a set of satirical posts mocking a religious community, along with a more recent podcast appearance in which he made remarks about blue-collar workers that several members found contemptuous, though others heard them as simply bleak. A petition with 1,900 signatures demands we withdraw the invitation; one of our sponsors has quietly indicated it is "monitoring the situation"; the student group that co-hosts our events is split, with a minority threatening to disaffiliate if the lecture proceeds.

Our bylaws give the committee discretion to rescind invitations but offer no criteria, and precedent cuts both ways: three years ago we stood by a controversial economist; five years ago we quietly cancelled a panel after similar pressure. My fellow committee members have been arguing past each other, and I've come to believe the disagreement is really about what rescinding an invitation is for. Some see it as punishment proportioned to an offense, and debate the offense's severity, the passage of time, and the absence of any apology. Others see it as deterrence or as boundary-setting that defines who our platform is for. Still others insist it is merely expressive — a way of stating what the association stands for without any claim about the invitee's desert — and that on that view the usual worries about proportionality and evidence simply don't apply. A few argue that rescission isn't sanction at all, since no one is entitled to a platform, and that the whole framing of "punishment" smuggles in a right the invitee never had.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before our Thursday meeting. It should recommend one of three courses — proceed as planned, rescind, or proceed in an altered format (moderated panel, response lecture, or the like) — with reasons the other committee members could actually engage with rather than restating the association's values. It should treat the strongest consideration on the losing side honestly, and it should say explicitly what would change the recommendation: which fact about the case, or which argument from the positions I've sketched, would tip the memo the other way. It should also flag where its own reasoning is most likely to fail — for instance, whether the chosen rationale holds up if the petition had 200 signatures instead of 1,900, or if the offensive material had been from last year rather than fifteen years ago. Please note that the memo will be read by the student group, so its tone must be one they can take seriously even if they disagree.
