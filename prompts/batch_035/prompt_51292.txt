I spent the better part of four decades in physical chemistry — reaction kinetics, spectroscopy, a stretch in pharmaceutical analysis — and since retiring I have been making my way, somewhat systematically, into philosophy of language with a small reading group of other retired academics. The topic that has taken hold of me is Putnam's Twin Earth: Earth and Twin Earth molecule-for-molecule identical as regards their inhabitants, the one difference being that the stuff in the rivers is H2O here and XYZ there, so that Oscar and Twin Oscar, in identical psychological states, nonetheless use "water" with different extensions; Putnam's famous summary being that "meanings" just ain't in the head. As a chemist I find the natural-kind cases almost embarrassingly congenial — my entire education was an exercise in what Putnam called the division of linguistic labor, deferring to the community's chemists about what gold and water really are, and I cannot tell an elm from a beech. What unsettles me is a different class of terms: the theoretical terms I actually used at the bench. "Mass" meant one thing in my mechanics courses and something subtly different after relativity; "atom," "gene," "phlogiston," "caloric," and "the ether" each have histories in which the community's best theories came and went while the word stayed put, or died with its theory, or survived a revolution transformed. And here is my half-formed thought, which I would like you to develop rather than dismiss: the Twin Earth test — hold the head fixed, vary the environment, watch the extension move — presupposes that the environmental contribution to reference can be specified independently of what any head contains. For "water" that seems fine; the world decides between H2O and XYZ whether or not anyone knows which is which. But for a term like "mass" or "gene," the reference is supposed to be fixed by a theory, and the theory is in heads. So either externalism is at its strongest for theoretical terms — deference and causal chaining through theory change do all the work, and my unease is misplaced — or it is at its weakest there, and the cases that most impress scientists are precisely the ones where the doctrine can't be stated coherently.

My reading group is split in a way I suspect is diagnostic. One member holds that phlogiston simply never referred, since phlogiston does not exist, full stop. Another maintains that phlogiston referred to something real enough — roughly to combustion processes mischaracterized — and that denying this makes the causal theory of reference unable to explain how any theory-laden science ever learns anything. A third says the disagreement is verbal and that "refers" needs disambiguating before the case can be decided. What I want is to turn my dilemma and their disagreement into a proper research proposal. Here is the situation: a nearby center for philosophy of science offers a one-year part-time visiting position for retired researchers, contingent on a two-page proposal, and I intend to apply. Please draft the proposal. State the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer — something like: under what conditions does a term retain its reference across a scientific revolution, and can those conditions be fixed in advance rather than decided retroactively by whichever verdict about phlogiston one already likes? Explain why the standard externalist positions leave it open, rather than rehearsing them. Set out the method concretely: I would want to fix criteria for continuity of reference first, then test them against worked cases — mass from Newton to Einstein, gene from Mendel to molecular biology, phlogiston and caloric as putative failures of reference — saying what evidence in each case would push which way. Tell me what a genuine result would look like, what would count as the project failing, and give me an honest assessment of whether one year, part-time, by someone who is a philosopher only by recent conversion, is a realistic scope for it — and if not, how to shrink the question so that it is. Do not supply citations or a bibliography; I have my own reading lists and will fill in the references myself.
