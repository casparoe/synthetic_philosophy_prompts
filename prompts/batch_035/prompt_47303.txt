So I've gone down a Thomas Szasz rabbit hole lately (the "myth of mental illness" guy, been at it since his 1960 paper) and I want a parody piece built on his actual views, not a strawman of them. Here's the concept: an intake / informed-consent packet from a psychiatric clinic that has fully converted to Szaszian doctrine, and now every single clause of its paperwork quietly asserts it. Formatted as a bureaucratic terms-of-service style document, numbered clauses, around 700 words, completely deadpan.

The humor has to come from his real positions: that "disease" only literally applies to bodily pathology, so mental illness is a metaphor (and a dangerously reifying one); that what patients actually have are "problems in living"; that psychiatric diagnosis is a moral and social judgment dressed up in medical vocabulary; that the true scandal is coercive psychiatry and the therapeutic state; and that the patient is a responsible, contracting moral agent rather than a sick object to be managed. So for instance the form should flatly refuse to diagnose anyone — instead there's a checklist where you itemize your problems in living — it should treat belief in the chemical-imbalance theory as a private religious conviction the clinic is happy to accommodate, handle medication the way a hardware store handles tools (you may rent them, they solve no disease, return them in the condition you found your brain), and include a clause noting that since no disease is being treated here, malpractice is legally impossible and only breach of contract claims will be entertained. Maybe a grievance procedure addressed "to the state, pending the separation of psychiatry and state." Feel free to give the clinic a self-serious name, like an Institute for the Contractual Resolution of Problems in Living.

One more thing, and it's the important part: while the document stays totally sincere and internally consistent, the satire should quietly land the standard objections to Szasz without ever stating them. Someone who knows this literature should notice, reading between the clauses, exactly where his view strains: what the form does with a client who cannot coherently enter contracts, whether the problems-in-living checklist is really any less arbitrary than the DSM it replaces, whether reclassifying despair as a contract dispute between rational adults is actually more dignifying than calling it an illness. The reader has to feel those pressures through the paperwork's own contortions, not through any winking from you. A specialist should come away thinking the author genuinely understands Szasz — and finds him fair game anyway.
