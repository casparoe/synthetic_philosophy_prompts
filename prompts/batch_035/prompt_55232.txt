I'm a graduate student in a philosophy of religion seminar that has spent the last few weeks on the ontological argument. We covered Anselm and Descartes, then Kant's famous objection that existence is not a real predicate — the one illustrated with his hundred thalers, the point being that an existing coin determines nothing more about it than a merely imagined one does, so positing its existence adds nothing to the concept. The class treated this as the end of the discussion, and I can see how it bites the classical versions: the move from "God exists in the understanding" to "God exists in reality" looks exactly like treating existence as a determining property that could perfect a concept. But last week we turned to Gödel's modal version — the one built on axioms about positive properties, a definition of God as whatever instantiates all positive properties, and a definition of necessary existence which is itself stipulated to be positive — and a classmate closed that discussion with the same slogan: existence is not a predicate, therefore Gödel's proof fails wherever it treats existence as a property. That felt too quick, but I couldn't articulate why, and I suspect the slogan is quietly equivocating between several quite different theses, only some of which Kant held, and perhaps none of which reaches a proof in which the property at issue is necessary existence rather than plain existence, and in which the work is done by quantification over possible worlds rather than by any move from concept to reality.

Please take the role of a logician who is also deeply read in Kant scholarship, and think carefully before answering — perhaps begin with some brainstorming about everything the one-liner has been taken to claim. Then lay the readings out as a menu I can bring back to seminar. At a minimum I'd want you to distinguish, with a verdict for each: that "exists" is not a first-level predicate of individuals at all but a second-level quantifier over them, in the manner usually traced to Frege and Russell; that existence is not a *determining* predicate, one that could enlarge a concept, which is the version Kant seems to have meant and which targets only arguments that try to build existence into a concept; that existence is a property every possible object has, so that the predicate would be trivially satisfied and do no argumentative work; and any other distinct readings you think are in circulation. For each, say whether it is plausible, whether it actually damages Anselm or Descartes, and — the part I care most about — whether it does anything at all to Gödel's argument, given that Gödel never predicates mere existence and instead derives necessary existence from the axioms about positivity. Where the readings fail to touch Gödel, say so plainly rather than stretching them; and if the slogan gestures at some residual objection to Gödel that it cannot quite state — for instance, a worry about the coherence of treating modal status as an ordinary property of a being, or about the axioms doing the theological work under a neutral-sounding name — please make that residual objection explicit and evaluate it on its own terms. Finish by telling me which reading my classmate most likely had in mind, and whether, on that reading, he is entitled to dismiss the proof or merely entitled to complain about its axioms. I want something sharper to bring to class than a slogan traded for a slogan.
