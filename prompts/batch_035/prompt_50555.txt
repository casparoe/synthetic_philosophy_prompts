I am a novelist, and I have been invited to contribute a long essay to an anthology on philosophy and the writing life. I have chosen Nietzsche's eternal recurrence, and I have a draft in note-and-paragraph form — an essay I am calling "The Sentence You Would Write Again." Before I send it to the editors, who are strict about the philosophy holding up, I would like comments from a careful outside reader, someone who knows Nietzsche better than I do and who will also tell me plainly whether the central move is interesting or merely quaint.

The draft as it stands:

First paragraph sets the scene. In the section of The Gay Science titled "The greatest weight," a demon steals into your loneliest loneliness and tells you that this life, exactly as you have lived it and are living it, you will have to live again, innumerable times, with nothing new in it — every pain in the same place, every joy in the same order. The question is whether you would curse the demon or whether you could say you had never heard anything more divine. I note that the thought, as posed there, is a question put to a solitary listener at night; there is no audience to perform for. Zarathustra, by contrast, teaches the recurrence almost as news about the world, and the notebooks from the mid-1880s even attempt something like a derivation from finite arrangements of force and infinite time. Nietzsche himself, in the autobiographical account, says the thought struck him by the lake at Silvaplana in August 1881, a story he clearly curated as part of his own legend. My essay keeps the doctrine and its proofs at arm's length and works with the test.

The second paragraph reports how I first used the test, and why I now find that use weak. The natural application for a writer is to the career: would I consent to write this book again, with the same wasted years, the same compromises with editors, the same reviews? I used to ask myself this annually, in a stocktaking mood, and I came to think it was worthless — a mood dressed up as a criterion.

The third paragraph contains the essay's real claim, and it moves the test from the career to the sentence, and from the past to the moment of revision. Drafting breeds a peculiar resentment: yesterday's pages are fixed, and the present will cannot reach back and change them. Zarathustra, in the chapter on redemption, says something close to this about the will in general — that it is a prisoner of time's "it was," that it cannot will backwards, and that its revenge against what is past is the source of its rage. A writer gnashing at last month's draft is a case study in exactly this. So I propose a revision question different from the perfectionist one. Perfectionism asks: is this sentence the best I can make it? My question — the recurrence question — asks: would I have to revise this very sentence, in this very way, innumerable times, and could I affirm that? The first question is about the sentence's quality; the second, I argue, is about my attachment. It asks whether I can let the sentence become past.

The fourth paragraph is the pivot, and I am not sure it holds. I raise the objection myself: does this not convert Nietzsche's thought into a self-help technique, a rule for better drafting? And is anything converted into an instrument for improving the future not, on Nietzsche's own terms, prudence — the calculating virtue of the herd — so that I have turned a test meant to break calculation into a new calculator? My attempted recovery: the recurrence question does not instruct me how to make the next draft better; it tells me to stop withholding approval from the present one. That is gratitude rather than prudence, and if it changes what I write next, it does so the way an ending changes the sentences that precede it, not the way a rule does.

The final paragraph dismisses, perhaps too quickly, the personal-identity objection: that even if the recurrence were true, the person who returns is a duplicate, not me, so a duplicate's blessedness is not my affirmation and the test loses its bite. I claim this misses the point — the demon addresses me, now, in my loneliness, and what matters is what I can say now; the metaphysics of who returns is beside the question. The essay ends by insisting that whether the thought is true was never the issue; the issue was whether it could be borne.

What I would like from you:

1. Accuracy. Where do I misstate or overstate Nietzsche? In particular, am I entitled to lean so heavily on the test version of the thought while waving off the doctrine, given how much weight he seems to have put on the doctrine himself? And is my use of the chapter on redemption fair, or am I pressing a passage about something else into service?

2. The pivot in the fourth paragraph. Is the reversal-and-recovery a genuine move or a conjuring trick? I have read that paragraph aloud several times and can no longer tell.

3. The dismissal in the final paragraph. Too quick? The identity worry strikes some readers as decisive, and I give it three sentences.

4. Whether the piece is interesting — whether the sentence-level application is a real contribution to how one might live with this thought, or a pretty conceit.

In your comments, please tell me how confident you are in each verdict rather than delivering flat judgments, and close by naming the single point on which you feel least sure. I would especially like you to flag any criticism of mine that rests on a contested reading of Nietzsche rather than on something in my text, since the anthology's readers will include philosophers who know the interpretive terrain.
