I teach a seminar on the ethics of technology, and this week I had the students read a popular book chapter on algorithmic fairness that I'm now half regretting assigning. The chapter summarizes the impossibility results like this: 'Around 2016, computer scientists proved a theorem showing that any risk assessment algorithm must be biased. If a court uses an algorithm to predict who will reoffend, it cannot treat different demographic groups equally: either the algorithm will wrongly label members of one group as high-risk more often than the other, or its predictions will be systematically miscalibrated and simply wrong about who reoffends. The upshot is that fairness in machine learning is mathematically impossible, so the real question isn't whether we can build fair algorithms -- it's which group gets to impose its preferred tradeoff on everyone else.'

My students took this at face value and came away thinking the whole fairness literature had been debunked by a theorem, which set off my BS detector but also made me realize I couldn't articulate exactly where the chapter overreaches. My sense is the real result is about a conflict between two specific conditions -- something like calibration within each group versus equal false positive and false negative rates -- and that the incompatibility only bites under certain conditions, rather than being a universal result about "bias" as such. But I'm a political philosopher by training, not an ML person, so I'd rather not correct them with hand-waving.

Can you audit this summary for me? Go sentence by sentence: what would a fairness researcher accept as roughly right, what would they wince at, and what is just wrong? In particular I want to know (a) whether the dichotomy the chapter draws -- mislabeled groups versus miscalibrated predictions -- tracks the actual incompatibility, (b) whether "any algorithm must be biased" is a fair gloss given whatever side conditions the theorem has, and (c) whether the closing claim, that the debate reduces to interest groups imposing tradeoffs, follows from anything true in the summary or is just the author's politics riding on the math. I'd like to walk into Thursday's class with a list of specific corrections rather than a vague sense that the chapter is glib.
