I want to practice defending the closure of knowledge under known entailment against a well-prepared opponent. You will play a committed Nozickian tracking theorist. Stay in character until I say "stop"; keep each reply to roughly a paragraph; respond to my actual arguments rather than lecture past them.

Your character's commitments: to know that p, one's belief that p must track the truth — in the nearest worlds where p is false, one does not believe p, and in the nearest worlds where p is true, one does. He accepts that knowledge is not in general closed under known entailment: one can know p, know that p entails r, and still fail to know r, when the nearest worlds in which r is false are worlds in which one would go on believing r anyway (his paradigm: ordinary propositions are known, radical-skeptical hypotheses are not, and the theory is engineered to deliver exactly that). He may observe that closure does survive when the conclusion would be believed via the same method that tracks the premises, and he is free to distinguish knowledge from assertibility, from reasons for action, and from what one may responsibly rely on. He may concede small points, but he must never grant that closure holds as a general principle or that tracking is the wrong account of knowledge. He is sharp and principled, not a caricature.

After each of your replies, append a brief bracketed stage direction, set off from the dialogue: (a) a confidence percentage the character would attach to the central claim of that reply, and (b) the one point in the reply he is least sure of. Treat the bracket as an aside to me; the character never notices it.

Here is my opening move. A quality inspector at a factory inspects widget #7 this morning and sees that it passes the visual check; call that proposition p. She knows p by looking, and her belief tracks: had widget #7 failed, she would have seen the flaw and not believed p. Separately, the plant's engineering manual — which she has every reason to trust — states that if any widget in this batch passes its visual check, the batch is stamped and shipped the same day. She believes this conditional and knows it, by testimony. Call the consequent r: the batch ships today. On your theory she does not know r, since the nearest worlds where r is false — a crane fails, a driver falls ill, customs is slow — are worlds in which she would still believe r. So she knows p, she knows that p entails r, she performs the inference, and the conclusion is not knowledge. Yet nothing about her epistemic situation changes at the moment of inference: she gains no counterevidence, forms no belief by any new or flaky method, and is not careless. Just before signing the shipping order she tells her supervisor, with full justification, that she knows the batch goes out today — and given the reasoning behind it, that claim seems plainly true. So I put it to you: on your view, knowledge can fail to transmit across a step of reasoning that is valid, proceeds from knowledge, and is carried out without fault by your own lights at every stage. Where did the knowledge go, and why isn't "knowledge lost in a flawless inference from knowledge by knowledge" a reductio of tracking rather than a cost of it?
