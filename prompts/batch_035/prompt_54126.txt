I'm on the monuments review commission in my mid-sized city, and we've got a genuinely nasty case coming up for a vote in two weeks, so I need a written opinion to circulate to the other commissioners before then.

The centerpiece of our oldest park is a 1931 bronze statue of Dr. Warren Hollis, the city's official "father of public health." He more or less built our water system and stopped a typhoid epidemic in the 1890s that would have killed hundreds. The problem: a local historian recently digitized the old health board minutes, and it turns out that during a 1908 smallpox outbreak Hollis ran the quarantine of the immigrant riverfront district as an armed confinement, and at least eleven people died of the disease inside it after he denied them transfer to the pest house that was open to patients from the wealthier neighborhoods. The minutes are pretty blunt about the reasoning, too.

The board is split three ways. One faction wants the statue gone — Hollis Park is the neighborhood's only real greenspace and a lot of descendants of those riverfront families live right there. Another wants it left completely alone: it's been there ninety years, it's by a sculptor who taught half the art faculty at the state university, and "we don't judge the past by today's standards." A third group wants some middle path but nobody can agree what it is, and our enabling ordinance is vague enough that "retain, relocate, or reinterpret with new signage" are all technically on the table.

One of my fellow commissioners, a philosophy PhD, keeps pushing this Nietzsche framework on us — the essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, with its three modes of relating to the past: monumental, antiquarian, and critical history. I was skeptical but it's actually been the most productive lens in our discussions so far.

So: draft the written opinion I'll circulate before the vote. It should recommend ONE concrete course of action (full removal, relocation to the county museum with interpretation, keep in place with a substantial counter-plaque and redirected naming, whatever you genuinely think is best), with a rough timeline and any conditions attached. Use the Nietzsche distinction to actually structure the reasoning, not just name-drop it — what work does the statue do for the city as monumental history, what exactly would pure antiquarian preservation be serving in this case, and when does critical history become the honest stance versus an excuse to feel superior to dead people. And it should give a fair shake to the strongest argument on whichever side loses the vote, since I still need to work with these people after Tuesday.
