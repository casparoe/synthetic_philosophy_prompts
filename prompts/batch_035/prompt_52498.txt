I co-organise a small online seminar on the cognitive science of religion, and after eighteen months of sessions, two of our most steadfast regulars — one a committed by-product theorist of the standard cognitive mould, who holds that religion is a spandrel of mental machinery evolved for other purposes (agency detection, teleological reasoning, minimally counterintuitive concepts being memorable for reasons that have nothing to do with gods), and one an adaptationist of the cultural-evolution school, who holds that religions are group-level cultural adaptations sustained by costly displays that solve the problem of getting selfish individuals to cooperate — have finally agreed to attempt an adversarial collaboration instead of another evening of arguing past each other. I have been given the job of drafting the joint statement they will both be asked to sign, and I want it to be genuinely signable: not anodyne mush that says nothing, and not wording that quietly smuggles in one side's conclusions while the other politely looks away.

The document should open with a numbered section of claims both could accept — candidates include: religious concepts recur across cultures in restricted, transmissible shapes; the underlying cognitive biases are real, pre-date religion, and are found in children; ritual participation is costly and correlates with commitment to the group; populations can inherit religious practices without any of it being genetically encoded. Then a table of the exact residual disagreements, each phrased so that the signatory on that side would accept the phrasing of her own view — for instance on whether costly ritual is an adaptation for cooperation or a by-product later amplified by cultural selection, and on whether either of them should even concede that the word religion names a natural kind rather than a grab bag. Then the cruxes. I suspect one is a timing question: whether belief in punishing, morally concerned gods appears before or after the rise of large, complex societies, and for that one I want each side's prediction about what careful comparative historical work would show, recorded in advance so that neither can reinterpret the result later. Finally, somewhere in the document, they should construct a shared taxonomy that neither currently uses consistently — by-product, cultural adaptation, genetic adaptation — and classify the main candidate traits under it (agency detection, costly ritual, moralizing high gods, ecstatic experience), flagging any trait whose classification they already agree on, since those agreements are easy to miss when everyone is fighting.

Please write it in British English, and keep it entirely self-contained: no citations, bibliography, or references to particular books, since the readers will be seminar members without library access, and every claim should be comprehensible to a bright graduate student who has read none of the primary literature. If you conclude that part of their dispute is verbal — say, about which of the three categories the word adaptation is doing duty for in each camp's mouth — say so plainly in a closing section, because that is precisely the question the rest of the seminar keeps tripping over.
