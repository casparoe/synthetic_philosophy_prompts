I'm a longtime reader of the rationalist blogosphere — LessWrong, Slate Star Codex and its successors, the adjacent effective altruist forums — so the vocabulary I actually reason in is Bayesian. Philosophy of science I know at second hand at best. I gather there is a literature on inference to the best explanation, starting with a paper by Gilbert Harman, and that Peter Lipton and Bas van Fraassen are central figures. What I want is a translation of that literature into my vocabulary, as faithful as you can make it, followed by a residue report.

Concretely: restate, in my terms, (1) Harman's founding thought that our evidence for unobservable entities is just that they would explain what we observe; (2) Lipton's distinction between the loveliness of an explanation and its likeliness; (3) van Fraassen's complaint that being the best explanation is no guarantee of being the most probable hypothesis; and (4) the standing worry that inference to the best explanation licenses ad hoc multiplication of hypotheses, each tailored to fit the data. For each, tell me whether it comes out as something I already believe under a different name, whether it translates only with distortion, or whether it has no expression in my vocabulary at all. Keep the translation honest: if you find yourself sneaking in philosophical commitments and calling them translation, say so.

Then take a definite position and defend it, rather than laying out the options for me to pick: is the residue a defect in my vocabulary — something the Bayesian idiom cannot say but should be able to — or a sign that the philosophers were tracking something Bayes already covers and merely lacked the machinery to state cleanly? My own working hypothesis is that inference to the best explanation is just Bayesian updating with the arithmetic rounded off, with loveliness functioning as a cheap heuristic for assigning priors. Tell me whether that hypothesis survives your translation, and if not, exactly where it breaks.
