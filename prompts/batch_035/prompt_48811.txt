I'm presenting at an interdisciplinary workshop on machine learning governance, defending a deliberately narrow thesis: that what the AI safety community calls "alignment" should be defined as intent alignment — building systems that competently pursue the goals their operators actually have — and that the broader aspiration of aligning AI with the values of humanity as a whole should be explicitly handed back to politics, law, and ordinary moral deliberation rather than being folded into a technical research program. My argument sketch: (a) the broad problem, value alignment with everyone, has no agreed success condition and cannot acquire one without first solving problems of legitimacy and preference aggregation that are political, not engineering; (b) the narrow problem is well-posed, tractable, and its solution is a near-prerequisite for any human social process to handle the broader questions at all; (c) conflating the two lets people gesture at the moral urgency of the broad problem while proposing only narrow solutions, or dismiss narrow solutions for failing at a job they never claimed.

The audience is about forty people: a cluster of political theorists and ethicists who think of legitimacy as the central category and will hear "operators' intents" as "the preferences of whoever happens to hold power"; a smaller group of long-time safety researchers who think the narrowing is a strategic and substantive mistake — that it licenses race dynamics, ignores the harms intent-aligned systems impose on third parties, and treats a deeply corruptible principal as if their preferences were a fixed target; and a few STS-flavored scholars who will treat the definitional move itself as a claim about who gets to set the research agenda. Please draft my Q&A prep sheet: roughly eight objections this specific audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do, each phrased in the vocabulary of the people most likely to raise it — the legitimacy theorist's version of the "who is the principal?" objection will differ from the safety researcher's version of the same worry, and I want to see both. For each, give a two-minute reply, a candid note on where the reply is soft or would collapse under a determined follow-up, and what I should concede up front rather than fight. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks. I'm particularly unsure how to handle the objection that operator preferences are endogenous — shaped in advance and in real time by the very systems meant to serve them — so treat that as likely to be asked and hard, and tell me whether my argument sketch has a real answer or only a deflection.
