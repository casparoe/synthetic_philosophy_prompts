I'm a PhD student in machine learning, working on interpretability; a lot of my day job is variance decomposition of model behavior — asking how much of the variance in outputs is attributable to the training data, how much to architecture, how much to random seed, how much to interaction between them. Recently a friend of mine in a behavior genetics program told me that height is around 80% heritable and IQ somewhere between 50 and 80%, depending on the study, and we got into an argument that I now realize I can't properly have, because I only half understand what the number means. My instinct was to read "80% heritable" as "80% caused by genes," which she says is exactly the misreading her field complains about, and I believe her, but her corrections came in vocabulary I don't fully command: additive genetic variance, shared versus non-shared environment, Falconer's formula, MZ versus DZ twin assumptions, gene-environment interaction and gene-environment correlation.

So here is my request. Restate what heritability is, and the main things it is not, in the vocabulary I actually use — variance components, random effects, interaction terms, identifiability, training versus test distribution, out-of-distribution generalization — as faithfully as you can. Specifically, translate: (1) the claim that heritability is defined relative to a population and an environmental distribution, which to me sounds like saying a variance decomposition is only defined relative to a training distribution and means nothing OOD; (2) the classic point that high within-group heritability says nothing about between-group differences — I gather there's an argument about seeds growing differently in rich versus poor soil that I'd love to see rendered as something about why in-distribution variance explained doesn't transfer across environments; (3) gene-environment interaction, which sounds like exactly the interaction term whose identifiability collapses when your genotype-by-environment grid has support in only a few cells; and (4) the twin-study design itself, which to my eye looks like a set of identifying assumptions pretending to be a measurement. Then give me the residue report: what translated cleanly, what you had to distort to fit, and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all — gene-environment correlation in particular, where organisms select or evoke their environments, seems to have no analog in anything I do, and I can't tell whether that's a gap in my vocabulary or a place where the biological notion was less well-defined than it looked. I want to come back to my friend with an opinion about whether the recurring public confusion over heritability is a confusion she should expect from anyone trained like me, or one that my training should have made impossible.
