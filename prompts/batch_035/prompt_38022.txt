I teach an upper-level epistemology seminar called "Knowledge, Closure, and Its Discontents," and I'm writing the final. I want one essay question, answerable in ninety minutes, on the dogmatism paradox, but the difficulty is that my students have memorized the standard positions and could write a decent-looking answer on autopilot. The question therefore has to hang on a case we never discussed in seminar, so that they have to apply the machinery rather than recite it. For the setup you can assume they know: the closure principle for knowledge, the reasoning that generates the paradox (a subject who knows that p can know that any argument concluding not-p contains a flaw somewhere, so she can apparently know in advance that engaging with such arguments will not change her mind — from which it seems to follow that the zealot who refuses to look is doing nothing epistemically wrong), and the familiar escape routes discussed in the literature: denying closure, holding that knowledge of p is lost or suspended once counterarguments are entertained, the "there is a flaw but I can't yet say where" phenomenon of unassessed evidence, and approaches that route the problem through higher-order evidence.

The new case, which the question should present in full: Professor Larsen, a historian of early modern Europe, knows that the Peace of Westphalia was concluded in 1648. A monograph arrives for her to review, arguing at length for 1649. She knows that if the monograph is sound, she did not know the 1648 date; so, by the reasoning above, she knows the monograph contains an error somewhere — though, until she reads it, she cannot say where. But Larsen has also read the empirical literature on motivated reasoning and expert overconfidence, and she takes it to show that experts who dismiss contrary arguments sight-unseen are frequently miscalibrated, including when they are right. So she possesses both the dogmatist's apparent license (she knows the monograph errs) and standing higher-order evidence that people reasoning as she is about to reason have often done so badly. The question should ask: (a) whether each main escape route from the paradox can say something principled about Larsen's situation, in particular about whether she may assign the monograph a low prior credence and whether that licenses skipping the work of locating the error rather than merely knowing one exists; (b) whether the options available to Larsen's theorist map onto the options in the epistemic akrasia debate, and whether that mapping shows the two debates are one or merely resemble each other; and (c) whether there is an account on which Larsen both knows the date and owes the monograph a genuine reading, or whether that combination is exactly what the paradox rules out.

Please deliver the question itself, a model answer of roughly 600–700 words, and a marking scheme worth 20 points built around five must-hit points, with each point's mark value stated and a one-line note on how candidates typically botch it. The marking scheme should explicitly separate an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves an A answer contains — for instance, I would expect an A answer to distinguish knowing-that-an-error-exists from being able to identify it, and to say something about whether the higher-order evidence changes what Larsen is permitted to do with her first-order knowledge rather than merely describing both facts side by side, whereas a B answer restates the case and gestures at closure. Two further constraints. First, this literature is genuinely divided — whether closure should be abandoned, whether the "flaw must exist" inference is even valid, and whether higher-order evidence defeats or merely complicates first-order knowledge are all contested — so the model answer must not present one escape route as the received view; wherever it takes a stand, it should say that it is taking one and that informed people disagree. Second, build this requirement into the marking scheme: award marks for recognizing which parts of the dialectic are settled (for example, that the paradox arises even for subjects with true and well-founded beliefs) versus which are open, and deduct marks for presenting a contested resolution as consensus.
