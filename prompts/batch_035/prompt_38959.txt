A podcast I listened to recently covered Douglas Hofstadter's "superrationality" experiments from his Scientific American columns in the early 1980s, and summarized them roughly as follows: In the Platonia dilemma, an eccentric trillionaire tells twenty people that if exactly one of them sends a telegram by noon the next day, that person receives a billion dollars; if more than one telegram or none arrives, nobody gets anything. Hofstadter's notion of superrationality says that since perfectly rational thinkers facing the same problem will all arrive at the same strategy, each should choose the strategy that is best on the assumption that everyone chooses it — in this case, sending a telegram with probability 1/20. The podcaster described this as basically the categorical imperative dressed up as probability theory, and said that superrationality amounts to doing what is best for the group. In the follow-up contest, the Luring Lottery, a million-dollar prize was to be divided by the total number of entries received, with each reader permitted to submit as many entries as they liked on a single postcard; the game-theoretically rational move was to submit an enormous number of entries, while the superrational move was supposedly for everyone to submit exactly one. According to the podcast, the results vindicated Hofstadter: nearly everyone submitted a single entry, a winner was drawn, and the magazine paid out. The podcaster concluded that the experiments prove cooperation is the genuinely rational strategy and that ordinary people are natural superrational thinkers. I found the story appealing, but it seems a little too tidy. How accurate is this summary? Please go through it and tell me which parts Hofstadter would endorse, which are conflations or overstatements, and whether the final conclusion survives the corrections.
