I've been an avid reader of the rationalist blogosphere and its adjacent forums for something like fifteen years now, and I've noticed that my corner of the internet is very good at applying decision theory and game theory to politics while being oddly silent about a prior question: before we ask whether a decision procedure is fair or efficient or incentive-compatible, who exactly is entitled to feed inputs into it at all? I'd like to run a guest post on this -- the so-called boundary problem in democratic theory -- but not as a survey of what famous people said. The thing my readers enjoy is being sorted by concrete cases rather than by labels, the way the classic "which theory of personal identity do you actually hold?" quizzes work. So what I want from you is a diagnostic quiz titled something like "which demos do you actually believe in?"

Take on the persona of a political philosopher who has taught democratic theory for years and thinks the boundary problem is the most underrated issue in the field. Think through the problem carefully before writing anything: the core difficulty, as I understand it, is that the natural-sounding principle that decisions should be made by those they concern either turns out to be circular (if only the demos can decide who the demos is, we've presupposed what we were trying to ground) or turns out to be wildly inflationary (a national central bank's rate decision affects commodity prices on the other side of the planet, so does everyone with a mortgage in São Paulo get a vote on the Fed?). Build the quiz from the logical space first: ten to twelve questions, each a concrete scenario with two or three answer options, phrased so a reader can answer from their gut without ever seeing the name of the position their answer supports. The scenarios should span the load-bearing cases: cross-border pollution from a town's factory, a fifteen-year-old bound by school and curfew policy, a permanent resident of three decades who never naturalized, tourists present during an election, future generations and long-lived infrastructure or climate decisions, colonial subjects governed by a parliament they never elected, and at least one question probing whether anything like animals or ecosystems could ever count. Then produce a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions in the logical space -- the everything-affected view, the everything-coerced-or-ruled view, views that tie membership to having a stake or to capacity for reciprocal participation, and the self-constitution view on which the people simply make themselves up and no external principle applies. Where standard names exist, give them; where a combination of answers is occupied by nobody, say so and speculate briefly on whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; and flag explicitly any pair of positions your questions cannot pull apart no matter how they're answered.

A few mechanical requirements. This will be pasted into a comment thread that strips all formatting, so write everything as continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables -- phrase each question as one flowing sentence inside the paragraph, as if you were explaining the whole quiz to me in a long letter, and describe the scoring key the same way, in connected sentences that a reader can follow without a grid. Don't cite sources or attach attributions to specific authors; I'd rather have the positions described plainly than have you guess at who said what. And please don't hedge your way into an "it depends" recommendation at the end -- after the scoring key, tell me in two or three sentences which position you think actually survives the scenarios best, and why, since my readers will smell cowardice otherwise.
