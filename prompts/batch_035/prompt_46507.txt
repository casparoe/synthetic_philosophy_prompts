Please argue against lexical threshold negative utilitarianism on the grounds that the threshold itself is unstable, not just where its critics usually say it is. The view says mild pains trade off against pleasure, but pains past some intensity can't be outweighed by any amount of pleasure, however large. Two things seem true at once: a pain just barely above the threshold and one just barely below are almost identical in badness, yet the view says an infinite quantity of pleasure compensates for one but not the other. And the view has to pick a stance on aggregation without either stance being livable: either many sub-threshold pains summed together can push you past the threshold (in which case the "lexical" barrier is really just about totals, and the whole separate-category story falls apart), or they never can, so a billion people in constant sub-threshold agony counts for less than a single person slightly above the line, which no one will swallow. Make the argument that any way of drawing the line either quietly collapses the view back into ordinary negative utilitarianism or commits it to discontinuities in value that are worse than the problem the threshold was invented to solve -- and please actually pick the strongest version of the argument, not a survey of objections.
