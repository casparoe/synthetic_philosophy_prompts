After some decades in geophysics, retired these three years, I have taken up moral philosophy in earnest, and my reading group has lately finished Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments (first published 1759; we read the sixth edition of 1790). Old habits persist: in my working life we spoke of fitting models to observations and of the rival models that survive a data set, and I confess I have treated Smith in the same spirit. I have kept a table of his rulings on the cases he discusses, in the hope of extracting the law he is implicitly running, and I now find that the table is consistent with more than one such law. I should like your help before our meeting on Thursday. Please write your answer in British English.

First, the table — Smith's rulings, as I have recorded them:

1. A person facing imminent death — the soldier on the eve of an engagement — who preserves perfect tranquillity: Smith's approbation is of the most extreme kind, and he remarks that it seems to exceed anything the usefulness of such composure could account for; it is directed at the person, not at the benefit.
2. The resentment of an injured party: approved precisely when it answers the injury in degree and in object, disapproved the moment it exceeds it; and punishment, on his account, is justified as no more than what the injured party could properly demand.
3. The same beneficent act performed from lively fellow-feeling he finds amiable; performed from a cold sense of what is proper, he allows it a certain correctness, but it engages his affection less.
4. The character that is merely proper throughout — that neither offends nor excels, and whose passions are always exactly what a spectator can go along with — he approves, but coolly; his admiration he reserves for cases in which there was the most to subdue.

So far as I can tell, all of my rulings are consistent with several distinct laws, which differ over whose sympathy fixes the verdict: the sympathy of the spectators actually present; the sympathy of an ideal, well-informed spectator — the man within the breast — reading the agent's true motive; or the sentence the agent passes on himself when he examines his own conduct. There may be further candidates I have not seen, and I should like you to reconstruct the field yourself rather than take my three on trust. My difficulty is that in every case I have recorded, all of these spectators happen to agree.

Now, two cases to be put before the group on Thursday, neither of them, so far as I know, discussed by Smith himself:

A. A widow of considerable fortune, living in a small town, has for fifteen years paid, quietly and without acknowledgement, the school fees of whatever children of the town needed them. She has bound the parents to silence, the schools have never learned the source, and she will carry the arrangement to her grave. Her motive, so far as anyone can tell — and her closest friends can tell a good deal — is plain fellow-feeling; there is no vanity in it and no hope of gratitude, since none will ever know.

B. A gifted administrator is appointed to reform a county's poor relief. His scheme is drawn up, imposed, and, as it happens, works: relief improves, no one is ruined, the paupers are better fed. Yet an intimate friend can see, though the administrator cannot, that the moving spring is not benevolence; it is the pleasure of arranging men as a hand arranges pieces on a board. He believes himself, quite sincerely, to be the public benefactor he appears.

I should like the following from you:

1. A reconstruction of the candidate laws consistent with my table, together with a statement of whether any of my four rulings already excludes one of them.
2. A prediction of Smith's verdict on the widow and on the administrator — not merely approve or disapprove, but the register: the cool esteem owed the merely proper, the affection owed the amiable, or the admiration owed the self-commanded. For the widow, please address a further question: does the total absence of spectators, actual and prospective, affect the degree? Is her act, in Smith's terms, praise-worthy though it can never be praised?
3. A candid statement of where my table underdetermines the answer, and which of the two verdicts you would in consequence be guessing at.
4. A further case, in Smith's own manner, that would best separate whatever pair of laws the two verdicts leave standing together, together with an account of what it would show.

I should be grateful if you did not open with a general survey of Smith's system; I have read the book, and it is the predictions I want.
