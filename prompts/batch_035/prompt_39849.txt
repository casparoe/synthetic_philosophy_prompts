I sit on the clinical ethics committee at a mid-sized NHS trust, and we keep getting stuck on the same kind of case: a patient with fairly advanced dementia who is, honestly, quite content — humming along to the radio, happy to see visitors, no distress — but whose past self left an advance directive refusing antibiotics (and in one case, even the flu jab) because they'd written something like "I don't want to be kept alive in that state." A colleague keeps invoking Dworkin's old argument in Life's Dominion, that the directive's authority comes from the person's critical interests — the stakes they have in the shape of their life as a whole, not just how each day experientially feels — so the earlier self should govern when the two selves disagree. Please explain the most important implications of this view for how our committee should actually handle these cases. I'm especially unsure about (a) whether the current patient's contentment can ever count against the directive, (b) what it says to relatives who insist "the person who wrote that directive isn't the one living this life now," and (c) whether it matters that one of our patients seems to have changed her values (a religious deconversion) between writting the directive and losing capacity. I'll admit the view appeals to me more than it probably should, so tell me where its limits are too. Please answer in British English.
