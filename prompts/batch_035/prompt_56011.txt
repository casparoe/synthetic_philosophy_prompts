I edit a small bioethics newsletter, and for our next issue on consent I want to run a "what theory of valid consent do you actually hold?" quiz. Please write it for me, with the answer in American English.

The quiz should have nine questions, each presenting one concrete case with two or three answer options. The cases must be ones on which the real theoretical positions genuinely divide — not questionnaires where the options are just dressed-up theory labels. Please make sure the following six cases appear, in whatever form you judge best:

1. A patient gives informed consent to surgery after being accurately told everything about the procedure itself, but is deceived about which surgeon will perform it (a resident will, not the famous attending she asked for). Does the consent to the surgery stand?
2. A landlord offers to keep a tenant's rent flat, in a tight housing market, in exchange for a "personal relationship." The tenant, weighing her alternatives, agrees. Valid consent?
3. Someone is intoxicated but coherent, initiates a sexual encounter, and expresses willingness throughout the next morning, while sober, without regret. Was last night's consent valid?
4. A boxer consents to being hit within the rules. Between rounds, his opponent secretly loads his glove with a hard object and injures him. Did the boxer consent to what actually happened, or does that question miss the point?
5. An unconscious patient is brought to the emergency room with no surrogate available, and the obviously beneficial procedure is one any reasonable person would want. Is proceeding permissible because of hypothetical consent, because of something else (say, necessity or presumed consent), or not at all?
6. A fully informed, uncoerced adult consents to being maimed for another person's gratification — the act goes exactly as agreed. Is the consent invalid because the act is impermissible, valid but not sufficient to make the act permissible, or valid and sufficient?

For the remaining three questions, choose your own cases; deception arising from the patient's own negligent misunderstanding, retrospective endorsement of an act initially performed without valid consent, and consent purchased under severe background inequality are all natural candidates, but you have latitude.

Then provide a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto the following positions, which must each appear as a distinct outcome: attitudinal theories of consent (consent as a mental state of acceptance), performative or speech-act theories (consent as a communicative act), normative-power theories (consent as the exercise of a Hohfeldian power), moralized theories (whether consent is valid depends on the moral character of what is consented to), hypothetical-consent theories as a distinct leaf, and a deflationary option on which consent talk does less moral work than it seems to. Since a single respondent's nine answers will rarely match one position cleanly, the key should say which combinations of answers pull toward which position and how to handle mixed profiles — perhaps by identifying which one or two questions are load-bearing for each theory.

Two further requirements. First, since some positions in the logical space may go unoccupied — for example, consistently answering that consent is a speech act while also holding that secret, unexpressed acceptance transforms obligations — note any such patterns the scoring key can produce, and say briefly whether nobody holds them because the combination is incoherent or merely unexplored. Second, flag any pair of positions your quiz genuinely cannot separate, rather than pretending a clean sort: for instance, if several theories of what consent *is* can agree on all nine answers while differing only about what consent *does*, say so and identify where a tenth diagnostic question would have to come from.

Please do not include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; the quiz and key should be self-contained. Write everything in American English.
