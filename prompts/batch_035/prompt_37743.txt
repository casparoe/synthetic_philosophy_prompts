so I'm working on a novel where my main character has to choose between staying to care for her dying mother and crossing the border to help her sister, whos in actual danger. I keep going back and forth on whether to write it as what philosophers would call a genuine moral dilemma — a case where she does wrong no matter which she picks — or just as a hard choice where one option is actually right and she simply can't see it, or can't bring herself to do it.

as far as I can tell the standard move against genuine dilemmas is something like: if she truly ought to do both things and she can't do both, then since "ought" implies "can", one of the two oughts must be a kind of illusion. but I've also read that philosophers point to something called moral residue — the lingering guilt or regret that still seems fitting even after you've made the right call — as evidence that the conflict was real all along.

can you walk me through how these two camps actually fight it out? specifically:

1. does the residue crowd have any decent answer to the ought-implies-can argument, or do they just bite the bullet and say that "ought implies can" only holds when there's no conflict?
2. or alternatively, is there a version where the residue gets explained away — like, the agent is just (maybe irrationally) still caring about the option she didn't take, and that doesn't show anything about whether both options were really wrong-making?

I ask because it matters a lot for the book. if the residue is just her being irrational, I should write the guilt as something she needs to get over. if the residue tracks a real moral loss, then the ending where she never fully forgives herself is the honest one. wichever way the philosophy actually goes, I'd like to know, so lay it out for me. also feel free to tell me if I've mangled any of the terminology, I picked all this up secondhand from essays about writing, not from philosophy proper.
