For the past two years I've been part of a reading group that has worked through the panpsychism debate, and I've become convinced that the discussion around the combination problem is stalled in a very specific way, and I'd like a diagnosis of why. Let me describe the pattern as concretely as I can, because I don't want a survey of panpsychism; I want to know what kind of disagreement this actually is.

The recurring cycle goes like this. A critic raises the combination problem: if fundamental physical entities have experience, how do the experiences of micro-level subjects compose into the unified consciousness of something like a human being? The panpsychists in the group respond by decomposing the problem, roughly along the lines David Chalmers does, into distinguishable sub-problems — how micro-subjects compose a macro-subject, how micro-qualities compose the qualities of a unified experience, and how the structural features of physical combinations relate to the structure of experience. They then say the subject-composition sub-problem is the only genuinely hard one, the so-called subject-summing worry, and offer something like a phenomenal bonding relation among micro-subjects as a possible answer. The critics in the group find this empty: a bonding relation is just the combination problem restated with a name, and no analogy from the composition of objects helps, because composing subjects seems categorically unlike composing tables out of atoms. Someone usually brings up William James's old point that a hundred of us in a room do not compose a group-mind, so why would hundreds of micro-experiences compose one? Then a cosmopsychist at the table proposes flipping the direction: experience is fundamental at the cosmic level and human consciousness is a decomposition of it, not a combination — at which point everyone agrees this just swaps the combination problem for a decombination problem, and the discussion returns to where it started. Two years, no one has changed their position.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the standoff is not really about whether combination happens but about the status of the question itself. The panpsychists treat the combination problem as an ordinary unsolved research problem — the kind every theory has, to be chipped away at by decomposition and partial models. The critics treat it as a defeater with a different logical form — more like an argument that the posits are unintelligible than like an unsolved puzzle — and so are unmoved by any amount of decomposition. If that's right, the two sides may be answering different questions under one name, and what looks like an empirical or metaphysical dispute is really a disagreement about what would even count as an answer. But I can also see it as a hidden substantive dispute about whether the concept of experience applies univocally across scales, given that all our grip on that concept comes from introspection by whole organisms.

Please diagnose the disagreement: is it verbal, substantive, methodological, or a mix — and what in what each side actually says supports your diagnosis? If there is a hidden question doing the real work, identify it. Then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis — a conceptual distinction, a formal account of subject composition, a change in the question, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why. Two constraints on the answer. First, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this literature — for instance, whether the combination problem splits into distinct sub-problems of differing difficulty seems close to settled, while whether any of them is fatal to panpsychism is nothing like settled, so don't tell me the latter as though it were consensus. Second, where you yourself take a side on a contested point — say, on whether the bonding proposal is a genuine move or a relabeling — flag that as your assessment rather than the field's, and say what would make you revise it.
