I work at a state heritage office and we keep having fights about whether a thing is still "the same thing" after it gets restored or rebuilt, and I have a feeling philosophers have basically been fighting about this exact issue for two thousand years under the name Ship of Theseus. So help me steal their tools.

Background: our office maintains a register of protected structures, and register status carries real obligations and grant money, so identity claims have consequences. When someone replaces every rotten board in an 1850s barn over 40 years, is it still the 1850s barn? When a covered bridge gets 80% new timber but stays standing in the same place the whole time? When a ship is raised from a harbor, conserved piece by piece with lots of new material, and put in a museum? Our current rule is basically vibes plus a line in our statute about "retention of historic fabric," which everyone interprets differently depending on what outcome they want.

What I want is a decision procedure a reviewer can actually run on an identity claim in an afternoon, no philosophy degree required. It should be grounded in what the actual metaphysics of persistence says -- I gather there are roughly three families of answers (stuff-continuity, continuity of form/structure/process, and "no deep fact of the matter, it depends on our interests"), and I suspect a good procedure uses all three as different lenses rather than picking one. So:

1. Give me the ordered questions a reviewer asks, what counts as enough of an answer at each step, and where the procedure terminates in a "yes it's the same," "no it isn't," or "here's the hard middle case."
2. Be explicit about which steps are genuinely factual and which are value calls dressed up as facts (like whether material continuity matters more than the structure staying in use -- I suspect that's not something metaphysics can settle for us, it's a policy choice, and I'd rather the procedure say so than pretend).
3. Handle the fission-style case: the barn that was dismantled, and someone rebuilt a barn from its parts on the original site while a preservation group simultaneously rebuilt a barn from modern materials incorporating a few original pieces on a site across town. Both are claiming to be the register entry. Whatever you say here, say it honestly, including if the right answer is that "same building" is a bad question and we should be asking a different one.
4. Then run the procedure on three real-ish cases from our files: (a) the covered bridge with ~80% new timber replaced in situ over decades, still carrying traffic; (b) the harbor ship raised, conserved, and now 60% new material in a museum hall; (c) a logging flume, originally temporary by design, that has been continuously rebuilt from scratch every ~15 years following the same plans at the same spot. One of these should come out in a way I won't like -- I won't tell you which, but flag it yourself.

Also tell me where this procedure will predictably misfire. Like, I already know it'll struggle with things that are supposed to be replaced, ropes and sails and the like, and with buildings whose "identity" maybe was always tied to their use rather than their boards. Be upfront about failure modes rather than giving me something that only works on tidy cases.

Make it usable: my reviewers have maybe two hours per claim, they're historians not logicians, and the output has to be defensible when a county commission yells at us. If a step needs a judgment call, name who should make it (reviewer, senior registrar, or the board).
