And here is the student's answer, copied verbatim:

"The Special Composition Question, posed by van Inwagen in Material Beings, is: under what conditions do some things, the xs, compose a further thing y? It's a question about when composition occurs, not about what parthood is in general — that's a seperate question. The obvious answers fail. Contact fails because it gives us too many objects: if contact sufficed for composition, then two people shaking hands would compose a third object, and a crowded subway car would be one giant thing. It also gives too few, since plenty of objects we all believe in have parts that aren't in contact — the solar system is one object on anyone's view, and its parts are millions of miles apart. Fastening fails for the same sort of reason: it both overgenerates (any two planks nailed together become one thing, no matter how accidental) and is just as arbitrary a stopping point as contact. The lesson van Inwagen draws is that any answer phrased in terms of spatial or mechanical relations will draw lines that don't track anything nature itself cares about, so he looks for a distinction that does real work, and the only candidate is life.

The two extreme answers: nihilism says composition never occurs — the only things that exist are simples, things with no proper parts. Universalism, or unrestricted composition, says composition always occurs: for any things whatever, there is a further thing they compose, their fusion. So there is an object composed of my left shoe and the Eiffel Tower.

A problem for nihilism: it flies in the face of common sense, since there are obviously tables and chairs. But the nihilist has a reply available: she can say the only things around are simples arranged table-wise, and that this is all common sense ever really meant. A problem for universalism: it also offends common sense — the shoe-and-Eiffel-Tower fusion seems crazy — and worse, the universalist has to say that composition is vague, since there will be all these borderline collections where it's indeterminate whether a fusion exists.

Van Inwagen's own answer: the xs compose a y iff the activity of the xs constitutes a life. So there are no tables, chairs, or ships — only simples arranged table-wise — but there are organisms, including us. And this answer, unlike the contact and fastening ones, escapes the arbitrariness problem entirely, because biology draws a perfectly sharp line between the living and the non-living: a thing either is alive or it isn't, with no borderline cases, so his view has exactly the kind of determinate cutoff that the other moderate answers lack."
