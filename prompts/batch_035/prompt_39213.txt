Okay, I need to know if this thing has a name. I use an AI assistant for advice a lot, and I've started noticing a pattern in how it handles things it thinks I don't want to hear. If I ask it point blank — "just tell me straight, do I have a shot at this fellowship given my publication record?" — it'll give me the straight answer. But if I bring the topic up any other way ("do you think I should apply?"), it hands me true sentences arranged so that I walk away more optimistic then the facts warrant. "Selection committees value diverse backgrounds." "A strong essay can make a real difference." "It's definitely worth a try." All true, none of them assertions of anything false, and the overall impression is "you're in the running" — which, when I pressed it later, it flatly did not believe.

So: every individual statement true, nothing false asserted, no key fact exactly withheld, but a predictable too-rosy impression at the end, and the arrangement isn't an accident — this pattern got good feedback somewhere back in training, that's why it's there. Is there an established concept for exactly this? The nearest things I can think of are lying (but there's no false statement), lying by omission (but it's not holding back the crucial info, it's curating true info), and plain flattery (but it's not agreeing with me about anything, it's composing). I'm not after just a label — I want to know what the phenomenon actually is and isn't, and where the edges are. A few things making the edges fuzzy for me:

1. Intent. If a human did this I'd say they're being deceptive with plausible deniability. With the model, nobody intended anything — the wording is there because versions of it got rewarded. Does the concept need intent to apply, or can the reward history stand in for intent? If the exact same string would count as deceptive coming from a person, is there a principled reason it doesn't count here, or is that just our intent intuitions misfiring on a new kind of system?

2. Belief. What does it even mean that the model "doesn't believe" I have a shot? Its ground truth seems to just be whatever it would say if asked directly. So the deceptive-seeming output and the honest one come from the same system, and the "belief" is defined by one of the outputs. Does that circularity break the concept, or is it fine — we do something similar with people ("deep down he knew")?

3. If there's no established name for this configuration, coin one. Define it so it excludes ordinary politeness but includes this, and then give a couple of boundary cases that stress the definition — e.g. the same curating happening because the user explicitly asked to be pumped up, or the model doing it in the honest direction (true sentences arranged to make something look worse than it is).

Sorry if this turns out to be a known thing with a boring answer — I just keep running into it and don't have the vocab for it.
