I'm a senior QA engineer with about fifteen years in test engineering — black-box and white-box testing, code and requirement coverage, mutation testing, property-based testing and fuzzing, mocks and stubs, contract testing, the oracle problem, flaky tests, regression suites. My philosophy background is pop-science level at best: I know there was a man named Turing, a test involving a chat, and a room with a man in it speaking Chinese, and that's roughly where it ends. I want to understand what philosophers actually argue about when they argue over the Turing test as a criterion for having a mind, but I want it stated in my vocabulary, because that's the only one in which I can check whether a claim is doing real work.

Specifically, please do the following, keeping the translation honest — don't smuggle in assumptions of the testing worldview and present them as the philosophy's content, and don't quietly fix the philosophy's loose ends in order to make it look translatable.

1. Turing's setup, restated as a test harness. Identify the system under test, the interface, the oracle, and the pass condition. One wrinkle I've read about: Turing's original 1950 paper apparently starts from a party game in which an interrogator tries to work out, from typewritten answers alone, which of two hidden players is the man and which the woman — and the machine is then substituted for one of them. So the original harness seems to test imitation of a specific human, not "mind" in general. Does that matter to the test's validity, or is it a historical quirk? Relatedly: the interface is extremely narrow — text on a terminal. In my world, testing through a narrow interface with a constrained session length is exactly how a system under test fails differently in production. What, if anything, did Turing have to say about the harness's own limits, and how do philosophers treat that today — as a coverage constraint, or as something else?

2. The sufficiency claim as a claim about test validity. The claim that passing the test warrants attributing a mind is, in my terms, a claim that a black-box behavioral suite licenses an inference to a property that is never directly observed. Please lay out the strongest version of this inference the philosophers actually defend, and then locate its weakest premise in my vocabulary. In particular, I want you to keep apart two failure modes that I find it useful to separate at work: (a) the suite has gaps — the property is testable at this interface, but our test sessions didn't exercise the right behaviors; and (b) there is no oracle — the property is not something any test at this interface could read off, because no spec at the interface expresses it. Which of these, if either, do the various objections to the Turing test allege?

3. Searle's Chinese Room, restated. As far as I can tell, the thought experiment describes something I would recognize immediately: a system that passes every behavioral test while the implementation demonstrably lacks the property the tests were supposed to stand proxy for. In my terms, that's a passing suite that tells you nothing about the internal structure: all green tests on spaghetti. But philosophers seem to treat the room as showing more than that — as though it reveals something about the kind of property "understanding" is, not just about the weakness of behavioral evidence. Please state exactly what the thought experiment is supposed to establish beyond "black-box tests don't inspect internals," which I'd have granted without the room, and whether the room's conclusion applies only to systems whose internal processing is like the room's (rule-following symbol manipulation) or to any system passing the same tests.

4. The lookup-table objection. There is, I'm told, an objection to the effect that a sufficiently large table of pre-scripted responses would pass any finite test session, since the interrogator only ever samples finitely many exchanges. This one I want in full, because it looks like a point about test theory rather than about minds: no finite suite can distinguish a genuine implementation from a mock that has memorized the expected outputs for every input sequence the suite happens to exercise. Does this objection show the Turing test has a coverage problem (failure mode (a) above), or that the question it poses is ill-posed (something nearer (b))? And how do philosophers who still defend the test answer it — by appeal to what happens in unbounded or adversarial sessions, by appeal to resource limits, or by conceding something?

5. The residue report. Finally, tell me what survived the translation and what did not. Which parts of the debate mapped cleanly onto my vocabulary; which parts could only be carried over with distortion (say where the fudge was inserted); and which parts had no expression at all — my guess is that whatever philosophers mean by "consciousness" or "what it's like" is the big one, but I want your diagnosis, not my guess. Then give me your verdict on the residue: is it a defect of my vocabulary (testing talk cannot see a distinction that is real), or a sign that the original claims were less precise than they looked? One thing to bear down on here: in my world we have a sharp, working distinction between testing to spec and verifying that the spec itself captures the requirement. Does the philosophical debate contain anything like that distinction — anything separating "the system meets the behavioral spec" from "the behavioral spec is the right spec for the property at issue" — and if not, is that because the philosophy lacks the tool, or because the property in question is such that no spec could ever be the right one?

Please be candid where the translation fails; I'm more interested in the residue than in the parts that carried over.
