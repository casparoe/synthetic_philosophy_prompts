For years now I've been sold on what you might call the Condorcetian case for majoritarian democracy: if each voter is even slightly more likely than not to choose the better option, and voters judge independently of one another, then as the electorate grows large the probability that the majority chooses correctly approaches certainty. The mathematical core is elegant, and I find the normative upshot intoxicating — democracy as a truth-tracking machine, where the ignorance of the median individual gets washed out by aggregation rather than needing to be fixed by experts. I lean on this in the democratic theory seminar I teach, and I've privately concluded that the independence and competence assumptions are mild enough that the theorem applies, at least roughly, to real mass elections: if the average voter in a country of fifty million were even 50.5% reliable, the majority would essentially never err, and every close election we observe would be a near-impossible event.

Lately, though, my confidence has been fraying, and I want a systematic audit rather than another round of my usual hand-waving. Tell me what would actually count against the view — not a lecture on the theorem itself, but a map of its exposure. Some things I already half-know and want treated seriously rather than dismissed: that correlated errors break independence, and that even a tiny correlation among millions of voters can drag majority reliability below coin-flipping; that I've heard there's a formal result showing that individual competence strictly better than chance doesn't by itself guarantee a competent majority once you allow heterogeneous, correlated voters, so the theorem's punch line silently depends on assumptions stronger than the ones I advertise; that close elections, which we observe all the time, are themselves evidence something in the setup is off — either voters aren't judging a common "right answer" at all, or their competence varies by question in a way that averages below the threshold more often than I'd like; and that the whole framing presupposes a fact of the matter about what the correct policy or candidate is, which may be incoherent for genuinely value-laden choices where I'd most want to invoke the theorem in democracy's defense.

I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I'd like honesty about which of them a committed Condorcetian could absorb as a refinement (e.g., by reinterpreting the theorem as a benchmark or an asymptotic ideal rather than a description of actual electorates) versus which genuinely bite. And please address the meta-question directly: if the view survives by being progressively qualified — independence relaxed to "weakly correlated," competence made question-relative, truth-tracking restricted to some thin class of issues — is it still doing the justificatory work I recruited it for, or has it quietly become unfalsifiable in a way that should embarrass someone who deploys it in front of students as an argument rather than a slogan? For what it's worth, the challenge I'd personally find most persuasive is the one about close elections — I've never gotten a straight answer about why, if aggregation works the way I claim, we don't see lopsided landslide victories for the correct side as the norm rather than coin flips.
