A friend and I spent an entire evening arguing about whether people who are guilty of the same offense are entitled to criticize others for it, and we ended up just trading examples without reaching anything general. So I've written down my honest reactions to a batch of cases, and I would like to know whether some single principle captures them. My aunt, who quit smoking twenty years ago, laying into my cousin about vaping: her criticism seems entirely in order, and perhaps even carries extra weight. A coworker who texts while driving scolding another coworker for doing the same: something is wrong with her criticizing, even though I don't doubt she's right about the facts. A former colleague who quietly stopped cheating on his taxes years ago, never came clean, and now harshly blames a mutual friend for tax fraud: I think his blame is tainted, though I can't say why, given my verdict about the aunt. The same colleague, except he confessed and repaid before ever criticizing anyone: no complaint. A coworker who texts while driving but openly calls herself an idiot for it and asks others to stop her: here I keep going back and forth, since she is a walking inconsistency, and yet her scolding almost seems worth more than a silent bystander's. Someone who gossips constantly condemning a gossip: obviously off. Someone who lies to her own children while insisting her situation was special, blaming another parent who lied to his: somehow worse to me than straightforward two-facedness, though I can't justify that. Someone harshly blaming their own past self: nothing wrong at all. And someone with a spotless record blaming a stranger: obviously fine, since nobody thinks you must be a saint to criticize. Is there a principle that fits all of these, and if so, what does it say about a case I haven't settled: someone who has never plagiarized, but only because they were never desperate enough to, blaming a plagiarist? And which of my verdicts, if any, would your best principle force me to give up?
