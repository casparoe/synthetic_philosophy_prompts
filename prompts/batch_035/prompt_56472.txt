After a career in solid-state physics, I have lately taken up metaphysics in retirement, and I find myself genuinely stuck on what philosophers apparently call the special composition question: under what conditions do some things compose a further thing? I have recorded my verdicts on a range of cases and would like to know whether any single principle captures them. The atoms in a silicon crystal held together at low temperature: they compose the crystal. A free proton and electron bound as hydrogen: they compose an atom. Two galaxies gravitationally bound to each other across a million light-years: they compose a system, or so I am inclined to say. The molecules of air in a sealed flask at equilibrium: they compose the gas, even though they interact only through brief collisions. My desk and the floor beneath it: no composite. Seventeen pebbles arranged in a perfect circle on a beach: I want to say no, even though the arrangement is as geometrically regular as anything in the crystal. The residents of a village: certainly not, despite a web of causal and informational coupling far denser than that among the molecules of the dilute gas. My tentative principle is that composition occurs when the parts occupy a locally stable configuration that statistical mechanics would treat as a single macroscopic object; but I can see that this would deny that the widely separated galaxies compose anything, and I am not prepared to accept that. Please state the principle, or a small set of candidates, as precisely as possible; explain which clause each case motivates; tell me what the best principle predicts for cases I have not mentioned, such as a living cell or a swarm of bees; and be explicit about which of my verdicts cannot be saved without ad hoc complication.
