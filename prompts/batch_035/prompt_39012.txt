National archives face a delusion problem they rarely name: they keep only a small fraction of the records generated by governments and societies — often well under five percent — and the choice of which fraction survives is made by archivists in advance of any future historian's questions, under a professional doctrine called appraisal. The profession nonetheless describes itself in curatorial, custodial terms: archivists supposedly preserve rather than interpret, keep rather than select, and leave judgment about significance to researchers. Meanwhile, the criteria actually used in appraisal tend to privilege the records of the powerful — well-organized institutional files that document official functions — while the traces of the governed, the poor, and the unwired often survive only by accident, in scraps, or on now-obsolete platforms. Some decades later, this becomes destiny: entire questions about the past turn out to be unaskable because the relevant records were appraised away, and no one can now reconstruct what the silences contain.

What theory of value — of what a society's memory is and what it is for — does the practice of archival appraisal presuppose? Does the custodial self-description cohere at all with an act that is arguably the most consequential and least visible form of editorial power a state exercises over its own history, or is the neutrality claim a genuine presupposition rather than a fig leaf — and if so, of what: perhaps a view on which significance is an objective property of records that patient archival science can detect? Then consider two recent revisions archivists have proposed: moving appraisal from the individual office or creator to whole social functions and domains, judged by how completely a slice of society gets documented; and turning selection over to the communities themselves, on the theory that a group's own memory of its records outranks the archivist's judgment. Do these revisions share the old presupposition, replace it, or reveal that appraisal never strictly presupposed any one theory of value and could have been carried along by several? Finally, tell me what an archive built on an explicitly counter-memory view — one that deliberately over-collects from those the state documents least, and treats documented absence as itself a record to be preserved through oral history and metadata about what was destroyed or never made — would have to give up, and whether the neutrality doctrine, once abandoned for appraisal, can survive anywhere else in archival work.
