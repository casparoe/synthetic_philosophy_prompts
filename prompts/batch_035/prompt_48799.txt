Here is the passage. After arguing that if all propositions are either true or false, then whatever is affirmed about the future must either obtain or not, Aristotle writes:

"Now if this be so, nothing is or takes place fortuitously, either in the present or in the future, and there are no real alternatives; everything takes place of necessity and is fixed. For either he that affirms that it will take place or he that denies this is in correspondence with fact, whereas if things did not take place of necessity, an event might just as easily not happen as happen; for the meaning of the word 'fortuitous' with regard to present or future events is that reality is so constituted that it may issue in either of two opposite directions. Again, if a thing is white now, it was true before to say that it would be white, so that of anything that has taken place it was always true to say 'it is' or 'it will be'. But if it was always true to say that a thing is or will be, it is not possible that it should not be or not be about to be, and when a thing cannot not come to be, it is impossible that it should not come to be, and when it is impossible that it should not come to be, it must come to be. All, then, that is about to be must of necessity take place."

My confusion is about the second half of this passage. The chain of inferences from "it was always true to say that a thing is or will be" to "it must come to be" looks to me like it slides between the necessity of the consequent and the necessity of the consequence — i.e., from the necessity of the conditional (if it was always true that P, then P) to the necessity of P itself, which is the classic modal scope fallacy. But I have three specific questions I'd like your committed reading to address. First, on the sentence "if a thing is white now, it was true before to say that it would be white": is Aristotle's premise here bivalence (every future-tensed proposition is already either true or false), or only the weaker "truth-value link" (that a present fact suffices for the past truth of the corresponding past-tensed prediction), which doesn't by itself say anything about undetermined futures? The difference seems to matter for whether the fallacy charge even lands. Second, what work is the "always" doing? The move seems to require that the prediction was true at every past time, not just some, and I can't tell whether that's doing real modal work or just rhetorical amplification. Third — and this is where I most want you to take a definite position — does Aristotle himself endorse the reasoning here, or is the whole paragraph meant as a reductio of the assumption he started with, such that his eventual solution (whatever exactly it is at the end of the chapter) is supposed to block one specific step rather than the argument as a whole? If it's a reductio, which step does his own reply actually target — bivalence itself, the inference from truth to necessity, or something about how "necessity" should be understood when applied to what is actual?
