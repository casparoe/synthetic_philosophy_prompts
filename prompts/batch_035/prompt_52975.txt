Construct a thought experiment in which a philosopher who endorses the standard gradual-replacement argument for mind uploading — the argument Chalmers defends with his fading- and dancing-qualia reasoning — is driven, if she applies that argument consistently, to a verdict she will resist: that survival fails, or is at best deeply indeterminate, even though every step of the replacement preserves a one-to-one functional correspondence. The twist I want concerns granularity. Instead of replacing each neuron with a silicon duplicate, replace each block of, say, a thousand functionally related neurons with a single trained recurrent device that reproduces the block's input–output profile over behaviorally relevant timescales, but whose internal states bear no correspondence to the states of the individual neurons — no state inside the device stands in for any particular neuron. Functional organization is thus preserved only at a coarse grain of description: the mapping from the old system to the new one is one-to-one at the level of thousand-neuron blocks and silent at the level of neurons. Constraints on the scenario: one paragraph; no dualism, no quantum effects, no perfect scanning; the technology may be stipulated as available, but it should be described coherently enough that a computational neuroscientist would not scoff; and, crucially, the verdicts must fall out of the theories rather than being stipulated — the scenario itself may not assert that anything is or is not preserved at the fine grain, since the question is precisely what the theories say about that. After presenting the scenario, do the following. First, run the standard gradual-replacement argument on the case and show exactly where it generates the unwelcome verdict, marking the step at which an assumption about the required grain of functional organization enters — an assumption the argument almost never states explicitly. Second, state what a biological naturalist and a straightforward psychological-continuity theorist would each conclude here, and say whether either of them is better placed than the functionalist. Third, identify which single feature of the case is doing the work, and tell me what happens to the case if that feature is removed — that is, if the mapping is made one-to-one at the single-neuron level. Finally, give the strongest defense a pro-uploader could mount, most plausibly by arguing that functional organization was always a level-relative notion and that the relevant level is fixed by whatever supports the causal profile of cognition; then assess honestly whether that defense, once granted, also lets in systems the pro-uploader meant to keep out. The purpose of this case is to stress-test the grain assumption that the uploading argument typically leaves tacit, so a construction that quietly builds the fine-grain requirement into the scenario itself should count as cheating.
