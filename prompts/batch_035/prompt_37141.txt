I'm on the publication review board of a mid-sized AI lab, and the thing we keep fighting about is what people call the unilateralist's curse -- when several of us each lean moderately toward publishing something, but if we all knew that everyone else also leaned that way, none of us would. The standard line (Bostrom et al.) is that the curse is systematic: individual near-indifference tips collective action over the threshold, and the fix is something like a designated neutral decider or a supermajority rule. Fine, but nobody's ever turned that into something a board can actually run in a two-hour meeting.

Write us a decision procedure for publication calls with a dual-use flavor (capabilities findings, evals that double as jailbreak recipes, data collection methods others could abuse). Constraints: we're 7 people with very different risk tolerances, we meet monthly, and we can't punt everything to an external committee because that would slow things down to a standstill.

The procedure should tell us: what to ask each member and in what order (before or after seeing each other's views -- this is the whole ballgame, so be explicit about how to sequence it), what counts as enough of a concern to trigger the collective-decision mode rather than just "you do you," and where to put a hard stop versus a defer-to-someone-judgment step.

Then run it on three cases: (1) a paper on a prompting technique that makes models comply with harmful requests, which the four ML people think is fine to publish and the two safety people quietly have doubts about; (2) a dataset analysis revealing that certain groups could be cheaply targeted with personalized misinformation, where each person individually thinks the public-good case is "probably" strong enough; (3) an eval result that would embarrass a competitor, where one member wants to publish mainly for the win and everyone else is fine with it. That third one should stress-test whether the curse is symmetric or whether our procedure wrongly treats "publish" and "don't publish" the same when they're not.

Be honest about where the procedure misfires and where it just relocates the judgment call. And note that a small lab may not have an unbiased decider at all -- what's the degraded version?

Start with the procedure itself, no preamble or commentary -- your first sentence should be the first step. Then the worked cases after. American English please.
