ok so I'm in a grad seminar on moral theory and we have to submit a "philosophical profile" — basically a list of our commitments within contractualism's territory, which the rest of the seminar then gets to attack. Mine is below and I want a sanity check on it before I get torn apart in front of everyone. Heres the package:

(1) I'm a contractualist: an act is wrong iff some principle permitting it could be reasonably rejected, and I take "reasonably rejected" in the individualist way — each person weighs the burden the principle would impose on them, and one person's burden can't be canceled out by benefits to other people. That anti-offsetting idea is honestly the whole reason I'm a contractualist; the separateness of persons thing. I can't stomach trading across people's lives the way utilitarianism does.

(2) Relatedly, I'm anti-aggregation: there is no interpersonal sum such that five people's headaches could ever outweigh one person's paralysis. Benefits dont add up across persons, period.

(3) But in rescue cases I think you're *required* to save the greater number. Five strangers drowning on the left, one stranger on the right, you can only reach one side: save the five. And not merely permissible — if you flip a coin instead, supposedly to give the one a fair chance, I think you do something wrong, and you wrong all six of them.

(4) The case that made me a contractualist in the first place is Transplant: it's wrong to kill one healthy person to save five, and what makes it wrong, to my mind, is purely the one's complaint — a principle allowing it is one he could reasonably reject all by himself. I take that to be the cleanest demonstration of where utilitarianism goes off the rails.

(5) Separately, I accept the verdict that you may impose tiny burdens on a great many people for the sake of a somewhat larger benefit to yourself — the classic version being that you can avoid a moderately bad headache of your own by giving a million people each a barely noticeable headache. For what it's worth I've never found this counterintuitive.

(6) And along the same lines, I think it's permissible to drive to the store for ice cream even though doing so imposes a tiny risk of death on pedestrians and cyclists, for a benefit to me that is obviously trivial. Any view that condemned this would be a reductio of itself.

Go through the pairs, tell me which are genuinely in tension and which only look like it, rank the real ones by severity, and for each say what the cheapest fix would be and what it costs me elsewhere in the list. If the whole package is beyond repair, or if I'm equivocating somewhere (e.g., on what counts as a "burden" or on whose standpoint matters for reasonable rejection), say that plainly instead of manufacturing clever reconciliations — I'd rather find out now than in the seminar. Dont be polite about it either, I want an audit, not a pep talk. And please write the answer in American English.
