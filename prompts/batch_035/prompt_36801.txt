I hold a fairly strong version of the simulation argument: not merely that Bostrom's trilemma is formally valid, but that the first two disjuncts both fail — that technological maturity is achievable, that mature civilizations do run detailed simulations of beings like us — and hence that the probability I am simulated is well over a half. I've already patched the argument against what I regard as the cheap objections, so please don't rehash those. In particular: I don't rest anything on posthumans being especially curious about their ancestors, since any sufficiently detailed historical or even counterfactual simulation of human-like minds generates the same observer-moments; and I'm unmoved by the claim that simulations would be too computationally expensive to be widespread, because recursively nested simulations and falling compute costs cut in the opposite direction.

What I want from you is the serious case against my position, organized as a map of its exposure. Specifically:

First, distinguish the defeaters. Which observations, arguments, or formal results would be decisive against high credence in simulation, and which merely awkward? For example: would discovering fundamental limits on efficient computation of fine-grained consciousness be decisive, or could a proponent absorb it by weakening the substrate-independence assumption? Would evidence about the marginal costs and attention-spans of mature civilizations count, and how would such evidence even be got?

Second, the probability itself. My worry is the measure problem: once simulations nest inside simulations, "the fraction of observers that are simulated" is not obviously well-defined, and the answer can shift with how one weights observers, observer-moments, or simulations of differing fidelity. Is my credence of "over a half" tracking anything coherent, or is it an artifact of an arbitrary reference class? Does it matter that if fidelity degrades with depth, the reference class is dominated by low-fidelity minds for whom questions like this one are never tokened — and if those minds are excluded, what principled line excludes them?

Third, what does my view risk at all? Does being simulated predict anything observable that rival views don't (physics quirks, resource-saving shortcuts, ceilings on simulated scale), or has it been insulated into unfalsifiability — and if so, is that a defect in this kind of anthropic reasoning generally rather than in my version of it?

Finally, rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones a committed proponent would himself concede as counting against the view (as opposed to which only critics press), and tell me honestly where you'd land: exposed, insulated, or genuinely well-protected. I'm most interested in whether the measure problem, more than any empirical consideration, is the thing that should actually move me.
