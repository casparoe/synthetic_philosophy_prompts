The question was: "Parfit's teletransporter scans and destroys your body on Earth while a machine on Mars constructs an exact replica with all your memories. Did you survive? Explain Parfit's view on whether identity is what matters in survival, drawing on his fission case."

And here's the student's answer:

"Parfit holds that what matters in survival is psychological connectedness. The replica on Mars is psychologically continuous with me, sharing my memories, beliefs, and intentions, so in the sense that matters I have survived the teletransportation. Identity itself is not what matters; what matters is what Parfit calls Relation R. Locke was the first to make this move, arguing that memory constitutes personal identity and introducing the notion of quasi-memory, which lets me apparently remember an experience without it having to be my own. Since the replica is R-related to me, I survive, and the preservation of strict identity is a bonus on top of that. The fission case is where the real argument happens: if my brain is divided and each half is transplanted into a different body, both resulting people are psychologically continuous with me, but I cannot be identical to two people, so my identity becomes indeterminate. It follows that it is indeterminate whether I survive, and since identity is indeterminate even in ordinary cases when we look closely enough, Parfit proves that survival is impossible for anyone. This is how we arrive at his conclusion that identity is not what matters: once survival is off the table, the only thing left that can matter is Relation R."
