I'm taking a second-year philosophy course on practical rationality, and we've spent the last two weeks on Buridan's ass — the donkey placed exactly between two identical haystacks (or, in the version my tutor says comes from al-Ghazali, a man equidistant between food and water), which, having no reason to prefer one over the other, does nothing and starves. My tutor also mentioned that the ass story appears nowhere in Buridan's surviving works, so I'm aware the attribution is shaky, but that hasn't stopped the problem from becoming standard. My question, though, isn't about the thought experiment itself. It's about a family of real institutional practices that look like they were designed to escape it, and I'd like help reconstructing what those practices philosophically presuppose.

The practices I have in mind are all tie-breaking by lot: when two candidates for a legislative seat emerge from a recount exactly tied, several jurisdictions settle the seat by drawing names from a container; when a school or housing program has more qualified applicants than places, some systems allocate the places by lottery among the qualified; some hiring committees, when two finalists are dead even after every criterion has been applied, have been known to flip a coin; and where lotteries are not used, institutions typically substitute arbitrary-but-deterministic tie-breakers instead — order on a waiting list, date of application, seniority — which at least gives the winner a reason-shaped thing without giving them a better claim. What strikes me is that these same institutions otherwise insist that decisions be justified by reasons: adjudication is expected to give reasons, hiring is supposed to be merit-based, and an official who said "we picked him arbitrarily" would be acting unlawfully. Yet at the tie, the institution deliberately embraces pure arbitrariness, and afterwards treats the outcome as fully legitimate — the lottery winner gets the seat, the job, or the flat exactly as if he had earned it over the loser.

So here is what I want you to work out for me. First, reconstruct the implicit theory of choice and legitimacy that this pattern presupposes. I can see a few candidates and I don't know which is right: that when reasons run out, indifference licenses random selection (roughly, an option is choosable without preference, which is exactly what the ass's predicament was supposed to show is impossible); that fairness at the tie consists in equal chances rather than in selecting the better candidate, so that randomness is not a second-best but the uniquely just method; or simply that deliberation has costs and the lottery is a termination convention that everyone can accept without anyone being preferred. Second, say whether the practice presupposes that the tie is genuine or only that the institution justifiably believes it is genuine — because if two job candidates are tied only because the committee declined to investigate further, the coin flip seems to launder a fact about the committee's laziness into a fact about the candidates. Third, tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined: which of the candidate theories it strictly needs, and which it merely fits comfortably with. And finally, say what would have to change in the practice if the presupposed theory were replaced — for instance, if we held (as some readings of the ass case suggest) that choosing without any preference at all is impossible or irrational, or if we held that apparent ties always generate a further duty to seek a discriminating reason rather than a license to stop looking. Is there some principled line between the contexts where a lottery is legitimate and the contexts where a lottery would be an insult to the claimants, and does the current patchwork of lotteries, waiting lists, and seniority rules track any such line, or is it just historical accident?
