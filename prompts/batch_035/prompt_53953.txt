I'm taking a graduate seminar built around David Lewis's Convention, and the professor likes to fire off rapid verdicts on whether some regularity or other counts as a convention. Her track record so far:

- Driving on the right in the United States: convention — and she insists that the statute codifying it makes no difference to its status.
- Greeting with a handshake rather than a bow: convention.
- Using dollar bills as money: convention.
- The regularity that speakers of English, by and large, tell the truth in English: convention — she calls it the paradigm case, to the surprise of half the room.
- Queuing at the bakery: convention, even though queue-jumpers are met with genuine moral indignation and not mere inconvenience.
- The prohibition on unprovoked assault: not a convention.
- Giving up one's bus seat to an elderly passenger: not a convention.
- Two colleagues who sign a written agreement to meet every Tuesday at nine, and keep meeting: not a convention so long as the agreement stands; but if the agreement lapses and they keep meeting on Tuesdays anyway, it becomes one.

She has also told us, maddeningly, that she thinks Lewis got some of his conditions too strong and others too weak, though she refuses to say which.

Next week she will rule on three new cases, and I want my predictions prepared in advance:

1. QWERTY. The layout was close to arbitrary when it was adopted, but network effects now mean that for any individual typist the alternatives are worse than QWERTY, given that everyone else uses it — and worse in a way that was not true at the point of adoption.
2. A reinforcement-learning experiment in which sender and receiver agents, with no apparent representation of each other's minds, settle after thousands of rounds into a stable signal–action pairing that serves both sides perfectly.
3. The norm in my country that men rather than women propose marriage. It plausibly solves a coordination problem of some kind, but a substantial fraction of the population — some on religious grounds, some on egalitarian grounds — believes the alternative is not equally good, and this disagreement is no secret to anyone.

Predict her verdict on each of the three cases. But I care more about the reasoning than the verdicts themselves: articulate the two or three rules that fit her track record equally well so far, say which of the new cases each rule decides differently, give your best guess about which rule she is actually running and why, and propose the single further case, of your own invention, that would best discriminate among the rules. Treat Lewis as a resource for formulating the candidate rules, but remember that her track record is the evidence — she has told us she is not simply applying his definition.
