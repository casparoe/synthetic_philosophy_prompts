I run a small analysis desk that maintains live probability estimates for a corporate client on geopolitical risks — port closures, coups, sanctions packages. Whenever something is happening, we get first reports whose reliability we can only estimate, and my analysts oscillate between treating each report as established fact and updating all the way, and being so afraid of being wrong twice that they barely move the number at all. I understand formal epistemology has a treatment of updating on evidence you're not certain of — I've run into the phrase "probability kinematics" — where you revise your credences across a partition to whatever confidence the report actually warrants, rather than conditioning on the report as if it were a certainty. Please turn that idea into a procedure my analysts can actually run in the ten minutes after a report arrives, with no more arithmetic than a spreadsheet and no philosophy on the page. Give me the steps in order, what each step asks for, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with "don't move the number yet," and where it should explicitly hand the decision to a named person because the question is one of judgment rather than calculation. Then run it on three cases: a single usually-reliable source reports that a head of state has been hospitalized; a wire story citing two anonymous officials says a sanctions announcement is coming Friday; and a case where the reliability of the evidence itself depends on the hypothesis — bad news leaks faster than good news, so a report's absence is informative. Include one case where the procedure yields an answer I won't like, and say plainly what the procedure cannot do — in particular whether the order in which two uncertain reports arrive should matter to us, and what that implies about how often we have to recompute from scratch. Please say how confident you are in each part of the procedure, and single out the step you'd least trust in a live situation.
