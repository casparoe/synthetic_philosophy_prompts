I'm a third-year philosophy student, and my seminar on contemporary moral theory has just finished the unit on Scanlon's contractualism and the aggregation problem — his rescue and transmitter-room cases, the individualist restriction on summing burdens across persons, and Taurek on whether the numbers should count. In an unlucky bit of timing, I'm also the student representative on my university's Student Welfare Fund Committee, and this week we must decide how to spend a one-off surplus of about $150,000. Two proposals made the final round. Proposal A would put the entire sum into new lighting and cameras for the footpath between the residential halls and the main library, reducing each of roughly 20,000 students' risk of serious assault or injury by a vanishingly small amount — on the university's own safety office estimates, the equivalent of preventing perhaps two serious incidents over a decade. Proposal B would put the entire sum into an emergency hardship fund for the twelve students currently facing acute crises — medical withdrawal costs, sudden loss of family income, eviction — each of whom would otherwise almost certainly be forced to drop out. One committee member wants the memo to simply total up expected benefits and pick A; another argues that no student facing ruin could reasonably reject B, while no student could reasonably reject A on the strength of a microscopic unprevented risk, so contractualism itself favors B. A third member proposes splitting the money evenly and letting both problems be half-addressed. Please draft the short decision memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting: it should recommend one course of action, say whether the split is a principled compromise or a refusal to decide, engage the aggregation question directly rather than assuming an answer, and give the strongest consideration on the losing side its due.
