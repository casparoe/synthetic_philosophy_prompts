I'm on the governing board of a mid-sized state secondary school (~600 kids, ages 11-16) and we're rewriting the behaviour policy after a bad year. The headteacher wants "visible consequences": kids who bully or vandalize stand up at year assembly, say what they did, and apologise in front of everyone. She says the deterrent effect is real, that parents ignore private sanctions as just a telling-off, and that it works alot faster than a long process. The pastoral team is against it — they want restorative conversations to become the default, where the kid sits down with the person they hurt (adult present), hears what the damage actually did, and agrees on some repair.

It's become personal for me in the last month: my own son got publicly called out by a teacher who just lost his temper (not under any policy — this was ad hoc), and watching him fake sick to avoid school for two weeks has made me think the shame side of this is uglier than I'd assumed. But I don't want to circulate a memo that's just me being emotional about one incident.

The deeper question I keep circling, and what I want the memo to actually engage with, is whether shame and guilt do different moral work. As I understand it, guilt attaches to the act — I did a bad thing — while shame attaches to the self — I am bad — and the psychology literature (I've read a bit of Tangney) suggests shame pushes kids toward hiding and denial while guilt pushes toward repair. Against that, there's a serious philosophical tradition, I think Bernard Williams is the big name, that treats shame as real morality in its own right, something about being seen by others mattering. And the head's view is basically the old shame-culture/guilt-culture distinction from mid-century anthropology (Benedict on Japan and so on): morality gets enforced by the community's eyes, and pretending every kid has internalised our norms is naive. So there's also a question my chair raised that I can't quite answer: is a school a small enough culture that we can, in effect, choose which emotion does the moral work — or does that choice get made by the families and it just lands on us?

Draft the memo I'll circulate before next month's board meeting. I need:

- A definite recommendation on the assembly-callout proposal (approve, reject, or a specific modified version) and on whether restorative conversations become the default. Not a blend-of-approaches-depending-on-the-child fudge — pick, and tell me what the policy actually says on paper.
- A fair treatment of the headteacher's strongest point, which I take to be: private guilt only binds kids who've already internalised the norm, so for the kids causing most of the harm, public shame may be the only lever we actually have. If you reject the callouts, the memo has to answer that head-on, not dodge it.
- Some treatment of my conflict of interest — should I declare it, and does my son's experience make my read of the evidence better informed or just biased?

One last thing: don't hedge into more-research-is-needed. The board has to vote on something. If you genuinely think the evidence underdetermines it, say so in one sentence, then commit anyway and give your reasons — that's a memo I can actually use on the night.
