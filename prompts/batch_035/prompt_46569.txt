Writing an essay for a methods seminar, and I've got myself stuck on the argument from silence. Everyone tells me it's a fallacy, then goes ahead and uses it anyway (Thucydides never mentions X, so X didn't happen / didn't matter — you know the drill). But I've started to think the standard treatment is a mess, because we lump together wildly different cases under the one label.

Here's what I want the essay to do. First, actually take a stance on whether the argumentum ex silentio can carry probative weight at all — not the wishy-washy "it depends," though you can then say what it depends on. Second, evaluate a specific candidate rule that my supervisor floated and I can't decide if it's any good: silence is evidence of absence only when the source had both the motive and the opportunity to mention the fact — that is, the silence has to be surprising given what else we know about the source's interests, habits, and working methods. Does this rule actually sort the good from the bad cases, or does it just push the problem back a level (since "motive" and "opportunity" are themselves reconstructed from the very sources we're trying to criticise)? That circularity worry is the thing I keep circling and can't resolve.

Ground it where you can in examples a reader without library access would know — Thucydides' near-total silence about Herodotus is the obvious one, or Aristotle's lost dialogues, or the silence of our sources on vast stretches of ordinary ancient life (slaves, women, peasants) — rather than obscure inscriptions nobody's memorised. Don't give me a bibliography or anything like that; I can't check citations from where I'm writing this anyway. But do argue properly, weigh both sides, and land somewhere definite by the end. A verdict like "the rule works but only for opportunity, never motive" or "the circularity is real but benign, here's why" is exactly the kind of thing I want. Aim for something like 1500 words? Bit more is fine. cheers!!
