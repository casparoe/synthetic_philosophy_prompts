I'm teaching the closing session of an ethics unit on aggregation in life-and-death decisions, built around John Taurek's 1977 paper "Should the Numbers Count?" (Philosophy & Public Affairs, Vol. 6, No. 4), where he argues that the mere fact that more people would die if I save the one rather than the many gives me no reason, as such, to save the many — and where, facing a choice between his friend David and five strangers, he says he might save David, or flip a coin to give each person an equal chance rather than let the arithmetic decide. For the final class I want to use Spielberg's Saving Private Ryan (1998), and I'd like you to write me a careful reading of the film through Taurek's question. Assume my students know the paper and the film; don't summarize either beyond what the argument needs.

The relevant facts of the film, so we're on the same page: three of the four Ryan brothers are killed within days of the Normandy landing, and General Marshall orders that the fourth, Private James Ryan of the 101st Airborne, be found behind enemy lines and brought home, to spare his mother the loss of all her sons — a decision the film has Marshall justify by reading aloud Lincoln's condolence letter to Lydia Bixby, the Civil War widow who reportedly lost five sons. Captain Miller and a squad of seven (plus Upham, an untested interpreter) are sent after him; one dies almost immediately, the medic Wade dies attacking a German position en route, and the men's resentment at risking eight lives for one nearly breaks the squad apart — capped by the dark joke of finding the wrong Private Ryan first. When they finally reach him, Ryan refuses to leave his unit defending a bridge, so Miller's remaining men stay to fight alongside him, and most of them die. At a stop along the way the squad spares a surrendered German soldier, nicknamed Steamboat Willie, over Upham's objections; the man reappears at the bridge battle and kills Miller, and Upham executes him. The film closes with the elderly Ryan at Miller's grave, asking his wife whether he is a good man.

Here is what I want the reading to do. First, take seriously that the mission's official rationale is structurally Taurekian: it is not aggregative at all — no one pretends that saving Ryan maximizes lives — but claim-based and particularist, an acknowledgment that one identified mother has already given enough. The squad's muttering is the voice of the numbers. Does the film ultimately endorse the rationale (the ending seems to insist Ryan was worth it) or expose it (nearly everyone who retrieved him is dead)? Second, there is a complication Taurek's paper never faces: his cases involve certain outcomes, whereas the mission imposes a risk of death on eight to achieve the certain rescue of one. Does expected-value reasoning reintroduce the numbers through the back door, and does the film notice this distinction or blur it? Third, the Steamboat Willie episode is a mirror case: mercy toward one identified, pleading man against the predictable cost to unidentified future lives — and the film lets that mercy be both admirable and catastrophic. What does that symmetry (and Upham's role in both decisions) suggest about whether the film's real theme is numbers or identifiability? Fourth, Ryan's refusal to be saved, and Miller's dying charge that Ryan earn the sacrifice, convert a rescue into a debt that the survivor must spend his life repaying. Taurek's framework has nothing to say about debts, gratitude, or the burdens of being the one — is this something the film adds to the debate that the philosophical literature on aggregation lacks, or is it a sentimental evasion of it? Finally, be honest about where the reading strains: this is an institutional decision under conditions of war, plausibly justified by morale and the state's implicit promise to families rather than by anyone's claims one-to-one, and the film mostly brackets that. Weigh all this, and conclude: does Saving Private Ryan vindicate Taurek, undermine him, or dramatize a question his paper never asked?
