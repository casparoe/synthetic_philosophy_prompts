I write backend systems for a living, so infinite lazy streams and countable sets are everyday tools for me, but I've never studied philosophy of mathematics. Lately I've been writing down my gut verdicts on cases involving actual infinities, and I'd like to know whether one principle lies behind them, or whether I'm silently running several different notions of "size" that merely look alike.

My verdicts:

1. The even numbers are the same size as the naturals — the pairing n ↔ 2n settles it. Yes, and I'd defend this to anyone.
2. The reals are strictly bigger than the naturals. Yes; the diagonal argument convinces me.
3. The rationals are also the same size as the naturals. I accept the enumeration and would sign off that they're equinumerous — and yet if someone asks whether there are equally *many*, I hesitate. The rationals are packed densely along the line while the naturals sit isolated at spots, and some stubborn part of me insists the rationals are somehow "more." So this one is a verdict and a half.
4. Hilbert's hotel is full, and one more guest arrives; everyone shifts one room up, and the guest is housed. I accept this without any sense of paradox: "full" is a finite-hotel word that simply doesn't apply to the infinite one.
5. The same shifting run on physical objects — an actually infinite stack of coins, and one more coin placed on top; the stack is no bigger than before, so it seems nothing was added — and I nod along, with the feeling that this shows actual physical infinities are impossible. That looks flatly inconsistent with my verdict on 4, and I can't locate the difference.
6. An infinite past, in which every past day is only finitely many days from today: I find that coherent, but the argument that today would never have arrived pulls me the other way. This is the case I keep going back and forth on.

Is there a single principle that captures all six? If not, I have a tentative diagnosis of my own: that my gut tracks two or three distinct notions — cardinality, density, and something like "amount of stuff present" — and that case 5 works on me by making me hear a cardinality fact as an amount-of-stuff fact. Please test that diagnosis against the cases rather than assuming it. State the principle (or the small set of principles) precisely, tie each clause to the case that motivates it, say honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate — and note that I'd much rather lose verdict 5 than verdicts 1 or 2 — and then tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't listed: the hotel is full and infinitely many new guests arrive at once, each current guest moving to double their room number. Should I accept that as readily as I accepted the single new guest, and if my gut rates the two differently, is the principle or the gut right? Keep the whole answer to at most 700 words.
