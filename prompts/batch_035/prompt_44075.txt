I'm writing my final term paper for a philosophy elective, and I'd like your help building its centerpiece. We just covered Aumann's agreement theorem: roughly, if two people have the same priors, and each knows the other is rational, and their posteriors are common knowledge between them, then they cannot agree to disagree — their credences must coincide. My teacher presented it as airtight, and a classmate has been running with it ever since: whenever two informed, honest people disagree about politics, he says, at least one of them must be irrational or arguing in bad faith. I think that conclusion is too fast, but I want to argue it with a constructed case rather than a shrug.

Here is what I need you to build: a thought experiment in which two honest, intelligent, fully evidence-respecting people exchange their credences back and forth — each hears the other's number and updates on it, then announces the new number, over several rounds — and yet end up in persistent disagreement, with neither doing anything we would call irrational. Constraints: the two should be ordinary humans making a practical decision (a doctor and a structural engineer deciding, say, whether to recommend evacuating a town before a storm — something like that, though you may choose your own setting); no idealized Bayesian agents, no exotic physics, no perfect record-keeping or telepathic access to each other's reasoning. And the case must not cheat: neither party may lie, make an arithmetic error, ignore evidence, or fail to understand what the other is saying. The disagreement has to survive the fact that each of them knows the other is smart, honest, and well-informed.

After the scenario, show the machinery. Lay out the assumptions the theorem needs, identify which one (or ones) your case breaks, and demonstrate that the broken assumption is what generates the persistent disagreement — show that if you repaired exactly that assumption while holding everything else about the case fixed, the two would converge. Then explain how my classmate would push back on the case, and whether his pushback has any force.

Finally, and I want a committed answer here, not a survey: in the real political disagreements my classmate actually has in mind — not the stylized ones, the ones between informed people who genuinely respect each other — which assumption of the theorem do you think fails in practice, and is his argument salvageable in some weakened form, or does it collapse entirely once the case is on the table? Take a definite position and defend it; don't lay out the options and leave the choosing to me.
