For the past year, two members of my reading group have been arguing about whether the world is becoming less violent, and they have settled into positions I'd describe as follows. The declinist holds that per-capita death rates from war, homicide, and atrocity have fallen enormously across the sweep of human history — from the raiding mortality of pre-state societies, through medieval European homicide rates that look almost unimaginable today, to the growing rarity of direct great-power war — and that this decline is among the most morally significant facts we know. The skeptic holds that the decline is largely an artifact of what gets counted: that normalizing per capita flatters a species whose absolute toll and destructive capacity keep growing, that structural and institutional harms (sanctions regimes, famine policy, mass incarceration, the violence of economic arrangement) are excluded by fiat precisely so the trend comes out pointing downward, and that the question "is violence declining?" is not one question until "violence" is defined — a definitional act that is itself normative rather than neutral. To keep the fight off the arithmetic, both parties stipulate that the standard empirical estimates in this literature are roughly right; their dispute is about what the numbers are numbers of, and which numbers matter. They have finally agreed to try a structured adversarial collaboration rather than another free-for-all, and they've asked me to draft the joint statement they should be able to sign.

Draft that document. I want a numbered section of agreed claims — including things like which empirical regularities both accept, and any concessions each side would honestly make about the weaknesses of its own framing. Then a disagreements table, with each residual disagreement stated in wording its own side would accept for its own view, plus one sentence per side on why it holds that view. Then the cruxes, and here I want you to take a stance on which sub-questions are the real load-bearing ones. I suspect at least three are in play, but you should decide: whether the per-capita versus absolute-numbers issue is a substantive moral disagreement or a rhetorical one; whether there is a reasonable core concept of violence that a fair-minded person could defend as the right one, or whether definitional choice runs all the way down with no neutral arbiter; and whether the long-run trend would survive definitions chosen by the other side — whether a hostile-but-fair definition flips the sign, makes it indeterminate, or leaves it intact. For each crux you identify, specify the concrete analysis, case, or thought experiment both parties would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance, before the analysis is described. For example, if the crux is what happens when deaths from institutional cruelty and preventable famine are counted as violence, record now what the declinist predicts about the direction of the twentieth-century comparison under that definition and what the skeptic predicts.

I'd like a committed piece of drafting, not a diplomatic one. Make definite choices about which disagreements are genuine and which were two words for one thing; if some portion of the dispute turns out to be verbal, say which portion and why, rather than hinting that maybe some of it is semantic. Don't manufacture agreement with claims so watered down that either party would sign them without noticing what they say, and don't soften the disagreements table into a hedge. Close with a short section taking a definite position on what would actually move this debate and on which parts of it are not empirically tractable at all — if you think, for instance, that no possible analysis could move either side on the fact-of-the-matter question, say that and argue it, rather than leaving it as one option among several.
