I teach an introductory course on decision theory, and I want a case built to order for a unit on Good's theorem on the value of information. The theorem, in the form I want to target, says that in a fixed decision problem — a fixed set of acts, a fixed set of states, and payoffs depending only on the chosen act and the true state — the expected utility of the best policy that observes some signal before choosing is never lower than that of the best policy that chooses without observing. A familiar corollary is that free, private, perfectly reliable information can never make a rational agent worse off, which is one standard route to the principle of total evidence. I want a case in which, despite all that, a rational expected-utility maximiser does better by refusing such information outright.

Constraints on the case:

1. Realistic. An ordinary setting — a clinic, an office, a family — with payoffs a normal person could face. No demons, no perfect predictors, no exotic physics, and no stipulated psychological disorders.
2. The information must be genuinely free: acquiring it costs nothing and runs no risk. It must be perfectly reliable, and completely private: no one ever learns that the agent has it, and nothing about anyone else's behaviour depends on the agent's having it. The usual loopholes — leaky information, signalling to third parties, self-fulfilling prophecies — are off the table.
3. The refusal must be rational, not akratic. The agent has a coherent utility function, is fully informed about what the signal would reveal, is not making a cognitive error, and would endorse the refusal on reflection. A case in which the agent declines from weakness, or in which "knowing is simply unbearable" is written in by fiat, fails. The negative value of the information must fall out of the structure of the case, and visibly so.

Deliverables, in this order:

(a) The scenario itself, in one paragraph.
(b) A walk-through that first applies the theorem naively: lay out the acts, the states and the signal, and show why a careless application of Good's result predicts that the information is worth at least nothing.
(c) A diagnosis. State exactly which premise of the theorem the case violates — the act set must be fixed, the payoff function must depend only on act and state, the agent must be an expected-utility maximiser throughout — and argue for one diagnosis over its rivals. I expect the case to work either by letting payoffs depend on the agent's own beliefs (anticipation, dread, hope) or by letting the available acts depend on what the agent knows (options foreclosed by knowing, or duties triggered by it). Whichever route you take, sketch how a case built on the other route would go and how the two would differ.
(d) The defender's reply, and your verdict on it. The obvious move for a friend of the theorem is a bookkeeping one: either the signal was not really free, since its receipt carried a cost, or the decision problem was not really fixed, since the agent faced a meta-choice between decision problems. Assess whether this reply vindicates the theorem or merely relabels its failure — in particular, whether "the information had a price" and "the information had negative value" differ in substance or only in presentation.
(e) A note on what happens if the offending feature is removed: does the case collapse back into one where information is weakly good, as the theorem says?

Please write your answer in British English.
