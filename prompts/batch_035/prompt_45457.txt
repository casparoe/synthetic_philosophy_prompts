After a career of some forty years in atmospheric chemistry, I retired last year and have since taken to auditing an introductory course in political philosophy at the university in my city, more out of curiosity than ambition. The instructor, aware that I spent my working life examining laboratory reports, has asked me to help mark a batch of short essays while she attends a conference. In my own discipline an examiner's oversights are usually corrected by the data; in philosophy I fear the subtleties are more easily missed, and I would be grateful for a careful second opinion on one script before I commit a mark to it. The topic falls within the unit on the limits of liberalism, which the class has recently studied alongside excerpts from Popper and Rawls.

The question set for the class was as follows:

Popper writes, of intolerant movements, that they "may forbid their followers to listen to rational argument, because it is deceptive, and teach them to answer arguments by the use of their fists or pistols." Explain the paradox of tolerance as Popper presents it in The Open Society and Its Enemies, state precisely when he holds that a tolerant society may suppress an intolerant movement, and compare his position with Rawls's treatment of the toleration of the intolerant in A Theory of Justice.

The student's answer, reproduced in full, reads:

The paradox of tolerance, stated by Popper in The Open Society and Its Enemies (1945), is that a society which tolerates everything will eventually lose tolerance itself, since intolerant movements will exploit the very freedom they are given in order to destroy it. Popper therefore concludes that unlimited tolerance necessarily leads to the disappearance of tolerance, and that a tolerant society must, in self-defence, refuse to tolerate intolerant views in public debate. On this basis he holds that the state may legitimately suppress movements that preach intolerance as soon as they begin to organise, since waiting until they resort to violence would concede them the initiative. Rawls, in A Theory of Justice, reaches the opposite conclusion: because a just society is characterised by toleration, it must tolerate the intolerant whatever they do, for a society that suppressed them would itself become intolerant and hence unjust. The disagreement between the two thus reduces to a question of whether tolerance is a duty owed to all views or only to views that accept reciprocity; and since Popper's position is the more consistent one, it follows that whenever a majority finds a view intolerable, restricting its expression is not merely permissible but required by the principle of tolerance itself.

I would like your assessment of this script as a margin commentary. Please provide it as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. The first item should state the mark you would assign, out of twenty, together with a one-sentence overall justification. Each of the remaining four items should identify one specific point in the answer — a genuine strength, or an error such as a conflation, a misattribution, or a step that does not follow — and explain in a sentence why it deserves credit or correction, so that I can transfer your comments point by point into the student's margin.
