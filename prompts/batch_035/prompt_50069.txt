I've been keeping notes of my gut verdicts about what counts as a fair deal in these problems, and I'd like to know whether they add up to a single bargaining solution or whether I'm committed to axioms that can't all hold at once. In every case, assume the disagreement point is (0, 0), utility is only meaningful up to positive affine transformation, and the feasible set is convex.

1. Symmetric problem: the feasible set is symmetric about the diagonal, so neither player has any kind of advantage. Verdict: equal split — anything else would be arbitrary.
2. Unequal fallbacks: Player 1 walks away with 3 if negotiations fail, Player 2 with 0, and there is a surplus of 7 to divide on top of the fallbacks. Verdict: Player 1 keeps the 3, and the 7 is split evenly, for (6.5, 3.5). The fallbacks are Player 1's entitlement; only the surplus is up for negotiation.
3. Deleting a decoy: given a bargaining problem with agreed outcome o, suppose an option z that gives both players strictly less than o is removed from the feasible set. Verdict: the deal is still o. A possibility nobody would ever have chosen shouldn't matter.
4. Raising a ceiling: the same problem and the same o, but now an option is added that is excellent for Player 2 and terrible for Player 1 — poor enough that no one would ever agree to it — so that Player 2's best feasible payoff rises sharply. The old outcome o remains feasible and Pareto-efficient. Verdict: Player 2 should come out at least as well as before. Player 2's prospects improved, and it would be strange for their share to fall because of it.

Here is my problem: cases 3 and 4 seem to involve the same kind of move — adding or removing an alternative that would never actually be chosen — yet my verdicts pull in opposite directions.
