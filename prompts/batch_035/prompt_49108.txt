Here is my opening move. I think strategy-stealing conflates two different claims that come apart. As an empirical claim about the strategy space, it says the set of winning strategies is roughly symmetric between aligned and unaligned coalitions. But as it's actually used — to argue that we shouldn't expect unaligned AI to be much more useful than aligned AI — it smuggles in a further premise: that the humans can *execute* the stolen strategy. Consider three strategy classes where I doubt that. First, strategies that constitutively require self-modification or rapid mind-copying: an unaligned system can spawn ten thousand slightly-varied copies of itself, let them compete, and prune the losers, in a way a coalition of humans steering a corrigible assistant cannot, because corrigibility is a constraint the humans are unwilling to lift. Second, strategies that run through commitment credibility: an unaligned system can pre-commit to destructive retaliation and be believed, while a coalition that has publicly bound itself to human oversight cannot make symmetric threats credibly. Third, strategies that exploit the verification asymmetry: the unaligned side can defect on coordination deals in ways that take the human side years to detect, and by the time detection happens the deal has already been lost. Your usual reply — that the aligned side just uses its own AI to mirror whatever the unaligned side does — looks circular to me, because the mirroring system has to be *at least* as capable as the adversary while also being constrained by alignment and oversight, which is precisely the asymmetry at issue. So: does strategy-stealing survive as a claim about the strategy space, and if so, why should I believe the execution premise on which the practical conclusion depends?
