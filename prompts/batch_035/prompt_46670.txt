I've just bought my first apartment and am furnishing it on a tight budget, which means buying prints online on the strength of gallery descriptions, critic blurbs, and customer reviews, mostly without seeing anything in person. Working out my actual policy from my gut verdicts, I get the following. Buying a print sight unseen because a critic praised it: fine. Choosing a wine by expert scores and assuring my dinner guests it's excellent, though I couldn't reliably taste the difference myself: fine, and I've done it. Telling my sister that the print I've hung is beautiful purely on the curator's word, before I've lived with it: not fine — I hedge to "the gallery says it's beautiful." Delegating the whole selection to a friend with better taste: fine. Repeating a sommelier's tasting notes at dinner as though they were my own: I've done it, and it felt slightly off, but far less off than the print case, though I can't say what differs. And when a trusted friend called a film I'd disliked brilliant, going back and finding it brilliant: fine — her word changed what I saw, not just what I bought. The wine and print cases look structurally identical — asserting an expert's verdict in a domain where I lack competence — yet my verdicts on them are opposite. So either some principle splits them on a ground I haven't named, or one of those verdicts has to go, and I'd much rather surrender the sommelier one. Please state the principle, or a small set of candidates, precisely; say which case motivates each clause; give its verdicts on cases I haven't listed, including whether I may tell guests the dessert is delicious on the pastry chef's say-so when I'm too full to taste it; and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot save.
