I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of science — twelve students, one 90-minute session — on the epistemology of scientific thought experiments. We've already covered the standard positions at a reading level (the "argument view" that a thought experiment is just a disguised argument, the Platonic intuitionist take, and the empiricist reconstruction on which thought experiments smuggle in previously acquired empirical information). What I want for this session is not another round of text analysis but a structured activity in which the students run into the puzzle themselves before I name it: the felt sense that Galileo's falling-bodies scenario, or Einstein's chasing-a-light-beam, delivers knowledge about the world, together with the embarrassment that no new observation was made. Please design the activity so the problem surfaces from the rules rather than from my commentary.

Here is the shape I have in mind, which you should refine, extend, or correct where it doesn't work: students are split into small groups, each group receives two imagined scenarios that superficially resemble each other as pieces of armchair reasoning but differ in some hidden feature — one is a genuine thought experiment with internal dynamics (the reader is invited to watch what happens, as with chained or falling bodies) and one is a static intuition pump that simply asks you to consult your gut. I'm imagining something like Galileo's falling bodies paired against a made-up contrast case of similar rhetorical form. Each group must first privately commit, in writing, to the outcome each scenario predicts, then deliberate to a verdict, and finally face a "cross-examination" round in which a rival group may challenge any step by demanding the group say whether the step was logic, remembered experience, or imagination-as-observation — a three-way sorting the groups will discover is unstable, since some steps refuse to fit any box cleanly. A tallying mechanism should make visible how often the private predictions diverge from each other, which sets up the question of how shared these "experiments" really are.

For the deliverable, please give me the full setup: materials, group sizes, an exact timeline for the 90 minutes, the two scenarios written out or sketched in enough detail to use verbatim, the rules for the cross-examination round, and a debrief script that maps specific moments in the activity onto the three theoretical positions from the readings. Also include (a) two or three things that will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — e.g., students treating the three-way sorting as obvious for cases where it shouldn't be, or the private-prediction step feeling artificial — with concrete fixes, and (b) an honest note on which part of the epistemology of thought experiments this activity simply cannot reproduce and I will have to supply in lecture, such as the way a thought experiment can fail empirically even when every step seemed sound.
