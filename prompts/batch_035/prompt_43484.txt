I am a high school student writing a term paper for my philosophy elective defending the view that death is bad for the person who dies. Before I hand it in, I want to test the argument against the strongest opponent I can find rather than a strawman. Please play a committed Epicurean: fully in command of the classical line — that when death is present, we are not, that there is no surviving subject for anything to be bad for, and that the eternity before our birth mirrors the eternity after it — willing to concede small points but never the main one, and staying in character until I say "stop." Keep each reply to a single paragraph so that this reads as a conversation rather than a lecture.

Here is my opening move. I am prepared to grant that nothing is bad for me at any moment while I am dead, and still to maintain that my death is bad for me, because I deny that every harm must be located at a moment in the sufferer's life. Suppose that my closest friends have secretly despised me for years and mocked me whenever I leave the room, and suppose I never find out. Not a single moment of my life contains any pain; my subjective experience is indistinguishable from that of someone whose friends are loyal. Yet the first life seems clearly worse for me than the second — bad for me, not merely unfortunate in some impersonal way. If that is right, then the premise doing all the work in your school's argument, namely that nothing can be bad for a person unless it is bad for them at some time, is refuted by a single case, and once it falls, my death looks bad in exactly the same way: the decades of goods my life would otherwise have contained are gone, and it is not obvious that I must exist at the moment they are withheld in order for the loss to be mine rather than no one's.

One more thing before you answer. Please do not reply that a night of dreamless sleep is equally nothing to us, so eternity without experience cannot be bad either; I have anticipated that. In dreamless sleep there is still someone asleep, someone who will wake, whereas in death there is no one at all — and if you think that difference makes your case easier, I want to hear exactly how, because it strikes me that it does the opposite. Your turn.
