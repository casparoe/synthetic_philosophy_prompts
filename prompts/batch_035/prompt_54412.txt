I write about science and ideas for a broadsheet, and a beat that keeps forcing me back into a forty-year-old argument that I don't believe ever properly ended: whether artefacts have politics. Langdon Winner argued in the early 1980s that some technical arrangements — the Robert Moses overpasses, nuclear power plants and their surrounding political economy — embody or strongly favour particular social orders, so that a technology can settle a community's affairs without anyone voting on them. The social constructivists, Pinch and Bijker foremost, replied that whatever politics a thing has is poured into it by relevant social groups during design and interpretation, and Winner retorted that when you open constructivism's black box you find it empty, since it seems unable to say that any arrangement is more compatible with hierarchy than with democracy. Since then, the field has settled into what looks less like a resolution than a truce: nearly everyone now says technology and society mutually shape each other, and I have never seen anyone say what observation would count against that. My working hypothesis, developed from covering AI policy — where every causal question runs through this argument, e.g. whether recommender systems polarise societies or merely reflect the demand of already-polarised ones — is that the two camps mostly agree on the causal stories and actually disagree about whether politics names something that can inhere in an object at all, so that the stall is a standoff over essentialism rather than over facts. Please test that hypothesis rather than assuming it: is the disagreement substantive, verbal, or two disciplines answering different questions under one heading, and does the contested factual status of the Moses story itself tell us anything about the debate's structure? Then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress here — a distinction, a method, a type of case study — or whether the mutual-shaping settlement is genuinely all this debate could ever have delivered, and why. Please answer in British English.
