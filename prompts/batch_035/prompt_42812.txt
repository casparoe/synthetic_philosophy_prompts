My study group has an argument that I think proves way too much, but I can't put my finger on the bad step. We were discussing choices people call hard — like moving abroad for the career you trained years for versus staying close to your aging parents — and the argument is supposed to show that the "hard" part is an illusion:

(1) If two options are comparable, then exactly one of these holds: one is better than the other, or they are equally good. (Comparability isn't the issue here — both options clearly matter to how her life goes.)
(2) In our case, neither option is better. So by (1) they are equally good.
(3) But if they're equally good, every reason for one is matched by an equally weighty reason for the other, so deliberation between them can't turn up anything that favors either — agonizing over it is like agonizing over which of two identical raffle tickets to take.
(4) And if one option were better, deliberation would be nothing more than finding that out.
(5) So either way, thinking hard about a choice is either unfinished ranking or theater. When my sister spent weeks on a choice like this, she was either doing a computation she could've done quicker, or performing.

I can't accept (5) — it really didn't look like ranking-detection, and I refuse to beleive those weeks did nothing. But I also balk at giving up (1), because the alternative seems to be inventing a fourth relation besides better, worse, and equal, and people I've heard gesture at options being "on a par" never explain how that's more than "equal" with extra steps — a way of sounding deep about a tie. So: which step should I reject, why did it look fine, and what do I pay for rejecting it? And if it's coherent for two things to be equally good without the reasons being point-for-point symmetric, spell out how, because right now that sounds like a distinction without a difference to me.
