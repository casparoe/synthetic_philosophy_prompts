I'm part of a small decision theory reading group, and another member and I have been arguing about sunk costs for what must be over a year now, at this point mostly past each other. We finally decided to try the adversarial collaboration route: a fixed number of sessions, steelman each other, and at the end produce a joint statement we can both actually sign. I'm writing to ask you to draft that statement for us — obviously we'll revise, but we want a strong first pass to argue over.

Here's roughly where we each stand. She's the hardliner: only consequences-from-now-on can justify a choice, sunk costs are rationally inert by definition, and whenever honoring sunk costs *looks* rational (staying in a PhD program because you're four years in, continuing a losing war because soldiers already died, finishing a bad movie) there's some disguised forward-looking reason doing the real work — information about project quality, reputational payoffs, option value, whatever. I'm the revisionist: I think there are residual cases where sunk-cost sensitivity is rational even after you condition on all her forward-looking factors, for two reasons. First, commitment: following through on projects at a loss is how you make your future threats, promises, and projects credible — to counterparties and maybe to yourself — and that credibility can't be had for free, someone who only ever follows through when it's ex ante profitable never builds it. Second, and she hates this one, the narrative thing: a person who abandons projects whenever the ledger says so has projects in a thinner sense than someone who sticks, and I think the ability to be a long-horizon agent is itself something rationality can require, not just a taste. A case we keep coming back to: a CEO who greenlit a now-failing project, and the question of whether continuing it partly because abandoning it would signal her earlier call was wrong counts as a legitimate reason or just an error dressed up as one. She says the moment you itemize reputation and signaling as consequences, I've conceded everything and we're just labeling differently; I say the disagreement about whether decision procedures can be assessed separately from single decisions is still live there, not verbal.

For the document itself: a numbered section of claims we can both sign (including the boring operational ones — what counts as a sunk cost, when sunk-cost sensitivity is pathological, which famous cases we agree the fallacy label fits); then a disagreements section, with each disagreement worded so that each of us would accept the phrasing as a statement of our own view — that's where we always fail when we do it ourselves; then the cruxes, and for each crux a concrete case, thought experiment, or piece of evidence we both agree would bear on it, with both our predictions written down in advance. Please also flag explicitly which of our disagreements you think are genuinely substantive versus which are us using "rational" in two different senses — my own suspicion is the commitment dispute is substantive and the narrative-identity dispute is at least partly verbal, but I'd want her to be able to live with that diagnosis too, so be careful with how you put it. Format it as the numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table with columns for the crux, her prediction, mine, and what would settle it. And do flag the one place where you think *I'm* the one being sloppy — she'll trust the doc more if it doesn't read like my side won.
