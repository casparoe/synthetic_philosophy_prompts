Two members of my philosophy of mind reading group got into an extended disagreement about whether the Chinese Room argument applies to today's large language models, and since I was the one chairing, they've asked me to referee. I'll recount the exchange as it unfolded.

(1) Dev opened by saying that the Chinese Room settles the question: a man in a room shuffling symbols according to formal rules can produce fluent Chinese output without understanding a word, and that is all an LLM ever does — manipulate symbols — so however fluent the outputs, there is no understanding in the machine.

(2) Maya replied that the argument was aimed at a very different kind of system. Searle's man follows an explicit rulebook, so his only contact with Chinese is through formal rules. An LLM's competence is not written into it as rules; it was acquired by training, a causal process that forced the model into alignment with patterns found in an enormous corpus of world-involving text. The thought experiment describes a system whose relation to meaning is exhausted by syntax and then discovers that this system has no semantics — which begs the question against systems whose relation to meaning runs through their causal history.

(3) Dev answered that causal history cannot matter. Imagine two machines with an identical input-output function: one assembled by hand-coded rules, the other shaped by training. If we say that only the second understands, we have made understanding depend on the manufacturer's process rather than the machine — as odd as saying a calculator adds only if it was trained rather than wired. Either the function is enough for understanding or it is not.

(4) Maya said that whether history can matter is exactly what is in dispute, so Dev cannot simply assume that understanding supervenes only on the current input-output function. Some properties are historical by nature: a piece of paper is money partly in virtue of its institutional history, and a perfect duplicate produced later by other means does not automatically count as currency. Meaning could be like that.

(5) Dev pushed back that this makes understanding extrinsic and observer-relative — precisely the kind of attribution Searle wants to rule out when he denies that a thermostat really understands temperature. If everything hinges on how outsiders choose to describe the system, the claim that the system understands becomes empty.

(6) Maya closed by arguing that Searle is in no position to insist that only the intrinsic properties of the running process matter, since his own positive view is that understanding depends on the specific causal powers of brain tissue — a fact about physical makeup rather than abstract function. If he can say that neuronal causal powers make the difference, she can say that the causal structure acquired in training makes the difference. And the thought experiment cannot adjudicate between these positions: run against a rulebook, it persuades; extended to cover training — imagine the man spending decades inside the room nudging billions of weights by hand until the room's outputs become fluent — the intuition that the man himself lacks understanding no longer tells us anything about whether the whole system does.

They ended stuck on whether Dev's move (3) or Maya's move (6) was doing the real work, and I honestly could not decide. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the six moves, say whether it succeeds, partly succeeds, or fails, and justify the rating. Where a move turns on a substantive philosophical principle rather than a fact about LLMs or about Searle, identify the principle explicitly. Then say who, if either, is right overall, and what, if anything, both parties are missing. I'd especially like you to address whether the training-time versus runtime distinction and the history-versus-function dispute genuinely change how the Chinese Room argument works, or merely restate the old dialectic in new vocabulary.
