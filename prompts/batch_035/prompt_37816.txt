In most liberal legal systems, the right to organize is affirmed — in constitutions, labor statutes, and international instruments — as a core freedom, and the strike is treated as its paradigm exercise. Yet the same systems draw a sharp boundary at the gate of the workplace. Workers may withhold their labor against their own employer, but secondary action — refusing to handle goods, picketing, or striking in order to pressure a third party, such as a supplier or customer of the struck firm — is banned or tightly restricted in many jurisdictions (the United States is the clearest case; Britain prohibited it outright for decades; some continental systems allow it only under conditions). Meanwhile, the same legal orders permit consumers to organize boycotts of precisely those third parties, and permit firms to refuse dealings with a supplier that also serves a competitor, without anyone calling this coercion. What theory of the right to strike does this settlement presuppose? Is the right being treated as an individual liberty exercised collectively, in which case it is puzzling that it ends at the employer's property line, or as an instrument of bargaining within a bilateral dispute, in which case the restriction tracks something else — leverage, third-party harm, proportionality? Does the asymmetry quietly presuppose that the wrong a strike redresses is bilateral, a dispute between one employer and its own employees, rather than structural — a presupposition that would explain why solidarity across workplaces gets recast as interference in someone else's bargain? Does the prohibition of secondary action, in particular, presuppose a distinctive view of coercion: that withholding one's own labor vis-à-vis one's own employer is a protected liberty, while the identical withholding, organized so as to affect a stranger's contract, becomes coercive? And is that view consistent with the same systems' tolerance of consumer boycotts and of employer lockouts that inflict hardship on parties to no dispute at all? Please reconstruct the implicit theory of the right to organize that this body of law rests on, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with — saying where the law is genuinely underdetermined, such that several theories of the strike would license the same rules — and tell me what would have to change in doctrine if the presupposed view were replaced by one on which the strike is a political act in a class-wide contest rather than an economic act inside a single employment relationship.
