My department has for some years run a team-taught module on liberty, and my co-teacher — a political theorist with the most consistent judgment on hypotheticals I have ever taught alongside — has accumulated a decade of verdicts on whether particular people are free. Her rulings to date: a serving prisoner, not free; the subject of a tolerant absolute monarch, who may imprison anyone at pleasure but has imprisoned no one and censors nothing, not free; an at-will employee whose considerate employer could dismiss her without cause tomorrow, and who measures her words in staff meetings accordingly, not free; a citizen bound by a law she campaigned against, which passed by fair vote and which she can contest in an independent court, free; a compulsive gambler who wants to stop and cannot — a case she pronounced the easiest of the lot — free; and a hermit alone on an uninhabited island, free.

As best I can reconstruct her implicit criterion, Berlin's negative liberty is dead by the second and third rulings (no one actually interferes with the monarch's subject or the employee), self-mastery is dead by the gambler (unmastered but subject to no one), and consent is dead by the employee (she signed the contract). What survives is either non-domination — no one may hold arbitrary power over you, whether or not they exercise it — or collective self-rule — you are free insofar as you have a genuine share in the making of whatever rules govern you — and, rather inconveniently for me, the entire record is consistent with both.

Next term we take hypotheticals from the students again, and I have prepared three: (i) a citizen of a state administered by an incorruptible civil service — or, if you prefer the science-fictional version, a well-designed machine administration — whose rules are public, prospective, evenly enforced, and overturnable through a working procedure of challenge, but where there are no elections and no citizen ever holds office; (ii) a woman in a household where her husband's word is legally final and unreviewable, though she votes, is eligible for parliament, sought the arrangement, defends it, and would grieve its end; (iii) a citizen of an electoral democracy in which one party has won every election for forty years under formally impeccable rules and redraws the boundaries whenever they grow inconvenient, so that her vote is cast, counted, and never decisive.

Predict her verdicts on the three; tell me which criterion she is implicitly running — non-domination, collective self-rule, or some third reading that fits the record as well — and defend your best guess, including what each surviving criterion predicts and where they diverge. Then tell me which further hypothetical would most cleanly separate the candidates, and whether she can rule on case (ii) without coming to regret her ruling on the at-will employee. For what it is worth, she is fond of remarking that the question to ask of any arrangement is not whether anyone is being hurt, but whether anyone could hurt anyone with impunity.
