You are a philosopher of history with a strong background in archaeology. Think carefully through the material below, and consider more than one interpretation of the track record before committing to a prediction.

My thesis advisor is fussy about the word "collapse." He thinks it is applied sloppily to almost any dramatic change, and he seems to apply a fairly consistent implicit criterion for when it is genuinely warranted. His verdicts so far:

- The Western Roman Empire in the fifth century: collapse.
- The Late Bronze Age palatial world around 1200 BCE: collapse — even though he concedes that in many regions rural life went on and recognizably new societies emerged within a few centuries.
- The southern Maya lowlands in the Terminal Classic: collapse — even though much of the population seems to have moved north rather than died out.
- Easter Island after the loss of the palm forest: collapse — even though the population survived, the chiefs kept some authority, and society reorganized around the birdman cult.
- The Black Death in Europe: not a collapse — despite killing somewhere between a third and a half of the population, because institutions, economic life, and culture were continuous and recovered within a few generations.
- The Meiji Restoration: not a collapse — even though the shogunate, the samurai class, and the whole feudal order were abolished within roughly a decade, because Japanese society was deliberately transformed rather than broken.

Next week's seminar case is the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991, and he says he is genuinely torn. On the one hand, the union, the party, and the planned economy ended within about two years, and the 1990s brought a severe economic and demographic crisis — which resembles his Rome verdict. On the other hand, the change was largely negotiated from above, and the successor societies remained urban, literate, and complex — which resembles his Meiji verdict.

Please do the following: (1) identify at least two candidate rules that each fit all six of his verdicts, making explicit where each rule is strained and what supplementary assumption would repair the strain; (2) say what each rule predicts for the Soviet case; (3) give your best guess at his verdict, with your reasons; and (4) name a further case, of a kind he has not yet ruled on, that would best distinguish the candidate rules — and say what each rule would predict about it. I care less about the verdict on the Soviet Union itself than about whether you can reconstruct the criterion he is implicitly running, and about whether his six verdicts actually force a single criterion or leave him free to decide the new case either way.
