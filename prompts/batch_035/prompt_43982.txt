I am a novelist in the middle of a book about an amateur theatrical society in a provincial town, centered on a former actor who has performed sincerity so competently and for so long that he can no longer find the original underneath the performance. To see what my material is really about, I have gone back to Hamlet with Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert in my other hand, and I would like a serious reading of the play through Rousseau's case against the theater — one that is tough on both of them.

The Rousseau I am working with is the one who answered d'Alembert's proposal to establish a theater in Geneva by arguing that the actor's trade is to seem what one is not, and that a society which honors that trade trains itself in the general art of seeming. Theater, on his account, manufactures polished emotion at no cost to the spectator: we weep generously for the misfortunes of fictional characters — precisely because we owe them nothing — and leave the playhouse with our actual cruelties intact, our sentiment discharged rather than exercised. His most unnerving exhibit is Molière's Misanthrope, in which the one sincere man is made ridiculous before an audience trained to laugh at him, so that the play which appears to mock hypocrisy ends by teaching the spectator that hypocrisy is the price of getting through the world. His substitute for the theater is the open-air festival: no stage, no actors, the citizens themselves as the spectacle, each seen by all and seeing all.

Read against this, Hamlet looks like Rousseau's exhibit A composed a century and a half before Rousseau. Consider the court: Claudius performs kingship and bereavement within a single speech, Polonius performs wisdom, Rosencrantz and Guildenstern perform friendship, and Ophelia is directed in how to feel. Hamlet is the one character who declares war on seeming — his first long speech insists that the inky cloak and the tears are "actions that a man might play" and that he, by contrast, "knows not 'seems.'" Yet within an hour of the Ghost's revelation he resolves to play mad as strategic cover, and his single decisive instrument against Claudius is not testimony, evidence, or confrontation but a play: he cannot reach the truth of the court except by staging the murder and watching the king watch his own crime. In one direction, this does exactly what Rousseau denied theater could do — it produces knowledge, and action follows; the performance works. In the other direction, everything Hamlet does he does through theatrical detours, and the delay that ruins him is arguably Rousseau's thesis dramatized: the man of the theater can only ever act by proxy, substituting representation for the act until the act is finally forced on him by someone else's plot.

So I want to know where the play finally stands. Is the advice to the players — that the purpose of playing is "to hold, as 'twere, the mirror up to nature" — Shakespeare's direct counter-theory to Rousseau, drama as a truth-detector rather than a dispenser of sterile feeling? Is Horatio, praised as the man who is "not passion's slave," Rousseau's natural sincerity smuggled into Elsinore, and is it significant that he is the one principal the plot leaves intact? And what of the ending, where Hamlet dies as the director of his own final scene, principally anxious that Horatio survive to tell the story right — theatrical self-consciousness preserved at the last breath, the audience's verdict substituted for the deed's?

Be honest with me where the reading strains. I can see that the play gives theater real power — the murder-play works, and the Ghost's word is confirmed — which cuts against Rousseau; and I can see that it charges theater with nearly everything that goes wrong, which cuts for him. Does Shakespeare emerge as Rousseau's answer, or as his precursor? And for my novel, tell me which is the deeper fact about a man who has performed sincerity for too long: that the performance has eaten the original, as Rousseau would have it, or that a performance can still, once, catch a conscience?
