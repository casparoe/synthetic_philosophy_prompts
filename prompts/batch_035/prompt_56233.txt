I work in a university technology-transfer office, so I spend my days on the receiving end of patent doctrine, and two things I have noticed about it seem to presuppose opposite theories of what an invention is. I would like help reconstructing what the system actually believes.

The first thing: when an application is rejected, or an issued patent challenged, as obvious under 35 U.S.C. §103, the touchstone is what a hypothetical person having ordinary skill in the art would have come up with. And one recurring move — I have seen it in examiner office actions, and the Supreme Court gestured in this direction in KSR v. Teleflex (2007) — is to point to the fact that several independent teams were converging on the same thing at the same time, with near-simultaneous filings and prior art that reads almost identically, as evidence that the invention was in the air: if ordinary practitioners were all about to arrive at it, it was not a creative leap, and it does not deserve a twenty-year monopoly. The doctrine has a built-in rationale of this shape, the inducement theory: if people in the field would have developed it anyway, without the lure of a patent, the patent adds nothing, so the invention fails as obvious. The second thing: the same system decides who owns that invention by a race. Since the America Invents Act of 2011, the United States awards the patent to the first inventor to file — a competitor's application filed even days earlier, and never published, becomes prior art that blocks ours — and the only escape is a derivation proceeding, which requires showing that the earlier filer took the idea from us. Before 2011, interference proceedings awarded the patent to whoever could prove earlier conception plus continuing diligence. Under both regimes, being the one who made the invention matters enormously, down to the date, even where everyone agrees the two teams invented independently.

So here is my puzzle. The first doctrine treats multiple independent invention as evidence that no individual deserves credit — the discovery was, in effect, inevitable once the groundwork was laid — while the second treats priority among those same independent inventors as the very basis of ownership. What theory of invention does the system presuppose? Is it a prepared-environment view, on which discoveries are the inevitable product of accumulated prior art, market pressure, and a pool of trained practitioners, so that the individual is interchangeable and credit is closer to a lottery, with the priority rules just an administratively convenient way of picking one interchangeable candidate? Or a heroic-individualist view, on which the inventive act is personal and non-duplicative, so that first-to-file is a crude proxy for first-to-invent? Or can a single coherent theory license both — for instance the inducement theory, under which patents are not rewards for past creativity but levers for future disclosure, and under which it is perfectly consistent to deny a patent for the inevitable while still racing the applicants? If you think the inducement theory dissolves the tension, say so, but then explain the nonobviousness bar being indexed to what a person of ordinary skill would achieve, since that looks like a threshold of individual achievement smuggled back in. I would also like to know where the practice is genuinely underdetermined — whether several theories of invention would equally well license the current rules — and what would have to change if we took one view seriously rather than the other. If the inevitabilist view were made explicit, would we get something like patents as prizes to whichever team the system needs to trigger disclosure, and no patent at all when the field was going to get there anyway (which is what §103 already approximates)? If the heroic view were made explicit, would we get something like the pre-2011 first-to-invent regime, with conception and diligence? As a comparison, it may help to look at how other institutions split the same difference: the Nobel Prize's rules, which cap an award at three sharers and refuse it to the dead, seem to run a heroic-individualist view with an inevitabilist admission clause.
