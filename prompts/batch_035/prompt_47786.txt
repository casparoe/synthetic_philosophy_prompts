Many legislatures can attach sunset clauses to laws: a statute or regulation expires automatically at a fixed date unless affirmatively re-authorized. Surveillance powers, tax provisions, even whole agencies get this treatment. Meanwhile the default for ordinary legislation is the opposite — permanence. A law, once passed, stays on the books indefinitely, and removing it takes an affirmative act of roughly the same difficulty as enacting it. So the system encodes two defaults at once: some laws must continuously earn their existence, while most need only never to have lost it.

What view of lawmaking does this arrangement presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory. Does the selective use of sunsets presuppose that legislators can identify which kinds of laws are dangerous — liberty-infringing, fiscally significant — and that inherited law is presumptively harmless? Chesterton's fence counsels that you shouldn't remove a fence until you know why it was put up, treating the obscurity of its purpose as a reason for caution; a sunset clause treats a justification that has gone stale and unexamined as presumptive ground for demolition. Notice, too, that this presumption is applied mainly to young fences: new statutes must re-justify themselves periodically, while the old corpus is protected precisely by the obscurity of its origins. Is the practice consistent with any Burkean respect for accumulated institutional wisdom, or does it presuppose that a law's value must be articulable on demand — that a fence whose purpose no one can now state has forfeited its claim? Finally, say what would change if the default were flipped globally, so that every law sunsets unless re-authorized: would that be rational housekeeping, or a decision procedure that quietly penalizes exactly the tacit, inarticulate benefits that made the old default tolerable?
