I've been teaching Nozick's entitlement theory and want a disciplined case analysis of the collapse of "forty acres and a mule" as a test of it. The episode: Sherman's Special Field Orders, No. 15 (January 1865) set aside confiscated coastal land in South Carolina and Georgia for settlement by freedpeople in forty-acre plots; later that same year Andrew Johnson's pardons returned most of that land to the former plantation owners, and the freed families who had settled it were dispossessed. Map the episode onto Nozick's three principles and say where each one bites. Justice in acquisition: were the plantation holdings legitimately held in the first place, given that the wealth and the cultivation behind them rested on slave labor? Justice in transfer: by the theory's own lights, was the Union confiscation an unjust taking, the freedpeople's settlement legitimate, Johnson's restoration another unjust transfer -- or is the whole episode just a sequence of state actions the theory has no way to classify, since Nozick gives us almost no criterion for when acquisition by conquest or confiscation counts as illegitimate? Justice in rectification: what exactly does rectification demand here, for the generation that was evicted, and for their descendants making reparations claims today? Then run the analysis in the other direction. Nozick's brief aside that a sufficiently long history of violations might justify redistributive, roughly patterned holdings is famous precisely because he never develops it -- and my suspicion is that in a region where essentially no holding has a clean chain of title, rectification, taken seriously, swallows the other two principles and the famous anti-patterned conclusions collapse into the very redistributive conclusions the theory was built to block. Tell me whether that suspicion is right, or whether there is a principled way to keep rectification narrow. Be honest about where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak: on the political process by which Johnson reversed the order, on the fact that the planters' titles themselves rested on earlier dispossession of indigenous nations, and on time -- whether rectification decays or survives generational change. I don't need the Reconstruction history rehearsed; I need the mapping, principle by principle, from 1865 to the present.
