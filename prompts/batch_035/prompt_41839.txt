I've been reading about Dennett's idea that the self is like a "center of narrative gravity" -- a useful fiction we posit to organize all the stuff going on in a brain and a life, same way we talk about a center of mass without thinking there's a little heavy dot sitting there. And it got me thinking: before I go read any more theories of what the self is, I want the spec first. So write me the desiderata -- what any adequate theory of the self has to get right. Things like: it has to explain why there's *something it's like* to be this organized bundle rather than nothing in particular (or else explain away that intuition), it has to say something about whether selves are discovered or constructed, it has to handle the confabulation data (split-brain patients, post-hoc rationalization, false memories) -- is a self that narrates itself badly still a self? -- and it should probably not flatly contradict what we know about how the brain parcels things up. Give the case behind each requirement, and tell me which ones you think are genuine requirements and which are just prejudices of one camp dressed up as demands (I suspect the "the self must be a unified thing" requirement is one of those). Then briefly: how does the center-of-narrative-gravity view itself do against your own list, and where do you think it cheats?

One thing I really want: please keep separate what's actually settled among people who study this from what's genuinely contested. Like, whether narrativity is a requirement of selfhood at all is a live fight (there's the whole objection from people who say they experience their lives as episodic, not as a story) -- so don't smuggle a contested view in as if it were the consensus. Flag the load-bearing assumptions as contested when they are. And if some chunk of your desiderata can't be jointly satisfied -- say, full narrative unity plus episodic self-experience plus whatever the neuroscience forces on us -- say that outright, because that's the part I most want to see.
