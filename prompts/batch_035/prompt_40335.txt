I have a soft spot for Walter Bagehot, but I've never managed to form a confident view of what he would say about the monarchy as it exists now. Please reason the question through carefully — I'd rather have a genuinely argued prediction than a quick verdict.

Bagehot's position in The English Constitution (1867) is well known: he divided the constitution into dignified and efficient parts, ranked the monarchy squarely among the dignified parts, and insisted that its usefulness depends on its remaining theatrical — a retired, hidden-away family that the public's imagination invests with mystery, since mystery feeds loyalty and, once you let plain daylight in upon the magic, the enchantment dissipates. His nightmare was precisely an accessible, chatty, publicly scrutinized royal house: he thought a domestic royal family living like other families would come to seem ordinary, and ordinary people would not reverence it. The office, on his account, works best when it does almost nothing and is seen almost never.

Now consider the actual development of the British, Scandinavian, and Japanese monarchies over the past seventy years: television cameras inside the palaces; royal weddings and funerals as mass media spectacles with audiences in the hundreds of millions; tell-all interviews and memoirs by family members themselves; the 2020 withdrawal of the Sussexes; daily tabloid coverage of royal domestic life; in Japan, an abdication and a very public debate about female succession. The royal family is now among the most exposed and discussed families on earth, precisely the opposite of the secrecy Bagehot prescribed. And yet the institution survives: the funeral of Elizabeth II was a global event, republicanism remains a minority position in Britain, and the Scandinavian monarchies sit at the top of national trust surveys. This looks on its face like a counterexample to his theory — maximal daylight upon the magic, and the loyalty did not obviously evaporate.

So my questions are these. On Bagehot's own principles, how would he diagnose the current situation? There are several routes available to him, and I'd like you to evaluate each: (1) he could bite the bullet and say the monarchy has in fact declined into mere celebrity, that what survives is not reverence but fandom, and that this is a degenerate, unstable condition which cannot last — that Bagehot is vindicated with a lag; (2) he could revise his mechanism and argue that mass media *reproduces* mystery at a new scale, that staging and pageantry are now manufactured industrially, and that the camera, far from letting in daylight, is itself the principal instrument of enchantment — the dignified part has migrated from the hidden household to the mediated spectacle; (3) he could bite a different bullet and say that a monarchy can be sustained by sheer constitutional inertia and habit rather than by reverence, in which case his theory of the dignified part needs major surgery, not just updating. Are there other diagnoses he would find more plausible? Which fits the evidence best, and which fits *his* cast of mind best — he was, remember, a journalist and an early analyst of the press and of public opinion, not a romantic traditionalist?

And then the harder part. Where does his framework fail outright? I suspect there are things going on that his dignified/efficient distinction cannot express at all: the monarchy's entanglement with national identity and the post-imperial question of what Britain is *for*; the way the royal family now functions as a canvas for arguments about race, class, and gender that are conducted *through* the institution rather than about it; the possibility that the public distinguishes between the family (fair game for gossip) and the institution (off-limits), a distinction his theory, which treats the household's privacy and the crown's mystique as one thing, has no room for. Tell me whether these are genuine residues or whether I'm wrong and he has the resources to handle them.

Finally, an epistemic request: tell me plainly where your prediction is most likely to fail, and what evidence or argument would move you to a different diagnosis. If the answer turns on a contested reading of Bagehot — for instance, on how seriously to take the secrecy mechanism versus his constitutional functionalism — flag that explicitly rather than burying it, and say which reading you are relying on and how confident you are in it. I would rather hear that the projection is underdetermined, and why, than receive a confident reconstruction that quietly substitutes your view of the monarchy for his.
