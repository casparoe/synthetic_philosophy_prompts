So I've been reading up on the frame problem lately and I can't figure out what the actual debate is about anymore. Here's my understanding, tell me where I'm going wrong. Originally this was a technical AI problem: if a robot executes an action like picking up a block, you don't want it to have to re-verify everything in the world that didn't change (the block's color, the sky being blue, etc.), so people invented frame axioms and later default logic and circumscription to handle that efficiently. Fine, and as I understand it that got more or less sorted by the 90s in the formal settings people cared about. But then philosophers like Fodor picked it up and started talking as if it's this deep problem about rationality — something about how a mind figures out which facts are relevant to a decision without checking all of them. To me that just sounds like the ordinary problem of salience/attention that psychology has studied forever. Humans and pigeons both have limited attention and they get by fine, so why would limited attention be some special indictment of classical computationalism, as Fodor seemed to think? So my actual question: is the "philosophical" frame problem just a fancy relabeling of the attention/relevance problem, or is there something genuinely new in it beyond the database-updating part? And if it really is just attention, doesn't that beg the question of why it's supposedly such a hard problem for symbol systems in particular — attention is cheap in any architecture once evolution hands you the right filters, no? One more thing: it'd be great if you could clearly seperate what's basically settled among people who actually work on this (e.g., how the narrow formal version was handled, and whether the formal and philosophical versions are even the same problem — that second one feels like something reasonable people still disagree about) from what's genuinely contested, and not pass off one side of some ongoing dispute as the consensus. I'd rather know where the live disagreements are than get a tidy story.
