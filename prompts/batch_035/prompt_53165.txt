As a novelist who writes political fiction, I find that Orwell tends to reach writers as a style guide — a short list of commandments about preferring plain words, cutting what can be cut, and shunning jargon — which my editor recites as though it settled every question of prose. I suspect this transmission misses his deeper point, which concerned the link between ready-made language and the avoidance of thought, and the political conditions under which prose decays. Please argue for the following position: on Orwell's actual view of the relation between language and politics, plainness of style is neither necessary nor sufficient for political honesty — a plainly worded lie can be more corrosive than a Latinate truth, and ornate prose can be scrupulously honest. In making the case, address what this implies for the novelist's craft, in particular whether a character who speaks entirely in stock phrases should be rendered as an object of satire or of tragedy.
