been meaning to get this sorted for ages — I read the econblogs back in the day, went through the whole socialist calculation debate rabbit hole (mises, hayek, lange, all that), and I've ended up with a pile of positions that I suspect don't all fit together. before I argue about this on a forum again and get taken apart, sanity-check me. here's roughly where I stand:

(1) Hayek's knowledge problem basically sank Lange-style central planning as of the 1930s, and the point wasn't that the planners were slow, it's that the tacit local knowledge they needed doesn't sit around waiting to be collected. it isn't there until the price system and profit incentive create it.

(2) that said, I think that was fundamentally a compute plus data legibility problem, and modern ML plausibly changes the picture. a sufficiently capable AI planning system could in principle run an economy at least as well as markets do. I'm not claiming it's around the corner, I'm claiming the a priori argument is gone.

(3) value is basically subjective — there's no fact of the matter about how good an economic outcome is that's independent of what people actually choose, and I take revealed preference pretty seriously.

(4) markets are emergent optimisation processes nobody designed, and I'm genuinely awed by how well they work without anyone understanding how. I think this is the strongest pro-market argument there is and it's not just status quo bias.

(5) but also a big chunk of what markets reward is rent extraction and Goodhart-ing on measurable proxies. GDP-style metrics track what we care about pretty badly, and the soviet problem of fake output statistics has a capitalist twin.

(6) concrete verdict: the fact that walmart and amazon internally allocate supply chains at a scale the entire soviet economy never had to cope with, and it works fine, is basically decisive for (2). planning obviously works at firm scale, so the only remaining question is whether it scales up, and the answer seems to be yes.

(7) separately, I'm in favour of subjecting economic outcomes to deliberate democratic control rather than treating whatever falls out of the market process as legitimate just because it's emergent.

my instinct is that the tension, if there is one, is between (4) and (7) — emergent order vs collective choice — and maybe between (3) and (5), since if I take revealed preference seriously then (5) starts to look like it's just me second-guessing what people actually picked. but I can't tell if those are real or if I'm pattern-matching to "markets vs democracy" as a slogan. go through the pairs, tell me which tensions are real and how severe, which ones dissolve on inspection, and what the single cheapest revision is that cleans up the most. I'd rather find out from you than in a comment thread with 400 replies. also flag anything in the list that's doing sneaky double duty — I have a feeling at least one of these commits me to more than I think it does.
