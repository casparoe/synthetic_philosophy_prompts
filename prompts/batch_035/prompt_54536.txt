I chair the Clinical Conduct Committee of a mid-sized teaching hospital, and I need a decision memo for two cases that came in six weeks apart but that I want handled in a single document, because together they force one question the committee cannot keep deferring.

The cases: Nurse A and Nurse B, in separate incidents, each administered a medication without performing the required allergy-status check. The circumstances were closely matched — same ward, the same night-shift staffing shortfall, the same kind of interruption at the medication cart, the same step skipped for the same stated reason. In Nurse A's incident, the patient happened not to be allergic to the drug; the error was caught at the next chart review and logged. In Nurse B's incident, the patient was allergic, suffered an anaphylactic reaction, and spent two nights in intensive care. Both nurses have clean prior records, and both reported their own errors unprompted.

Our sanctioning policy directs sanctions "proportionate to the outcome of the incident," which reads as requiring a significantly harsher sanction for Nurse B. Our charter separately requires consistency across "relevantly similar" incidents, and the committee is split on whether these two are relevantly similar. One member argues that the difference between the incidents is pure luck, that it fell outside anything either nurse controlled, and that letting it determine the trajectory of a career is exactly the kind of unfairness our charter is meant to prevent. Another answers that hospitals sanction conduct through its consequences as a matter of course, and that nothing unfair happens when a risked outcome actually materializes. A third worries that equal sanction would signal to patients and the public that we treat a near-miss as gravely as a serious harm, and that maintaining that trust is part of our remit, not an optional extra.

Please draft the memo I will circulate ahead of the recorded vote. It should:

1. State one clear recommendation the committee can vote on, formulated precisely enough to be actionable — I am open to equal sanctions, differentiated sanctions, or differentiated sanctions paired with a formal mechanism (for example, a published harm-tier rubric, or a mitigation letter in Nurse B's file explaining the harm-independent assessment), so specify exactly what you recommend and how it applies to each nurse.

2. Give reasons the whole committee can weigh, treating the strongest argument on the losing side honestly and at full strength rather than disposing of it with a label.

3. Address head-on whether the difference between the two incidents is best described as a difference in what the nurses did, a difference in what happened to them, or a difference in what they brought about — and state which of those the sanction should track, and why that tracking is the one the policy and the charter can jointly sustain.

4. Set out the precedent our recommendation creates for the next pair of cases, including a variant in which the harm is graver, a variant in which a third identical incident ends in no harm at all, and a variant in which the checking step was skipped not under pressure but for convenience.

5. Remain usable by colleagues who are not philosophers: at most one paragraph of explicitly philosophical framing, and no citations or reading lists.

The memo should be no longer than what the committee will actually read before a Thursday meeting.
