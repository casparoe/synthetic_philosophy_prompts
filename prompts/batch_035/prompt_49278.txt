I am the analytic lead for a state health technology assessment committee — the standing body that reviews clinical and economic evidence and recommends to the state health department which candidate interventions (new drugs, screening programs, telehealth expansions, staffing models) should be funded ahead of which others. Every six weeks we receive a batch of dossiers, each involving reallocations of care worth tens of millions of dollars across the state's population, and our recommendations are publicly defensible by design: they are regularly challenged at legislative hearings and in the press. The committee itself is eleven people — clinicians, a biostatistician, two patient representatives, a hospital administrator, a health economist, an ethicist — none of whom will ever work through a formal proof, all of whom must be able to explain to a skeptical legislator why one program was ranked above another. We have one three-hour meeting per batch. The secretary of health signs off on the final ranking but has asked that our reasons be structured rather than ad hoc.

Our chair has become interested in Harsanyi's 1955 aggregation theorem and its companion impartial-observer argument: roughly, that if a social evaluator ranks risky prospects by expected utility while satisfying a Pareto condition with respect to individuals who are themselves expected-utility maximizers, the social ranking must be a weighted sum of individual cardinal utilities; and that if the evaluator instead takes each affected person's place with equal probability behind something like a veil of ignorance, the weights come out equal. The chair wants this as the spine of an actual decision procedure, not a seminar topic. Write me that procedure.

Specifically, I need:

1. A step-by-step procedure the committee can run on a dossier within a single three-hour meeting: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points at which the procedure outputs or updates a ranking, and the points at which it must stop and flag the question as one of values for the political principals rather than one the framework can settle. As you simplify, preserve what actually does the work in Harsanyi's argument — the equal-probability construction, the expected-utility assumptions, the Pareto condition — so that committee members understand what they are committed to when they follow the procedure, and say which simplifications are safe and which quietly discard the theorem's justification. Be explicit about what the theorem does not settle: whether and how the cardinal utilities being summed are interpersonally comparable; whose utilities enter the sum (the chronically ill, future beneficiaries, taxpayers who bear the cost); and how risk attitudes should be handled — in particular, whether a person's aversion to a risky prospect is a fact about their well-being at stake or a fact about their psychology that the impartial observer should not inherit. That last question matters to us because Rawls's maximin objection to Harsanyi turns on exactly it, and my committee members reliably start talking like Rawlsians the moment a dossier contains a small probability of catastrophe.

2. Worked applications of the procedure to three dossiers we have actually faced or expect to face:
   (a) a proposal to add critical congenital heart disease screening to the standard newborn panel — a very small expected benefit per infant, spread across essentially every birth in the state;
   (b) a gene therapy for a rare, fatal pediatric neurodegenerative condition that affects roughly two dozen children statewide at a price of several million dollars per patient, alongside dozens of cheaper competing uses of the same budget;
   (c) an expanded prostate cancer screening program that would prevent some deaths over twenty years while exposing a much larger number of men to biopsy complications, overdiagnosis, and treatment side effects.
   For case (b), I will be honest that I expect the procedure to give an answer I will not like, and I want to see whether it does, and what the procedure's own escape hatches are when it does.

3. A candid section on where the procedure will misfire: the characteristic ways a committee using it in good faith reaches the wrong answer or no answer, the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and what the committee should do when two runs of the procedure on the same dossier produce different rankings.

Throughout, please write in American English.
