My cousin is doing a law & philosophy course and came back from a seminar pretty fired up, and he explained the Hart–Fuller debate to me like this: they argued about what to do with Nazi law after the war. Hart was a positivist, so he thought morality and law are totally separate questions, which meant the Nazi statutes still counted as valid law, and his position was basically that the postwar German courts were right to treat them as binding and enforce them as written. Fuller replied that this just shows positivism can't handle wicked regimes: on his natural law view, a legal system that evil stops being a legal system at all, so there was no law there to enforce, and the grudge informer cases were easy because the informers had never actually violated any real law. Fuller more or less won the debate — Hart ended up admitting that the Nazi experience had refuted the positivist idea that you can identify law without looking at its moral content, and legal scholars now mostly agree that the informer cases settled it against positivism.
