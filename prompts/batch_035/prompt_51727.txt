My roommate is an economist and she finally walked me into a corner I can't get out of. The game she set up: two players, alternating moves, six nodes, everything visible. At each node the mover can "take" or "pass." Passing doubles the pot, but at the last node there's no more passing -- if it gets that far the last mover just takes. Taking ends the game and gives the taker 80% of the pot. So taking at node 1 gets you a small pile, while passing all the way to node 6 gets you a big one. She says the actual numbers don't matter, and I think she's right about that part.

Her argument:

1. At the last node the mover takes, since more money beats less.
2. At the second-to-last node the mover knows (1), so she takes rather than pass and recieve the smaller share at the end.
3. Repeat up the chain: take at your first opportunity. This is just iterated dominance -- no assumptions beyond people preferring more money, and everyone knowing everyone is rational, and everyone knowing that, and so on down.
4. Nothing in the reasoning depends on the doubling or the six nodes. Any finite game with a commonly known endpoint and common knowledge of rationality unravels the same way from the back.
5. Now take any cooperation held together only by repeated interaction -- roommates trading chores, friends repaying favors, dealers staying honest with repeat customers. Once the end is common knowledge (someone moves out, the market closes, whatever), (4) applies: there's a last round where defecting dominates, and it unravels backwards until no cooperation is rational at all.
6. Every human interaction is finite with a knowable end. Relationships end, people die, and everyone knows it.
7. So rational people can never sustain cooperation through repeated interaction alone, without external enforcement. Not rarely -- never. Once you both know the friendship has a last day, refusing a friend's request is the rational move.

I think (7) proves too much. But she's smug because every time I try to locate the flaw she says I'm denying (1), and I don't want to deny that more money beats less. My suspicions: something is off in the move from (4) to (5) -- taking at node 1 requires me to have a belief about what she'd do at node 2, but if I actually pass, that pass already tells her something that changes what's rational at node 2, doesn't it? Also not convinced that "people die eventually" and "this game has a commonly known endpoint" are the same claim. Which step do I reject, and what am I paying for it? I'd most like to keep (3), the backward induction logic inside the idealized game, but tell me honestly if even that has a problem with nodes the argument says would never be reached.
