I'm a novelist, and for a few years now I've been circling a historical figure of my own -- someone who did real good and real harm, the usual mess -- and I keep tripping over the 'man of his time' defence. You know the one: we can't judge Churchill or Jefferson by our standards, because basically nobody in their world saw things straight. My problem is that whenever I actually try to imagine my guy's world from the inside, the defence starts to look weird, and I think Hilary Mantel already ran the experiment for me.

Take Wolf Hall. Mantel gives us two men. Thomas More is, philosophically, the sincere man of his time: he burns heretics and genuinely believes he's saving their souls, and the novel doesn't pretend he's a hypocrite -- it shows the conscience as real. And then there's Cromwell, who the novel lets us love partly because he keeps flashing anachronistic signals: a kind of practical religious tolerance, sympathy for the people everyone else in the book treats as trash, a contempt for cruelty that reads like our contempt. Plenty of readers and critics have said this is a cheat -- that Mantel installed a modern man in a Tudor doublet so we'd have someone to root for, and that the real Cromwell was a fixer who ruined a lot of lives. It doesn't help that it's a flip of the older story: Bolt's A Man for All Seasons made More the saint and Cromwell the schemer, and Mantel more or less swaps them.

So here's what I want: a reading of Wolf Hall through the 'man of his time' defence. Does the novel take a position on it -- and is that position actually the defence's opposite, since the only way Mantel could make the book work at all seems to be finding (or invent) the one powerful man who approximates our standards? Is More there to show us what actually, consistently believing your era's values looks like, and is the horror of that meant to tell us the defence buys too much? Or is the Cromwell move fair, on the grounds that some people in every era do see through it -- abolitionists existed in Jefferson's America, so 'nobody knew better' was always going to be false, and Mantel is just dramatising that fact in one character? And then the harder bit: where does the novel resist the reading? Because Cromwell isn't clean -- he engineers Anne Boleyn's fall and a string of other deaths, the dissolving of the monasteries makes him rich and powerful, and in the later books the revenge and the ruthlessness keep moving toward the front. If he's our proxy, the novel also seems to be saying that even the man who sees through his era has to act with its tools, and gets shaped by them.

Also, honestly, I want to know what fiction is doing here that the straight debate about Jefferson and Columbus doesn't. Philosophers argue about whether the defence works; a novelist has to actually inhabit someone, and it looks like you can't inhabit a world sincerely and come out with anyone to admire unless the world already contained someone half-admirable. If that's a real constraint on narrative -- not just Mantel's particular choice -- then novels are quietly committed to the claim that every era contains its own internal critics, which is a factual claim the 'man of his time' defence has to be false. Or maybe I've got it backwards and you can write the sincere-man-of-his-time novel -- More's book, so to speak -- and I just haven't found it. I'd take either answer. But do the reading properly: don't let the novel become an illustration of the debate, and don't bend the debate to fit the novel.
