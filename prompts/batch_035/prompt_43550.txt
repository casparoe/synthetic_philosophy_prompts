I'm an undergraduate taking a seminar on twentieth-century historiography, and we've just spent three weeks on Braudel — the Mediterranean, the three-tier scheme of the longue durée, the conjoncture, and the event, and the way he privileges structures over what he dismissed as the short-term "surface" of history. For my final paper I want to make an argument about the internal architecture of that scheme, and I'd be grateful for comments on whether the argument works, where it's unfair to Braudel, and where the reasoning is loose. My thesis, roughly, is this: Braudel's hierarchy is not merely a claim about how long things last; it encodes a claim about a specific asymmetry of constraint. Each tier constrains the one above it without determining it — geography sets what economies can plausibly be, economic structures set the field on which politics and events play out — and the tiers are supposed to be insulated in one direction: an event cannot punch downward and rewrite the structures beneath it. I then argue that the Columbian Exchange is a genuine counterexample to that insulation: it begins as an event-level phenomenon (a handful of voyages, decisions, accidents of exploration, well within the time-scale of the evenementiel) yet it rewrote what I want to call the soft half of the longue durée — the global distribution of crops, animals, and pathogens, and the demographic facts that followed — even though it left the hard half (continental geography, ocean currents, the basic facts of climate) untouched. So the paper proposes splitting Braudel's longue durée into a hard layer of physical geography and a soft layer of biota and human ecology, with the claim that the famous insulation between event and structure only really holds for the hard layer, and that the soft layer is historically permeable in both directions. I close by wondering aloud whether modern climate change breaks even the hard layer, since industrial activity — itself a conjoncture-level development, I'd say — is now altering the climate facts that Braudel treated as the fixed stage of all history, which would make his stage the most contingent thing of all.

Here is roughly what I have so far as a draft of the argumentative core, in condensed form. First section: exegesis of the three tiers and the claim that the tiers differ not just in duration but in causal texture — structures don't just last longer, they act slower and mostly by making certain outcomes impossible rather than others necessary. Second section: the Columbian Exchange as a test case, with the mapping worked through — which parts of it belong to which tier, and exactly where the downward arrow appears (I'm concentrating on the potato in Europe and the biological catastrophe in the Americas, since both are common knowledge and neither depends on contested detail). Third section: the hard/soft split as a repair to Braudel rather than a refutation of him, since I want to say Braudel was right about the ocean and wrong about the disease pool. Draft conclusion: a half-formed suggestion that my split maps onto the modern distinction between geological and ecological time, which I suspect is why contemporary environmental historians have found Braudel newly usable rather than merely historical.

What I'd most like feedback on: (1) whether my reading of the hierarchy as an asymmetry-of-constraint claim is actually fair to Braudel, or whether I'm attributing to him a rigidity about downward causation that his own text doesn't have; (2) whether the Columbian Exchange really is an event punching downward, or whether a Braudelian would just reply that the Exchange is better described as a conjoncture — a slow process of transfer, quarantine, and adaptation that merely began with an event — and whether that reply defeats my argument or actually supports the hard/soft split; (3) whether the hard/soft distinction earns its keep or is ad hoc rescuing; and (4) whether the closing gesture toward climate change is a strength of the paper or a fashionable flourish I should cut, given that it risks turning the whole essay into an argument about the Anthropocene rather than about Braudel.
