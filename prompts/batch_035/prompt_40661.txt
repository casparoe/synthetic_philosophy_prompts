I edit a special issue of a cognitive-science journal devoted to adversarial collaborations, and two of my contributors have been arguing for a decade about consciousness and time. One defends what she calls a "minimal theater": she grants Dennett everything about the homunculus, about parallel distributed processing, about there being no single place where "it all comes together" for a little inner observer — but she insists that some contents do become globally available, that their arrival is what consciousness is, and that this convergence has enough structure (a limited-capacity workspace, a serial bottleneck in attention, contents that persist over short durations and can be reported at will) that denying it the name "theater" is a rhetorical move, not a scientific one. The other is an orthodox Dennettian: multiple drafts, content-fixation at multiple moments in multiple channels, narrative gravity as an abstraction, and the charge that any view positing a place or moment where contents become conscious is committed to something incoherent. They have finally agreed to collaborate, and I need the joint statement they should produce — a document both could sign their names to, not a verdict from me.

Please draft it with the following structure.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims. These should cover what both sides accept about the empirical phenomena that have long been their ammunition — the color phi phenomenon, metacontrast masking, the cutaneous rabbit, backward masking, change blindness, split-brain behavior, postdictive filling-in of various kinds — as well as what both accept about the standard arguments: what Dennett's arguments against the "Cartesian materialist" strawman actually establish, and what they leave open; what the global-workspace and attention-based accounts actually commit one to; and what both would say to the charge that one side is just using "consciousness" to mean something the other side doesn't care about.

Second, a table of disagreements, with one row per issue and the following columns: the issue; the theater-side formulation, in wording she would accept as a fair statement of her own view; the multiple-drafts formulation, in wording he would accept as a fair statement of his; the crux — the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one of them; a concrete case, experiment, or thought experiment both agree bears on that crux; and each side's prediction about that case, written down in advance as preregistered commitments, not as spin after the fact.

At least three cruxes should appear, and I want two in particular included. One is the timing question: whether there is a fact of the matter about the objective time at which a particular conscious token occurs — whether, in the color phi case, the experienced reddish-green disk "became conscious" at some determinate moment in the brain's timeline, or whether that question has no determinate answer, only facts about when various discriminations became available for various purposes. Phrase the disagreement so that neither side is made to sound confused. The second is what I call the revision case, and I want you to develop it: a face is briefly presented and re-presented in quick succession, with slight parameter changes, such that early processing codes it as a smile and later processing re-codes it as a grimace, all within roughly two hundred milliseconds and prior to any report. The minimal-theater side should predict there was a determinate first-pass content that was experienced and then revised — an Orwellian structure of experience followed by memory-editing — while the multiple-drafts side should predict there is no fact of the matter about which early draft was experienced, only about the content that went on to control report and memory. Make the table's test column say what result would count against each side, and make their predictions genuinely risky — able to come out wrong — rather than hedged. The third crux is up to you, but it should be at the same level of specificity rather than a general restatement of the theater-versus-drafts slogan.

Third, a short closing section, labeled "Residual Disagreements," in which you explicitly sort the remaining disputes into those that are substantive (a difference in prediction or in what facts there are) and those that are verbal (a difference in what the word "consciousness" or "theater" is being used to pick out), with a sentence of justification for each sorting. If you conclude that part of their decade-long fight was about words, say which part, and why — and if part of the verbal dispute turns out to have substantive downstream consequences (e.g., because the word does inferential work in medical or legal contexts), note that too.

Do not include a bibliography or literature review; refer to positions, arguments, and phenomena by name only, since citations will be added at the proof stage. Keep the tone of a negotiated document — careful, slightly stiff, drafted by two people who each refused to sign the previous draft — rather than of a referee's report. The whole thing must not exceed 1,200 words.
