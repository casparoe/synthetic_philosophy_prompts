A colleague of mine in legal philosophy has an argument she claims shows that courts never judge anything, and I can't find the seam in it. First the setup, which is the standard discursive dilemma. A three-judge panel decides a negligence case. Let P be that the defendant owed the claimant a duty of care, Q that the defendant breached that duty, and let the court's own rule be: if P and Q, the defendant is liable. Judge A accepts P and rejects Q; Judge B rejects P and accepts Q; Judge C accepts both. So each judge rejects liability except C. Taken proposition by proposition, the majorities accept P (A and C) and Q (B and C) and reject liability (A and B) — a collective set that contradicts the very rule the court applies.

Her argument:

(1) Judgments are states of subjects, and there are no group minds; so if a panel ever judges, its judgment must be constituted entirely by facts about the members' judgments — nothing over and above the members and their attitudes.
(2) Whatever maps the members' judgments onto the panel's must be even-handed: it must treat members identically (the verdict can't depend on who said what) and propositions identically (the verdict on any proposition must depend only on the members' judgments about that proposition). Anything less is rigged from the start.
(3) The procedures satisfying (2) that return a verdict on every proposition are in effect the quota rules, and majority is the only fair quota. On this case it returns the inconsistent set above.
(4) An inconsistent set cannot be the content of a judgment: a body that both asserts and denies hasn't thereby judged anything.
(5) The usual escape — vote on the premises and let liability follow — violates (2): the panel's stance on liability now turns on which propositions get labeled premises, and that is a fact about agenda design, not about the members' judgments on liability.
(6) By (1), facts about agenda design cannot constitute a judgment either, since the agenda is not a member.
(7) So either the panel's verdict is inconsistent or it is an artifact of agenda-setting: either way the panel never judges. Parliaments never legislate, juries never convict, "the court held" is a façon de parler — and collective judgment exists only where voting is superfluous (unanimity), evaporating precisely where it is needed.

I have a feeling the trick is somewhere in (5) and (6), because the premise-based verdict still looks made of nothing but member judgments — yet I cannot say what in (1) or (2) is supposed to exclude it. Tell me which premise to reject, why that premise looked innocent, and what rejecting it costs. For what it's worth, I would most like to keep (1): I have no use for group minds.
