I'm a science and ideas journalist working on a long-read about how scientific fields change their minds, and the case I've picked is the continental drift affair. You probably know the shape of it: Alfred Wegener puts the idea out there around 1912, the establishment geologists — especially the Americans, who were openly brutal about it at that 1926 oil-geologists symposium — bury it, and then in the 1960s the field flips almost wholesale once seafloor spreading and the magnetic striping on the ocean floor turn up. Everyone and their dog calls it a "paradigm shift" now, so I'd like the piece to actually earn that phrase instead of gesturing at it. I read Kuhn years ago, mind, and what I want is a careful mapping onto his framework, then an honest account of where the mapping strains, and then a verdict on what the episode does back to Kuhn — whether it's a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case his model never anticipated.

A few things I specifically want wrestled with rather than glossed over:

- Most of the people who rejected drift weren't defending a rival theory of mountains and oceans; they were defending a method — roughly, "we don't do global speculation from coastlines, we do fieldwork". Does Kuhn's apparatus even have a slot for a discipline whose paradigm is partly a discipline about which questions count as legitimate ones?
- The words didn't change but the meanings did. "Continental drift" before the 1960s meant continents somehow ploughing through solid oceanic crust, which looked physically absurd; after, it meant blocks riding on a mobile mantle. So a lot of the 1920s argument looks, in Kuhn's terms, like the two sides talking past each other — incommensurability in miniature, decades before anyone had a reason to notice it. Pull that thread properly, please.
- The fix came largely from oceanographers and people towing magnetometers at sea — outsiders, again, just like Wegener himself (a meteorologist!). The revolution was twice delivered by people who never shared the land geologists' training. What does that do to the story where a crisis in normal science breeds the revolution?

For format, structure the piece in these sections, in this order:

1. "The episode, in brief" — around 150 words, just the facts a reader needs, no editorialising.
2. "Mapping it onto Kuhn" — around 400 words: who counts as the relevant community, what the paradigm actually was, what the anomaly or crisis amounted to, and what the eventual conversion looked like.
3. "Where the mapping strains" — around 350 words: the places Kuhn's model has to be forced to speak, and where it stays silent.
4. "What it says back" — around 250 words: confirmation, anomaly, or a case he never anticipated?
5. "What I'd tell my editor" — around 100 words, the one-line upshot, no new material.

Keep it casual, use British spelling, and please don't pad it out — my editor will cut anything that smells like a textbook recap of The Structure of Scientific Revolutions, so assume the reader has already heard of normal science and paradigm shifts and go straight for the interesting bits. And if you find yourself tempted to quote Kuhn verbatim, paraphrase him instead; I don't want the piece leaning on quotes I can't check against the archive.
