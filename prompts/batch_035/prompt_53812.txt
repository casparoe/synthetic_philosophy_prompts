A quantitative historian and a historian of early modern Europe, both friends of mine, spent an entire dinner arguing about cliodynamics, and they've asked me to get a second opinion on who got the better of it. The dispute, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) The quantitative historian opened by claiming that Peter Turchin's structural-demographic work has finally been tested rather than just fitted: around 2010, Turchin's models — built on long-run series for real wages, elite overproduction, and popular wellbeing — flagged the 2020s as a period of rising political instability in the United States and Western Europe, and instability duly arrived. So, he argued, the standard objection that cliodynamics is unfalsifiable curve-fitting has been met by an out-of-sample success.

(2) The early modernist replied that predicting "instability will increase somewhere in this decade" is barely a prediction at all: the terms are elastic, instability of some kind in some Western country over a ten-year window is close to a sure thing, and the mechanism on offer is conveniently post hoc — after any turbulence you can always find an oversupply of elites to point to. A theory that can't lose isn't predicting, it's prophesying.

(3) The quantitative historian answered with an analogy: Kepler's laws were also bare regularities fitted to planetary data with adjustable parameters and no underlying mechanism, and nobody demands that a science have its Newton before its regularities count as knowledge. If predictive fit on its own was good enough for astronomy in its cradle, it's good enough for cliodynamics now.

(4) The early modernist said the analogy breaks at exactly one decisive joint: planets do not read Kepler's tables and adjust their orbits, but societies are reflexive. Once a regularity about instability becomes public, the very elites and policymakers it warns have both motive and capacity to act on it, so any true historical law that gets discovered and broadcast tends to be the one that stops working. Cliodynamics can be like astronomy only for a world of rocks.

(5) The quantitative historian's closing move was that (4) actually strengthens his case rather than weakening it: if policymakers successfully defuse the pressures the model identifies — by, say, reversing elite overproduction — the model's account of the causes is vindicated by the successful intervention, exactly as epidemiological models are vindicated when a predicted outbreak is contained. Reflexivity is a confirmation channel, not a defeater.

They then started arguing about secular cycles in medieval and early modern England and my notes give out.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the five moves, say whether it is sound as stated, and — importantly — whether it actually answers the specific point it was aimed at, or only sounds like it does. Then tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing that a referee should have raised. Since I have to report your verdict back to two people who will each look for the weakest step in it, please also tell me explicitly what would change your mind: which argument or consideration, if it failed or were supplied, would flip your adjudication of the overall exchange, and where in your own reasoning you would locate the point of greatest vulnerability.
