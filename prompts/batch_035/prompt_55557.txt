I'm a research engineer on an interpretability team, and after our group spent a week arguing about what our sparse autoencoders had actually found, we split into two camps — and, as the one person both sides still speak to, I've been asked to draft the joint statement we'll circulate ahead of our next research retreat. The dispute: the "realists" hold that the interpretable features we extracted were already present in the model, and our tools merely revealed them; the "deflationists" hold that the decomposition was something we imposed — one useful carving among many we could have trained — and that asking which features the network "really has" is a category mistake.

Draft the document both camps could sign. It should contain: (1) a numbered section of claims both sides accept — about what interpretability methods demonstrably achieve (e.g., steering behavior, localizing computations, predicting model outputs), and about what it would take for a feature to count as "discovered" rather than "constructed"; (2) a disagreements table in which each side's position on each point is worded so that its own members would sign it — covering at least whether there is a fact of the matter about the correct decomposition, whether successful steering shows the intervened-on feature was already causally active, and whether "the model represents X" is ever literally true; (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions that, if settled, would move at least one side; and (4) for each crux, a concrete test or observation both sides agree bears on it — e.g., whether independently trained autoencoders on the same model converge on the same features, whether features recur across different architectures trained on similar data, or whether interventions defined via one decomposition generalize to held-out behaviors — with each camp's prediction written down in advance. Finally, flag any part of the dispute you judge to be merely verbal, and propose the reformulation that would dissolve it. Keep the tone neutral and precise: this is a document two people who have been arguing for a month must both be able to put their names to.
