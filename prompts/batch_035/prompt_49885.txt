The question was:

Moore claimed that by holding up two hands and uttering simple sentences about them he had given a rigorous proof of an external world. (a) State exactly what Moore takes the conclusion of the proof to be, and how he understands the key term. (b) State the three conditions Moore says a rigorous proof must satisfy, and say whether, by his own lights, the proof satisfies them. (c) Explain why many philosophers nevertheless find the proof unsatisfying, and assess whether that dissatisfaction can be stated in a way Moore could accept as a flaw in his proof.

And here's the answer I have to grade:

Moore brings up Kant's remark that it's a scandal of philosophy that no proof of the existence of things outside us had ever been given, and says that now, finally, he can remove this scandal. The proof is given in A Defence of Common Sense: he holds up first one hand and then the other, and says, first, that here is one hand, and, second, that here is another. Since hands are external things, and he obviously knows the premises, the conclusion follows that there are external things.

On what "external" means: Moore says the relevant sense is external to our minds, not just outside our bodies (though his hands are external in both senses). To say things are external to our minds is just to say that they exist whether or not anyone is perceiving them, i.e. that the world is there even when nobody is looking. So the proof establishes that the physical world persists unperceived, which also means it counts as a refutation of the dream hypothesis from Descartes, since if we knew things existed without being perceived we'd know they weren't just dreams.

Moore lays out three conditions for a rigorous proof: the premises must be different from the conclusion; the premises must be known with certainty; and the conclusion must follow deductively from the premises. He says his proof satisfies all three, and he's right that it does, though with a caveat I'll come to. An extra point he makes is that he's more certain of his premise about the hand than he is of any premise in any philosophical argument for skepticism, so if the skeptic's argument and his proof conflict, the skeptical argument is the one that has to go.

For (c): the reason the proof feels unsatisfying is that a skeptic just won't accept the premise that here is a hand — that's exactly what she doubts. So the proof is dialectically useless: it identifies no flaw in the skeptical argument, it just pits one certainty against another. Moore in effect concedes this himself when he admits that the premises aren't really known with certainty after all, so the proof fails his own second condition, which is why he retreats to claiming only that the premise is more certain than the skeptic's premises.
