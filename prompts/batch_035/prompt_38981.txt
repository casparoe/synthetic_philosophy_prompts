been lurking lesswrong and the alignment forum since about 2015 and this is finally a question i can't resolve by reading more posts, so here goes.

i do mech interp at a mid-size lab. every single week, someone comes to me with a claim that the model "has" some feature — a deception feature, a sycophancy feature, a backdoor-trigger feature — usually spotted on an SAE dashboard or with a linear probe, and then we spend the whole retro arguing about whether it's Real or whether we're just pattern-matching our own concepts onto a pile of matrices. the same fight, every week. and the stakes are not low: our whole safety story to leadership (and increasingly to auditors, ugh) is that we can point at what the model believes and wants. if the features are partly a thing we project rather than discover, that story is in trouble.

so: write me a decision procedure my team can actually run when someone claims to have found a representation. questions in order, what counts as a pass and a fail on each, where the procedure has to stop and hand off to human judgment because the question is value-laden or just genuinely open. ground it in the actual underlying issue, which as far as i can tell is: are these features sitting there in the network waiting to be found, or are they partly constituted by us choosing a decomposition, the way a smiley face in a bun is partly constituted by the person looking at the bun. i want the checklist to force engagement with the standard traps, e.g.: linear probes that hit 99% accuracy because almost anything is linearly separable if you have enough dimensions (compare the random-probe baseline before you celebrate); the thing where a handful of activations look beautifully interpretable but the same small set also lights up for a dozen unrelated inputs, so the "feature" is doing less work than it appears; whether the decomposition is canonical or just one of many bases that fit the activations about equally well; and whether the causal test — ablate the feature, watch the behavior change — actually settles the realism question or just pushes it back a level (behavior changed, sure, but did it change *because* of the thing we described, or would a dozen other interventions have changed it too).

constraints: it has to be runnable in an afternoon by someone who's read the forum posts but none of the philosophy papers, it can't require solving intentionality before thursday's review meeting, and it should say explicitly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, because i've noticed we're good at pretending those don't exist.

then run it on three of our actual recurring cases:

1. an SAE feature that fires on hex numbers, the string "0x", and (annoyingly) photographs of hexagons. fires consistently, ablation removes the model's ability to read hex literals. real representation of hex, or a mess we named after the first thing we saw?

2. a probe for "the model is about to lie" that gets 97% on held-out training conversations and 61% on fresh ones. procedure verdict, please.

3. the one i actually care about: a "deception" feature where ablation changes behavior exactly as predicted, out-of-distribution too, and i *still* get the creeps calling it a deception feature rather than "the thing whose ablation has this effect." tell me if the procedure has anything to say about case 3 or if it's officially above its pay grade.

and crucially: include at least one case, either one of these or a new one, where the procedure gives an answer i won't like — i want to believe our features are real, they're load-bearing for the audit — and be honest about what the procedure can't settle. like, if the model is equally happy with several incompatible decompositions and interventions work on all of them, does "the model has a deception feature" even mean anything more than "this intervention works"? i've read enough to know that's not a gotcha, it's the whole question, but i'd like it operationalized enough to argue about at the retro instead of around.
