In my philosophy of physics course we've just finished a unit on the Everett interpretation, and I have one term left before I decide whether to apply to graduate school in philosophy of physics. I'm writing my term paper on quantum suicide and quantum immortality, and I want to use the paper to figure out which open questions in that corner are actually worth years of my life rather than one semester. So I'd like a prioritized map of the live issues, and a defense of the ordering.

By way of background, here are the candidate questions as I currently see them, roughly sketched. (1) The identity question: does fission in branching even preserve the "self" whose survival quantum immortality is supposed to guarantee, or does each pre-experiment self have countless descendants rather than one continuer — and if so, is the whole idea just a confusion about personal identity dressed in physics? (2) The classical-analogue question: whether a coin flip, a random number generator, or even deterministic pseudo-randomness gives you the same "I always find myself surviving" reasoning, which would suggest the argument owes nothing to quantum mechanics as such and everything to anthropic self-selection. (3) The measure problem: even granting Everettian branching, what does the Born-rule weighting of branches do to the notion that "some future version of me observes survival," given that most of the measure sits with the branches where I die? (4) The practical-implications question: whether anything follows for decisions under risk — buying insurance, taking the Russian roulette bet — or whether, as I suspect, the correct decision-theoretic advice is identical to the textbook one and the whole practical payoff is overrated. And (5) whatever I've missed that you think belongs on the list.

For the prioritization, please tell me which questions are upstream of which — e.g., whether (1) and (3) must be settled before (4) can even be posed coherently — which are tractable for a philosopher with only undergraduate-level quantum mechanics (I can follow the formalism but can't do original physics), and which are neglected enough that a term paper could make a genuine contribution. Also flag which question you'd tell me to deprioritize because its answer, whatever it turns out to be, wouldn't change much downstream. Finally, tell me what would have to happen — in the literature or in my own understanding — for me to revise this ranking, since I'll be using it to decide what to read deeply over the summer and whether this area is worth applying to graduate school for at all.
