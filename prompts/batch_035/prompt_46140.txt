For my final project in my school's Ethics and Moral Philosophy elective, I have to write a piece of satire that "demonstrates mastery of a concept by making a specialist laugh rather than wince" (our teacher's exact phrasing), and I've chosen the concept of supererogation — acts that are good, and praiseworthy, to do, but not wrong to refrain from doing. I think I understand the idea itself, but I'm much less confident that I understand its pressure points well enough to make fun of them in a way that lands, so I'd like your help drafting it.

Here is the assignment I'd like you to complete. Write an Onion-style news article, headline included, of roughly 450 to 550 words, reporting that a local man has been issued a formal citation by his town's Office of Moral Standards for "exceeding the scope of his duties" — not for doing anything wrong, but for doing so much good that he has destabilized the local moral economy. The humor has to come from getting the actual structure of supererogation right, not just from absurdist small-town bureaucracy. In particular, the article should exaggerate real features of the concept: the asymmetry at its heart (the man deserves praise for what he did, yet nobody can coherently blame his neighbors for not doing it, and the citation keeps failing to specify what law he broke); the recalibration problem, in which acts that were once above and beyond become the new expected baseline once someone performs them; and the worry, which we discussed in class, that a strict maximizing consequentialist neighbor should refuse to recognize the category at all, since for him every action short of the best is simply a violation waiting to be counted. Please include at least one quote from a Kantian neighbor insisting that genuine respect is owed only to what is required, and at least one quote from the cited man himself, who is torn between enjoying the praise and resenting that it has quietly lowered everyone else's standing. I would also like, if you can manage it without breaking the deadpan tone, a nod to the idea that a person who only ever performed supererogatory acts might end up too exhausting to be around — the sense that moral perfection crowds out the other things that make someone a good dinner guest.

Please keep the piece deadpan throughout, in the flat matter-of-fact register of a wire-service news report, and never have the narrator wink at the reader or explain the joke. A specialist reading it should be able to tell that the author knows why the permissible/supererogatory boundary is philosophically fragile, while an ordinary reader should just find it funny. Write it in American English, and if you use any technical terms, deploy them the way a small-town official misusing paperwork would, not the way a journal article would.
