Assume the role of a philosopher of the historical sciences who is also well read in the archaeology of the societies in question — someone equally at home in the conceptual literature on explanation and in what actually comes out of the ground. I am writing a paper on whether "collapse" is a genuine comparative-historical concept or a storyteller's prop, and before I argue either way I need the specification for its rival: a theory of societal collapse. Please think carefully before committing to anything final — start with a few sentences of rough brainstorming of candidate requirements, then present the polished list.

Keep one purpose in view throughout: the theory has to be good enough for comparative historical social science, where "good enough" means able to adjudicate real, disputed cases on the strength of the material record — not merely able to interpret whatever is found after the fact.

The cases that keep tripping me up, and which the requirements should therefore be shaped around:

1. The Maya "collapse": in the southern lowlands, dynastic monuments and palace institutions wind down over roughly two centuries, while in the very same period the northern cities of Yucatán flourish — and Maya peoples, languages, and communities persist to this day. A collapse of a society, of a set of institutions, or neither?

2. Post-Roman Britain: the administrative apparatus of the western Empire dissolves over a long stretch of centuries, yet skeletal evidence has been read as showing that average stature — a tolerable proxy for childhood nutrition — rose in the following period. If a "collapse" left the peasantry better fed, I want to know what exactly is supposed to have collapsed.

3. Rapa Nui: the received story is ecocide, a society destroying itself through deforestation; a revisionist story blames rats and later European contact, and denies that any pre-contact catastrophe occurred. A theory of collapse has to be able to adjudicate between these, which means it must say in advance what evidence would count.

4. The end of the Late Bronze Age: within a few decades on either side of 1200–1100 BCE, palace economies across the eastern Mediterranean fail, some writing systems vanish, whole cities are burned or abandoned — and Egypt persists, and life goes on for most people. Is that one event or many, and who gets to say?

The deliverable, in this order:

A. The requirements list, each with the case or consideration behind it. Among the questions the requirements should settle one way or another: whether the theory must distinguish the fate of elites from the fate of populations (monuments stop being built; people keep eating), and whether a verdict of collapse can be indexed to one level while other levels continue; whether collapse must be treated as a state or a process, graded or all-or-nothing; whether the concept is required to be observer-neutral, given that what registers as collapse for a dynastic elite can register as relocalization for villagers; and whether the theory must generate testable expectations about the material record — settlement patterns, paleobotany, skeletal indicators of stress — rather than absorbing whatever turns up.

B. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable if collapse is to remain a scientific concept, and which are prejudices of particular research traditions (the catastrophist lineage on one side, the resilience/transformation school on the other). If any subset of requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — for instance, if demanding both rapidity and a whole-society unit of analysis would rule out the Roman case by definitional fiat — say so explicitly; that is the part I most want. Also tell me which single requirement, if strictly enforced, excludes the most candidate theories.

C. Briefly — two or three lines each, as I will do the full scorecard myself — how Tainter's diminishing-returns-on-complexity account, a Diamond-style ecocide account, and a resilience/transformation view would each fare against the list.

Refer to positions and arguments rather than bibliographies: no reading lists, no citations with page numbers, no author-by-author surveys. Write so that a careful reader with no web access and no specialist training could evaluate the result on its merits alone.
