I'm in a reading group that's spent this term on panpsychism, and the combination problem keeps getting billed as the thing that sinks the whole view: how do billions of tiny experiential subjects in my neurons add up to one subject -- me -- rather than a room full of people shouting past each other? One guy in the group, who's sympathetic to panpsychism, keeps answering with a musical analogy that I can't get out of my head. He says a chord is literally made of individual notes, the notes don't vanish or stop being notes when played together, and out of them you get a new experiential unity -- the chord -- that is unmistakably one thing to listen to. So compound subjects are everywhere, he says, and we're only very impressed by the one in our own skull because we're on the inside of it. The conclusion he wants to licence is that the combination problem isn't a special embarassment for panpsychism at all: it's just ordinary emergence that we've dressed up as a mystery because the emergent thing happens to be us.

Can you audit this for me? I want the mapping laid out explicitly -- what plays the role of the micro-subject, the macro-subject, the fusion, the "being one thing" -- and then where the chord picture breaks down. A couple of things I half-notice but can't articulate: a note inside a chord is still individually audible, whereas the panpsychist apparently needs the micro-subjects to not be separately experienced once they're combined; and the unity of a chord seems to be a unity for a listener, which looks like it presupposes exactly the sort of subject the analogy was meant to explain. Also a chord is arguably only one thing relative to an ear and some attention, and my consciousness doesn't seem to be unified merely relative to anybody. But what I'd really like to know is which of these disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion he's pushing (that combination is ordinary, not mysterious) and which are just noise -- maybe the audibility point is, since after all I can't hear your experiences either -- and whether some better analogy would get him more or less what he wants. Or is the fact that every candidate analogy (chords, waves superposing, cells in a body, clubs merging) seems to fail in the same place itself the deep point about what the combination problem is?
