I am a junior and one of the editors of our school's literary and arts magazine, and our theme for this year's print issue is "Deep Time." For my own submission I want to write about something I encountered in AP World History and have not been able to stop thinking about: the French historian Fernand Braudel's claim that history runs on at least three clocks at once. There is the almost unmoving time of geography and climate — mountain ranges, coastlines, prevailing winds — which changes so slowly that no single human lifetime could register the change; there is the slower social and economic time of trade routes, grain prices, and the rise and fall of empires; and then there is the fast, noisy time of kings, battles, and headlines, which Braudel compared to the foam churned up on the surface of the sea, while the deep water beneath hardly notices.

I have a formal scheme in mind, and I would like the poem's structure to do the philosophical work rather than merely decorate it. Please write a poem of roughly twenty-four to thirty lines, in five stanzas, observing the following constraints:

1. The final line of each stanza must be word-for-word identical — a single calm line about something almost geological, such as a coastline, a mountain range, or the temperature of deep water. This repeated line is the slowest clock, and its refusal to change across the whole poem is the point. Choose its image carefully: it should be something that would plausibly be recognizably the same across, say, a thousand years.

2. The lines above it should change completely from stanza to stanza, and the stanzas should sit at different speeds of history. Include at least one stanza of the middle time — trade routes silting up, a grain price drifting over decades, an empire's slow sprawl — and at least one stanza of pure event, full of names, dates, and noise, written so it feels faster and more crowded than the others.

3. At least once, let a stanza's syntax run straight into the constant line, so that a battle or a headline seems for a moment to spill over into geological time — but the constant line's words must remain exactly what they are everywhere else. The interruption should happen only on the surface, in the grammar; the deep line itself must never budge.

4. End the poem with the constant line followed by two new lines that ask, without answering, where a single human life belongs on these clocks — whether the fastest clock is the only one a person can actually live in, and what it costs to be foam.

One last request: the poem should never use the words "Braudel," "longue durée," "geography," or "history." Someone who knows the idea should recognize it from the structure alone, and someone who doesn't should still find it a good poem. I would rather leave our faculty advisor guessing than explain the scheme in a footnote.
