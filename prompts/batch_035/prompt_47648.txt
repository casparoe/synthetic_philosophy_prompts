A podcast I listen to on public affairs ran an episode on theories of egalitarian justice, and the presenter compressed the whole "equality of what?" debate into the following:

"Sen made famous the question 'equality of what?' There are three candidate answers: welfare, resources, and capabilities. Welfare egalitarians want to equalize how satisfied people end up, and this view was more or less finished off by the expensive-tastes objection: if my contentment requires vintage wine and yours is fine with beer, equality of welfare obliges the state to top up my cellar while you get nothing, and since nobody accepts that, welfare egalitarianism is refuted. Rawls is usually read as a resource theorist: his primary goods — rights, liberties, fair opportunities, income and wealth, and the social bases of self-respect — form the currency, and the point of measuring these rather than welfare is simply that they are easier to observe. Dworkin made the resource view precise with the envy test, enforced through his famous auction: a distribution of resources is equal when nobody would rather have anybody else's bundle. But he had to smuggle welfare back in to handle unequal talents and disabilities, so his view quietly collapses into the welfare egalitarianism he set out to attack. Sen's own answer is that we should equalize what people actually manage to do and to be — their achieved functionings, like being nourished or taking part in the life of the community. This handles diversity that resources can't: two people with the same bundle differ in what they can do with it when one is ill or lives in an unhealthier climate. So the resource view is untenable, and capabilities are the only game in town."

I found this broadly persuasive, but two things nagged at me: the one-line treatment of Rawls struck me as suspiciously practical, and the claim that Dworkin "collapses into" welfare egalitarianism sounds like an opponent's spin rather than a report. I don't have the background to check any of it.

I'm particularly interested in places where the presenter treats a distinction the authors themselves went out of their way to draw as though it didn't exist — for instance, whether Sen's target of equalization is really achieved functionings, and whether Rawls's reason for using primary goods rather than welfare was measurement convenience.
