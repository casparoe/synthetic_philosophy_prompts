A small philanthropic fund has roughly two million dollars a year for the next three years to spend on research into the net welfare of wild animals — the question of whether the typical wild animal's life is worth living, and whether interventions like habitat preservation, rewilding, or even habitat destruction make things better or worse in aggregate. The fund's trustees are convinced the topic is plausibly enormous in scale (most of the individual animals alive at any moment are small, short-lived invertebrates with high reproductive rates and high mortality), but they have no sense of how to slice the problem into tractable pieces. They have explicitly ruled out funding advocacy, so this is about research priorities only.

Please draft the prioritization analysis the fund's research director will circulate at the trustees' next strategy meeting. Among the questions you should weigh are at least the following, though you may add or discard others:

1. The sign question: is the average wild animal's life positive or negative in welfare terms, and can we ever get evidence that would move reasonable people's credences rather than just restating priors?
2. The correlation between ecosystem productivity and aggregate suffering: if habitats with more total biomass and faster turnover contain more lives and more deaths, does 'healthy ecosystem' mean more suffering rather than less, and what would that imply for conservation as a default good?
3. The counterfactual replacement question: what actually happens to animal populations when land is converted — whether, for example, agricultural monoculture supports fewer or more sentient lives than the wild habitat it replaced, including the invertebrate and soil fauna most people never count.
4. Invertebrate sentience: how much of the value of resolving it depends on the sign question being resolved first, and whether work on it is better done by the fund or left to academic neuroscience it can merely track.
5. r/K dynamics: whether animals at the fast-reproducing, high-offspring-mortality end of the spectrum dominate the aggregate calculus, and whether that makes demographic modeling more or less important than welfare assessment per animal.

The trustees want more than a ranked list. For each question you prioritize, explain why it is upstream or downstream of the others, how tractable it is given that direct measurement of subjective experience is impossible, and what a concrete intermediate result would look like — something a three-year grant could actually deliver and that would change what the fund does in years four through ten. They are also suspicious of questions that sound important but whose answer would not change any intervention decision, so flag any of the five that fail that test and say so plainly. Finally, address the strongest objection to the whole enterprise: that if the sign of wild animal welfare is unknowable in practice, the fund should hold its money or spend it elsewhere, and the ranking exercise is a way of avoiding that conclusion rather than answering it.
