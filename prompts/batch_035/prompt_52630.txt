I hold a lexical threshold version of negative utilitarianism: below some intensity of suffering, happiness can outweigh it in the ordinary aggregative way, but above the threshold a single instance is worse than any amount of happiness, however distributed across any number of beings. Most critics treat the view as resting on a denial that pleasure and pain are commensurable, but I've come to doubt that this is where the real issue lies: the view could be true even if both were measurable on a common cardinal scale, with the question instead being what kinds of aggregation are permitted at the top of it. What would count against my view? I know the standard refutation of strict negative utilitarianism — that it would prefer an empty world to any world containing a single pinprick — but the threshold is supposed to block exactly that, so tell me whether it does, and at what cost: does the threshold hold against vast numbers of instances each just below it, or against pains that fall just on the wrong side of the line? Can the threshold be set non-arbitrarily, or does each new counterexample force another qualification until the view is insulated against everything — and if so, is that a problem for a view of this kind, or just what threshold views are like? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish what a committed defender of the view would concede as counting against it from what they would dismiss, and say which intuitions the view ultimately stands or falls with — for instance, whether it can accommodate the procreation asymmetry without collapsing into it.
