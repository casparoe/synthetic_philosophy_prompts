In my philosophy of religion seminar, our instructor has now used the same one-liner twice to end a discussion: existence is not a predicate. The first time was against Descartes's argument that existence belongs to a supremely perfect being as surely as having three angles belongs to a triangle; the second was against a student presentation of Gödel's modal ontological argument. The first deployment sounded decisive. The second sounded suspicious, because Gödel's version doesn't obviously insert existence into a definition of God at all — it works from axioms about positive properties and S5 modal logic to conclude that something necessarily exists, and I couldn't see where a predicate of existence was ever being illegitimately posited. When I pressed this, I was told it was the same fallacy in fancier dress. I want to know whether that's right.

Please lay out, as a list I can bring to seminar, the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing — from the trivially true to the genuinely radical — and for each, say whether it's plausible and, crucially, what it would actually defeat. For what it's worth, I can dimly see several candidates: something merely grammatical about how the verb "exists" functions; something Frege-and-Russell-like, on which existence is a second-level property of concepts rather than of objects; something stronger about the relationship between a thing's concept and its existence; possibly a modal gloss. The verdicts should be keyed to targets: which readings, if any, sink Descartes's premise; which touch Anselm's Proslogion 2 argument, where nothing is defined into existence either but rather the fool's isolated mental existence is compared with existence in reality; and — the part I care most about — whether any reading does anything against Gödel's version, where necessary existence is treated as a positive property and funneled through axioms rather than definitions. Conclude by saying which reading my instructor most likely needs for the dismissal to work against Gödel, and whether that reading is one a reasonable person should accept.

Since I will be defending this list in class: for each verdict indicate roughly how confident you are, and at the end single out the one point in the whole analysis where you are least sure of yourself — I would rather know in advance where to expect the pushback than be surprised by it.
