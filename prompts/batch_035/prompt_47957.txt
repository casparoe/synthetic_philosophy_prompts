I teach a 200-level social epistemology seminar — sixteen students, one 90-minute session — and we are about to begin Miranda Fricker's work on epistemic injustice. Design a two-stage activity in which students encounter both testimonial and hermeneutical injustice from the inside before either term has been introduced. In the first stage, some students' testimony should be discounted under a rule they do not know exists, arranged so that the discounted students are in fact the best informed; in the second, students should have to interpret a set of first-person reports while the concept that would organize those reports is distributed unevenly — ideally to the very students who were discounted in stage one, so that the compounding of the two injustices becomes visible rather than asserted. Provide concrete roles, rules, materials, a timeline, and a points scheme that does not use real money. Then provide a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the distinction between testimonial and hermeneutical injustice, and that confronts the obvious objection directly: the activity manufactures the prejudice by rule, whereas on Fricker's account the credibility deficit must track a prejudice the hearer actually holds — so the debrief should work through whether rule-induced discounting still counts as testimonial injustice or merely simulates its mechanism, and what turns on that difference. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong on a first run — students leaking the rules to each other, the second stage collapsing because everyone in the room already possesses the concept in question, the points scheme coming to feel like trivia — and how to handle each. Finally, be frank about which features of the two injustices the activity cannot reproduce and I must supply myself in the debrief: I have in mind especially the temporal depth of a hermeneutical gap, which in the classroom lasts fifty minutes and in real cases can last generations.
