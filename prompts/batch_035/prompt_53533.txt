I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training at all — my background is backend work and a lot of applied ML, so I know Bayes from spam filters and A/B tests, not from seminar rooms. Lately I've been going down a rabbit hole on a problem I didn't even know had a name: what do you do when your evidence is itself uncertain? You don't get to cleanly observe "E"; you observe something that makes E probable without settling it. Apparently the standard tool is a thing called Jeffrey conditioning (Richard Jeffrey's update rule), and the classic illustration is someone judging the color of a piece of cloth by candlelight — they can't be sure what they're seeing, so they update on a weighted mix of "it's green" and "it isn't," weighted by how confident they are in the look of the thing.

Here's the real episode I want analyzed through that lens: the first gravitational wave detection. Timeline as I understand it (this is all public record):

- The signal hit both LIGO detectors on Sept 14, 2015 — two black holes of 36 and 29 solar masses merging into one of 62, with roughly 3 solar masses' worth of energy radiated away as the wave itself. The waveform was so clean that people inside the collaboration initially wondered whether it was a test signal — partly because five years earlier LIGO had done a "blind injection" (sometimes called the Big Dog event), where they secretly faked a signal and let the collaboration spend months analyzing it before telling them it wasn't real.
- The collaboration then sat on the result for about five months doing checks. During that window, rumors leaked. A famous physicist tweeted about a rumored detection, science news outlets wrote "is it real?" pieces, and the official announcement didn't come until Feb 11, 2016. The event is now called GW150914.

The interesting agent is an outside physicist (or honestly just anyone following the news) during fall 2015. They don't observe "LIGO has a detection" — they observe "rumors are circulating that LIGO has a detection in hand." That's textbook uncertain evidence: the rumor is compatible both with E being true (there really is a clean signal) and with E being false (a misread, an overeager leak, someone's wishful thinking).

Spell out how that plays out here, including roughly which of those terms is doing the actual work in this case (a rumor is, after all, way better evidence about E than a candlelit glance is about cloth).

But a rumor has a source, and the source's track record seems like it should be part of the update. Except if you model the tweet itself as ordinary evidence about E, then you never actually need Jeffrey's rule at all — or you get some regress where nothing is ever observed directly.
