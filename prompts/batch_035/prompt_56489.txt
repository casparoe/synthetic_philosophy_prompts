I've been using a popular meditation app for about a year, mostly for sleep and stress, and nearly every session ends with some version of the same reassurance: you are not your thoughts; you are the awareness that watches them. Lately I've been reading about Buddhist doctrines, specifically the no-self teaching (anatta) and the analysis of persons into five aggregates, and I've started to wonder whether the slogan my app repeats is actually Buddhist at all, or whether it smuggles in a self that Buddhism explicitly denies. If I'm not my thoughts, what is the "I" that remains as the watcher? That sounds like a self to me — a witnessing subject standing behind experience — and my impression is that the no-self doctrine refuses to leave anything standing there, not even a silent observer.

So here is what I'd like. Take the one-liner "you are not your thoughts; you are the awareness that watches them" and lay out the distinct precise claims it could be making, as a menu rather than an interpretation. For example, I can imagine readings ranging from a grammatical point (thoughts occur in experience with you as their subject, not their topic) to an attentional-instruction reading (treat thoughts as passing events rather than action items) to a genuinely metaphysical one (there exists a standing consciousness that is distinct from, and untouched by, its contents) to something like the witness-self posited in Samkhya-yoga, where purusha is a spectator entirely separate from mental activity. For each reading, tell me: is it trivial, substantive but defensible, or false; is it compatible with the anatta doctrine as classically formulated, or does it re-introduce a self at one remove; and would the reading actually deliver the calm the meditation instruction is promising? It's fine to bring in the five aggregates framework, the distinction between denying a substantial self and denying any subject of experience at all, or any other standard machinery — I just want the readings ordered from most defensible to least, with a verdict on each.

Two things I specifically want you not to skip. First: which reading do the people repeating the slogan probably need for the practice to work — is the meditation instruction doing real work only on the weaker readings, so that the metaphysical-sounding version is doing the selling while the deflationary version does the work? And second, where does this leave someone like me: if the strongest defensible reading still falls short of the doctrine (or contradicts it), should I conclude that the app is teaching something else entirely and just using Buddhist furniture to furnish it? I'd rather have an honest disambiguation than a defense of the slogan, so if some readings collapse into each other or turn out to be unfalsifiable, say that plainly.
