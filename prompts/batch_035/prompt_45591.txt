I'm the newest member of the ethics committee at a mid-sized hospital, and I've started to suspect we don't actually have one consistent rule for when someone is dead — just a pile of precedents that several different rules would fit equally well. Our record so far:

1. Textbook brain death: massive hemorrhage, coma, no brainstem reflexes, failed apnea test, two exams six hours appart. Heart still beating on the ventilator. We declared death and organs were recovered.
2. Cardiac arrest in the ER; the team worked the code for half an hour, called it, waited five minutes, declared death. No procurement, the family had declined donation.
3. A woman in a permanent vegetative state — unconscious two years, cortex gone on imaging, but she breathes on her own, brainstem intact. Family asked whether she could be declared dead so her organs could be donated. We said no: she's alive, and withdrawing the feeding tube would be letting a living person die, not declaring a dead one.
4. A DCD case: devastating brain injury but not brain dead, family withdrew the ventilator, heart stopped, five minute wait, death declared, organs recovered.

Next month we get: a patient who meets every clinical criterion — coma, no brainstem reflexes, failed apnea test — but imaging and labs show the hypothalamus is intact and doing its job: no diabetes insipidus, temperature regulated fine. Our neurologist says this happens more often than people assume. The transplant team is definetely keen and the family is on board.

So: do we declare death? Honestly I care less about the yes/no than about everything around it. Which rules actually fit our record so far? What does each of them predict for this case? What's your best guess, and why? And which further case would best tell the surviving rules apart? Also flag anything in our existing record that already looks shaky.
