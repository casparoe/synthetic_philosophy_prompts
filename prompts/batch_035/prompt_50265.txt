so I'm a novelist, four books in, all of them fairly realist, and the new one follows a woman who prices catastrophe risk at an insurance firm during the year before a financial crisis. I've gotten a bit obsessed with Keynes for this — not the policy Keynes everyone knows, but the earlier Keynes who wrote a whole treatise on probability: probability as degree of rational belief rather than frequency or just subjective vibes, the idea that some probabilities can't be compared at all (you can't always say one thing is more likely than another), and then the later Keynes of the famous stuff about not really knowing what the odds are on a war or the copper price thirty years out. The more I read the more I realize there are all these live interpretive fights in the literature, and as a fiction writer I can't chase all of them.

Here's my actual problem. I have maybe a year of research before I need to be drafting, and I want the epistemology under the novel to be honest — I don't want to cheat and smuggle in a character who magically "just knows" when she's facing real uncertainty versus a calculable risk, if that distinction even holds up. So: which of the open questions in Keynes-on-probability-and-uncertainty matter most for what I'm doing, and which are overrated for my purposes even if they matter a lot to economists?

The candidates I've picked up from my (admittedly half-understood) reading, roughly:

1. Whether "weight of argument" — Keynes's idea that two beliefs can have the same probability but differ in how much evidence backs them — is a real separate dimension or just collapses back into probability somehow. If it's real, it seems almost like a novelistic concept: a character can be 60% confident on thin evidence or thick evidence, and it should feel different from the inside.

2. Whether the later radical-uncertainty Keynes actually repudiates the Treatise, or is continuous with it — whether the guy who thought there were objective logical relations between evidence and conclusion gave up on that, and when, and how much.

3. Whether Keynesian uncertainty is supposed to be a fact about the world being non-ergodic (the future genuinely isn't like draws from a stable distribution) or just a fact about our ignorance — because those write very differently. In one version my character's models are doomed; in the other her problem is that she's limited.

4. Whether Keynes's "conventional judgment" story — the bit where we decide to feel confident about the long run by pretending existing conditions will continue, basically because it keeps us sane — generalizes beyond financial markets, to things like marriage and career and whether to have kids, which is honestly where my novel wants to go.

Rank those for me, and defend the ordering — I want the criteria you're using, not just the list. For the purposes of the ranking, assume the deliverable is a novel in which characters make decisions under uncertainty and the reader has to feel the difference between the kinds of uncertainty they face, not a dissertation. So tractability for you means something like: how much would getting this question wrong actually show up on the page, and how hard is it for a reasonably careful novelist to get it right. Tell me also which ones I should just stop worrying about — where the scholarly fight, even if it's real and unresolved, makes no visible difference to fiction. And what would change your ranking: e.g. if I commit to the character being a hardened practitioner rather than a young theorist, or if the crisis she's navigating is one where the people with the most data did the worst.

One more thing — spare me the obvious point that novelists don't need to get the philosophy right, plenty of great novels get their science wrong. I know. I'm the kind of writer who can't fake it; if I don't believe the epistemology, the prose goes dead on me and I can hear it. That's the constraint I'm working under.
