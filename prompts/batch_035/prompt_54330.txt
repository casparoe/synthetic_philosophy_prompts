Honestly I'm stuck on what to make of the transitivity of "better than" debate. I'm a 2nd-year PhD student in philosophy working on axiology, and it seems like nobody has moved in decades. The economists and decision theorists treat transitivity as pretty much constitutive of rational preference: if you prefer A to B and B to C but C to A, you're incoherent, and you're vulnerable to a money pump, so an agent's preferences over outcomes must form a transitive order. Meanwhile philosophers like Larry Temkin use spectrum cases to argue that the relation "better than" itself just isn't transitive -- chain together lives or experiences that differ only imperceptibly or by some tiny dimension of value, each one clearly better than the next, and yet the end of the chain is obviously worse than the start, so at some link "better than" has to break.

Here's the thing though. When you push each side, the economists I've talked to seem fine conceding that the *evaluative* relation might fail to be transitive as long as *choice* is coherent, and Temkin himself says choices should still be made in a consistent, transitive way even if the underlying ranking isn't transitive. So my working hypothesis is that this is two debates being run under one name: one about an axiological relation (what the world's ranking is like) and one about norms of choice (what an agent must do), and each side keeps refuting the other's claim as though it were an answer to their own question. Is that diagnosis right, or is there a genuine substantive disagreement hiding in there -- maybe about whether "better" is essentially comparative in a way that choice can't inherit? And given your diagnosis, what would count as actual progress here: a new kind of spectrum case, a formal distinction between evaluation and choice, or just the two literatures finally reading each other?
