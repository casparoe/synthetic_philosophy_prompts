Analyze the 2005 Corrupted Blood incident in World of Warcraft as a case study in the debate over whether virtual objects and virtual harms are real, and then assess what the episode does to the philosophical positions. For reference, the salient facts: a raid boss in the Zul'Gurub dungeon inflicted a debuff that dealt damage over time and spread between characters standing near each other; the effect was intended to be confined to that dungeon, but players found they could dismiss an infected hunter pet inside the raid and re-summon it in the outside world, after which the debuff behaved like a contagious disease in the game's major cities. Low-level characters died repeatedly, corpses piled up in the streets, and players displayed recognizable epidemic behavior: many fled the cities or avoided them, healers stationed themselves as volunteer medics, and a minority deliberately ran infected characters into crowds to keep the outbreak going. Blizzard eventually ended it with an emergency patch, and real-world epidemiologists later wrote the episode up as an unusually realistic model of population behavior during an outbreak.

Please do a careful mapping rather than a generic pro-and-con. Specifically:

1. Identify exactly what the candidate harms are. Characters who died simply respawned, and the death penalty in that game was trivial; the costs that remain are things like wasted time, frustration, and disruption of a shared social space. Say which of these, if any, a theory of virtual harm has to vindicate for the episode to be a case of genuine harm.

2. Distinguish the two live positions: the view that virtual objects and events are real (digital objects that really exist, events that really happen, just implemented in hardware) versus the view that they are fictions or illusions we participate in. Ask whether the first view automatically delivers realism about virtual harms, or whether one can consistently be a realist about virtual objects while denying that anything bad happened to anyone in any serious sense.

3. Pay attention to the griefers. If a design flaw alone had spread the debuff, few would speak of harm; the malicious intent seems to be doing the work. Consider whether this shows that the real harm was always an ordinary offline harm to the player's experience, and whether conceding that undercuts the claim that virtual harms, as such, are real.

4. Say what, if anything, follows from the epidemiologists' interest. The event turned out to be scientifically useful as a model of real epidemics; does that give any evidence about the metaphysical status of what happened, or is it just similarity without relevance?

5. Finally, be honest about where the relevant theories are silent: they may have nothing to say about whether disrupting a game's social space wrongs anyone, or about Blizzard's role as a sovereign who legislated the harm away by decree. Conclude by saying whether the episode supports realism about virtual harms, tells against it, or reveals that the debate was never really about cases like this one.
