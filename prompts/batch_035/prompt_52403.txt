For a number of years I have followed the literature on higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia, and I have come to believe that the central dispute has settled into a stalemate its participants seldom acknowledge. Let me lay out the terrain as I see it, and then ask you to diagnose the disagreement itself rather than adjudicate it.

Take the akratic combination first: a subject believes p while also believing that her total evidence does not adequately support p, or, more pointedly, while believing that she should give up believing p. Nearly everyone in the literature condemns this combination as irrational; the fight is about why, and it fans out into at least three sub-disputes. First, there is a dispute about the status of higher-order evidence: does it behave like ordinary first-order evidence, simply adding weight to one side or the other, or does it operate in a structurally distinct way — undercutting, screening off, or reversing the support one's first-order evidence provides? Those fond of the slogan that evidence of evidence is evidence line up on one side; those who think higher-order evidence is special, because it bears on the reliability of the very processes that generate the first-order support, line up on the other. Second, there is a dispute about explanation: some hold that the irrationality of the akratic combination is garden-variety incoherence, describable with the existing tools of partial and undercutting defeat, while others think it reveals a distinct epistemic norm — something like a prohibition on stepping on one's own evidence — that demands its own story. Third, and least often stated, there is a quiet disagreement about possibility: some suspect the akratic combination cannot be sustained at all, because the higher-order belief defeats the first-order support so thoroughly that the subject cannot rationally go on believing p, in which case the problem of akrasia partially dissolves into the problem of defeat.

The intuitions that keep these disputes alive come from a small stable of cases: the subject on medication she knows undermines her reasoning who rechecks the calculation anyway and holds the result; the judge who believes a defendant guilty while believing she is too drunk to be trusted; the mathematician who believes a proof is sound because she has checked it, in the teeth of a poor track record with her own checking; disagreement about the correct response to disagreement itself. Meanwhile the parallel debate between conciliationists and steadfast theorists about peer disagreement has reached a mirror-image impasse: conciliationism seems to follow from very weak-looking impartiality principles, yet every version faces stubborn counterexamples, and nobody has moved in years.

Here is my hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis. I suspect that a good part of the standoff is verbal, and that the operative ambiguity concerns the word evidence: if evidence is read broadly, as anything that bears on the truth of p, then higher-order evidence is ordinary evidence and the deflationary side wins by stipulation; if it is read narrowly, as input to first-order inquiry, the structural side wins by stipulation; and the real questions hide in the fact that both sides slide between the readings. But I also suspect something non-verbal is doing real work — namely, that the two camps are answering different questions under one heading, one about rational requirements on combinations of attitudes, the other about what a responsible inquirer, with a limited perspective and a life to lead, should actually do.

Diagnose the disagreement for me. Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually says — the principles they endorse, the cases they press, the replies they standardly give — rather than in a survey of positions. Tell me how much of the standoff is verbal in the way I have suggested, how much is a substantive dispute about the structure of epistemic normativity, and how much, if any, rests on bedrock intuitions about cases that neither side can argue for. Is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed, or merely that it is hard? And given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress — a formal model of higher-order evidence, a conceptual distinction, a new kind of case, a change of question — or argue that none should be expected. Please write your answer in American English.
