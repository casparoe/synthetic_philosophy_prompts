I'm a novelist, and the book I've been circling for two years now is about a woman declared brain dead after a hemorrhage whose husband refuses to accept it, so I've ended up reading way more of the clinical and philosophical literature on death than any fiction writer reasonably needs. Somewhere along the way I've actually settled on a position, and I'd like someone to stress-test it rather than just nod along like my editor does. The position is this: what dies, when a person dies, is the capacity for consciousness -- everything that matters about being someone. So the real line between life and death is the permanent loss of that capacity, which means whole-brain death criteria are actually too conservative (breathing being driven by a brainstem doesn't make you alive if there's nobody home), and circulatory death only counts as death because stopping the heart destroys the brain shortly after -- the heart is a proxy, not the thing itself. On my view, a patient in a permanently unconscious state with a functioning brainstem -- no awareness ever again, by any account -- is dead in the only sense that could matter, even though under current law she'd be treated as a living patient.

Now, I know this isn't some wild idiosyncratic take of mine -- it's roughly the higher-brain or consciousness-based position, and plenty of philosophers and even some clinicians have argued versions of it. But that's exactly why I want the adversarial version: what would actually count against it? And I don't mean the standard hand-wringing, I mean the full map of exposure. Which cases would genuinely hurt it -- I keep thinking about the real-world reports of brain dead bodies kept on support for months, ones that went through puberty, even carried a fetus to some stage of gestation, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's evidence against me (a body that can grow and gestate sure doesn't look dead) or exactly what my view should predict (the person is gone; the body is a well-maintained organ system, like a heart on ice)? And the anencephalic infants, who never had the capacity to begin with -- does my view imply they never lived at all, and is that a bullet to bite or a fatal wound? Which intuitions does my position stand or fall with, what does it predict that the rival views don't, and is there any observation -- in medicine, in law, wherever -- that could come along and refute it rather than just embarrass it?

Two things I want you to take seriously. First, the argument I've found most seductive from my side, and I want to know if it's as good as it feels: the dead donor rule in organ transplantation is supposed to mean you never take organs from the living, but donation-after-circulatory-death protocols involve taking hearts after just a few minutes of stopped circulation, well before the loss is irreversible in the strict sense -- which suggests the whole framework quietly depends on something like my view (the brain and consciousness) while officially pretending to depend on the circulation story. Is that right, or am I misreading how the "permanent versus irreversible" distinction is doing the work? Second, I want honesty about whether my position has been qualified into unfalsifiability -- I can feel myself saying things like "dead in the only sense that matters," which is the kind of phrase that can absorb any counterexample by redefining "matters." If consciousness-based definitions of death have been insulated that way, tell me whether that's fatal for a definition of death specifically or just par for the course in conceptual analysis. Sort the defeaters from merely awkward to decisive, and say which ones a committed defender of the view would concede as counting against it versus dismiss. This is genuinely for the book, but also, having spent this long in the ICU literature, I'd like to know if I've talked myself into something fragile.
