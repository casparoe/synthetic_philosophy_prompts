I'm a novelist working on a book set in a coastal town about sixty years from now, and the hinge of the whole plot is a decision made in the opening chapter: the town council, back in the present day, can either fund a seawall plus cheap fossil-fueled growth that keeps everyone's lives comfortable for a generation, or go for an expensive, disruptive decarbonization plan that makes the first few decades noticeably worse for the people then living. Here's the part I need help with: I want the choice to be one where the children born under the two paths aren't just differently-off, they're literally different people, because the disruption of the green plan changes commuting patterns, who meets whom, marriages, everything, so the grandchildren of the town under plan A don't exist under plan B. Every one of those future lives is worth living either way — nobody is born into misery — but under the seawall path the town's great-grandchildren have to relocate inland, lose the cemetery and the harbor, lives still worth living but clearly shaped by a decision made before their parents were born.

So what I'm asking for is this: construct the scenario properly, to specification, as a thought experiment rather than just a plot device. The specs are: (1) no sci-fi, no time machines, and no villain — the causal chain from the council's vote to different people existing has to be mundane and believable (I want readers nodding along, not rolling their eyes); (2) the future people under both paths must have lives worth living, so no future individual is worse off than they would otherwise *have been* — the non-identity problem has to do real work here, not be hand-waved; (3) the two verdicts I need are, roughly, that a person-affecting view of intergenerational ethics says no one is wronged by the seawall vote, while something like a total or impersonal view says the vote was still wrong, and the case has to actually generate those verdicts from the theories, not just stipulate them. After you lay out the scenario — one paragraph of it, with the human texture a novel needs — walk me through how each view reaches its verdict, tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work (I suspect it's the "lives worth living either way" clause, but I'm honestly not sure), and what happens to the verdicts if I remove that feature. Also give me the strongest way a defender of the person-affecting view would attack the whole setup, since one of my characters needs to make that argument convincingly and not be a strawman.

One more thing, and please take it seriously: tell me where the construction is most likely to fail or cheat — like, whether the scenario quietly smuggles in a harmed person, or whether "different people would exist" is doing less work than it appears to once you remember that identity is also fixed under the green plan. And I want you to say what would change your verdict about whether the case works at all: if, say, the relocation turns out to count as a harm to the seawall-path people by their own lights (they grieve the harbor), does the person-affecting view get a foothold after all and my neat opposition collapses? If the case is fragile in some way I haven't anticipated, I'd rather know now than three drafts in. Don't be confident just because the plot needs you to be.
