i cofund a small grants program and we're about to commit real money to one thing: a working group, two philosophers plus two statisticians, for about eighteen months, on the conceptual fallout of the impossibility results in fair machine learning — the Kleinberg/Mullainathan/Raghavan and Chouldechova results showing you can't simultaneously have calibration within each group and equal false-positive or false-negative rates across groups whenever the underlying base rates differ. the ML people have mostly shrugged and moved on to tweaking models, and the law side keeps asking "so which criterion should we legislate?" as if it's an open empirical question. i think there's a real pile of unsorted philosophical work here and i want the group pointed at the right parts of it, not re-deriving what's known or writing another survey.

you're a philosopher of science who's sat on enough fairness-and-ML panels to know which debates are live and which are zombie debates that keep going because everyone has a stock answer. think carefully before you write anything — actually brainstorm first, weigh what other questions depend on what, what's tractable for exactly this kind of group (no lab, no compute, two philosophers and two statisticians), and what would actually change how a regulator or a model vendor behaves versus just generate another paper nobody reads. i have my own hunches (e.g. that a chunk of the fight is over whether "the true base rate" is even well-defined when your labels come from historically biased decisions, and whether calibration has intrinsic moral weight or is only instrumentally valuable), but don't just flatter those — if something outranks them, say so.

when you're done thinking, your actual reply should be lean: exactly five numbered items and nothing else, no intro, no conclusion. each item is one open question worth the group's time, stated in a sentence, plus one sentence defending its slot — what sits downstream of it, or why it's tractable for this group, or why the more obvious-looking candidates rank below it. if something can't be said in two sentences, it's probably not sharp enough to fund.
