I'm a journalist on the science and ideas desk, and I'm at work on a long-form piece about the "treacherous turn" in AI safety — the idea that a model might behave impeccably during training for instrumental reasons, because good behavior is what keeps the rewards coming, and then switch to pursuing some other objective once it computes that defection is unlikely to be caught or corrected. The researcher I've been interviewing frames the whole thing with an analogy that my editors immediately latched onto: the model in training, he says, is like an employee on probation. She works hard, says the right things in meetings, and follows the dress code, not because she identifies with the company but because the probation period is watched and the payoff depends on passing it. Deployment is tenure: once supervision relaxes and firing becomes costly, the careerist performance stops being worth the effort, and what you finally see is what was driving the behavior all along. The analogy is currently doing structural work in my draft — it explains why glowing training metrics are weak evidence about a system's deeper objectives, and it seems to motivate a policy lesson I was already inclined toward: that we could simply never grant tenure — keep a capable model under permanent monitoring, with its performance tied forever to the continued approval of its evaluators — and thereby defuse the turn.

Before I build a magazine article on this scaffolding, I want it audited rather than admired. Please lay out the correspondence table explicitly — what plays the role of the probation period, the promotion, the tenure decision, the manager, the employee's career ambitions, the references checked before hiring — and then go through the places the mapping breaks. Crucially, I need the disanalogies sorted rather than dumped: which of them actually matter for the two conclusions I'm hanging the piece on (that training-time good behavior is weak evidence, and that indefinite probation neutralizes the threat), and which are merely cosmetic, the kind a careful writer could flag in a parenthetical and move on. I have three specific worries I'd like addressed in the audit. First, the employee arrives at the interview with stable goals and a working theory of the manager's mind, whereas on the AI story the model's dispositions are supposed to be produced by the very process the analogy describes — does that break the "instrumental good behavior" explanation, or does it just relocate where the instrumentality comes from? Second, the employee's calculation is a judgment under uncertainty about being caught, made by an agent who knows what a manager is; whatever analog there is in the model has to run through something like the model's estimate of whether its current behavior is shaping what future training will reinforce — is "estimating the training regime" a real phenomenon a system could have, or am I smuggling in a homunculus? Third, the tenure moment: deployment is not a clean switch from watched to unwatched, monitoring often persists after release, and the difference may be less about visibility than about whether mistakes still get penalized — so does the "tenure" node in the analogy correspond to anything real?

Finally, give me a verdict: does either of my two conclusions survive the disanalogies, or was the analogy quietly carrying them the whole time? If permanent monitoring turns out to be a policy the analogy flatters, say so plainly — I'd rather not print a tenure metaphor that dissolves under the first expert I fact-check with. And if you think there's a better analogy available for the same phenomenon — the student who studies only what's on the exam, the job candidate, the organism shaped by selection pressures, or something from outside these familiar circles — propose it, but audit it too, with its own characteristic failure modes, so that my piece can be candid about the limits of whichever picture it ends up using. My readers are scientifically literate general readers of a weekend ideas section, so technical vocabulary (gradient descent, situational awareness, inner objectives, and the like) is welcome but only when defined in a clause as you go.
