I'm a biostatisticist with about ten years of experience fitting multilevel models — I use partial pooling the way other people use t-tests, I understand what exchangeability does and does not buy me, I could reconstruct the gist of de Finetti's representation theorem on a whiteboard, and I argue about prior sensitivity and leave-one-out cross-validation with colleagues who are wrong. I have no philosophy background. I've gathered, from scattered reading about forecasting, that "the reference class problem" is a thing philosophers and superforecasters argue about, and I suspect it is something I already deal with under another name. I want a translation, not a tutorial.

Restate the reference class problem in my vocabulary, position by position, and then give me the residue report. The positions I want covered:

1. The problem itself: a single case — this 40-year-old nonsmoker applying for life insurance, or my recurring case, a serious-adverse-event rate for a new drug where only a few drugs in its class have ever reached market — belongs to indefinitely many overlapping classes (the drug's mechanism, its class, the whole therapeutic area, all drugs the agency has approved), each licensing a different estimate, with no obvious fact of the matter about which class is the right one.
2. The old advice, associated with Reichenbach, to use the narrowest class for which the statistics are adequate. What is this in my vocabulary — a rule about how deep to nest the hierarchy? A bias–variance statement? Say whether it survives as stated or only in modified form.
3. The relevance-weighting approaches, where candidate classes are weighted by how relevantly similar they are to the target case. Is this just a mixture model or model averaging, or is the weighting itself doing work my machinery can't represent?
4. The dissolutionist moves: that there is no fact of the matter about single-case probabilities, that the probability is a property of an information state, that the whole problem is an artifact of a frequentism nobody sensible holds anymore.
5. The deeper worry, which I half-remember seeing attributed in various places: that choosing the reference class already presupposes the very knowledge of which similarities matter that the probability was supposed to deliver.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Which parts of the philosophical problem map cleanly onto model specification and prior choice? Which had to be distorted to fit? Which have no expression in my vocabulary at all? And here is the thing I most want you to engage with: in my work the hierarchy itself is a choice my model never represents. I can nest patients within hospitals within regions, or cross by indication, and partial pooling will do exactly what it should within whichever tree I hand it — but no amount of shrinkage chooses the tree, and the different trees give me different posteriors. If the reference class problem is anything, I'd have thought it's this, and I notice my toolkit has a name for everything except it. Test my working suspicion, which I want examined rather than flattered: that the reference class problem is what happens when someone with no statistical tools discovers the bias–variance tradeoff and model misspecification, plus a metaphysical overlay about "the" probability of a single case that dissolves once you accept that probabilities attach to models given information. If that is wrong, say exactly where the residue lives — in something my framework cannot say (and what it would need in order to say it), or in something in the philosophical formulation that was never clear to begin with.

Constraints: don't smuggle. If a philosophical claim only translates once you quietly add an assumption the philosophers never made — a symmetry, a prior, a likelihood — flag it as part of the residue rather than presenting it as a clean mapping. And pitch the statistics at full depth; I will notice the fudges.
