So for a philosophy of language course I'm writing a paper on slurs, and over the semester I've basically talked myself into a position and now I'm scared it's one of those views that survives by being endlessly adjustable. The view, roughly: a slur isn't primarily a contempt-expressor and it doesn't primarily describe anyone badly -- it presupposes a social ranking. It works like an inverted honorific or title, the way addressing someone as "your grace" presupposes they occupy a rank; the slur presupposes its target group sits below the speaker's, and that content projects. That, I want to say, is why the thing stays foul under negation, in the antecedent of conditionals, inside reported speech, even from speakers who obviously hold no contempt -- the presupposition rides along wherever the word goes. And it gives me a story about reclamation I actually like: in-group uses aren't a special permission to utter a forbidden word, they're the target group refusing the ranking itself, which is why reclaimed uses feel like they have genuinely different meaning rather than the same meaning said louder.

The rivals I'm trying to beat: the expressivist line where slurs work like interjections or expletives, displaying an attitude rather than contributing content; the prohibitionist line where the word just encodes that uttering it violates a norm, full stop; and the fully pragmatic accounts where the offense is all cancellable context stuff. What I want from you: what would actually count against my presupposition-of-rank view? Give me the defeaters ranked from fatal through serious through merely awkward. Some things I already half-worry about, feel free to add more: uses of slurs by speakers with zero contempt (sociologists, people quoting song lyrics, kids who don't know what they've said); slur-like words for groups the speaker doesn't actually rank below anyone; slurs that seem to degrade people for contingent properties rather than group membership at all; and whether honorifics even have the projection profile I'm borrowing, or whether I'm quietly taking a feature from one class of words and stapling it onto another.

Two more things. First, tell me which objections a committed defender of the view would concede genuinely counts against it versus which they'd wave off, and whether the waving-off is legitimate or whether it's the view being insulated by ad hoc patches -- if the honest answer is "it's been qualified into unfalsifiability and that's actually fine for this kind of view," I can live with that, but I want it said out loud. Second, and this is the part I'll actually be graded on: be explicit about where your own ranking is most likely to fail. If you ranked objection A above objection B, what consideration would flip that, and if you end up concluding the view is basically fine, what would I have to discover about how slurs behave (some class of uses, some cross-linguistic pattern, whatever) that should make me abandon it? Don't be diplomatic, I'd rather find out now than in the margins.
