Me and my friend Josh got into a huge argument about this after watching a documentary on the Concorde (he's an econ undergrad, so of course). His take, delivered with total confidence: "The sunk cost fallacy is one of the most established results in decision science. Past expenditures are never relevant to what you should do next — the money's gone either way, so only future costs and benefits can matter. Psychologists proved decades ago that people violate this constantly, which is exactly why the British and French kept pouring money into the Concorde, why companies finish doomed IT projects, why people sit through terrible movies. So anyone who honors sunk costs — finishing a book they hate, staying in a relationship because of the years already put in — is straight up irrational, and the fix is to just train yourself to completely ignore what you've already spent."

I went along with most of it at the time (the movie thing stung, I recently sat through a three hour film out of pure guilt), but on the walk home something bugged me and I couldnt put my finger on what. Like, when I finish my degree because I've already done three years of it, is that really the same bias as the Concorde? It also felt like he was sliding between two claims — "past spending can't change what's optimal from here on" and "the fact that you're thinking about the past at all proves you're irrational" — but maybe those are the same and I just don't get it? And intuitively it seems like knowing what I chose or valued in the past could tell me something about my situation now, though I couldn't turn that into an actual objection and he was pretty clearly the expert in the room.

So go through his little speech for me: which parts would an economist or decision theorist actually endorse, which parts are overstated or quietly conflating different things, and does his final move — that anyone who honors sunk costs is irrational, full stop — actually follow from the parts that are true? Also curious whether the advice at the end, "just train yourself to ignore what you've already spent," is as safe as he makes it sound, or whether a careful version of his own view would add some fine print there.
