I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm writing a feature on what physics supposedly tells us about the nature of time. A popular physics book I've been reading summarizes the situation like this: 'Special relativity shows there is no objective now: observers moving relative to each other disagree about which distant events are simultaneous, so there can be no fact about what is happening "right now" anywhere else in the universe. From this, Einstein's one-time teacher Hermann Minkowski derived the block universe: past, present, and future events all exist in exactly the same sense, and the passage of time is an illusion. On this picture your birth and your death are both out there in spacetime, and free will is incompatible with modern physics.' I have the feeling this slides from a settled physics result into a contested philosophical position, but the author is a working physicist and I'm not confident where the overreach begins. Take the role of a philosopher of physics who is impatient with popularized distortion, think carefully about each claim before committing to a verdict, and then audit the summary claim by claim: what a physicist would accept, what a philosopher of physics would accept only with heavy qualification, and what is just wrong. In particular, tell me whether the move from the relativity of simultaneity to the block universe is a derivation, an inference, or an additional thesis someone might reject while accepting all of special relativity, and whether the free-will conclusion follows from any accurate part of the summary — I'd rather not build my piece around an error I can't see.
