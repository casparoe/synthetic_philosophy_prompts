I've arrived at mathematical platonism by way of the indispensability argument: our best scientific theories quantify over mathematical objects, and we should be ontologically committed to all and only the entities quantified over by our best theories, so mathematical objects exist in the same sense that electrons do. I hold two refinements of my own. First, I take the view to be robust under scientific revolution: even as physics is rebuilt, whatever mathematics ends up indispensable to its successor will be close enough in ontology that the platonist concedes nothing that matters — the ontology may get re-cut, but it will not shrink in a way that should worry a realist about mathematics. Second, I hold that if the continuum hypothesis ever gets settled, it will be settled by indispensability-type considerations — by appeal to what mathematics our best science needs — and not by the intrinsic self-evidence of axioms that Gödel appealed to.

Tell me what would count against this view. I don't want only the standard objections (Benacerraf's epistemic challenge, Field's nominalization program) rehearsed; I want a map of what the view actually risks. Specifically, please address:

(a) Whether a completed Field-style nominalization of current science, one that preserved explanatory power and not mere predictive adequacy, would be fatal — or whether the platonist could then retreat to some worse-science-is-still-better-than-abstract-objects position without that retreat being an ad hoc qualification of the original argument.

(b) Whether the observation that practicing scientists use mathematics while plainly not asserting its existence — Melia's weaseling maneuver — genuinely undermines the confirmational-holism premise, or whether it only shows that scientists' surface assertions are an unreliable guide to their commitments.

(c) Whether the view's index-relative ontology — revising what mathematics exists as science revises itself, as Maddy presses — leaves it predicting nothing its rivals don't, and whether that kind of insulation is a problem for a view of this kind or an appropriate feature of a broadly naturalist one.

(d) Whether the awkward fact that indispensability arguably fails to underwrite the parts of mathematics platonists actually care about — large cardinals, the continuum hypothesis, anything beyond what physics happens to consume — is a defect in the view or, since my second refinement bites the bullet here, something it should wear openly as a consequence.

Where you can, use explanatory applications of mathematics — the prime-numbered life cycles of periodical cicadas as a resistance to predators being the canonical case — as a test bed for which defeaters are serious: ask what it would take for such cases to be nominalized without explanatory loss, and whether anything short of that should move me at all.

Rank the defeaters you identify from fatal through serious to merely awkward, say for each whether a committed indispensability platonist would concede it as counting against the view or would be entitled to dismiss it, and end with an honest assessment: if nothing short of a finished, explanatory-power-preserving nominalization of science could move me, is the resulting view falsifiable in any meaningful sense — and if not, is that a defect in the view or just what it means for an ontological thesis to be evaluated holistically, in Quine's spirit, rather than refuted point by point?
