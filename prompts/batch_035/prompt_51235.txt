I'm on the curriculum sub-committee for a school history qualification, and we've got a concrete decision to make at next month's meeting, so I need a written opinion to circulate beforehand.

Background: our flagship paper is "The Rise of the West, 1750–1914" — industrialisation, Enlightenment, empire, told basically from a European vantage. Two of our members propose replacing it with a global paper built on what they call synchronic cross-sections: instead of one narrative arc, students take snapshot years — say 1571, 1687, 1789, 1884 — and study what's happening simultaneously in Qing China, the Ottoman world, Mughal and then colonial India, West Africa, and Europe, plus the connective tissue (silver flows, sugar and chattel slavery, print, steam). Their pitch is that this finally kills the Eurocentrism by refusing to make Europe the protagonist of everyone's story.

The obvious objection, which another member raises every single time: a history needs a centre of gravity, and if you claim to narrate from everywhere at once you're either being dishonest (some perspective gets smuggled back in anyway — the very choice of 1789 as a snapshot year is itself Eurocentric!) or you hand the students a calendar rather than a history, with no account of why anything happened or how it hung together. And there's a third faction who reckon the whole debate is confused: all narration is from somewhere, so the honest fix is just to relabel the existing paper as European history, plainly, and add a seperate non-European paper — polycentrism through separation rather than synthesis.

Draft the memo I'll circulate. It should recommend one concrete course of action — adopt the cross-section paper as proposed, adopt with modifications, relabel-and-separate, or keep the current paper but rework its framing — with the reasoning spelt out, and it has to give the strongest version of the objection to whatever you recommend, not a strawman. Where you think the "a view from everywhere is really a view from nowhere" worry has a point, say so; where you think it's confused about what a perspective even is, say that too. And be honest about whether the cross-section approach can actually carry causal explanation or whether it trades narrative for juxtaposition, and what that trade is worth for sixteen-year-olds specifically. Lead with the recommendation in the very first sentence, then the reasoning — no preamble and no restating the background we all already know.
