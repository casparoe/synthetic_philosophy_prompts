I'm taking a philosophy of mind course, and we've just reached split-brain cases. What strikes me is not the experimental results themselves but the way everyone around the patient keeps behaving, institutionally speaking, as though nothing had changed. After a corpus callosotomy performed for refractory epilepsy, the patient remains one legal person: one vote, one set of rights, one medical record. When a further procedure is proposed, it is the patient — in practice the speaking left hemisphere — who gives or withholds informed consent; capacity assessments and courtroom proceedings treat the patient as a single subject of experiences and decisions. Yet the experiments that made these patients famous, in which stimuli are lateralized so that each hemisphere answers independently, suggest that after the surgery the two hemispheres can hold different beliefs and make different choices, with only one of them able to talk about it. The non-speaking hemisphere can still express preferences by pointing or arranging letters, and sometimes those answers diverge from what the patient says aloud.

So my question is what this cluster of practices presupposes about personal identity and the unity of consciousness. Does the law, along with the clinic, quietly rest on a bodily-continuity view, on which you remain the same person so long as the same living body and brain persist, whatever happens inside it? Would a Lockean view on which persons are constituted by psychological connectedness — at least on its stronger readings — actually struggle here, since the connectedness between the hemispheres is severed while each hemisphere remains connected to itself over time? Or is the practice philosophically underdetermined: compatible with several theories of personal identity, so long as none of them is pushed to the point of counting the isolated right hemisphere as a separate person?

Please reconstruct the implicit view as precisely as you can, indicate which rival theories the practice quietly rules out, and then tell me what would actually have to change if we took seriously the idea that there are two subjects: would the right hemisphere need its own consent, its own advocate, its own record? And is the observation that these patients seem to function as one person in ordinary life, outside the laboratory, a reason to think the presupposed view is correct — or mainly a reason the question never comes up?
