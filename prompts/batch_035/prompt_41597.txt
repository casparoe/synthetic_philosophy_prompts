Something I've never quite been able to square: the EU's whole enlargement setup seems to quietly assume Fukuyama was right, while another part of the very same treaties seems to assume he was wrong. Hear me out.

To get in, a country has to clear the Copenhagen criteria — stable democracy, rule of law, protection of minorities, a functioning market economy. And the implicit bet behind letting a country in once it clears them seems to be that, once these things are in place and the country is inside the club, they're basically self-sustaining. That's presumably why there's no mechanism for kicking a member out: the treaties treat accession as a door that only opens inward, as if there's some point pass which a liberal democracy just doesn't revert. Which is basically a end-of-history claim, no? Not a declared final endpoint of political development, exactly, but at least a one-way ratchet: get over the threshold and history stops pulling you backwards.

Except the same treaties also contain Article 7, a procedure for suspending a member's rights when there's a clear risk of it going authoritarian, and in recent years a budget conditionality mechanism that withholds money over rule-of-law breaches. Those only make sense if backsliding is a live, ongoing threat from members who are already in — Hungary being the obvious case everyone has in mind. So one half of the system is built like liberal democracy is an absorbing state, and the other half is built like it isn't.

So here's what I want to know. What theory of political development does the enlargement machinery actually presuppose — a genuinely directional view of history, a weaker claim that economic and institutional entanglement "locks in" democracy once you're inside a big single market, or something even more modest like integration just raises the cost of autocracy until it's not worth it? Can one consistent theory underwrite both the one-way door and the backsliding alarms, or do the two halves rest on incompatible pictures that just happen to sit in the same treaty text? And say I'm right that the presupposed view is quasi-Fukuyaman: what would have to change in practice to make the whole thing consistent — or is the current setup actually fine because the criteria only need to hold at the moment of accession, and everything after that is somebody else's department? I'd also take an answer that says the whole thing is philosophically underdetermined and several different theories of history would license the exact same design, but if you go that route, tell me which theories and why they all fit.
