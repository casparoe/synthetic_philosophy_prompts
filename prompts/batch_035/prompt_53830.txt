My dad died back in March, and while clearing out his apartment I found an old tape of the original 1954 Gojira — the Japanese cut, not the Raymond Burr one — which turns out he'd kept from a tour he did in Japan in the 70s. I finally watched it last week and it kind of wrecked me, not at all the rubber-suit campfest I was expecting. Now I've gotten myself roped into hosting a film night at my local library with a "movies and philosophy" theme, and I want to do this one, but I want to actually do it justice rather than just say "Godzilla = the bomb" and call it a night.

So here's what I want from you. The film is obviously soaked in the shadow of Hiroshima and Nagasaki — Godzilla is woken by hydrogen bomb tests, there's contaminated fish causing a market panic (that same year fishermen got irradiated by a real test), doctors walk the wards with Geiger counters, and there's that awful scene of the mother on the ferry holding her kids as the city burns. But the famous moral debate it seems to be speaking to is the one about whether the 1945 bombings were justified: the consequentialist defense that they ended the war and saved more lives than they took, versus the people who say vaporizing cities full of civilians can't be squared with any of it, whatever the arithmetic.

The thing I keep chewing on is that the film's central dilemma doesn't actually map onto that debate, and I want a reading that takes both the film and the ethics seriously instead of fudging it. Dr. Serizawa has a weapon worse than the bomb, he's horrified by it, and he only agrees to use it after witnessing Godzilla's destruction — then he burns his notes and dies in the bay so the secret dies with him. Is that the film's answer to the justification debate: yes, use the terrible weapon to stop ongoing mass death, but only once, and then erase it? That's a fantasy the actual decision-makers never had. Or is the disanalogy the whole point — Godzilla is an enemy that can't surrender, can't be deterred, has no civilians and no cities of its own, so the film stages a world where the consequentialist case is airtight precisely so it can ask what's left over, the guilt and the wish that the weapon had never existed at all? Meanwhile Dr. Yamane, the old scientist, wants Godzilla kept alive rather than killed, which you could read as the researcher's detachment or as something like the deterrence-realist position — the danger is knowledge, the knowledge is valuable, so live with it.

So, for my session: where does the allegory hold, where does it trade on the disanalogy, and does the film actually know the difference? Does it take a position on whether the bombings were justified, or does it dodge by relocating the whole question from wartime enemy action to what is basically a peacetime accident — the test that wakes Godzilla wasn't aimed at anyone? Does the oxygen destroyer ending dramatize something the philosophical literature on the bombings misses — like the fact that the people who used the bomb then spent the rest of their lives trying to uninvent it, which no trolley problem has a slot for?

Give me a reading that's faithful in both directions: don't reduce the film to an illustration of the ethics debate, and don't bend the ethics to fit the film. Point me to the specific scenes I should build the discussion around (I'm assuming people have seen it or will watch it beforehand). And be honest about where the film resists this reading — like whether the "one-time weapon" resolution is the film getting at something true, or just a beautiful cheat. I'd also like to know what you'd say the film adds that the arguments alone don't — my group is smart but mostly hasnt read any philosophy, so I need to know what the movie is doing that a well-written paragraph about just war theory isnt.
