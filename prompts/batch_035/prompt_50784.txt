I'm a PhD student working at the intersection of analytic philosophy of meaning and the continental canon, and next month I'm presenting to a departmental colloquium a paper I've been developing for two years. The thesis, briefly: Camus's absurdism in The Myth of Sisyphus is best read not as a psychological report about alienation but as a substantive normative position in what we would now call the meaning-in-life debate. On my reading, Camus holds that the absence of cosmic significance cannot undermine terrestrial meaning, and that the defiance he demands in the face of the absurd functions like a claim that rebellion itself can contribute to a meaningful life. I then argue that this puts Camus in genuine conflict with Wolf's fitting-fulfillment view: Camus's Sisyphus, on my reading, has meaning through his lucid embrace of his task even though nothing about boulder-rolling connects to anything of independent value -- which is precisely what Wolf's variant of the Sisyphus myth, in which a desire for rock-rolling is implanted so that Sisyphus loves his work, is designed to rule out. The paper's payoff is that this is a real dispute about whether subjective attraction plus lucidity can suffice for meaning, with Camus on one side and the hybrid theorists on the other, rather than the usual dismissal of absurdism as a mood rather than a theory.

The audience is the hard part. The department is composed almost entirely of analytic philosophers of meaning who work in the Wolf and Metz tradition and who are, frankly, skeptical that Camus has an argument rather than a posture. Among them is one scholar trained on the French side who knows Camus's corpus, his engagement with the existentialists, and the secondary literature intimately, and who is on record disliking translations of Camus into the vocabulary of Anglophone value theory. The Wolf students will come with the thought-experiment machinery loaded; the Metz-oriented people will want to know whether Camus's view, so reconstructed, is a pure subjectivism, a hybrid, or something that can't be classified by our taxonomy at all; and the Camus scholar will want to know what the reconstruction costs in fidelity. I have a sketch of replies -- that Nagel's version of the absurd, where the mismatch is between our seriousness and the possibility of taking ourselves seriously from nowhere, is a different target from Camus's confrontation with a silent universe, and that conflating them is what makes absurdism look like a mood -- but I suspect this cuts less ice with this audience than I'd like.

Prepare me a Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this specific room, ordered roughly by how much damage each would do if I flub it, each phrased the way that faction would put it -- the category-mistake objection ("absurdism is about the human condition, not meaning in life, and you've changed the subject"), the equivocation objection about Nagel versus Camus, the objection that the implanted-desire Sisyphus already refutes me, and whatever else this room would genuinely raise. For each, give me a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. Two constraints on the document: first, do not treat anything about interpretation of Camus as settled -- where a reply depends on a contested reading of his text, flag it as contested rather than presenting it as what he clearly meant, and tell me which points I should concede are underdetermined by the text; second, mark clearly the places where I would be presenting a contested scholarly view as if it were consensus, since this room will notice, and one of them is writing a paper about exactly that.
