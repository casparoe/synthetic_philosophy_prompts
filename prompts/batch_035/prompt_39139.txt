I hold Dennett's real patterns view of intentional states: beliefs and desires are real in the way patterns are real, and the intentional stance is vindicated precisely when describing a system in terms of its beliefs yields predictive and explanatory compression that neither the physical stance nor the design stance can match. Tell me what would count against this view. I'm especially interested in the liberality problem: if predictive compression is the whole story, thermostats, colonies of bacteria, and arguably the solar system all instantiate belief-like patterns, and it becomes unclear where the line is or why crossing it matters — I want to know whether that objection is fatal, merely awkward, or a feature the view should openly embrace. Consider also whether misrepresentation is a genuine defeater: whether a pattern-based account can distinguish a system that believes something false from a system that believes something else, or whether that distinction requires resources the view has foresworn. And take up one newer case I can't classify: a large language model that predicts the utterances of believers remarkably well by exploiting exactly the compressible regularities the view says beliefs consist in — does its success count for the view (belief-talk tracks real, exploitable patterns) or against it (the patterns can be exploited by a system to which belief-attribution is clearly inappropriate, suggesting the view omits something the design stance or rationality norms were supposed to supply)? Throughout, distinguish the objections that a committed defender of the view would concede count against it from those they would absorb or reframe, and rank the defeaters from fatal to merely uncomfortable. Finally, tell me where your own assessment is most likely to fail: which objections turn on a reading of Dennett that his defenders would reject, and what would have to be true — for instance, a successful independent account of content — for your ranking to come out substantially differently.
