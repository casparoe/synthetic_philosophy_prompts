I do the research desk at a mid-sized education charity in the UK, and my job has quietly turned into being the person who deals with heritability numbers. Twice a month, sometimes more, someone senior forwards me a claim that looks like: such-and-such trait is 60% heritable, therefore the programme we fund is doomed / therefore tutoring is a waste / therefore the attainment gap is basically fixed and we should all go home. Some come from actual academics in genetics, some from think tank reports, some from a trustee who has been reading an extremely confident corner of the internet. Nobody here has any genetics training including me (I did one stats module at uni), and I usually have about an hour before whoever asked wants a yes/no with a paragraph of reasoning.

I've got the gist — heritability is a population statistic, not a fact about an individual, and it's conditional on the environment in the population studied — but the gist keeps failing me in the weeds. Like: does it matter that twin studies assume twins share their environments? Does heritability go up when you make schools fairer, and if so does that mean our own mission raises the number the other side quotes at us? What's the difference between "heritable" and "untreatable" given that shortsightedness is wildly heritable and also got dramatically more common in East Asia for environmental reasons?

What I want is a proper decision procedure my team can actually run on an incoming claim, not an essay. Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each one, where the procedure stops and I can say "this conclusion doesn't follow, reject it", and where it has to hand the decision to a person because what's left is a values question rather than a genetics one. It should be honest about which steps are just judgement calls dressed up as steps. And note explicitly what the procedure *can't* do — e.g. if a study is fine and the statistic is real and someone's inference from it is technically valid, I need to know that too.

Then run it on three real-ish claims from my inbox: (1) "reading ability is around 70% heritable, so one-to-one catch-up tutoring is money down the drain"; (2) "childhood self-control is substantially heritable, so our behaviour mentoring programme will wash out by adulthood"; (3) an internal one from our own comms team, who want to say that because home environment matters more than genes for early literacy, heritability studies justify our whole portfolio. My suspicion is the procedure backs the tutoring and kills the comms line, and I need to know if that's actually right or just what I want to hear. One of these should end with an answer I won't like — flag it clearly if so.
