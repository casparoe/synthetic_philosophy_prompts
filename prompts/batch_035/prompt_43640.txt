For my junior-year philosophy elective, our final project is a satirical piece whose humor has to come from a real philosophical problem rather than from mocking people who hold a view. I have chosen the question of whether virtual objects and virtual harms are real, and I would like your help drafting the piece I have in mind.

The piece is a claim-denial letter, roughly 700 words, written in the dry, over-formal voice of a claims adjuster at an insurance company that operates inside a fictional massively multiplayer online game. Call the company Vaunt Mutual and the game Ashenreach. The claimant is a player whose rare flying mount, the Onyx Drake, was deleted in a server rollback caused by a botched patch, and the letter denies the claim while citing numbered policy clauses. The comedy should come from the fact that each clause quietly takes a philosophical position on the ontology of virtual objects and the reality of virtual harms — the adjuster never argues philosophy openly; he just applies policy.

Some things I would like worked into the clauses or the surrounding prose, in whatever way serves the piece best:

- A distinction between the Drake "as an object" and the Drake "as an arrangement of data the Company remains free to rearrange," deployed to deny that anything was destroyed rather than merely changed.
- Something on the type/token problem: since the developers can mint unlimited identical Drakes, the letter should argue that the claimant's copy cannot have been the rare thing, and therefore that nothing rare was lost.
- A force-majeure clause classifying patch notes, rebalancing, and server maintenance as Acts of God, on the grounds that the developers stand to their creation as nature stands to us.
- A clause establishing that harm to an avatar is not harm to the player, with the denial resting on exactly that distinction.
- Something acknowledging that the claimant purchased the Drake with real currency, immediately followed by an argument that ownership of unreal objects cannot confer a real insurable interest.
- A required form on which the claimant must state whether the Drake existed prior to the rollback, and explain, in her own words, the difference between deletion and retroactive non-occurrence.

At least once, the adjuster's composure should slip: a sentence or two in which he concedes that the hundreds of hours were really spent, that the guildmates really gathered to mourn, that the claimant's distress was, regrettably, real — before he retreats back into policy language and denies the claim anyway. I want that slip to be the emotional center of the piece rather than a throwaway joke.

The target of the satire is the deflationary dismissal of virtual loss — the "it's just a game, nothing was really lost" position — and it should be a fair target: someone who actually holds that view should be able to recognize their strongest arguments inside the clauses, not a caricature of them. At the same time, the letter should remain fully readable to someone who has never thought about virtual ontology at all. Please keep the tone deadpan and bureaucratic throughout, format it as a formal business letter with numbered clause citations, and close with an appeal process that is itself ontologically loaded — for example, review by a Board whose decisions, per policy, exist only in-game.
