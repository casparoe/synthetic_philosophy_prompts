I've gone full Pettit-pilled: I'm convinced that freedom is non-domination, not just non-interference, and that Berlin-style negative liberty misses something real -- the slave with a kindly master who never actually meddles is still unfree, because the master could interfere on a whim and the slave lives accordingly. But I want to stress-test the view before I lean on it in my thesis. What would count against it?

Here's what I'm already chewing on, so don't just repeat it back to me. If domination is just the standing capacity to interfere arbitrarily, then seemingly everyone with any power over me dominates me: my employer, the bank, the algorithm that prices my rent, maybe even a random burglar (he can interfere, after all). Pettit's answer is that interference which tracks my interests -- like non-arbitrary law -- doesn't dominate even though it interferes. But that criterion seems to be doing ALL the work, and I can't tell if that's a feature or a sign the theory is hollow. If arbitrary vs. non-arbitrary carries the whole distinction, what's left of the theory? Also worrying: once you say freedom requires protections, legal standing, effective capacities, aren't you just sliding into positive liberty, Taylor's effective self-mastery? If non-domination collapses into one of the two classic concepts, then the whole "third concept of liberty" framing dies, and that framing is a big part of why I found the view attractive.

So: what should genuinely move me? I want the cases, arguments, and results from adjacent areas that would count against non-domination as the right analysis of freedom, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Which of these would a committed republican concede as counting against their view, and which would they wave away with a qualification? I'm especially interested in whether the view risks anything empirical -- Pettit leans on how ordinary people and older republican writers actually talked about freedom, so if the linguistic evidence pointed the other way, would he care, or would that too get explained away? And give me an honest verdict at the end: has the view been insulated by qualifications to the point where it can't lose, and if so, is that a fatal flaw for a conceptual analysis like this, or just par for the course?
