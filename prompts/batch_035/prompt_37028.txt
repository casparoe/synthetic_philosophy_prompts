I am writing an essay for a philosophy-and-film volume on Lars von Trier's Melancholia (2011), and I want to build it around Samuel Scheffler's afterlife conjecture from Death and the Afterlife: the claim that many of our values, projects, and sources of meaning depend not on our own survival but on the largely unexamined assumption that humanity will outlast us — that there will be future people who come after we are gone. Scheffler tests the claim with an imminent-doomsday thought experiment; Melancholia is essentially that experiment staged, with extinction certain, dated, and known in advance. I would like a reading of the film through the conjecture, one that takes both the film and the argument seriously — neither reducing the film to an illustration nor bending the conjecture to fit von Trier's imagery.

The film's two-part structure seems to me to split Scheffler's scenario at an interesting joint, so I would like you to address both halves. In Part One, "Justine," no cosmic threat is yet known: a bright star is identified as Antares, and the centerpiece is Justine's wedding — the paradigm ceremony of a shared future — which disintegrates over a single night into depression, infidelity, the quitting of her job, and the calling-off of the marriage. Does this half dramatize the conjecture in reverse? On this reading, Justine's anhedonia would be a kind of preverbal grasp that the framework of future-oriented ritual is contingent, an insight the other characters only arrive at later and only when the fact is announced. But say honestly whether that is an interpretation the film supports or one I am imposing, given that her depression plausibly has ordinary causes and pre-dates everything cosmic.

Part Two, "Claire," then seems to run the conjecture forward. Claire's life is structured almost entirely by her son Leo's future; when collision becomes certain, her world collapses, her husband John kills himself, and she frantically attempts a flight that cannot succeed. Meanwhile Justine — catatonic at the film's midpoint — becomes calm and capable. Two moments matter most to me. The first is Justine's refusal of Claire's plan to spend the end with candlelight and wine on the terrace, which she finds absurd, in favor of inventing, with Leo, a "magic cave" out of branches — a ritual everyone present knows is void, performed for a child, which visibly changes their final minutes. Is that scene evidence against the conjecture (meaning available at the very end, with no future at all), or is it something the conjecture can explain — a make-believe that borrows whatever force it has from the now-defunct assumption it openly fakes? The second is Justine's declaration that all life on earth is evil, and her apparent certainty that there is no life anywhere else: is her serenity a form of acceptance of the conjecture's lesson, or a renunciation of value altogether that the conjecture has no vocabulary for?

Finally, tell me where the film resists the reading, and what it adds to the debate that Scheffler's argument alone does not. I have in mind the opening slow-motion tableaux set to Wagner, which aestheticize the end in a contemplative register quite unlike the structure of projects; if the film locates a kind of value in experience or vision that survives when the future does not, that may be something the conjecture's framework simply has no place for. Please anchor your reading in specific scenes — the wedding night, the electric-grid failures and fleeing attempt, John's death, the terrace, the cave — and conclude by saying what you think the film shows about the conjecture that the philosophical literature on it, as far as you know it, has not said for itself.
