So I'm a software engineer, no philosophy degree, but I joined this mixed reading group a while back (mostly coworkers plus a couple people who did philosophy in college) and last month we did a session on Molinism. Most of it went over my head but one piece stuck with me and I keep turning it over: middle knowledge, i.e. the idea that God knows, logically prior to any decision to create anything, what every free creature would freely do in every possible circumstance -- not what they must do, not what they in fact do, just what they *would* do. The part I find slippery is that these counterfactuals are supposed to be true but not made true by anything God does and not determined by the creature's nature either, since the creature is free. Anyway I volunteered to run our next session (14 people, 90 minutes, everyone has at least skimmed the basics) and I don't want it to be another sit-around-and-talk thing.

What I want is a game where the group actually feels why this is hard, before anyone names it. My rough idea: one person plays the world-selector, everyone else plays free creatures, and the world-selector has to achieve some goal -- like, get at least N of 10 "creatures" to freely cooperate, or build a world where a community survives a drought without anyone hoarding -- by choosing which circumstances to place each creature into, from a menu of scenario cards. The creatures decide however they want in the moment, maybe with a private character sheet of tendencies they can follow or ignore (so choices aren't random but also aren't predictable). And here's the wrinkle I keep chewing on: to simulate middle knowledge, the world-selector would need some kind of access to what each creature *would* do in each circumstance without ever asking them -- my half-baked version is an "oracle phase" of short pilot rounds where the selector gets to probe each creature's responses, but they then have to place their bets on the real round and the creatures are free to act differently than they did in the pilot. Payoffs in points, no money. Rules, materials, timeline, all of it.

Then the debrief: I want it to map what happened onto the pieces of the idea -- the counterfactuals being true before any decree, the problem of picking *which* world to actualize when you have all this knowledge and creatures still screw things up, the grounding question that I assume will surface as somebody asking where the pilot-round data even came from and whether it made anything true. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I already suspect a faction of players will deliberately collude to spite the world-selector just to wreck the plan, and honestly I don't know if that's a bug or the free will defense showing up on its own), and be blunt about which feature of the real doctrine the game flat out cannot reproduce -- I have a feeling the "no track record, still knows" part is exactly the un-game-able part, and I'd rather know that going in than pretend my oracle phase solved it.
