I've been invited to give the keynote at a civic evening in The Hague marking the 350th anniversary of Spinoza's death. The audience will be roughly half early modern philosophy scholars and half city councilors and civil servants, and the evening falls just before a council debate on expanding the city's citizen-assembly scheme. Please write the speech. Its spine should be a tension internal to Spinoza himself: in the Theological-Political Treatise he calls democracy the most natural and most free form of state, yet the same book insists that ordinary people are easily led by fear and superstition, while the unfinished Political Treatise suggests that the multitude, deliberating together, can judge better than any of its members alone — so his case for democracy rests on aggregation and institutional design, not on the wisdom of the typical voter taken one at a time. I'd like the speech to turn that tension into something the councilors can actually use in their assembly debate: what Spinoza's argument really supports is not rule by majority sentiment but arrangements that transform private passions into collective counsel, and the speech should say plainly what follows from that for how an assembly is composed and run, and what does not. Constraints: no Latin, no chapter-and-verse citations, no anachronism that would make the scholars wince (Spinoza excluded women, servants, and the propertyless from political participation, and the speech must not pretend otherwise or gloss over it), and it must land for the non-philosophers without condescension.
