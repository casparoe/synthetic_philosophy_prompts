I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I would like a careful comparison of two attitudes towards inconsistency that I keep encountering in the logic literature, because I cannot tell whether they are genuinely different positions or the same position described at two levels of abstraction.

The first attitude is dialetheism: the claim that some contradictions are literally true, so that a sentence and its negation can both be true of the world, with the liar paradox usually cited as the flagship case. The second attitude I have no canonical name for, so let me call it paraconsistency as an engineering practice: designing and reasoning within logical systems in which a contradiction does not entail everything, so that an inconsistent body of information remains usable, while remaining entirely neutral about whether any of the contradictory information is actually true. My understanding is that this is where inconsistent databases, merged knowledge bases, and automated reasoners that must not collapse at the first conflict belong.

Please structure the comparison around the following questions, which strike me as the places where the two attitudes might come apart.

1. Is paraconsistency-as-practice a philosophical position at all, or merely a formal property of certain logics (non-explosiveness) that anyone can exploit for any purpose? Is there a recognised term for those who use paraconsistent logics while rejecting true contradictions, and what exactly commits them to that rejection?

2. Semantics. My working hypothesis is that dialetheism needs a semantics in which truth-values overlap (gluts), whereas the engineering practice can keep an entirely classical two-valued semantics and simply weaken the inference rules, treating a contradiction as a defect to be quarantined rather than a fact to be recorded. Is this hypothesis correct? And if so, does it give the dialetheist something the engineer genuinely lacks, or only something extra that the engineer has no need of?

3. A concrete case from my own work, to test the comparison. When two legacy systems disagree about a customer's date of birth, three behaviours are available to us: (a) fail everything downstream of that record, which looks like classical explosion; (b) mark the field as conflicting, let every other query proceed, and answer any query touching the date of birth with the word conflicting, which looks like quarantine without a verdict; and (c) return both dates to every consumer and let each consumer decide. Can each of these behaviours be matched to one of the two attitudes, or to some stance in between? In particular, would a dialetheist say that (c) makes both dates true, or merely that it refuses to adjudicate, and does the answer to that change whether (c) counts as dialetheist reasoning?

4. Finally, are the two attitudes separable in both directions? I can see how someone could use non-explosive logics while believing that all contradictions are false. What I cannot see is the reverse: a dialetheist who insists on explosive classical consequence. Please tell me whether that combination is genuinely incoherent or merely unattractive, and why.
