Two classmates of mine, Dara and Marcus, spent an entire study session arguing about the surprise examination paradox, and I could not follow who was winning. The setup they used: a teacher announces that there will be an exam next week (Monday through Friday), on exactly one day, and that on the morning of the exam the students will not know it is that day. The students reason that the exam cannot be on Friday, since by Thursday evening it would be the only day left and hence no surprise; but with Friday eliminated, it cannot be on Thursday either; and so on back to Monday. Conclusion: no surprise exam is possible. The teacher then gives the exam on, say, Wednesday, and the students are genuinely surprised.

Dara defended the elimination argument; Marcus attacked it. The exchange ran as follows.

(1) Dara: The argument is just modus tollens applied five times. If the announcement is true — one exam next week, no advance knowledge — then Friday is impossible, and every subsequent elimination is exactly as valid. So either the announcement is false or the exam cannot be a surprise. That is airtight.

(2) Marcus: The Friday step is special. On Thursday evening the students can eliminate Friday only if they still trust that an exam is coming that week. But once they have run the elimination argument, they no longer trust that. So when you try to eliminate Thursday — "if we reach Thursday morning, we would know it has to be today" — the "we would know" fails, because the premise that the exam must still be coming has been undermined by their own earlier reasoning.

(3) Dara: That confuses the students' psychology with the logic. The announcement is a proposition about how the week will go, and if it is true, it has its logical consequences whether or not the students feel confident at each stage. What they would "trust" is beside the point.

(4) Marcus: It is not beside the point, because "you will not know which day" is not an ordinary proposition about the world. Its truth depends on what the students will know, and what they will know depends on the deductions they make — including deductions drawn from this very announcement. It is closer to "you will never believe this announcement" than to "it will rain on Wednesday." A sentence of that kind can be perfectly true while being impossible for its audience to accept.

(5) Dara: Even granting all that, look at the outcome: the exam was Wednesday, and the students were surprised. The announcement was true and their conclusion — no surprise exam is possible — was false. A sound argument cannot have true premises and a false conclusion, so whatever the announcement's peculiar status, the students' reasoning somewhere failed as reasoning, not merely as psychology.

(6) Marcus: Which is exactly why the elimination argument cannot be sound. "The announcement is true" is not a premise the students can coherently add to what they know; accepting it is what generates the contradiction. I would say the announcement is true but unknowable to them, in the way "it is raining but you don't believe it" can be true while being something you can never know. The mistake is treating a heard and accepted announcement as just another ordinary premise.

Dara closed by objecting that if Marcus were right, teachers could announce surprise exams forever and never be wrong, which she found far too convenient. Marcus replied that on Dara's view the students' surprise on Wednesday morning becomes a kind of miracle rather than a foreseeable failure of their prediction. They asked me to referee, and I honestly could not. Then state who comes out ahead overall, and identify anything both of them overlooked.
