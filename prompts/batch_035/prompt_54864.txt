I run a monthly philosophy discussion evening at my local public library — a standing group of about fifteen, mostly retirees plus a few curious undergraduates, no philosophy background beyond what they've absorbed, and at least two regulars who open every session by declaring that taste is subjective and therefore there's nothing to discuss. Next month's topic is aesthetic testimony: whether you can come to know, or even justifiably believe, that a work of art is good on someone else's say-so. I have forty-five minutes, a whiteboard, and no homework assigned, so whatever lands has to land live. Don't write the explanation itself for me — I want the plan for delivering it.

Specifically, I want the dependency structure first: what has to be in place before the question even registers as a question. My instinct is to start with ordinary testimony — a friend telling me my train leaves at six, which nobody doubts I can know that way — and let the asymmetry with aesthetic verdicts emerge as the puzzle, but I'm unsure whether to frame the contrast myself or try to provoke the group into noticing it, and where exactly in that setup the key distinction between believing-that-the-film-is-good and being able to appreciate the film has to be introduced. Second, the carrying example: I've been using a trusted friend whose recommendations have never once missed telling me, before I've seen it, that a new film is excellent. Is that the right load-bearing case, or does it invite the track-record reading of the debate (the question of whether critics are good predictors) that I'm trying to avoid? Should I build around something where the gap between verdict and first-hand experience bites harder — wine, food, a painting you'd have to travel to stand in front of? Say what the example must contain and what it must be scrubbed of.

Then I need the misconception map. The ones I anticipate: (a) taste is subjective, full stop, deployed as a conversation-stopper; (b) hearing the whole debate as being about whether critics have good track records — a factual, sociological question — rather than about whether receiving a verdict is the right kind of route to an aesthetic belief at all; (c) assuming that if testimony can transmit the belief that the film is good, it thereby transmits the appreciation, so watching it afterward becomes redundant; and (d) reading any resistance to deference as snobbery or gatekeeping. For each, tell me at which point in the sequence to defuse it — I'd much rather head these off than referee them once they've hardened into positions mid-discussion. I also want to be explicit with the group about what I'm refusing to cover at this level, and I want my list of refusals checked: I assume the general epistemology of testimony, anything Kantian, and the finer literature on whether testimonial aesthetic belief is somehow less valuable than first-hand judgment are all out of scope, but tell me if one of those is actually load-bearing and belongs in. Finally, give me one closing question — not a quiz question with a right answer — whose response would show me whether a participant has genuinely grasped the distinction the session turns on, rather than merely having enjoyed forty-five minutes of arguing about critics.
