I'm a clinical faculty member in an MSW program, and this fall, for the first time, I've been assigned the session on Foucault in our required theory course — one seventy-five-minute class for second-year students who are concurrently in their first field placements. The syllabus uses a few chapters from Discipline and Punish, and every past instructor's notes tell me the session lands badly: the students came into this field to help people, they hear a French theorist telling them that helping professions are machinery of control, and they either defensively dismiss him or guiltily conclude that social work is simply oppressive and there is nothing to be done. I don't want either reaction. What I want is for them to leave able to see how a case file, a treatment plan, and a standardized intake instrument do their work — including how those things genuinely help people, and how the helping and the sorting and the normalizing are often the same movement. That, I take it, is the actual point of the power/knowledge idea for this audience.

I'm asking you not to explain Foucault to me — I know the material reasonably well — but to plan how I should teach it. Specifically: what has to come first, given that my students will arrive thinking of power as a thing bad actors possess and wield as repression, and that the panopticon will strike them as a metaphor for security cameras unless something is in place before I show it to them. Which single example should carry the whole session — I'm torn between building it around the intake assessment they're actually learning to administer in placement, or around the school counselor's file that some of them carry from their own childhoods — and what that example must contain and what it must lack. I also want a map of the misconceptions in the order they'll actually arise: the "power is just repression" reading, the literal-surveillance reading of the panopticon, the deflated "if power is everywhere then resistance is pointless" conclusion, and the cynical "knowledge/power means all knowledge is just bias" gloss on the hyphenated phrase — with the point in the sequence where each should be headed off. Please also tell me what to openly refuse to cover in one session (I assume biopower, the full genealogy, the later work on the self) and how to say so without it sounding like I'm hiding the hard parts. Finally, give me one closing question I could pose that would tell me whether they've actually gotten the idea — something that tests whether they can apply the productive-not-merely-repressive conception to their own documentation practices without treating it as either a revelation of guilt or an invitation to burn their files.
