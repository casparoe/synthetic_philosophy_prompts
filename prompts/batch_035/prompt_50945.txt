As a PhD student in axiology, I've been struck by how the debate over the transitivity of 'better than' has died down rather than been resolved. The transitivity side holds, roughly in the spirit of Broome, that betterness compares the intrinsic characters of complete outcomes: if outcome A beats B on its internal features, and B beats C on its, then the A-versus-C comparison is already fixed and cannot come out the other way. The spectrum side, in the spirit of Temkin and Rachels, constructs chains of outcomes — each obtained from its predecessor by trading, say, a modestly higher intensity of pain for a much shorter duration — in which every adjacent comparison clearly favors one end of the chain, while the comparison of the endpoints clearly favors the other; from this they infer that 'better than' is not transitive, or is 'essentially comparative', holding only relative to a set of alternatives rather than between outcomes considered in themselves. Each camp treats the other's central intuition as either a datum or a confusion, and after decades the positions look essentially unchanged. My working hypothesis is that the two sides are tacitly answering different questions — one about a two-place relation among fixed outcomes, the other about a three-place relation indexed to the choice set — and that the dispute is therefore partly verbal. But I want a committed diagnosis, not a menu: tell me what kind of disagreement this really is, why you read it that way, and what, if anything — a new case, a formal result, a change of question — would count as genuine progress.
