Two philosophers of probability on my faculty have agreed to run a formal adversarial collaboration, and I have been asked to draft the joint statement they will both sign. I want you to write that draft. The two parties: a committed precise Bayesian, who holds that a rational agent's credences at a time must be representable by a single, sharp probability function; and a committed imprecise probabilist, who holds that an agent's epistemic state may only be representable by a set of probability functions (a "credal set"), with the agent's credence in a proposition being an interval rather than a number. They have been arguing for two years, in public and badly, about the following cluster of issues, so the document must engage with them concretely rather than at the level of slogans:

1. The Ellsberg choices. Party B's side takes ambiguity aversion to be rational; Party A's side takes the standard Ellsberg preferences to show either that ordinary preferences are irrational or that they must be redescribed so as not to conflict with sharp expected utility. A agreed-claims section should state the two-urn setup and the observed pattern of preferences as an empirical matter, without ruling on whether the pattern is rational.

2. Dilation. There are cases where, for some event A and some observation B consistent with every function in the credal set, conditioning on B yields an interval for A that strictly contains the prior interval: more information makes the agent less precise. Both parties accept the mathematics. They disagree about whether dilation is a defect of the imprecise framework, a neutral theorem, or an accurate description of real evidential situations.

3. Decision rules. When a credal set does not deliver a unique expected-utility ranking, what should the agent do? Candidate rules include E-admissibility (an option is choiceworthy if some function in the set ranks it highest) and the maximin-over-expected-utilities rule. The two parties disagree about whether a single rule must be settled, and about what each rule implies in Ellsberg-type cases and in sequential-choice cases.

4. Belief inertia. If two imprecise agents begin with different credal sets and then share all their evidence, there are conditions under which their credal sets need never merge: pooling can preserve divergence indefinitely. Party A counts this a serious cost, in part by contrast with the precise framework's convergence results under commonly accepted conditions; Party B denies that the comparison is apt or the cost real.

5. Sure-loss arguments. Whether an ambiguity-averse agent can be shown to accept a sequence of bets guaranteeing a loss, and whether such arguments, even when valid, show anything more than that the agent's dispositions violate a specific coherence axiom the precise framework builds in.

The document should have four parts. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: the mathematical results both accept, the descriptions of the choice problems both accept, and any characterizations of the other side's view that each side has already endorsed as fair. Second, a disagreements table: each row a single point of residual disagreement, with the precise-Bayesian's statement of their own position in one column and the imprecise probabilist's in the other, each phrased so that the author of that cell would sign it — no straw-manning, and no wording that smuggles in the other side's evaluative terms. Third, a cruxes section: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party, with — for each crux — a concrete case, argument, or proof obligation that both parties agree would bear on it, and each party's written-in-advance prediction of what the case would show. Fourth, a section identifying which parts of the residual disagreement, if any, are verbal — merely downstream of choosing different things to mean by "credence," "belief," or "rational" — and which are substantive.

A final constraint on the drafting: throughout the document, and in a short editorial note at the end, keep what is mathematically settled clearly separated from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this. Do not present either camp's contested position — for instance, on whether Ellsberg-type preferences are rational, on whether dilation is pathological, or on whether the failure of belief inertia is a cost — as though it were the consensus view of the field; and where the draft's agreed-claims section must lean on one camp's preferred formulation of something the other camp would dispute, flag that dependency rather than letting it pass as neutral.
