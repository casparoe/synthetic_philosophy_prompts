so we just did a civics unit on voting and majority rule, and it got me thinking about something weird that keeps happening whenever a group i'm in has to decide stuff. i've collected my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and i can't find the pattern, so i'm hoping you can tell me whether there even IS one. one admin thing first: please write your answer in British English (colour, recognise, "at the weekend", that sort of thing — i'm in the UK and this is going into a school project).

here's the cases and what my gut says about each:

1. my friends picking a film. five of us vote, three pick the horror film. i hate horror films and i think they're picking the worse film, but my gut says we should watch it and i'd be wrong to sulk about it. majority just decides, even when the majority is probably wrong about quality.

2. our group project had to pick a topic. we all agreed the topic needs to be interesting to the class AND doable in two weeks. then, in a seperate vote, most of us (3 of 5) thought topic X was interesting, and most of us (3 of 5 again, but different people) thought X was doable. but when we voted on X as a whole, only 2 of us wanted it, because every person had a nagging doubt about one side or the other. my gut: we should have gone with X. if we agree on the criteria, and majorities pass each criterion, that settles it, even though the final vote says no.

3. a hiring thing i saw on a TV show: a panel deciding whether to hire someone, where the rule is she needs to be qualified and a good fit with the team. most of the panel think she's qualified, most think she'd fit, but overall a majority vote no, each member with one deal-breaker worry. my gut here: don't hire her. and idk why, because this is basically the same setup as case 2, but it feels totally different.

4. a trial (also TV, so treat it as hypothetical, i've probably garbled the law): the panel can convict only if the accused did the act and intended it. a majority of the panel holds he did it, a majority holds he intended it, but a majority overall votes to acquit. my gut: the acquittal stands. you can't convict when most of the people looking at the whole picture say not guilty. same shape as 2 and 3 again, opposite verdict again.

5. the class voted to make the one kid nobody likes carry everyone's bags on the school trip. majority voted yes. my gut: not ok. the majority doesn't get to decide that at all — some things just aren't up for a vote.

6. me and my mates deciding where to eat, split 2-2, so we flip a coin. seems completely fine, even though a coin knows nothing about food. but if a coin decided case 5 it would obviously be wrong.

so my question: is there one principle behind all these verdicts? things i suspect are doing work (pure guessing): how serious and irreversible the decision is, whether the deciders are equally good at judging the question, and whether the thing is even the group's business. but cases 2, 3 and 4 are the real puzzle — identical structure, and my gut gives three different answers. either seriousness/irreversibility is doing hidden work, or the overall votes carry information the premise votes don't (like each person's doubt being genuine evidence), or i'm just being inconsistent and should flatten some verdicts.

what i'd like back: the principle (or a couple of candidates if one won't cover it) stated precisely, which case motivates each bit, an honest statement of which of my verdicts you had to throw out, and predictions on two cases i haven't decided: (a) a teachers' panel expelling a student, where expulsion needs both "he did it" and "it was serious enough" — majorities on both, but a majority of the teachers vote against expulsion; (b) five friends each ranking holiday destinations, where the pairwise majorities go in a circle (a beats b, b beats c, c beats a) — is there a right answer there, or does the group just not have a preference, full stop?
