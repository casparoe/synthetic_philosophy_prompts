For thirty-five years I was an experimental physicist, the last twenty of them running a materials characterization group at a national laboratory, and since retiring I have been working through the philosophy of economics in roughly the order an earnest amateur does: Smith, Marx, and then the socialists versus the Austrians. The debate that has seized me is the socialist calculation debate — Mises's claim that without market prices for capital goods a planner cannot allocate rationally, Lange's reply that planners could grope toward equilibrium by trial-and-error price adjustments, and Hayek's later shifting of the question: that the problem was never solving equilibrium equations but mobilizing knowledge that no central body ever possesses in articulated form. I have a standing argument about this with an old colleague from my lab days, a computer scientist who spent his career in optimization and distributed systems. He holds that the debate was settled prematurely and then settled wrongly: that Hayek's 1945 complaint was, at bottom, a claim about computational and informational costs, that those costs have since fallen by many orders of magnitude, and that firms like Walmart and Amazon already administer planned domains larger than some national economies — so a planning authority equipped with modern demand forecasting and input-output optimization could match market allocation while internalizing externalities that markets ignore.

I hold, or half-hold, that he is changing the subject: that the knowledge Hayek cared about is tacit, local, and revealed only in action, so no amount of instrumentation substitutes for it — though I confess I cannot quite say what Amazon's internal logistics operation is doing if not exactly the thing I deny is possible. We keep circling without landing blows, so I would like you to write the dialogue we cannot manage ourselves. You are a professional philosopher of economics, deeply versed in both the Austrian and the market-socialist literature and unsentimental about both. Before writing, brainstorm the strongest version of each side: for the planner, that "computation" was always a quantitative claim about costs that have collapsed, and that prices are themselves just a lossy, one-dimensional compression of dispersed signals that a sufficiently instrumented economy could read in fuller form; for the Hayekian, that the planner's data is a sediment of choices made under institutions the plan would abolish, so the knowledge problem is self-undermining rather than merely hard, and that entrepreneurial novelty is something neither prices nor plans anticipate but only one of them tolerates.

Then write the dialogue — three or four substantive exchanges each, with the characters responding to each other's actual arguments rather than delivering set pieces, conceding what should be conceded, and pressing on the genuinely load-bearing question, which I take to be: is the disagreement ultimately about computation, about the incentive-compatible revelation of tacit knowledge, or about the generation of novelty? Neither character should be a strawman; if one genuinely has the better of a point, let it show, and let the other adapt. Please append a brief note after the dialogue stating how confident you are that each character's arguments remain faithful to the actual positions in the literature rather than convenient reconstructions, and singling out the single claim in the dialogue you are least sure about.
