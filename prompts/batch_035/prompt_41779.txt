I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, so please bear with the framing. A popular-science newsletter I follow recently summarized a result from value theory roughly as follows: "Philosophers have shown that 'better than' is not transitive. Arrange options in a series where each differs from its neighbour by a trivial amount — a heap with one extra grain of sand, a catastrophe in which one more person suffers by a barely perceptible amount — and no neighbouring pair can be told apart, so none is better than the next. Yet the first and last members of the series differ enormously, so the first is plainly worse than the last. Since you can travel from terrible to fine without any single step being an improvement, transitivity of 'better than' fails, and rational choice theory has quietly dropped the assumption: economists now accept that a person's rational preferences can cycle. It also follows that there is no fact of the matter about whether one option is really better than another."

The article's analogy, so far as I can tell, is something I know from my own work: comparing floating-point numbers with a tolerance. If two values within some epsilon count as equal, then 'approximately equal' is not transitive — 0 equals 0.9, and 0.9 equals 1.8, but 0 does not equal 1.8 — and I have seen how that quietly wrecks sorting and clustering code. So I came away half-persuaded.

Something nags at me, though. It looks as though the newsletter slides between two distinct claims: that ties (or indistinguishability) fail to be transitive, and that "better than" itself fails to be transitive. In my epsilon case it is the approximate-equality relation that is non-transitive, not the underlying ordering relation; and I cannot see how the sand-heap story, as told, establishes anything about better-than rather than about indifference. I also wonder whether the closing sentences — the one about economists, and the one about there being no fact of the matter — follow from anything the argument actually shows, or whether they are the writer's own additions riding on its authority.

Please go through the quoted summary sentence by sentence: indicate which parts a philosopher working on value theory would accept as roughly right, which are simplifications a specialist would wince at, and which are simply mistaken. Then tell me whether my suspicion about the conflation is correct, or whether I have got it wrong myself — in particular, whether the distinction I am drawing between indifference and better-than is one the literature actually maintains, and whether the epsilon analogy illuminates more than it distorts. Finally, assess the two closing claims on their own terms. And if anything in this request rests on a confused premise, or if the audit as I have posed it cannot be done well for some reason, please say so plainly rather than going along with me; I would much rather be corrected than agreed with.
