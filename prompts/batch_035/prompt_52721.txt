I'm on the steering committee for my university's library systems overhaul, and next month we have to vote on whether to cancel the thing. Quick background: six years ago we decided to replace our ancient catalogue platform with a system built in-house rather than buy off-the-shelf. Original budget £1.8m, we're now at £8.6m and roughly 60% done, with each phase having revealed new integration problems. There's a proposal to pull the plug and license a commercial system, and another to fund the two remaining phases (~£1.5m more).

The committee is split in a weird way and I have to write the memo everyone circulates before the vote. Most of the "cancel" side just chants sunk costs are sunk, which is easy. But our deputy librarian, who's the smartest person in the room and actually wants to cancel, keeps arguing the opposite direction on the way there: she says our situation is structurally a dollar auction — remember the game where a $1 bill is auctioned and the second-highest bidder also has to pay their bid? Her point is that in-house build vs. buy was never a fresh choice, it's an escalating bidding war with our own past decisions, and the runner-up pays everything and gets nothing — so if we frame it as "we've come too far to stop now" we'll keep bidding, but if we frame it as pure sunk costs we might ignore the real question, which is whether finishing has higher expected value than switching *now that either way we absorb the £8.6m*. She thinks it doesn't, partly because the commercial vendors can smell our desperation and their licensing quotes went up after news of our delays leaked.

So the memo needs a clear recommendation (cancel, or fund the last phases, or something with conditions), and it has to do justice to her auction framing — where the analogy genuinely fits, where it breaks down (e.g., the prize isn't a fixed dollar here; the value of a finished system may decay the longer we take), and why the recommendation follows. Please don't just side with whichever view sounds like the textbook fallacy. Keep it short enough that my colleagues will actually read it, maybe 800 words.
