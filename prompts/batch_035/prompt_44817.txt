Something that's been bugging me: string theory has been around for what, forty-plus years now, and it still hasn't produced a single prediction we can actually go out and test. Worse, the whole landscape/multiverse business seems set up so that almost anything we could ever observe is realized somewhere in the ensemble of vacua. And yet the practice continues at full speed — top departments hire string theorists, funding agencies pay for it, hep-th churns out papers by the thousands. Some of this is surely just sociology and sunk cost, but set that aside for a second. What I actually want to know is what view of theory confirmation this institutional practice presupposes, if any. Naively you'd think it presupposes something beyond the old Popperian line that a theory earns credibility by surviving risky tests.

For context, I've seen people defend the status quo with roughly three arguments. First, the no-alternatives point: nobody has managed to build a consistent quantum theory of gravity without basically importing string theory's core ideas, and the fact that it keeps being the only game in town is itself treated as evidence for it. Second, unexpected coherence: the theory kept turning out to require things (extra dimensions, certain symmetries) that then dovetailed with other bits of physics in ways the theorists never rigged in advance — the theory kept surprising them and the surprises kept working out. Third, a meta-induction: research programs that looked this internally successful and attracted this much talent in the past generally ended up on the right track, so bet accordingly.

So here's what I'm asking. Does the ongoing institutional practice strictly presuppose that arguments like these are sound, or is it underdetermined — could a strict falsificationist still justify the funding, say by treating string theory as cheap basic research with option value? Are those three arguments really evidence in the ordinary Bayesian sense, i.e. do they raise the probability that string theory is true, or are they something else dressed up as evidence (self-reports of a field about its own attractiveness, arguably)? And if you concluded the Popperian view is right after all, what concretely would have to change — would hiring commitees actually need to stop hiring string theorists, or does the practice survive almost any philosophy of confirmation you throw at it?
