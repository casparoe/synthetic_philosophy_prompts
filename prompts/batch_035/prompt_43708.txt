As a novelist I keep writing a certain gesture, and I wonder whether it has a name. A character who holds an advantage deliberately and visibly gives it up: the merchant who pays the asking price without haggling when everyone knows he could beat it down; the duellist who offers to let his opponent shoot first; the suspect who volunteers the incriminating detail before anyone thinks to ask. In each case the act is worse than useless on its own terms — it discards money, safety, or a clean record — yet it lands as a display of confidence or good faith, seemingly because only someone who could afford the loss would choose it. I first noticed it in nineteenth-century fiction, where it does a great deal of work, but I now see it everywhere, from weddings to job interviews to diplomacy. A biologist friend murmured something about peacocks' tails when I described it, but the tail is a burden carried continuously for a lifetime, whereas my cases are single, discrete acts of foregone advantage, freely chosen on each occasion. I'm also aware that economists have written about signals that are costly, and that game theorists say something about burning one's bridges. So: is there an established concept for this exact configuration, and if several crowd it, which one fits best, where does each stop fitting, and what are the boundary cases? I'm not after a bare term but the carving — what precisely the gesture is, what it is not, and where it shades into ordinary generosity, vanity, or plain stupidity. My craft depends on it: in fiction this gesture can be read as pride, as courtship, or as cold calculation, and I need to know whether those are one thing or several.
