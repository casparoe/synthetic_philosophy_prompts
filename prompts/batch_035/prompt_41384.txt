I'm a retired neurophysiologist — forty years recording from monkey cortex, first visual, later prefrontal, and for the last three years, since hanging up the electrodes, hopelessly hooked on philosophy of mind. At the evening class I take, I built an argument that I'm now stuck inside. I'm pretty sure it goes wrong somewhere, but I can't find the joint, and I'd like help locating it.

Here's the argument. (1) When a mature science explains the appearances that made us posit something, without keeping the posit, then the posit never existed — that's how it went with phlogiston and caloric: the burning and the warmth were real, the stuff we invoked to explain them was not. (2) Neuroscience is on track to do exactly this for belief and desire: no neural state will ever turn out to have determinate propositional content, be selectively active when you hold that p, and feed into inference the way beliefs were supposed to. I'd bet my pension on this — in my own working life I watched the start of it happen: the "expectation" talk about dopamine neurons just got replaced by reward-prediction-error curves, and the curves explained things (like the blocking effect) that "the animal expects" talk never could. (3) So beliefs and desires are like phlogiston: nobody has ever had one; the behaving-as-if was real, but it was produced by machinery that contains no reasons. (4) The cut goes through science itself: no reviewer ever held a grudge, no colleague was ever really persuaded by my data, since persuasion is a belief-to-belief transaction — and I have to admit, looking back, this fits some reviewers I've known suspiciously well. (5) And it goes for me right now: I'm not being reasoned into this conclusion by its premises; I'm just in a state that disposes me to type these sentences.

The conclusion in (3) I can actually live with — I never much liked the folk anyway. What I can't swallow is what the argument does from there. By the same reasoning, nobody in the whole history of science was ever moved by evidence: every mind-change I ever witnessed, mine included — including the ones that made me drop a hypothesis I'd loved for a decade — would strictly speaking not be a case of being convinced, just a case of later machinery differing from earlier machinery. And here's the thing that keeps me up: the pull I feel from premises (1) through (3) — is that pull a reason-responsive state, or just more machinery? If it's only machinery, then "I find the argument compelling" says nothing in the argument's favor, and I've noticed I can't make myself beleive that while also typing the argument out. My classmate says the whole thing is self-refuting, since to assert it I must believe it, but that seems too quick to me: her move needs the premise that assertion requires belief, and that premise is just the folk theory that's on trial — you can't convict the defendant by citing the very law he's accuseded of breaking. Still, something has to give somewhere. So: which premise or step should I give up, and what does giving it up cost me? I'd honestly rather keep (2), it's the part my career makes me most confident about, but tell me straight if that's the one that has to go.
