I'm a novelist, and I'm stuck on a scene. My narrator runs a charitable foundation that allocates its funds by a simple priority rule: a benefit counts for more the worse off its recipient is. At a family dinner, her estranged sister — a lapsed political theorist — corners her with an argument meant to show that this rule, prioritarianism, falls to the leveling-down objection that was supposed to have vindicated it over egalitarianism, or else that the objection was never any good. For the scene to work, the argument has to be genuinely strong: the sister cannot be defeated by a cheap reply, and I cannot have the narrator win on rhetorical force while the reader senses the philosophy is rotten. So I need to know where the argument actually goes wrong, if it does. Here it is, as the sister delivers it, numbered:

(1) The priority view says that the moral weight of a benefit depends on how badly off its recipient is when they receive it: the worse off, the more it counts. It is the standard successor to egalitarianism, preferred precisely because it is said to be immune to the leveling-down objection.

(2) The leveling-down objection says that no acceptable view may judge an outcome better than another — in any respect, however partial — when in that outcome someone is worse off and no one is better off. A view that can find even one respect of superiority in pure leveling down stands condemned.

(3) How badly off someone is depends only on the absolute level they occupy, not on how they got there. A person lowered from 70 to 60 and a person who has always been at 60 are equally badly off, and benefits to either carry the same weight.

(4) So the weight of every future benefit to a person is fixed by the level an outcome leaves them at: the lower an outcome leaves someone, the more their subsequent benefits will count.

(5) Now compare two outcomes identical in every respect except one: in the first, a well-off person R stands at 70; in the second, R stands at 60, and nothing else differs — no one gains anything. The second outcome is a pure leveling-down of R.

(6) By (4), the second outcome is better than the first in one respect: every benefit R will ever receive afterward counts for more. That is a respect in which someone is worse off, no one is better off, and the outcome is better. By (2), that is forbidden.

(7) So either the priority view is condemned by the very objection that was supposed to vindicate it, or the objection was always unsound and the egalitarian was never refuted. Either way, the sister wins the dinner.

My narrator would most like to keep (1) — the foundation's entire allocation practice is built on it — and I suspect the trouble lies somewhere in (4) through (6), but I can't pin it down, and I have reread the scene too many times to trust myself.
