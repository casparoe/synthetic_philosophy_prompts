I'm a PhD student in molecular biology with an interest in philosophy of science, and at our interdisciplinary reading group last week two people got into an extended exchange about whether DNA is literally a code or only metaphorically one. The philosopher in the group opened (1) by arguing that "code," "translation," "message," and "decoding" are terms of art imported from engineered symbol systems: a code is a set of conventions laid down by agents who intend to communicate, and in the cell there is only chemistry — causal regularities, no one using anything as a sign of anything, so all the coding vocabulary is post-1953 metaphor riding on an analogy with telegraphy and computing. (2) The postdoc in our lab answered that the genetic code is a concrete, specific mapping — 64 triplets assigned to twenty amino acids plus stop signals — physically implemented by the enzymes that charge each tRNA, and arbitrary in the relevant sense, since the same chemistry could carry a different assignment, which is presumably why researchers have managed to build strains that read altered versions of the code. (3) The philosopher conceded it's a real physical mapping but said that makes it a natural sign, not a code: its near-universality just reflects common descent, the assignment got locked in early, and "this codon means lysine" is like "smoke means fire," whereas "chat" meaning cat requires convention, which presupposes a community of users. (4) The postdoc replied that the mapping isn't just any causal covariance: it is degenerate in a structured way, with redundancy at the third position buffering translation errors, and neighboring codons tend to specify biochemically similar amino acids — structure that looks less like a frozen accident and more like something at least partly shaped for reliable transmission. (5) The philosopher rejoined that "shaped for reliable transmission" either smuggles purpose back in or, read purely behaviorally, proves far too much: on that standard a thermostat reads temperature and every piece of negative feedback in nature counts as a code, so the claim becomes trivially true and the metaphor has simply swallowed the concept.

Neither would budge, and I genuinely couldn't referee it. Could you go through the exchange move by move, saying who is right at each step and why? Then tell me whether the disagreement is ultimately substantive or verbal: is there a principled three-way distinction between a designed code, a natural sign, and whatever it is the ribosome-tRNA system is, or does one of them have to give?
