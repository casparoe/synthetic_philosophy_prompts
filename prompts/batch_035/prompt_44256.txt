I am a retired radio astronomer who spent most of his career on receiver design and survey instrumentation, and only in the past few years, with more time on my hands, have I begun reading philosophy seriously. I have lately been working through Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach, and the chapter on the location of meaning — with its three-level apparatus of frame message, outer message, and inner message — struck me as bearing directly on an artifact I have long thought about in a loose, untheoretical way: the Voyager Golden Record. I would like your help in working the example out properly.

By way of reminder of the salient facts, since I doubt either of us can consult anything: in 1977 NASA attached identical gold-plated phonograph records to the Voyager 1 and 2 spacecraft, assembled by a committee chaired by Carl Sagan. The records carry sounds and images of Earth, greetings in dozens of languages, music from many cultures, and even an hour of recorded human brainwaves. The aluminum cover is etched with what amount to instructions: a map of pulsars that locates the Sun, a depiction of the hydrogen hyperfine transition serving as a clock and ruler, and sketches of the record itself, indicating how it is to be played and in which direction to spin it. Now, the analysis I want: map the episode carefully onto Hofstadter's distinction. Identify what exactly plays the role of the frame message here — the part that is meant to announce that this is a message at all and invite decoding; then the outer message, the layer of decoding instructions; then the inner message, the content supposedly delivered once decoding succeeds. Please be disciplined about the mapping rather than merely gesturing at it, and say where the scheme strains. One place I suspect it does: the etched cover has to do two jobs at once — to proclaim itself a message and to teach how to read that very proclamation — and the physical form of the disk, a grooved spiral, is apparently meant to serve as its own hint. It looks to me as though recognizing the frame message presupposes some of the shared physics that the outer message is supposed to establish, which threatens a circularity. Is that a real problem for the scheme, or does Hofstadter's framework already have room for it (e.g., in the thought that outer messages function as triggers for pre-existing decoding machinery rather than as self-contained instruction sets)?

Finally, run the analysis in the other direction, as I believe a good case study should: what does this episode show about the theory itself? Two things I want your honest judgment on. First, a suspicion I have held since the launch coverage, though never seen argued: that the record is better understood as a message from humanity to itself — a statement of what we wished to be — than as a message to whoever might find it, and that if this is so, it tells us something about where the meaning actually resides: in the senders and their hopes rather than in the disk. Does the location-of-meaning framework, which allows meaning to be triggered rather than contained, accommodate that naturally, or does it misdescribe the case? Second, please indicate where the three-level scheme is simply silent about this artifact, and resist the temptation to stretch it to cover everything — for instance, does it have anything at all to say about the choice of *what* to put on the record, the editorial act of the committee, which strikes me as the most philosophically interesting part of the whole business and yet seems to fall outside all three levels?
