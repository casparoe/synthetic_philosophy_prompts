Two friends of mine got into it after a philosophy meetup and asked me to referee, and honestly I couldnt. The topic was the Moore sentence -- "it's raining but I don't believe it." (1) Dan said it's a contradiction, full stop. (2) Maya said obviously not -- there are plenty of possible situations where it's raining and you don't believe it, so it's nothing like "it's raining and it's not raining." Dan actually conceded that one. (3) But he came back saying it's still inconsistent in a broader sense, because asserting it defeats itself the way "I am not speaking" or "I don't exist" does -- the very act of saying it makes things go wrong. (4) Maya said fine, but then the problem is pragmatic, it lives in the speech act, not in the meaning of the sentence, and a sentence can't be "true but impossible to assert without absurdity" unless something about the rules of assertion is doing the work, not the proposition itself. (5) Dan pushed back with the diary case: if I write "it rained yesterday but I never believed it" in a diary meant to be opened after my death, that's completely unproblematic, yet "it's raining but I don't believe it" said right now is not, and the contents look analogous. He said this shows first-person present-tense belief talk works differently from third-person and past-tense talk -- the meaning itself shifts with the context. (6) Maya answered that avowals can be wrong -- people discover they don't believe things all the time, self-deception, confabulation, whatever -- so saying "I don't believe it" isn't some performance that guarantees anything, and she accused Dan of sliding between "can't be asserted without absurdity" and "the words don't function as a belief report at all." (7) Dan's last word was that there's also the sibiling sentence, "it's raining but I believe it's not raining," which feels off in a subtly different way, and that whatever story Maya tells about assertion had better not treat the two exactly alike. Please go through their exchange and rate each of the moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with your reasons -- not just who wins overall.
