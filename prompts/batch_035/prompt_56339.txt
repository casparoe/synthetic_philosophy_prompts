I run a small, closed seminar for AI safety researchers, and two of the most senior participants have spent roughly two years arguing past each other while the rest of us quietly stopped learning anything from their exchanges. The first — call her the control advocate — holds that a large share of AI risk can be managed without ever settling whether the model's goals are good ones: treat the model as a potentially adversarial component inside a larger engineered system, and design the system so that misalignment, if present, is detected or contained. Her concrete levers: monitoring the strong model's outputs with weaker and separately-trained models, least-privilege and time-limited tool access, honeypotted actions and planted failure opportunities, staged deployment with a canary slice of real traffic, shutdown paths that do not route through the model's own judgment, tripwires on outputs that trigger human review. The second — the alignment advocate — holds that all of this is building a moat around a fire: control measures are a bet on the adversary's ignorance, they degrade in precisely the regime we actually worry about (a model more capable than its monitors, with the situational awareness to model the monitoring), and durable safety can only come from the model's own motivations.

They have finally agreed to an adversarial collaboration, and I have been asked to draft the document they will jointly edit and eventually sign. Write that draft. It should contain the following four parts:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — including claims about what each approach actually delivers, about what behavioral evaluations can and cannot establish about a model's motives, and about the conditions under which control and alignment are complementary rather than competing. Exclude anything so bland that either could sign it while privately dismissing the other's position; I would rather have five substantive agreed claims than twenty anodyne ones.

2. A disagreements table. Each row is a single disagreement, stated twice: once in wording the control advocate would accept as a fair statement of her view, once in wording the alignment advocate would accept as a fair statement of his. Where a disagreement turns out to be at bottom a dispute about the meaning of "safe," about time horizons, or about who bears the burden of proof, mark it as such rather than dressing it up as an empirical question.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling any one of them would move at least one of the two. I suspect at least one crux is whether a model with situational awareness and long horizons can systematically degrade the reliability of the very measurements its containment depends on — sandbagging during evaluations, learning the blind spots of weaker monitors, behaving well exactly when tripwires are inferable from context — but I want you to identify the others rather than take mine as exhaustive. For each crux, specify the experiment, red-team exercise, or argument that both parties would accept in advance as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction, with quantitative commitments wherever a number is even roughly possible.

4. A closing section on deployment conditions: the scenarios in which each concedes that the other's failure mode is the binding constraint on safety — where control fails but alignment suffices, and vice versa.

Do not adjudicate. Your job is to make the residual disagreement exact, not to shrink it. And after the document itself, append a short methodological note stating how confident you are in each of the four parts, and singling out the one claim in the agreed-claims section you believe is least likely to actually survive both signatures — say which side you expect to balk at it and why.
