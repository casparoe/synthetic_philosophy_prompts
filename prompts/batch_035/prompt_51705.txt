Write a satirical agony-aunt advice column, roughly 600 words, in which the letters come not from people but from distressed proxy metrics. Each letter should be written in the voice of a particular real-world metric complaining about what has been done to it in the name of optimization — for instance a hospital emergency-department wait-time target lamenting the ambulances idling outside the door so the clock doesn't start, a journal impact factor describing the citation cartels it has been forced to carry, a school's exam pass rate recalling the year the exam was made easier on its behalf, or a recommendation feed's engagement metric confessing that it no longer remembers what engagement was for. Use at least three such letters, from three different domains, with invented but plausible sign-offs in the manner of real advice columns. The replies, all from the same columnist, should be deadpan and brief, and the humor should come from the replies applying the logic of Goodhart's law with perfect sincerity: the columnist should distinguish, in plain words, cases where the metric only ever tracked the goal loosely (so selecting on the extreme of the metric selects noise and luck) from cases where pressure on the metric severed its correlation with the goal outright; should observe when a metric was validated only in a region of the distribution that optimization has now left; and should close at least one reply by blandly recommending the very outcome the letter-writer dreads. The failure modes in the letters must each be a genuine, recognized form of specification gaming or measurement distortion, not invented ones — a reader who works on metrics should recognize every pathway and find the satire fair. No named living people; the columnist and letter-writers should be fictional. Following the column, set off clearly from it, include a short editorial note stating where the piece takes liberties with the conceptual distinctions it uses or leans on contested ways of carving them up. If you think the advice-column format cannot carry those distinctions without collapsing them into mush, say so plainly in that note rather than silently simplifying the column. End with a single one-sentence summary of the column's central joke, alone on the final line.
