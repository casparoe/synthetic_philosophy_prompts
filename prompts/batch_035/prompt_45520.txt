I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on formal epistemology — twelve students, one ninety-minute session — and next week we reach anthropic reasoning. The readings covered the dispute between the self-sampling assumption (reason as if you are a random sample from the observers in your reference class) and the self-indication assumption (assign higher credence to hypotheses on which more observers exist, since you are more likely to exist at all under those). My students can paraphrase both assumptions on command, but they treat the disagreement as a technical quibble rather than as a fork with uncomfortable costs on both sides: on one side the presumptuous philosopher, who is prepared to bet a thousand to one on the teeming universe for no evidence beyond his own existence, and on the other the self-sampler's quieter embarrassment, who ends up strongly discounting any hypothesis under which she would almost certainly never have been drawn into existence to be consulted at all.

I'd like you to design an in-class simulation that makes them feel this fork before I put either name on it. My rough idea involves staged "creations": across several rounds, students learn whether they exist or not under one of two rival cosmologies — a sparse universe with very few observers and a teeming one with vastly more — and the survivors place small-stakes bets (candy tokens, not money) on which cosmology produced them. The payoffs should be arranged so that whichever reasoning rule actually tracks the truth wins out across rounds, forcing the two camps to watch their winnings diverge. In the debrief, I want specific moments in the game mapped onto the two assumptions, the presumptuous-philosopher discomfort staged concretely (someone should visibly commit to wildly lopsided odds on the teeming universe based solely on the fact that they exist), and an equally concrete articulation of what refusing that bet costs you. Please also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and be frank about which feature of the real problem no classroom simulation can reproduce and I will simply have to assert.

Present the design as a numbered list of exactly five items — rules and materials; round structure and betting mechanics; the debrief mapping; predictable failure modes and fixes; honest limits of the simulation — each item one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
