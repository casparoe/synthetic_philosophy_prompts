I teach a small seminar on the philosophy of nationalism (16 students, mostly juniors, one 90-minute block). We've just done Hobsbawm on the invention of tradition and Anderson on imagined communities, and honestly I can tell from discussion that it's going in as trivia — "fun fact, lots of traditions are fake" — rather than as anything they feel. I want an activity where they do the inventing themselves and only later realize how natural and even honorable it felt.

Here's the setup I'm imagining, but please improve it or restructure if you have better ideas. It's 1873 in a fictional country, call it Veldaria, that just won independence after a scramble between two empires. The students are the Provisional Ministry of Culture, split into three committees (Rites & Calendar, Costume & Heraldry, Origin Myth & Textbooks). Each committee gets a different "fragmentary medieval chronicle" — which, what they won't know, is something I'll write the night before in deliberately clunky pseudo-archaic English, with a few plantable hooks in each (a harvest rite, a borderline-sacred grove, a battle at a river ford, an ambiguously heroic founder figure). The committees only see their own fragment, and the fragments deliberately contradict each other on one or two points (where the founder died, whether a certain oath was sworn to the grove or the river). Their job, in about 30 minutes: each committee produces (a) a national holiday with a rough ritual script, (b) some item of "traditional" national dress or symbol, and (c) a paragraph of founding mythology — each explicitly citing the chronicle as authority, and freely smoothing over the gaps where the parchment is "damaged."

Then a second phase for Anderson: each committee writes a one-page "newspaper of record" announcing the new tradition to the whole country, and we hand out all three papers and have everyone read all three at the same time. I want them to get a physical feel for print simultaneity — that the sense of being one people reading the same thing at the same breakfast table is a manufactured experience too, and they just manufactured it, out of paper I printed an hour ago.

Debrief should map specific moments onto the theory: the forged chronicle (I'll confess at the end that I wrote it that morning, and if they want a real parallel I can tell them about James Macpherson, the 18th-century Scot who claimed to be translating an ancient Gaelic epic he had very largely written himself), the committees unknowingly canonizing contradictions, the dress codes invented from nothing, the newspaper phase for the imagined community. And the debrief should push the philosophical question I actually care about: once they know it was invented last Tuesday, is the tradition thereby fake, or worthless? Anderson is pretty explicit that "imagined" isn't an insult — every community larger than a village is imagined, including the ones with genuinely long pasts — and I want them to grapple with whether the manufacturing story is debunking at all, or just a genealogy. That's the distinction I want them to leave with.

Practical things I need: timing breakdown for 90 minutes, materials I can produce with a printer and index cards, how to handle the fact that some students will know Hobsbawm's tartan example and might spoil the irony for the others. Tell me what will predictably go wrong on first run — I'm guessing half the room camps it up and does the whole thing in air quotes, which would kill the effect — and how to keep them earnest. And be honest about what the activity fundamentally can't reproduce and I'll have to supply in discussion: I can simulate the manufacturing but not the century of forgetting in which the invention itself gets misremembered, and no 90-minute exercise can give them the actual stakes people had in believing it. No citations or reading lists needed, just the activity.
