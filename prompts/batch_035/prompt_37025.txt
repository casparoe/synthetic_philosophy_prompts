I'm a PhD student in social policy writing a thesis on benefit conditionality, and I also convene the evening seminar our department runs for frontline practitioners — welfare rights advisers, Jobcentre staff, local authority housing officers — as part of its continuing education programme. For the final session of the term I'd like a diagnostic quiz, titled 'What do you actually think about conditionality?', which sorts these practitioners by their reactions to concrete cases rather than by the labels they would pick if asked directly. Almost none of them have read the political philosophy; all of them have views forged on the job, often inconsistent ones.

Please write eight to ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, none of which may simply ask whether the respondent believes the undeserving poor exist or whether they support workfare. The following cases must appear: a claimant sanctioned for missing an appointment because the bus ran late; a lone parent who declines a job offer because the shift pattern makes childcare impossible; a man who has not worked since age twenty-five and lives frugally on a small inheritance, now facing hardship; a claimant who underdeclares savings from a redundancy payment; and a disability assessment the respondent believes has produced the wrong verdict. I would also like one question probing whether the respondent's support for conditionality is hostage to their estimate of fraud rates — ideally phrased so they must say whether their view would survive learning that fraud were an order of magnitude rarer than they believe.

Build the scoring key from the logical space rather than from a list of names. The branching commitments should be: whether responsibility for one's need is the right basis for differential treatment at all; whether a duty to contribute is owed to fellow citizens as such, or only arises once one starts drawing on their taxes; whether sanctions can be justified paternalistically, as measures that help claimants, rather than as desert; and whether the expressive and stigmatising effects of conditionality count as a cost even when it is behaviourally effective. The leaves should include desert-based conditionality of the luck-egalitarian kind, reciprocity-based duty-to-work views, social-citizenship entitlement views on which need alone triggers support, a sufficientarian case for an unconditional basic income, a frankly paternalistic-utilitarian workfare position, and the libertarian residual safety net. For any combination of branch answers that leaves a leaf unoccupied — for example, accepting desert-based conditionality while rejecting any monitoring apparatus to verify desert — say briefly whether the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Flag also any pair of leaves your key cannot separate; for instance, if no question distinguishes need-tested entitlement from universality, the social-citizen and basic-income leaves will blur, and I would rather that were admitted than papered over.

One further thing. In my interviews, practitioners consistently treat gateway conditions (requirements satisfied once, in order to begin receiving support) very differently from continuing conditions (requirements maintained in order to keep receiving it), even though the academic literature runs the two together. Please build in at least two questions that are otherwise identical but differ only on this dimension, and have the key report, as a separate axis cutting across the main leaves, whether the respondent's pattern treats them alike.

The quiz and key should be usable by someone with no internet access: name only positions a philosophy graduate would already know, and invent your own labels for anything else. Please write everything in British English, using UK welfare vocabulary (claimants, sanctions, Jobcentre) throughout.
