I want a probability estimate, decomposed into its load-bearing sub-claims, on a question in epistemology: your credence that epistemic akrasia can be rational. Please work it out on a concrete case rather than the general debate, because the general debate mostly recycles stock intuitions.

The case: you and a colleague each spend an hour independently evaluating a subtle but purely mathematical claim — say, about the convergence of some stochastic process. You reach opposite conclusions, each with high confidence; you are at 0.9 that you are right. A reliable third party then informs you that, before the exercise, a fair coin was flipped and exactly one of the two of you, selected at random, was given a primer that reliably inflates confidence in whatever conclusion its reader first reaches, while leaving task competence itself intact. You have no access to which of you received it, and the primer leaves no introspectible trace. Now suppose you re-examine the mathematics, can honestly find no error, and your first-order assessment still says 0.9 — while your total evidence, including the higher-order fact about the coin, arguably says something lower. Holding 0.9 while judging that, all things considered, you should hold less: that is the akratic combination in question. (If it helps you sharpen the edges, also consider the variant where the third party would have informed you of the coin flip only if it had landed on you: there, the very arrival of the higher-order evidence is itself evidence, and I would want to know whether that changes its weight.)

Here is what I want. First, your credence that in this case the rational response is an akratic one — that a subject can be fully rational while believing, to some degree, that her own degree of belief is not what she ought to have. Then the decomposition, with a number and a one-line justification for each term: the probability that the justification conferred by first-order evidence is independent of higher-order evidence about how that first-order evidence was generated; the probability that, if the two do interact, the total-evidence picture is right and akratic combinations are never ideally rational, though they may remain blameless; the probability that epistemic rationality evaluates time-slices rather than the whole process leading up to them, and that this distinction does real work here; whether you think an "ought implies can" style constraint on epistemic oughts, given that we cannot believe at will, belongs in the decomposition as a further term; and anything else you judge essential. Show how the terms combine into your final figure. Finally, tell me which single term your final credence is most sensitive to, and what argument or kind of evidence would move that term the most.
