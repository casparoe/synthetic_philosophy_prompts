I am a postgraduate student working on the Reformation, and in our historiography seminar our supervisor has twice now ended discussion by invoking Ranke's motto. The most recent occasion was when another student, reading Hayden White, suggested that our supervisor's account of the 1520s owed as much to how he had plotted the material into a narrative as to the archival evidence beneath it; we were reminded that, since Ranke, the historian's task is simply to show how things actually were, and that this settled the matter before it could be opened. Having gone back to the 1824 preface to the Geschichten der romanischen und germanischen Völker, I find the motto less simple than it sounds. Ranke writes that his book eschews the lofty offices of judging the past and instructing the present, and "will blos zeigen, wie es eigentlich gewesen" — and the German eigentlich is rendered by some translators as a claim about what actually happened and by others as a claim about what essentially happened, which are very different demands, the second of which looks almost incompatible with the naive empiricism the phrase is standardly used to police (by E. H. Carr's critics of Carr, among others). So the same sentence seems to hover between a platitude, a methodology, and an idealist metaphysics of historical epochs. What are the distinct precise claims the motto could be expressing, from the near-trivial to the contentious to the false, and, for each, whether it is defensible and whether it would actually support using the phrase to close down a White-style point about emplotment? Please give exactly five readings as a numbered list, one or two sentences per item, with no introduction or conclusion.
