My book club read a popular book arguing that moral psychology differs systematically across the political spectrum: people on the left tend to rest their moral judgments mainly on two foundations — preventing harm and detecting unfairness — while people on the right also give real moral weight to loyalty to group, respect for authority, and sanctity or purity. Afterward, two members got into a genuinely heated argument about what, if anything, follows.

Marta, who is doing a master's in social policy, said the finding implies that left-leaning people are partly morally blind: if there are several real dimensions of moral value and one political tribe can only perceive two of them, that's like being unable to taste bitterness — a deficit, not a mere difference of taste. Jonah, who is a philosophy PhD student, said this smuggles in normativity: perceiving more dimensions is an epistemic advantage only if the extra dimensions track real moral value, and descriptive psychology can't tell you that; someone who "perceives" the moral relevance of astrological influences isn't better off for perceiving more.

Marta answered that the additional foundations aren't astrology: they look like adaptations to real features of social life — coordinating groups, holding institutions together, avoiding pathogens — so they respond to something in the world, which is at least prima facie evidence of real moral signal. Jonah replied that this confuses causal origins with epistemic vindication: disgust may have evolved for disease avoidance, but if I treat my disgust at some harmless practice as a moral perception, evolution doesn't make me right; and the same debunking applies to every foundation, harm and fairness included, since those are equally products of selection for spreading genes — so either evolutionary pedigree vindicates all the foundations or none, and neither result favors the right.

Marta's last move was that if evolutionary pedigree vindicates nothing, the "blindness" framing dies — but so does Jonah's implicit assumption that harm and fairness are the obviously real dimensions and only the others are candidates for debunking; he can't get his favored starting point for free. They then turned to me to referee, and I honestly couldn't.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: who is right about which specific claims, whether either gets the better of the argument overall, and what, if anything, both are missing. I suspect the disagreement turns on something neither of them states outright — if you agree, say what it is and how making it explicit would resolve the argument.
