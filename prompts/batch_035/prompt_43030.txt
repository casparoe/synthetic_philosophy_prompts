My mother died three weeks ago, and in the days after the funeral my family argued about grief in a way I have not been able to settle since. I would be grateful for a careful adjudication of that argument, because my own state of mind over the past fortnight has swung between the two positions without my being able to see clearly which of them is right.

The disagreement was between my cousin Theo, who is finishing a degree in philosophy and arrived at the wake with a copy of the Zhuangzi, and my uncle Robert, my mother's younger brother. Theo had been reading the chapter usually rendered something like Perfect Happiness, which contains the well-known passage in which Zhuangzi's wife dies: Huizi comes to condole and finds Zhuangzi sitting on the floor, drumming on a basin and singing; Huizi reproaches him for excess, and Zhuangzi answers that when she first died he was, of course, moved — he says he could hardly have been otherwise — but then he traced her beginning back through no life, no body, no breath, through the changes that produced breath, form and life, and finally the change back to death, a sequence like the progression of the four seasons; she is now at rest in the vast chamber of the world, and to wail as though something had gone wrong would be to fail to understand what is appointed — so he stopped.

Theo began from there. (1) My mother's death, he said, is a phase in the transformation of things, not the annihilation of a substance; the changes that composed her are dispersing and will re-form, as clouds condense and rain falls. Nothing is lost to the world, only to us. (2) Grief of the wailing kind rests on a false reading of death: it treats a change of state as though it were an anomalous rupture that ought not to have occurred. (3) So the depth of a person's grief at a death measures not the depth of their love but the depth of their confusion about what has happened — and the fitting response to my mother's death is closer to Zhuangzi's singing than to our weeping.

Robert replied that this proves far too much. (4) If the principle is that whatever is a natural transformation is not grievable, then nothing whatsoever is grievable and nothing whatsoever is to be resisted: his sister's cancer was a natural change in a body, and so was the stroke that followed it; the damp eating the wall of my mother's house is; a landslide is. Medicine, seat belts, flood defences and courts all exist to obstruct the natural course of things, and if the principle condemns grief at death it should equally condemn all of those. Either Theo must say that practising medicine is itself confusion, or he must draw a line between the natural changes one may fight and the natural changes one must welcome — and the moment he draws that line, it is no longer the principle of transformation that is doing the work.

Theo answered that the story itself protects him from the charge of inhumanity. (5) The text is explicit that Zhuangzi was moved when she first died, and stopped only after reflecting; the teaching is not to feel nothing but not to add a second storey to the feeling. The first movement of sorrow is like a wound's discharging; what understanding removes is the appended judgement that something has happened that ought not to have. And the same chapter contains the other story he had brought for us: Zhuangzi finds a skull in the wilds, uses it as a pillow, and in his dream the skull tells him that in death there is nothing to prefer to life — no ruler above, no subjects below. So grief, beyond its first movement, rests on a claim to knowledge about death's badness that none of us possesses. He is not counselling numbness; he is counselling that one not act on a belief one is not entitled to.

Robert's last word was that the second storey makes the position unfalsifiable, and that the scepticism is distributed very selectively. (6) How much grief is the natural discharge and how much the appended judgement? Whatever remains at the end can always be classified as attachment by the man holding the theory. And the claim that we cannot know death is bad cuts everything: if we cannot know that, we cannot know that anything is bad, and nobody lives like that — least of all the author they are both quoting, who plainly thought the suffering of the maimed and the mutilated under tyrants was bad, and never told the men whose feet had been cut off as punishment to go and sing about it. His equanimity, Robert said, appears exactly where resistance is impossible and nowhere else, which suggests it is resignation re-described as understanding.

Theo got no chance to reply, because the meal was called. I have been unable to stop thinking about the exchange since. Some days Robert seems to be defending my right to feel what I feel; other days Theo seems to be offering the only bearable account of where my mother is. Please adjudicate the disagreement move by move, as it actually unfolded: say who is right about which specific point, whether either of them granted anything he should not have, what (if anything) both are missing, and who comes out more right overall. It would help me if you were concrete about the costs of the position you end up endorsing, since I shall have to live with it. Please write your answer in British English.
