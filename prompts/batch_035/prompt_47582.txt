I teach an applied ethics course at a mid-sized university, and next week we reach the unit on the ethics of eating animals. I have about 20 students, one 75-minute session, and I want to design an activity around a specific and under-discussed corner of the debate: the arithmetic of animal deaths per unit of food. The uncomfortable fact students keep missing is that the moral ranking of animal products is not the intuitive one. One cow yields hundreds of kilos of meat while one chicken yields one or two, so a fixed amount of beef involves orders of magnitude fewer animal deaths than the same amount of chicken; and even the plant-based options are implicated, because cultivation and harvesting of crops kill rodents, birds, reptiles, and invertebrates in the fields, and the question of how much small-animal suffering a bowl of rice or a plate of tofu "contains" is genuinely open. I want the students to run into these results themselves, through the structure of the activity, rather than being told them in a lecture — ideally they should commit to a ranking first, on instinct, and then discover that their carefully engineered justification supports choices they started out wanting to reject.

Here is the shell I have in mind, which you should flesh out, correct, and improve: students are the menu committee of a campus dining hall that has adopted a policy of minimizing animal harm in its default offerings, subject to real constraints (budget, nutrition requirements, the fact that the kitchen can only change the default entrée, not what students choose to add). Each small group gets a simplified data sheet — servings per animal, animals affected per hectare of the relevant crops, serving sizes — and must set a weekly default menu and write a one-paragraph public justification for it. The design problem I need your help with is how to stage the sequence so that the surprises land in the right order: first the beef-versus-chicken inversion (the groups that 'protect animals' by pushing chicken over beef discover they multiplied deaths), then the crop-cultivation problem (the groups that go plant-based discover there is no clean hands option), and finally the moral-weights question, where the data sheet deliberately gives them no help and they have to argue about whether a cow's death weighs more than thousands of insects' or whether a broiler's short miserable life is worse than a longer one.

Please give me the full activity: group structure and roles, the data sheet contents (plausible round numbers are fine — students are meant to compute with them, not audit them), the timeline across the 75 minutes, the exact moments where each reveal is triggered and what triggers it, and the debrief. The debrief should map what happened onto the three problems above, and should be honest about the two things the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself: that real-world effects run through markets and demand rather than one-shot choices, and that assigning moral weights across species is a question the arithmetic dramatizes but cannot answer. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — my guess is some groups will refuse the constraint and just argue for veganism, and others will get lost in the numbers — and how I should handle each failure mode.
