Our philosophy-of-mind reading group has been working through Dennett's Consciousness Explained this term, and I've been assigned to lead next week's session on the multiple drafts model and the surrounding attack on the Cartesian theater. That assignment has suddenly made a piece of group folklore urgent for me: the Dennettian one-liner that consciousness is fame in the brain, or cerebral celebrity. Our most senior member — the one who has read everything Dennett ever wrote and lets you know it — deploys it constantly, and last meeting he used it twice as a conversation-stopper. When one of us asked what makes some widely efficacious contents conscious while other equally efficacious ones apparently stay unconscious, he said the fame line as if it were an answer. When I pressed that ordinary fame presupposes an audience — someone has to know and care about the celebrity for the fame to exist, which sounds uncomfortably like re-installing a little observer inside the theater Dennett just demolished — he said the line again, plus a remark that fame in the brain is fame among the processes, not fame for an inner spectator, and moved on.

I don't want to show up next week with only a vague sense that something is being glossed over. My suspicion is that the slogan is compressing several distinct theses of very different strength, and that only some of them could do the dismissive work it's being put to. Maybe the trivial reading is just that conscious contents are the ones with widespread downstream effects — reportable, memorable, apt to control speech and planning later — which everyone in the room could accept and which leaves my question untouched. A stronger reading might identify consciousness with that political influence outright, no further fact to be had, in which case my demand for the difference between conscious and merely efficacious is confused on its face. There may also be a reading on which fame's being a matter of degree does real work, softening the demand for a sharp conscious/unconscious boundary that the multiple drafts model is famous for denying. And I'd like to know whether the disanalogies I keep noticing — the audience problem, the fact that fame is normally fame in a medium and about a person, the fact that a celebrity and their fame seem like two things while a content and its influence seem like one — are embarrassments Dennett must repair or are exactly the points he's trying to make.

So please help me prepare. Lay out the distinct precise claims the slogan could be expressing, ordered if you can from weakest to most radical, and give a verdict on each: trivially true, substantive and defensible, substantive but probably false, or just confused. Say explicitly which of those readings, if any, would actually justify treating my audience question as dissolved rather than merely deferred. And since I'll be presenting this to a live group, please write it as continuous prose only — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables — the way one would write a long letter, with the readings introduced one by one in the flow of the paragraphs.
