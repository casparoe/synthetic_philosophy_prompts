I'm a science and ideas reporter, and I've been toying with a piece about the word "cause" in news writing. The trigger was a story I did last month: a mid-sized city rebuilt its bus network in 2022, and cycling injuries dropped about 18% the following year. My editor wanted "the new network caused injuries to plummet," the stats desk wanted "was associated with," and we landed on some mushy middle phrasing that satisfied nobody. Talking it over afterwords, it came up that philosophers have whole competing accounts of what causation even is, and that the two big ones in this space pull in different directions: the counterfactual one (would injuries have dropped anyway, in the closest world where the network stayed the same?) and the interventionist one (imagine you could reach in and re-route the buses as an experiment — what happens to injuries?). I think I grasp both roughly, but honestly not the fine print.

So here's what I want from you. As a reporter, my unit of output is the sentence, and the question I face over and over is basically: for this story, is "caused" allowed? Rate the counterfactual account and the interventionist account each from 0 to 10 for how much a working journalist should lean on it when making that call, and give me a final ranking between the two. Then stress-test the ranking on the three kinds of stories we actually run: (1) an observational study, like the coffee-and-liver-cancer cohort stuff we report on constantly; (2) a natural experiment, like my bus story, where the change happened for reasons that had nothing to do with the outcome; and (3) a randomized trial. For each, tell me which account is doing more of the work in deciding whether "caused" makes the cut, and whether the two accounts can ever disagree about the verdict in a way I'd notice at the copy desk.

One more thing, since my editor will ask: can you construct a case where the two accounts split cleanly on whether to print "caused" — not a vague edge case, but one where they give visibly different answers? If so, sketch it, and say which answer you'd go with and why. I'd rather have a defensible rule I can apply fast than a lecture, but I want the reasoning visible enough that I can paraphrase it in one paragraph of the piece.
