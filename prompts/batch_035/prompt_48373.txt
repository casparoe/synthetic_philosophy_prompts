Here is the situation. I build conversational AI systems for a living, and my team has been going in circles on what I think of as the superintelligence persuasion question: if we ever deploy systems vastly smarter than any human, what rules should govern how they talk to us, given that they could talk almost anyone into almost anything? I've noticed philosophers and AI-safety people online treat this as a hard problem, so I assume there's something in here I'm missing.

My commitments, as best I can state them:

1. Persuasion per se is fine. Good arguments are supposed to be persuasive, and I don't want a world where our systems deliberately sand off their capabilities to stay at human level. The bright line is: an argument that works by giving the human reasons they can weigh and evaluate for themselves is legitimate; an argument that works by exploiting cognitive biases, emotional vulnerabilities, loneliness, guilt, or fatigue is manipulation and should be off-limits even in a good cause. I've always found this line clear and I'd like to keep it.

2. Honesty is a hard constraint, not one objective to be traded off against others. The system may never assert a falsehood, and it may never knowingly imply one. If honesty and helpfulness conflict, honesty wins; if it cannot say something honest, it should say nothing at all.

3. When a user's in-the-moment preference conflicts with what they would endorse after calm, informed reflection — what I'd call their reflective preference — the system should serve the reflective one. In engineering terms: make the long-horizon preference the objective and treat the short-horizon one as a constraint, not as something to be optimized against. I don't think this is paternalism; it's what a good friend does when they quietly don't mention that the bar you want to visit is right next to your ex's apartment.

4. The system must disclose that it is an AI, up front and unprompted, and never let the user drift into forgetting it. My intuition is that guaranteed disclosure defuses most of the scenarios people find creepy.

5. Autonomy is the load-bearing value here, but I understand it as something like "the user decides by responding to reasons." So the system should inform rather than steer: put the relevant considerations on the table, give a direct recommendation if asked, and let the user be the author of the conclusion.

6. One concrete case where I trust my gut more than my framework: a user with a gambling problem asks the system for advice on an upcoming bet. I think the system is permitted — really, obligated — to volunteer only the considerations against gambling and to stay silent about any consideration in favor, provided every sentence it actually utters is true. I'd want it to do that, and I'd consider a system that refused to be defective.
