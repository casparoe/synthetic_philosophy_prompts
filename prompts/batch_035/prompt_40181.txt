I belong to a reading group that began with game theory and has drifted into the philosophy of economics, and one phrase now does more work in our discussions than it appears capable of bearing: that some proposition is common knowledge among the players.

It was deployed twice recently, in ways that strike me as different in kind. During a discussion of the centipede game, a member objected that backward induction requires each player to rely on the other's rationality precisely at decision points that the induction itself predicts will never be reached — and, worse, at points the theory treats as impossible to reach. The reply was that rationality is common knowledge among the players, and this was treated as settling the matter. On another occasion the same phrase was used far more casually, to license the assumption that two players who have studied the same payoff matrix may each rely on the other having studied it.

I would like to know what the phrase actually commits us to. Please lay out, as a menu rather than a single favored analysis, the distinct precise theses an ascription of common knowledge could be expressing: for instance, the bare claim that each party knows the proposition; claims involving some finite depth of nested knowledge, that I know that you know, and that you know that I know; the literal infinite hierarchy of nested knowledge; and any reading on which the mutual knowledge is generated by a shared, publicly observable basis rather than possessed outright, each party deriving the whole structure from what they jointly perceive. For each reading, say whether it is trivially satisfied in everyday cases, substantive but defensible, or too strong to be true of any actual believers, and note which, if any, corresponds to the way the phrase is used in ordinary conversation. Then address the two deployments directly. Which reading was required to close the objection in the centipede discussion, and does it bear that weight — in particular, does the appeal require the literal infinite hierarchy, or can the shared-basis reading, or something weaker still, do the same work? And is there any reading under which the casual use about the payoff matrix is legitimate, given that no one has checked anything resembling an infinite hierarchy in that case?
