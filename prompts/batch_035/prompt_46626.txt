I'm teaching the closing module of a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of personal identity, and for the final week I want to pair Kripke's origin essentialism — the thesis that a person could not have developed from a different sperm and egg than the ones from which that person actually developed — with Kazuo Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go. The novel seems built for the question: the clones spend their adolescence hunting for their possibles, the people they were copied from, as though their identities were exhausted by their genetic origins, while the society that created them justifies harvesting them precisely by treating origin as destiny. Please give me a seminar-length reading that takes both the modal metaphysics and the novel seriously. Does Kripkean origin essentialism, which individuates each clone as a distinct person with an origin no one else could share, actually arm the clones against the donors' ideology — or does it do the other side's work too, by making origins metaphysically load-bearing in a way the novel quietly refuses? Which scenes should anchor the discussion — Ruth's possible at the Norfolk office, Miss Lucy's insistence that the students already know everything, Tommy's imaginary animals, or Kathy's final vigil at the Norfolk fence — and where does the reading strain? I would like the class to come away able to say whether the novel contributes something to the debate over origins that the arguments by themselves cannot.
