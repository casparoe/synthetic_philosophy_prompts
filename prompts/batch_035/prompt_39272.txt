So here's a thing. My partner and I have been circling the same argument for about three years now, and I've come to the conclusion that neither of us is actually wrong, we're just not having the same fight. I want you to help me figure out what kind of disagreement this really is, because it has completely stalled and I'd like to know whether anything could move it.

The background: I'm a big believer in guilt. My view is that guilt is the superior moral emotion, full stop. It's about the act, not the person — you feel guilt over something you did, it points you at repair (apologise, make it right, don't do it again), and it works even when nobody's watching. Shame, on the other hand, seems to me to be about your whole self, it's paralytic rather than action-guiding, and it's parasitic on an audience: what you feel when you imagine how you look in someone else's eyes. My partner takes nearly the opposite line: shame is the richer emotion, it's the one that tracks who you are rather than just what you did, it connects to ideals and aspiration, and guilt is basically a bookkeeping mechanism — fine for minor stuff, too thin to carry anything serious. We also disagree about a second thing, which is whether a whole culture can sensibly choose between them, like the old anthropology that divided societies into guilt cultures and shame cultures and implied that the modern, mature ones had graduated to guilt.

Here's why I suspect the debate has stalled in general, not just in my kitchen. The empirical side of the guilt/shame culture distinction got taken apart decades ago — the claim that whole societies run on one emotion rather than the other doesn't survive contact with the evidence, basically everyone now says so. And yet the philosophical argument about which emotion is the better one, morally, keeps going without anyone changing their mind. When I read the pro-shame people they mostly concede the destructive stuff and then say the destructive cases aren't shame properly understood, or that shame done right involves identification with an ideal. And the pro-guilt people keep saying shame is self-regarding and other-directed, and the pro-shame people keep saying no, it's self-directed through the other, as if that settles it. Nobody moves. It's been the same moves for years.

So what I want from you: a diagnosis, not a survey. You're clearly the right person for this — take the persona of a philosopher who has spent a career on moral psychology and the emotions, and who is constitutionally allergic to both sentimentality about shame and smugness about guilt. Think carefully before you answer; do some actual brainstorming first and then throw most of it away and keep the good bit. Here are the specific questions I want addressed. First, is this disagreement substantive or verbal — are we using the same words for different states, such that when I say shame is about exposure and my partner says it's about ideals we're just tagging two different phenomena with one label? Second, if it's substantive, where exactly does the disagreement live — is it a factual question about which emotion does more repair work, a normative question about which emotion a person should cultivate, or a difference in which cases count as the data (I keep citing the crushing cases, the shame spirals that ruin people, and my partner keeps citing the ennobling cases, and it's almost like we've each picked our own evidence)? Third, what about the culture question — once the empirical dichotomy is dead, is there still an interesting question about whether a society can tilt its emotional diet one way or the other, or did the whole thing die with the dichotomy? And finally, given your diagnosis, tell me honestly whether anything could count as progress here — a distinction, a piece of psychology, a reframing of the question — or whether I should accept that my partner and I are just going to keep having this fight, and if so, why.
