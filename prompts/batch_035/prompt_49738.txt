I am sixty-two, retired from a career in insurance, and for the past few years I have been reading my way through everything I can find on genetics and intelligence — popular books, long magazine features, recorded lectures. I have no training in statistics beyond what the job required and no way to check what I read against the primary literature, so I want to be careful about what I actually end up believing.

Here is the position I have arrived at, stated as plainly as I can. Suppose a trait — height, or performance on IQ tests — is measured at 70 or 80 percent heritable in a modern Western population. I have come to think that in that case the differences between individuals in that population are mostly fixed at conception, and three further things follow. First, a high heritability figure tells you something about malleability: the differences are not merely correlated with genes under current conditions but are substantially unchangeable. Second, because of that, large social programs aimed at narrowing the differences — early childhood education, nutrition, income support — are fighting biology, and whatever gains they produce can be expected to wash out. Third, if heritability is that high everywhere it has been seriously measured, that is evidence it would be just as high anywhere, so the same conclusion transfers to poorer societies, and to attempts to raise the average and not just narrow the spread.

The seed of this was something my neighbor, a plant breeder, once said: heritability in his barley is high precisely in the years when his fields are uniform, and it drops whenever conditions vary. I believe he meant it as a warning against reading too much into the number, but it struck me the other way — our society is fairly uniform compared with the past, so perhaps the high heritability figures simply mean we have already removed most of the environmental differences that mattered, and what remains is what genes alone can explain. My brother-in-law, a quantitative geneticist, says my view runs together three or four distinct claims, at least one of which he calls settled and wrong, but he refuses to unpack it at the dinner table.

So here is my request. Take each of my three claims separately, since I suspect they stand or fall independently, and tell me what would count against each: which observations, which kinds of studies, which arguments, including things that have arguably already happened — I have read, for instance, that average IQ scores rose steadily across the twentieth century, which sounds like it ought to embarrass claim three, though I am not confident I understand why or whether it really does. For each claim, rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, tell me what a knowledgeable defender of the view would himself concede counts against it versus dismiss, and whether the claim can be kept alive indefinitely by adding qualifications — and if it can, whether that is a problem for a claim of this kind. Throughout, please separate what is genuinely settled among people who work on this from what is still contested, and do not hand me a contested reading dressed up as the expert consensus; I would rather be told plainly that the field disagrees than be reassured. Finish by telling me which parts of the position I should give up immediately and which I could reasonably keep.
