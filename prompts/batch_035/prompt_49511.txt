I teach philosophy at a mid-sized state school, and every spring I get pulled into undergrad advising, which mostly means sitting through a bunch of twenty-minute appointments with juniors who are staring down choices that will genuinely change who they are. Go to grad school in philosophy or bail out to law school. Take two years off to do fieldwork abroad. Stay with the partner who wants kids now, or break up and keep the whole thing open. I teach L.A. Paul's transformative experience stuff in my decision theory seminar, so of course I see the relevance immediately -- and then it fails me completely in practice. Either I slide into follow-your-heart mush, or I start explaining revealability to a twenty year old who just wants to know whether to fill out the GRE form. Knowing the theory is apparently worth nothing here.

So here's what I want from you. Act as a decision theorist who has spent real time on the transformative choice literature and who is good at building tools for people who are NOT decision theorists. Please actually think before writing, and consider brainstorming the design a bit first: what a rational-choice framework can even promise when the agent can't know what she'll be like afterward, and where any procedure has to quietly give up on that framework. Then give me a procedure I can run in a twenty-minute appointment -- the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it terminates with "this is a values question and it's yours, not mine," and where it hands things over to a coin or some other default, if that's genuinely what the theory recommends. I want you to be upfront about the spots where the procedure is a judgment call dressed up as a step.

Then run it on three cases: (1) a student deciding between a philosophy PhD and law school, (2) a student deciding whether to study abroad for a full year in a country where she doesn't speak the language, and (3) a student deciding whether to propose to her partner, who wants children, when she doesn't know if she does. Include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer I won't like -- I suspect the grad school one is exactly where my own biases are worst, so don't spare me.

Two more things. First, I'd like you to separate what is genuinely settled in this literature from what people who know it well still fight about -- e.g., whether testimony and narratives give you any real advance grip on what an experience will be like, or whether the whole problem defeats rational choice theory or can be patched. Don't present a contested line as the consensus, and flag where you're taking sides. Second, boring requirement: write in American English.

And a last confession: I'm partly testing whether this framework deserves its place in my syllabus. If the honest answer is that the most Paul gives an advisor is a vocabulary for explaining why she can't decide for the student, say so -- but then tell me what the procedure is doing instead of deciding.
