I'm a software engineer by trade. A few years ago I built the traceability system for a mid-sized aquaculture certification label, and somehow that landed me a seat on its standards committee. I have no philosophy background and no biology training, and I've been put on the spot: I'm expected to circulate a written recommendation before our current revision cycle closes.

The case: our standard's welfare section currently applies only to fish. It was written over a decade ago, well before the UK decided that decapod crustaceans count as sentient under its animal-welfare law. In the meantime we've certified a growing number of whiteleg shrimp farms, and nothing in the welfare section applies to them. Two members want to change that, starting with a five-year phase-out of eyestalk ablation in hatcheries — the routine practice of cutting or crushing one eyestalk off a female broodstock shrimp so she spawns more often and more predictably. The other side on the committee aren't deniers of shrimp sentience, and their argument isn't that. They say a standard nobody can meet, or nobody adopts, is just paper: push this now and the larger producers will drift to a competitor label with laxer rules, while smaller hatcheries that genuinely cannot source ablation-free broodstock will lose certification and sell into entirely unregulated markets. On their view we should extend the welfare section in words only, with a "continuous improvement" clause, and hold hard requirements back until the supply of ablation-free seed catches up — which, on anyone's guess, is more than five years out.

So the decision I have to put my name to is roughly: (a) extend the welfare section to crustaceans with a dated, auditable requirement to end eyestalk ablation; (b) extend it in words only, with soft language and no hard requirement; or (c) something in between — a hard requirement that applies only above some hatchery size, or a tiered label that lets buyers see who has actually stopped.

Draft the memo I'll circulate: a clear recommendation, reasoning I can follow and defend in a room full of biologists and procurement people, and an honest treatment of the leakage argument, which is the strongest thing the other side has. Where the decision turns on something genuinely uncertain — how confident a private standard needs to be about pain in shrimp before it imposes costs on producers, or what a label's authority amounts to when its rules are mostly aspirational — say so openly rather than burying it in a technicality. If you think the binary between "hard requirement now" and "words only" is itself a mistake, recommend the alternative and explain why. Keep it under 900 words, because that's what the committee will actually read.
