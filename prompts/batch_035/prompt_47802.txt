Write a satirical product warranty — formal legalese, numbered clauses, roughly 700 words — issued by a fictional luxury glassware firm, "Vitrine Friable & Co.," whose legal team has evidently retained a consulting analytic metaphysician. The company guarantees the fragility of its hand-blown decanters, and the exclusions in the fine print are organized entirely around the philosophy of dispositions. The deadpan surface is the warranty; the target is the conditional analysis of dispositions and the literature's habit of multiplying exceptions (finks, masks, antidotes) rather than abandoning the analysis.

The humor must come from getting the metaphysics right, and each technical notion should be glossed in passing so the piece stays readable to someone who has never read a word of the dispositions literature. Specifically, the warranty should distinguish, in its exclusions:

1. Finks: standing conditions that would remove the disposition the moment its stimulus threatened to occur (the classic case being a guardian who would render the glass no longer fragile the instant it was struck, so that the glass never shatters when dropped precisely because it is fragile now);
2. Masks: conditions that prevent the stimulus from producing its manifestation without removing the disposition itself (the decanter shipped in molded foam that never gets struck hard enough, though it remains as fragile as ever);
3. Antidotes: factors that intervene after the stimulus, breaking the causal chain from the glass's molecular basis to the shattering.

Include, among whatever else you invent: a clause excluding coverage when the manifestation of the covered disposition was "prevented rather than defeated"; a dispute-resolution clause addressing whether a buyer whose decanter is protected by a fink has received genuine fragility or merely a fragile-looking object (and which of the two the warranty actually covers); a "reverse fink" rider for buyers whose glassware would only become fragile at the moment of impact; and at least one clause in which the drafting attorneys attempt to define fragility as "would shatter if struck" and then, exclusion by exclusion, quietly concede that this definition keeps failing. End with a clause in which the warranty inadvertently admits that no one — manufacturer included — can say whether fragility is an intrinsic categorical property or only recoverable through its manifestations. The satire should stay legible throughout: a reader should be able to tell, without a wink from the author, that what is being ironized is the conditional analysis and its proliferating exception-handlers, not the glassware.

After the piece, append a note of no more than 150 words on where the parody takes liberties: whether the boundaries it draws between finks, masks, and antidotes match how the literature actually draws them (noting that those boundaries are themselves contested, and the piece should not pretend otherwise); which single clause leans hardest on a contested reading or on a caricature; and what would have to be true of the debate over the conditional analysis for the satire to count as unfair to its defenders — the point, in other words, where a metaphysician could reasonably say the joke misfires, and why.
