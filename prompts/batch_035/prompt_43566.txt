I should say up front that I have no academic training: I left school at seventeen and have spent my working life doing bookkeeping for small businesses. But I have always been fond of arithmetic, and for the past year I have been occupied with a puzzle about pricing risk that I cannot settle to my own satisfaction. I would like you to write me an essay about it — one that does not end on a fence.

Here is the arrangement. A fair coin is flipped again and again until it first comes up heads. If that first heads arrives on flip number n, the payoff is fixed by n alone: when n is odd the house pays me 2^n/n dollars, and when n is even I pay the house 2^n/n dollars. So there is a one-half chance of my winning $2 (n = 1), a one-quarter chance of my losing $2 (n = 2), a one-eighth chance of my winning about $2.67 (n = 3), a one-sixteenth chance of my losing $4 (n = 4), and so on without end. The payoffs grow roughly like 2^n divided by n while the chances shrink like 2^-n, and the two rates cancel exactly: round n contributes +1/n or -1/n to the expected value according to whether n is odd or even. Added in the order in which the rounds occur, the contributions give 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 and so on, which settles at about 69 cents — the natural logarithm of 2, I am told, though I confirmed the first dozen partial sums myself with a spreadsheet.

The trouble is what happens when the same figures are added in a different order. That series converges only conditionally: gathered together, the positive contributions come to plus infinity and the negative ones to minus infinity, and by interleaving the two groups at chosen points one can make the total come out to any number one likes. Nothing in the game itself fixes an order of accounting — the rounds do not arrive in order of size or importance — so it seems that the game's expected value is not any particular figure at all. It is undefined. I have since heard that philosophers discuss a very similar arrangement under the name of the Pasadena game, but I have not read that literature and do not want the essay to lean on it; I want the argument worked out from the description I have given.

Let me lay out where my own thinking has got to, so you can push against it. First, the obvious answer is 69 cents: that is what the tally comes to if one simply adds the contributions round by round, in the order the rounds occur. But I cannot see what makes the order of occurrence the right order for pricing. It looks like an accident of how I described the setup, not a fact about what the game is worth. Second, I have noticed how the game actually treats someone who plays it once: the single likeliest outcome is winning $2 on the first flip (one chance in two), and altogether I come out ahead in two out of every three plays. It strikes me as strange that a game I usually win, whose likeliest result is a clean gain, should be worth a meagre 69 cents — and stranger still that its worth should turn on something as bookkeeping-adjacent as the order of summation. Perhaps expectation is simply the wrong instrument for a game played once. Third, I tried inventing a rule of my own: value the game at the worst figure that any legitimate grouping of terms yields, treating the accounting ambiguity like an adversary. But the rule collapses immediately — since some grouping yields minus infinity, my rule prices the game as certain ruin, and no one would ever play at any price, which seems far too stern for a game I usually win. Yet I cannot say where the rule goes wrong, because I cannot say which groupings are illegitimate without assuming the very thing at issue.

Write an essay that takes up the question of what a rational person should pay to play this game, and, more broadly, what decision theory ought to do when an expected value fails to be defined. Weigh the candidate answers — price it at 69 cents on the strength of the round-order tally; declare it unpriceable and refuse to attach any figure to it; look to something other than expectation, such as how the game distributes its outcomes over a single play; or strike out in some other direction entirely, including defending or repairing my failed worst-grouping rule. Please argue from the setup as I have described it: I have no way of checking references, so do not fill the essay with citations or reading suggestions. Most importantly, do not end on a non-committal note — reach and defend a verdict about what the game is worth, and say plainly what becomes of expected-value reasoning once games like this are admitted.
