After thirty-five years in quantum optics I retired and have spent the last few years reading philosophy seriously, and I've been invited to present at a university philosophy department's work-in-progress seminar. My thesis is that Fitch's paradox of knowability, correctly understood, refutes unrestricted semantic anti-realism, and that every standard escape route pays too high a price. I came to Dummett's anti-realism through the measurement problem, so it genuinely bothers me that the best formal argument against him seems to go through.

The derivation I'll present, in brief: assume every truth is knowable. Take any truth p that is in fact unknown; then the conjunction of p with its being unknown is also true, so by the knowability principle it is possible that someone knows that conjunction. But knowledge distributes over conjunction and is factive, so that person would both know p and know that p is unknown — and since knowledge is factive, that means p is both known and not known. So the conjunction is unknowable; hence either some truths are unknowable or no truth is unknown, i.e., all truths are known. I'll argue that the derivation uses only distribution of knowledge over conjunction plus factivity, that it is valid in ordinary classical epistemic logic, and that the familiar fallbacks — restricting knowability to observationally basic statements, restricting it to propositions whose negation is not already known, or reading can-be-known counterfactually — either cripple the anti-realist's connection between meaning and verification or quietly reintroduce realist commitments.

The audience: mostly philosophical generalists, plus one senior figure who is a committed Dummettian and has published on exactly this, and two logicians who will check every step. I also carry the liability of being a physicist in a philosophy seminar, which shapes what people will think they can get away with pressing. Prepare my Q&A sheet: the objections in roughly the order they will come, ranked by how much damage each would do, phrased the way this audience would phrase them — I fully expect someone to charge that the proof covertly assumes that knowing something implies knowing that you know it, and I want to be ready for exactly why that charge misses; the Dummettian will have a prepared line about what we, collectively and over time, can verify; the logicians will ask about intuitionistic logic and about whether the modal step is doing hidden work. For each objection, give me a two-minute reply and a candid note on where the reply is soft, and tell me which question I should be quietly hoping nobody asks.
