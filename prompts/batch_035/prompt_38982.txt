I have spent twenty years in writing workshops, where the injunction "write what you know" is repeated with the authority of settled doctrine — most recently by a colleague who invoked it to tell a student that her novel about a mathematician in 1913 Göttingen was a dead end, since she is neither a mathematician nor German. The workshop closed ranks around him, and the student, though her project survived, left believing she had been refuted rather than merely instructed. I have been unable to say precisely what the slogan had claimed in that moment, and I would like it disambiguated before I have to referee this dispute again. Lay out the distinct precise claims "write what you know" could be making — from the trivially descriptive (writers draw on their experience), through the epistemic (only first-hand knowledge yields the concrete, load-bearing detail that fiction requires), to the fully normative (a writer has decisive reason to write only what she knows). Then take the normative readings through the debate over internal and external reasons, as it runs from Bernard Williams to his critics: is any reason to write what one knows grounded in the writer's existing motivational set — so that the injunction states at most a conditional, one that fails for a writer who wants nothing from her own biography — or can knowledge of a subject supply a reason to write about it independently of any antecedent desire? For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, substantive but defensible, or straightforwardly false, and above all tell me which, if any, actually supports my colleague's use of the slogan — closing down a project on grounds of biography — as opposed to merely counselling more research.
