I serve on the capital planning committee of a mid-sized regional transit authority, and at next month's meeting we must choose between two bids for replacing our aging fare-collection system. Bid A is a conventional array of full-height barrier turnstiles tied to an analytics dashboard that flags fare-evasion hotspots for targeted enforcement patrols. Bid B is a barrier-free, proof-of-payment system with random roving inspections, of the kind common on European networks. One board member has circulated a memo arguing that Bid B is not merely cheaper or smoother but is the politically right artifact: gateless stations treat riders as trusted passengers rather than suspects, and he invokes the famous essay on artifacts having politics — the one with the low parkway overpasses supposedly built so that buses, and the people who ride them, could not reach the beach. Another member replies that this is romanticism: the politics lives in the fare schedule and the enforcement budget, both of which this board sets whatever metal we install; a turnstile no more legislates than a doorknob does, and a gateless system will simply displace suspicion onto the roving inspectors, who will profile.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before the meeting. Write as a philosopher of technology advising a public body, not as a vendor. Before committing to a recommendation, think through the correspondence between our two bids and the two members' claims about where the politics of an artifact resides. The memo should recommend one bid outright, give reasons the board can act on, and engage the strongest consideration on the losing side — including the awkward possibility that the overpass story, even if dubious as history, is still doing real work as an example. It should also specify what follow-on policy commitments (inspection protocols, staffing, data-handling rules) our chosen artifact would require from us to remain coherent with the reasons for choosing it, since the disagreement between my two colleagues seems to turn on exactly where the politics of the artifact ends and the politics of its administration begins. Take a definite position rather than laying out options for us to pick between, and flag any point where your reasoning depends on a contested reading of the artifact-politics literature. Please write in American English.
