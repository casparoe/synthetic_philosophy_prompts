I'm a senior and co-president of my school's environmental club, and I've run into a problem I can't solve by arguing harder at meetings. Our club regularly takes positions on local projects — endorsing a wetland restoration, opposing a housing development, funding a tree planting — and our reasoning is always the same: more habitat and more wildlife is better. But I've recently read about a view that challenges this from the inside: that because most wild animals are members of species that produce enormous numbers of offspring, most of which die young of starvation, disease, or predation, the average wild animal's life may contain more suffering than wellbeing — in which case some habitat destruction, by shrinking animal populations, might reduce net suffering rather than increase it. I don't know whether I believe this, and neither does anyone in the club, but I also can't pretend our old assumption is obviously safe anymore. At the same time, we're a high school club, not a research institute, and "maybe nature is bad, actually" is not a message we can put on a poster, nor one we should adopt on the strength of one philosophy essay.

So here is what I'm asking for. Please turn this debate — about whether ecosystems, and particular kinds of ecosystems, are on net good or bad for the creatures living in them — into a decision procedure our club can actually run. The constraints are real: we meet twice a month, we have at most twenty minutes per proposal, nobody in the room has training beyond AP Biology, we have no internet access during meetings, and our faculty advisor (a biology teacher) has to sign off on anything we formally endorse, so the procedure's output has to be explainable to her in a few sentences. The procedure should give us the questions to ask about a proposal, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each question given that we can only reason from general ecological common sense (things like which animals have many offspring versus few, what trophic levels imply about population sizes, how quickly populations rebound); and where the evidence runs out and we're left with a pure values question — for instance, whether our club even exists to help individual animals, or to protect species and ecosystems as such, which I suspect no amount of biology can decide for us. It should say explicitly where the procedure stops with a "don't take a position" and where it hands the decision back to a club vote because the remaining question is about what we value, not what is true. Please be honest about which steps are real checks and which are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on three proposals we actually face this year: (1) restoring a drained field into a wetland that would mainly become breeding habitat for frogs and fish, animals that lay thousands of eggs of which almost none survive; (2) opposing a subdivision that would fragment a mature forest with long-lived birds and mammals that raise few offspring; and (3) converting a strip of the soccer field into a native pollinator meadow, which would dramatically increase the local insect population. Include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer I won't like — I joined this club because I love nature, and if your procedure tells me the wetland is the worst of the three for animals, I want to see that reasoning spelled out rather than softened. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: the questions about animal welfare and population sizes that no amount of deliberation in a high school classroom can settle, and what we should do in the meantime when a proposal can't wait.
