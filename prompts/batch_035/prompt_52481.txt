I've been chewing on the Allais paradox for a while now, mostly from youtube videos and wikipedia rabbit holes, not from any formal training. Quick recap of how I understand it: you pick between a sure $1 million versus a gamble that's mostly $2.5 million but with a small chance of getting nothing, and most people take the sure thing. Then in a second pair of choices, where all the options are gambles with roughly the same odds, the same people suddenly go for the bigger payout. Taken together the two choices violate the independence axiom of expected utility theory, since the second choice is basically the first one with a common chunk of probability swapped in on both sides.

I actually ran a version of this on myself with made-up stakes and noticed something I haven't seen anyone mention (though maybe someone has). My preference for the sure thing in the first pair completely evaporated when I imagined the choice being made for me by a coin flip, or when I imagined never finding out what the other option would have paid out. The "irrationality" seems to live entirely in the imagining myself knowing I walked away from a guaranteed million and ended up with zero. No knowledge of the counterfactual, no paradox. That makes me suspect the Allais preference isn't really about probability weighting at all but about regret that only exists when the comparison is vivid.

Anyway. I want to understand whether a die-hard expected utility theorist has a real answer to this, or whether they just fold their arms and say preferences are preferences. Write me a dialogue between two characters who are both sharp and neither is a pushover: one defending the independence axiom as a genuine requirement of rationality, the other sympathetic to the Allais responder. I'd like them to actually engage with each other — the EU defender should get to make her best moves (money pump arguments, sure-thing reasoning, maybe the point that the axioms only bite across the two choices taken together), and the Allais sympathist should get to push back with substance, not just vibes.

Mostly I want the dialogue to hit on three things. First, whether my regret observation helps the Allais side or actually hurts it — if the preference disappears when you can't see the counterfactual, does that show it was never about the outcomes themselves, and is that a defense or a confession? Second, whether the independence axiom is supposed to be self-evident or derived from something deeper, because it never felt self-evident to me but the money pump stuff is uncomfortable. Third, somewhere in there, what each of them thinks rationality is even for here — if nobody ever gets money pumped in real life, is conforming to the axioms still worth anything?

Keep it conversational, like two smart freinds arguing at a kitchen table, not a lecture with two voices. And don't let either one win too easily — I want to come away genuinely unsure which one I'd side with.
