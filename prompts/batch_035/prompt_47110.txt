After a career in radio astronomy, I retired some years ago and have since devoted myself to a serious reading of philosophy of science. What drew me in was the hope that Occam's razor might be more than a matter of taste — that the preference for the simpler hypothesis could be justified exactly, in the way I had been taught to justify anything. The formal theory of Kolmogorov complexity and Solomonoff induction appeared to supply this, and for a time I was content. Then I constructed the following argument, and it now appears to prove far too much: not merely that the razor is well grounded, but that it cannot discriminate at all.

(1) Among hypotheses that fit the data equally well, the rational preference is for the one with the shorter description; this is the razor made precise, and it underwrites the formal prior.
(2) The length of a description is defined only relative to a description language, formalized in the theory as a universal Turing machine.
(3) By the invariance theorem, for any two universal machines U and V there exists a constant c(U,V), independent of what is described, such that nothing costs more than c bits more under U than it does under V.
(4) Hence a change of machine cannot reverse a comparison of two hypotheses' description lengths, except within a margin that is bounded by a fixed constant.
(5) A fixed constant is negligible in the manner of lower-order terms in asymptotic analysis: the quantities we are comparing grow with the data, and the correction does not.
(6) Therefore, for scientific purposes, which hypothesis is the simpler one is independent of the language; simplicity is an objective feature of the hypothesis, and the choice of machine is a convention, like the choice of units.
(7) Now take any hypothesis at all — say, a doctrine that the world, complete with its fossils and with the light of distant stars already in transit, was created a few thousand years ago. I can build a universal machine that contains a one-bit instruction printing out that doctrine in full.
(8) Under that machine, the doctrine's description length collapses to a few bits plus its adjustable content, and by (6) this collapse is a mere change of convention, which I have no standing to call cheating.
(9) So the razor, made precise, either endorses the doctrine or delivers no verdict; it cannot do the one thing it was brought in to do, which was to exclude it.

The conclusion is absurd, and yet every step looked like something I had already accepted — (3) is a theorem, and (1) is what brought me to the subject in the first place. I feel the shift somewhere between (4) and (6), between differences being bounded and differences being negligible, but I cannot pin it down. Tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs; and tell me whether the way out is to deny that simplicity is objective, or to concede that some machines are more natural than others — in which case I should like to know what privileges them, without the privilege amounting to the razor smuggled into the language a second time.
