Last night I had a long argument with a friend that I expect to be reprised next month, in a more hostile register, by an audience I have to face. I'm giving a job talk defending a pragmatist theory of truth, and I would like you to prepare my Q&A sheet.

My thesis: to call a belief true is to say that it is the verdict an idealized community of inquiry would converge on — where "idealized" does not mean infallible; it means patient, self-correcting, and recruiting. The last word is the novel part. I claim that convergence by itself is not enough, because a community can settle into a stable agreement that would dissolve if newcomers with ordinary error and bias had to re-derive the verdict rather than inherit it. So I add a transmissibility condition: a converged verdict counts as truth only if it survives being re-derived by successive generations of initially disagreeing inquirers. Truth, on my view, is a fixed point under both inquiry and recruitment.

My friend, who is a committed Jamesian, spent three hours on this and made four moves against me. First, that "survives re-derivation by newcomers" is just a long way of saying "what works": a verdict survives only insofar as it keeps functioning for the people who hold it, so I have smuggled the cash-value criterion in through the back door. Second, that transmissibility is a fact about pedagogy, not about truth — quantum mechanics was true while being nearly unteachable for a generation, so my condition either misclassifies it or quietly weakens into something Peirce already said about the community of inquiry. Third, that from inside bounded inquiry we can never check what unbounded inquiry would converge on, so my fixed point is a promissory note we can never redeem. Fourth, that once I start bolting extra conditions onto convergence, I have conceded that convergence wasn't doing the work on its own — which is exactly the wedge a correspondence theorist needs. I didn't think I lost, but I didn't clearly win either.

The audience next month will be harder than my friend. It's a department of mainstream analytic epistemologists, most of them correspondence theorists or deflationists, plus a couple of formal epistemologists who think in priors and convergence results — and, this matters, no Peirce scholars and no historians of any kind, so nobody will be checking what Peirce actually said. They will treat my view as a proposal on the table and attack it as such.

Please draft my prep sheet. I want the objections this audience will actually raise — I expect some of my friend's moves to reappear in their mouths, though phrased their way, not his — ranked by how badly each could sink the talk. For each objection, give me a reply that fits in about two minutes when it's asked, plus a candid note on where that reply leaks. Include the single question I most dread, along with the most honest answer I could give if it comes anyway. And finish with a short list of the points from last night's argument that I should concede on stage rather than contest. Please write the whole thing in American English.
