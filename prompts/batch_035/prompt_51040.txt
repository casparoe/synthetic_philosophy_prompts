I teach moral philosophy at a mid-sized state school, and last week we hosted a small workshop on consciousness where the after-dinner conversation went completely off the rails (in a good way). Two of the visitors — I'll call them Marco and Dee, a metaphysician and someone working in animal ethics — spent two hours fighting about whether panpsychism, if true, would actually change anything morally. Marco went first: if experience goes all the way down to electrons and quarks, then there's a staggering amount of experience in the universe, and since we have no reason to expect fundamental experience to be pleasant rather than unpleasant, the expected amount of suffering in a single thunderstorm plausibly dwarfs the entire history of factory farming. He conceded this sounded unhinged but insisted it was just arithmetic. So taking panpsychism seriously, he said, should destabalize practical ethics — or at least make our whole hierarchy of moral urgencies look arbitrary. Dee thought this was way too fast: suffering isn't just any old experience, it's experience with negative valence, and there are decent reasons to think valence is a structural or functional property that only shows up in sufficiently organized systems. Panpsychists typically attribute only something like proto-experience to fundamental stuff, so on their own view it's morally inert until you bolt on an extra claim about valence that panpsychism itself doesn't provide.

Marco had two comebacks. First, the valence-requires-structure move struck him as ad hoc: if you're willing to say experience comes in degrees all the way down, why not valence, which looks biologically primitive — even very simple animals seem to feel pain, not merely represent damage? Second, and he thought this was the deeper point: if you insist valence only shows up at some level of organizational complexity, then panpsychism has quietly collapsed into emergentism about the only feature that matters morally — which is exactly the emergence that panpsychism was supposed to help us avoid. Dee wasn't moved. She said the combination problem is a problem about subjects (how micro-experiences sum into one mind), not about qualities, so you can be a good panpsychist and still hold that valence is a higher-order property — the view isn't collapsing, it's just non-uniform, and nobody promised uniformity. She also had a decision-theoretic point: even if Marco is right about valence, acting on "maybe electrons suffer, and there are something like 10^80 of them" is a textbook Pascalian situation — a smallish credence times an astronomical payoff — and giving in to that kind of reasoning is how you end up sending your entire paycheck to whoever can write down the biggest number. Marco's last word was that the credence isn't small: panpsychism is a live option that serious people actually defend, so if it's even 10-20% likely and valence extends down, the numbers swamp everything else, and refusing to let that affect your priorities is just motivated reasoning to keep our moral picture comfortable.

They ran out of wine and adjourned without resolution, and now the grad students who overheard the whole thing keep asking me who won — and honestly I couldn't decide, since both said things I half-agree with. Rate each step (Marco's opening, Dee's reply, Marco's two comebacks, Dee's two responses, Marco's final word) as clearly right, clearly wrong, or somewhere in between, with brief reasons. And please tell me if there's an important move or consideration neither of them made — I have a suspicion there definately is one, but I can't quite put my finger on it.
