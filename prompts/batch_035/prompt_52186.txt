Next month I am giving the keynote at the fiftieth-anniversary dinner of a Black filmmakers' cooperative founded by my late mentor, who ran it for four decades on an unrepentant Du Boisian principle. She read Du Bois's 1926 essay in The Crisis, "The Criteria of Negro Art," the way other people read scripture: all art is propaganda, always has been, and the artist who claims to have escaped that condition has merely been conscripted into someone else's. The room that evening will contain two generations of the co-op in barely concealed tension — the older members who chose their subjects for their political weight, and the younger members whose formally adventurous, sometimes deliberately apolitical, sometimes frankly commercial work is being honored with the co-op's own awards that same night. Write the speech. It must honor her conviction without making a strawman of it and without lecturing the younger filmmakers, which means giving the Du Boisian position its strongest form — that the choice between propaganda and beauty was itself a false one, since in a society structured by race no audience ever meets a work without its social meaning already attached — and then showing honestly where that view presses hardest on the work being celebrated, and where the younger artists have a real answer rather than mere evasion. About ten minutes, suitable for a mixed audience of filmmakers, donors, and family, and it should end with a toast rather than a conclusion.
