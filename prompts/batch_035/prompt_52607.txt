So here's my situation: I write novels, mostly literary fiction, and last year I got into an argument at my own book club about whether a character of mine had "become a villain" by the end. He does one genuinely cruel thing per chapter, each one slightly worse than the last, and at no single moment does he tip over — and I realized mid-argument that I have no idea what I think happens to a word like "villain" when you stretch it one notch at a time. Editors do this to me constantly too: a manuscript goes through maybe twelve drafts, each one changing maybe 2% of the sentences, and everyone still calls it "the same book." Is it? Apparently when did one grain become a heap.

I keep a small newsletter for other writers and I want to do a "which theory of vagueness do you actually hold?" quiz as a fun issue. Not the wikipedia-summary kind of thing — I want it to sort readers by what they say about cases, not by which label they like the sound of. Maybe 8-10 questions, each one a concrete scenario, with two or three answer options apiece, and then a scoring key at the end mapping answer patterns onto the actual philosophical positions.

The cases should come from stuff writers actually recognize: the character who gets meaner chapter by chapter, the draft that drifts into a different novel, the friend you keep calling "tall" who shaves his head and is suddenly "bald," a fictional town where everyone is middle-aged, "a few drinks" becoming "a drinking problem," that kind of thing. No question should just ask "do you think words have sharp boundaries?" — that's cheating, the reader has to be tricked into revealing a position, not asked to recite one.

The positions that need to show up as endpoints: the one where there IS a sharp cutoff somewhere, we just can't ever know where (which I gather is called epistemicism — correct me if I've got this wrong); the supervaluationist-ish idea that borderline sentences are neither true nor false but not for fuzzy-logic reasons; the degree-theory idea that things are sort-of-true to different extents; the view that it's all about context and shifting standards, so the question "is he tall?" is incomplete until you fix who's asking and why; and if there's a respectable "the question is just confused / 'bald' doesn't cleanly refer" position, I want that too. If some pair of these can't really be seperated by cases — if a reader answering honestly could land in two of them at once — please say so at the end rather than papering over it, I don't want to publish a sorting hat with a hidden broken hinge.

One more thing, and I mean it: I am a novelist, I will confidently repeat things I half-remember at dinner parties, so somewhere in the quiz or after the scoring key I need a short honest note on what's actually settled among philosophers who work on this versus what's genuinely up for grabs. I've gathered that basically nothing here is consensus, but I don't want to hand my readers a contested take dressed up as the official answer. If, say, everyone agrees the sorites paradox is a real problem but nobody agrees on the solution, tell me that explicitly, so I can put my smug face away at the right moments.

Length-wise, whatever fits the job — probably comparable to those personality quizzes that eat your whole coffee break. And it should be entertaining enough that a novelist's newsletter audience actually finishes it, not written like a logic textbook. Thanks!
