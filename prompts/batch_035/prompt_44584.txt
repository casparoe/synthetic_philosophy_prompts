I've been keeping notes on which gambles I would actually take, and my own choices don't hang together in any way I can see, so I want someone to tell me whether there's a principle underneath them or whether some of them are simply mistakes. I have no training in any of this; I just started making offers to myself and recording what I'd pick.

The pair that started it: a guaranteed $1 million, or a gamble paying $5 million with 10% probability, $1 million with 89%, and nothing with 1%. I take the guaranteed million without hesitating — turning down a sure million for a 1-in-100 shot at nothing feels like madness. But offered an 11% chance of $1 million versus a 10% chance of $5 million, I take the $5 million chance — when nothing was ever coming to me for sure, the extra 1-in-100 risk of getting nothing barely registers. And I've noticed the two pairs are secretly the same comparison: in each, 89 times out of 100 both options hand me the identical result (a million in the first pair, nothing in the second), so my choice should turn only on the remaining tickets — where the options offer roughly the same things, yet I choose opposite ways. I'd have thought there was an obvious rule here: where two options agree on what happens in most situations, my choice between them should depend only on the situations where they differ. My choices break it.

More verdicts since:

- Take away only the guarantee: $1 million with 99% probability, nothing with 1%, against $5 million with 10%, $1 million with 89%, nothing with 1%. I still take the almost-sure million — but now I have to deliberate, where at a true guarantee I didn't hesitate at all. One percent seems to be doing far more than one percent's worth of work.
- Shrink everything ten-thousandfold: a guaranteed $100 against 89% of $100, 10% of $500, 1% of nothing. I take the gamble without a flicker. The guaranteed $100 exerts no pull on me at all.
- Flip it all to losses and I flip with it: a guaranteed loss of $1 million against 89% losing $1 million, 10% losing $5 million, 1% losing nothing — I gamble. And in the matching second pair, I take the 11% chance of losing $1 million over the 10% chance of losing $5 million. In the loss world I gamble both times, consistently.
- The one I keep going back and forth on: if the first pair is run as a two-step draw — first you learn whether you're in the 89% where both options pay the same, and only then does your earlier choice take effect — I find myself taking the $5 million shot even in the first pair. That bothers me, because I can't see why the order in which I learn things should change what the tickets say.

My half-formed theory is that a sure thing sets a floor, and what I'm really weighing is how it would feel to end up below a floor I could have had — above all the 1% where I walk away with nothing knowing I refused a million. But that theory doesn't obviously explain why the guaranteed $100 leaves me cold, or why I turn consistent the moment everything is a loss. Is there a single principle that captures all of these verdicts? If saving them all requires something hopelessly complicated, say so, and tell me which verdicts to give up and why. Then tell me what the surviving principle predicts for cases I haven't listed — for instance, at what point "almost sure" becomes close enough to sure to grip me, or what it says about the two-step version of the loss pairs.
