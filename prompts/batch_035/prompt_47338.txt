I've been going down a rabbit hole on Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals and I think the best way for me to finally *get* the debate is to see it argued well from both sides. You're a professional philosopher of language with a serious background in probabilistic semantics, so please don't dumb anything down — think carefully before writing, and maybe sketch the strongest points each side has before you draft the dialogue itself.

Write a dialogue between two characters:

1. **Prop** — defends the orthodox view that indicative conditionals express propositions with truth conditions (roughly the Stalnaker line: "if A, B" is true iff B holds at the closest worlds where A holds). She accepts that Stalnaker's thesis — the probability of the conditional equals the corresponding conditional probability — fails in general, but thinks that's a feature of our credences, not evidence that conditionals aren't propositions.

2. **NTV** — defends the Adams-style no-truth-conditions view: indicative conditionals don't express propositions at all; asserting one is more like accepting B conditionally on A. For him, Lewis's triviality results are the smoking gun: if you insist both that conditionals are propositions AND that their probabilities always equal conditional probabilities, everything collapses to triviality. Since the probability connection is non-negotiable, drop the propositions.

A few requirements:

- Neither character is a strawman, and they must actually respond to each other's points — no alternating monologues.
- They should have a real exchange on at least: (a) whether the triviality proof forces giving up propositions or only forces restricting Stalnaker's thesis (say, to the agent's actual credence function at the time of utterance, or to "reasonable" credence functions); (b) the embedding problem — NTV has trouble saying what "If (if the shipment arrives Friday, we get the bonus), then we pay the dockers extra" even means, while Prop has trouble saying what probability to assign the embedded conditional; and (c) a conditional bet — a bookie takes your money on "if it rains tomorrow, the match is cancelled," and the bet is void unless it rains. Let them argue about whether void-if-no-rain bets favor NTV (the thing wagered on never gets observed to be false when the antecedent fails, so what work is its truth value doing?) or actually help Prop.
- Include at least one case of your own invention rather than recycling the textbook examples — e.g. a nurse triaging during a hospital blackout who has to act on "if the generator fails, ward 4 gets the ventilator" — and let the characters genuinely disagree about what the case shows. Don't engineer it so one side obviously wins.
- End with the two of them agreeing on one precise formulation of what the triviality result actually establishes — if such a neutral formulation exists — or failing to, and knowing exactly why they failed.

They can get a bit exasperated with each other but keep it collegial.
