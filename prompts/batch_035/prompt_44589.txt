I supervise a materials testing lab, and over the years I have settled into a practice for which I would like the established name and formulation, if one exists. When an instrument I only partly trust gives a reading, I neither treat the reading as fact nor ignore it: I move my confidence toward what it says, but only part of the way, taking a smaller step the more dubious the instrument is on that occasion. The same habit shows up outside the lab — when a colleague I trust about four-fifths of the time passes on a rumor, I shift my belief toward the rumor by roughly that fraction. I worked this proportionality rule out myself; it was only later that I dimly recalled, from a statistics course long ago, a rule for updating by odds which assumes the observation is certainly correct, and saw that my practice differs precisely in declining that assumption.

Two features of the practice trouble me, and I would like to know whether anyone has carved them properly.

First, my trust in the instrument is not fixed independently of the reading. I notice that I credit the machine more when it confirms what I already suspected and discount it when it surprises me. I accept that this is a bad habit, but I suspect it is not merely a bad habit: it looks as though the right treatment would have to say something about cases where the observer's reliability is entangled with the very question at issue, and my simple rule has nothing to offer there.

Second — and this is what I most want a name for, if it has one — when I let two such partial readings move me in succession, I sometimes arrive at a different final confidence depending on which reading I take first. I cannot tell whether this order-dependence is my own sloppiness, a symptom that the procedure is incoherent, or a genuine feature of situations in which evidence is less than certain.

So, three questions. Does the practice of partial updating have an established name and an exact formulation? What precisely does that formulation assume, and at what point do the assumptions fail — in particular, does it have anything to say about the entangled-reliability cases? And is the order-dependence itself a recognized phenomenon, with conditions under which it appears and disappears? A bare term will not satisfy me: I want the carving — what the rule actually says, when it applies, and where its edges are.
