I'm a retired geochemist — spent most of my career on isotope dating — so my instinct whenever I see a probability estimate is to ask what the error bars actually are. Lately I've gotten into philosophy, read Bostrom's simulation argument and then the longtermist literature, and I've landed on a position I'd like stress-tested.

The position: the simulation argument dampens fanaticism about the far future. By fanaticism I mean the move where you accept, say, a 10^-15 shot at 10^30 flourishing future lives over a sure reduction in malaria deaths now, because expected value swamps everything else. My thought is that once you take the trilemma seriously — either civilizations go extinct before reaching posthuman tech, or posthumans dont run ancestor simulations, or we're almost certainly inside one — the clean arithmetic behind those fanatical bets stops being clean:

1. If we're probably simulated, the far future I'm supposedly securing may be running inside someone's machine, and the entities whose values matter long-term are the simulators, whom I can't influence by donating to an x-risk charity in the ordinary way. Or the whole sim gets shut down. Either way the payoff side of the bet takes a haircut.

2. The trilemma's other horn — extinction before posthuman — also caps the far future, which cuts down the astronomical payoffs that make fanaticism attractive in the first place.

3. More generally, deep uncertainty about our own level — base reality vs simulation, run by whom, under what termination policy — functions like a huge systematic error term. A scientist learns early that when the systematic error swamps the signal, you dont get to act on the signal. Fanaticism assumes you can resolve probability times value to enough significant digits; the simulation argument says you can't.
