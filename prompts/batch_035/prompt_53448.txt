The practice question the student was answering was:

"Explain the symbol grounding problem as Stevan Harnad posed it in his 1990 paper, making use of his example of someone trying to learn Chinese using only a Chinese-to-Chinese dictionary. Then explain how the symbol grounding problem relates to Searle's Chinese Room argument: what the two share, and where they differ. Here is the student's answer:

"The symbol grounding problem was first identified by Searle in his 1980 paper, where he coined the term. The problem is that a formal symbol system manipulates symbols purely on the basis of their shapes, according to explicit rules, and none of this gives the symbols any meaning. Harnad illustrates this with the example of a monolingual English speaker who tries to learn Chinese using only a Chinese-to-Chinese dictionary. Every definition the speaker looks up just sends them to other equally meaningless symbols, so they can never get off the merry-go-round: the meanings in the dictionary are parasitic on meanings that already exist in the head of some reader who understands the language. For a system's symbols to mean something, the meaning has to be intrinsic to the system rather than borrowed from us.

Searle's Chinese Room argument and the symbol grounding problem are really two ways of making the same point. In the Chinese Room, a man who understands no Chinese sits in a room with a rulebook. Chinese symbols come in through a slot, he follows the rules to produce other Chinese symbols, and the outputs are so good that people outside take him for a fluent speaker. But he doesn't understand a word of it, and neither does the room, because he is only manipulating symbols syntactically, by their shapes. Syntax is not sufficient for semantics. This shows that symbols can never be grounded by symbol manipulation alone, so the symbol grounding problem has no solution for any machine, and Searle's argument establishes that the project of strong AI is impossible.

One might object to Searle via the systems reply: the man doesn't understand, but the whole system — man plus rulebook — does. Searle responds that the man could internalize the whole system and still not understand. Harnad also proposed the Total Turing Test, in which a machine must convince judges that it is conscious.

As for how grounding could be achieved, Harnad's solution was to abandon symbolic systems in favor of connectionist networks. Because neural networks are sub-symbolic and learn from experience rather than following explicit rules, they don't have the symbol grounding problem at all, and that is where he thought real cognition happens.

An objection to this solution is that current neural networks still make stupid mistakes, so we are a long way from human-level understanding."
