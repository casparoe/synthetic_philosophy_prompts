Like many people here, I've spent years reading LessWrong, the Alignment Forum, and adjacent blogs, and I've noticed a recurring rhetorical move that I want to understand better rather than keep nodding along to. In discussions of minds, AI, and self-improvement, people reach for Gödel's incompleteness theorems as if they were a general-purpose result about self-reference, and they get deployed for at least three distinct purposes: (1) Penrose-style arguments that human mathematical understanding can't be computational, since any formal system is limited by incompleteness in a way we allegedly aren't; (2) arguments that a self-improving AI faces an in-principle obstacle, because it can't prove that its successor or its own modified reasoning is sound without stepping outside itself; and (3) loose "the map can't contain the map" gestures — that any agent modeling itself, or any system verifying itself, must be incomplete in something like the way arithmetic is. The same community that makes these appeals also, on other days, insists that minds are computations and that AI systems are artifacts whose behavior will yield to ordinary engineering analysis.

Treat the recurring appeal to Gödel as a practice in its own right — a stock argumentative move in online rationalist and AI-risk discourse, with standard phrasings and standard conclusions. What exactly does that practice presuppose? I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory of what the incompleteness theorems say, and of what it takes to transport a result about formal systems into claims about agents, that these appeals only make sense under. Which of the three uses above actually transfer the theorem's content, and which smuggle in extra premises — e.g., that the system in question is axiomatizable, recursively enumerable, sufficiently expressive of arithmetic, and that consistency or soundness is the property whose unprovability matters — that may fail for the messy, non-idealized systems being discussed? Does the practice presuppose that a proof is the only route to justified self-trust, or only that it's a convenient route? Is the community's joint commitment to both computationalism and Gödelian humility actually in tension, or is there a coherent reading on which the two fit together — for instance, one on which the theorems tell us something about the costs of perfect self-knowledge rather than its impossibility? Distinguish what this argumentative practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, identify where it's underdetermined so that several readings of Gödel would license the same conclusions, and say what the discourse would have to stop claiming — or start claiming with extra argument — if it replaced the folk version of incompleteness with the precise statement of the theorems and their established extramathematical applications, such as they are.
