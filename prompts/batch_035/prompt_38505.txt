For thirty years I was a fisheries oceanographer, and a good part of my career was spent on stock assessments for groundfish — work that eventually made me an eyewitness to a textbook tragedy of the commons, complete with quotas that were scientifically advised, legally binding, and steadily ignored. Since retiring I have been reading widely in political economy and the philosophy of collective action, and I find myself dissatisfied with how the subject is usually discussed: every author seems to be promoting a favored remedy (privatize, regulate, empower the community) rather than stating what a theory of commons governance is supposed to accomplish. Before I read further, I want the specification rather than another candidate. So please write me the desiderata: what any adequate account of why some common-pool resources are depleted and others are governed sustainably must deliver.

Let me suggest some of the cases and considerations I'd want the list to engage, while leaving the judgment about them to you. The theory must presumably get right both the long-lived communal institutions — alpine pastures, irrigation systems, inshore fisheries that have persisted for generations without either privatization or state enforcement — and the rapid collapses that occurred where formal regulation existed on paper. It must respect distinctions I suspect are often collapsed: the difference between a fishery and a lighthouse or an uncrowded park (resources subtracted by use versus not), the difference between the problem of overuse and the problem of underprovision, and the difference between the commons as a property regime and the commons as an abstract strategic situation. It must be consistent with what non-cooperative game theory actually establishes — that defection dominates in one-shot play, and that this changes under iteration with conditional strategies — without smuggling in the further claim that anything outside that framework is unscientific. And it should probably say something about my own habit of thought: in stock assessment we always separated the stock from the flow, and I keep wanting to know whether a commons failure is a biophysical problem or an incentive problem, though I suspect that dichotomy may itself be one of the prejudices I am asking you to audit.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it; mark which are non-negotiable and which reflect the commitments of particular camps (a presumption of self-interested rationality, a presumption that solutions must scale, and so on); and tell me whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable — that is the part I most want to see, since a specification that cannot be met is itself a finding. Then, briefly, score Hardin's prescription of privatization or state regulation and the Ostrom-style account of self-governance against the list, and identify the single requirement that does the most to exclude candidate theories. A table is fine for the requirements themselves, but I'd like the reasoning in prose.
