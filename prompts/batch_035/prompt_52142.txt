my mom died in march. it's august now, and i've noticed something strange: i keep playing the same handful of sad songs on a loop — ones she liked, ones about losing someone — and it helps. not in a "facing it" therapy way exactly. it's more like the sadness is better company then distraction is. i mentioned this to a friend doing a philosophy phd and she said, oh, that's the paradox of tragedy, aristotle and hume were already arguing about it. then she said something that's been stuck in my head since: the standard answers apparently split into two camps. either the sad feeling itself gets converted into something pleasant by the safety and distance of art, or the sad feeling stays genuinely unpleasant and just coexists next to a separate pleasure. she said those two are supposed to be rivals. honestly, from inside my grief, i can't tell which one is happening, and it's started to feel like the kind of thing where the answer matters.

so here's my ask. write me a poem, somewhere in the 16–24 line range, no fixed form, with one line that repeats verbatim three times as a refrain — same words every time, no changes allowed. the refrain should be about the grief itself, and the trick is that each time it comes back, the stanzas around it should of shifted what the line feels like: first time it lands like a wound, by the third time it should read more like company — but without you ever touching the line itself. i want the structure to carry the question, whether a feeling can be the same feeling and yet change what it is to have it, because that's basically that conversion-versus-coexistence thing in verse form. and crucially: don't resolve it. the final appearance of the refrain should work under both readings — the one where the sadness got sweetened, and the one where it stayed raw and something else got sweet next to it. someone who knows the debate should be able to read the ending twice, once for each camp, and have both of them claim the poem.

two more things. keep my mom out of the specifics — no details about her, i don't want that. the poem should be about the listening, the loop, the feeling. and afterwards, add a short note telling me: (a) which of the two readings the poem is secretly leaning on, if it leans at all; (b) what would have to be different in the poem for the other reading to win; and (c) where you think it's most likely to fail at staying neutral — which stanza betrays it. i'd rather know exactly where it cheats than have you pretend it doesn't.
