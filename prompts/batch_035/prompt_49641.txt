I write as a retired physiologist who spent a working life measuring autonomic responses in human subjects — skin conductance, baroreflex gain, vagal tone — and who, in the two years since retiring, has been reading philosophy of mind with the enthusiasm of a convert and the habits of a bench scientist. The literature on emotion, as I understand it, divides into camps I cannot adjudicate from the outside. On one side are those who hold that an emotion is at bottom an evaluative judgment or appraisal: fear of the bear is the appraisal of the bear as dangerous to me, here and now. On the other side are those in the lineage of William James, for whom the emotion is the feeling of bodily change: we are afraid because we tremble, not the other way around. To my eye these look less like rival theories than like claims about different segments of one physiological circuit; yet the philosophers evidently think something important turns on which is true. I would like to see that stake made explicit.

Please write a dialogue between a philosopher committed to the appraisal view and one committed to the Jamesian feeling view. Both should be equally capable exponents; neither is to be a foil, and each should engage the other's actual arguments rather than a convenient misreading of them. Conduct the dialogue around three cases drawn from my working life, which I state precisely so that they can be argued about rather than merely alluded to:

Case A. A patient with a complete spinal cord transection above T6 reports that when a fire alarm sounded on his rehabilitation ward, he knew it was fear, yet describes the episode as a thought of fear rather than an instance of it — evaluation intact, the gripping bodily feeling attenuated.

Case B. A healthy subject given a beta-blocker before a public-speaking task reports that the pounding heart and tremor are gone; asked about the fear itself, some such subjects say it is undiminished, others that it is the same fear but colder, at one remove.

Case C. A listener reports chills at a passage of music — gooseflesh, bristling, a catch in the throat — while denying, apparently sincerely, that anything about the music bears on her welfare or projects in the way appraisals are usually said to be about things.

The dialogue should turn on these cases, in something like the following order. First, whether Cases A and B are instances of emotion with the feeling largely absent, or episodes of something else — judgment without emotion. Second, whether Case C is emotion with the appraisal absent, or mere bodily reaction without emotion. Third, whether the two parties can converge on a state-space description — a bodily-feeling dimension and an appraisal dimension capable of varying independently — while still disagreeing about which dimension, if either, constitutes the emotion rather than merely accompanying it. I would also like them to take up a suggestion I keep meeting in my reading: that a third camp treats emotions as perceptions of value, fear being a perception of danger as present, the way vision presents a surface as red. Have them ask whether this perceptualist view is a genuine third position or a repackaging of one of theirs.

Do not let them settle the matter by stipulation. If part of their dispute turns out to be verbal, have them say which part and why, and isolate what remains substantive. Close with each character naming, as precisely as they can, the single finding or argument that would move them to the other side — I have seen enough experiments in my life to know that a well-designed question, agreed to in advance, is worth a decade of assertion.
