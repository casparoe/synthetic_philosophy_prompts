I am a novelist, and I am planning my fourth book. Its conceit is this: the narration belongs to a single consciousness which, for most of the book, mistakes itself for many people — it believes it has lived several lives, held several names, died more than once — and the plot of the book simply is its slow discovery that the many persons were one all along. Whether this is a device or a disaster depends, I have learned, on a philosophical question I am not equipped to settle.

I raised the premise at a dinner at my flat, and two guests — Ellis, a retired physicist who has read Schrödinger the way other men read scripture, and Kavita, who has spent her career teaching Indian philosophy — took the question up between the main course and the cheese, and did not put it down until the taxi came. I will report the exchange as faithfully as I can, because I want it adjudicated properly, and I promise not to shade it in either direction.

Ellis began. He said my premise stands on respectable ground, and that the ground was laid by Schrödinger himself. In his old essays and his late book on his worldview, Schrödinger argued, borrowing from the Upanishads, that consciousness is one; the manyness of minds is appearance. Ellis said this was not, for Schrödinger, decorative mysticism but a considered answer to a genuine embarrassment in the foundations of physics: physics describes a single world, yet that world is somehow observed by many observers, and nothing in the physics tells you how many watchers stand behind the many bodies. Schrödinger's resolution was to deny that the plurality of minds is a fact of the same order as the plurality of organisms. So my narrator — one consciousness under the illusion of being several persons — occupies exactly the position Schrödinger assigned to each of us, and a premise endorsed by one of the founders of quantum mechanics can hardly be called unphilosophical.

Kavita said the ground was borrowed, and borrowed badly. In Advaita Vedanta, she said, the one consciousness — Brahman, identical with the innermost self — is not a self among things and cannot be an observer. What the tradition calls the error is precisely individuation: the carving of the one into a somebody with a point of view, a history, a set of memories. And that error is not corrected within a life; it is dissolved in realization, which is release — the end of the person, not the awakening of one. A narrator, by contrast, needs everything Advaita declares unreal: a voice, a memory, a perspective. So my premise is self-undermining. The moment the novel succeeds as a novel, it has quietly refuted its own narrator, because a consciousness that could tell a story would have to be exactly the sort of consciousness the doctrine says never truly existed.

Ellis said self-undermining was too strong. Schrödinger never claimed the one mind has no thoughts; he claimed the manyness of thinkers is the illusion. A voice can be genuine, Ellis said, while the ownership of that voice by this or that person is the mistake — and that is precisely the story I want to tell: the record of the mistake, in which the narrator gradually finds that its memories of being several people never belonged to several people. As for the point of view, that is what the ending is for. The book could close with the going-out of the perspective, the way the Upanishads close on silence rather than on a proposition. The illusion is allowed its equipment, Ellis said, because the whole book is the equipment being dismantled.

Kavita replied that this is where the borrowing shows. Schrödinger took from Vedanta the conclusion and left behind the method. In the tradition, realization is prepared by long discipline under a teacher, and it is not a psychological event that happens to a mind with a biography. What Ellis is proposing, she said, is a mind that becomes enlightened by accumulating insight, like a heroine of a Bildungsroman — the one thing the tradition says cannot happen, since the person who travels the road is not the one who arrives. And she added that Schrödinger was candid about this himself: in his late essays he admitted in effect that he had not reasoned his way to the view but had absorbed it from the Upanishads, finding there what reason could not deliver. A novelist who builds on this, she said, is building on someone else's epiphany and calling it architecture.

Ellis's last word was that an inherited epiphany is better than no epiphany at all. The mind-body problem, he said, is no nearer a solution than in Schrödinger's day, and the proposal that the number of minds is not a fact of the same order as the number of bodies remains one of the few moves on the board that does not add new entities to the world. He was willing to concede the novel might fail; he insisted only that if it failed, it would fail as a novel and not as metaphysics. And Kavita's alternative, he said — a plurality of genuine narrators, each sealed in its own skull — is not more sober than his view, merely more familiar.

Kavita's last word was that familiar is what fiction trades in. A novel that requires the reader to accept a metaphysics before caring about a character has put the cart before the horse, she said. Schrödinger was free to believe; the novelist has to be believed.

They left it there, and I have not been able to referee it myself. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: say which of the claims each of them made are right, which overreach, and where each of them concedes more or less than they should. Then tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing. And finally, since I have a book to write and not merely a curiosity to satisfy, tell me what follows for the novel — not just who wins the argument, but what the verdict actually commits me to. Please answer in continuous prose only, as one would write a long letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
