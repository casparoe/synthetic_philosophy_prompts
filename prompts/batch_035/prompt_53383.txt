So I'm taking this decision theory course right now and we just covered Good's theorem (that the expected value of getting more info before you decide is never negative, as long as the info is free and you update properly), and now I've landed in an actual situation where it matters and I'm not sure it applies. I'm on my university's student union allocation board, and next month we have to decide between two options for a big chunk of money: either fund a full renovation of the study lounge, or spend it on extending the campus shuttle's late-night hours for two years. Roughly 60/40 the board currently favors the renovation, but a bunch of us feel pretty unsure.

At our last meeting, one member proposed we spend a few thousand dollars from our discretionary fund on a campus-wide survey (usage tracking for the lounge, a poll on the shuttle, etc.) before we commit the money. Her argument was basically "more data can't hurt, information always has nonnegative value." Someone else pushed back and said no, information obviously CAN hurt you sometimes, and now it's become a whole fight. The chair asked each of us to write up a short position paper before the next meeting.

Here's what I actually want to figure out, and what I'd like you to write up as the memo I'll circulate to the board: does the "information can't hurt" intuition survive contact with our actual situation? From what I remember from lecture, Good's theorem holds for a single Bayesian agent with free information, but our situation seems to break the assumptions in a few ways: (1) the survey costs money, which is easy enough to fold in, but (2) we're a committee, not one agent — we have different priors and different values, and we vote, so I'm worried there's some way the survey makes the group decision worse even if it makes every individual member's expectations better, like if the info shifts some marginal member's vote in a way that's bad from the perspective of the people who were already confident, or something about how majority voting doesn't aggregate posteriors the way a single agent would; and (3) apparently whichever option is leading in the poll gets announced before the vote, and the student newspaper will publish it, which the renovation-people say could itself change behavior (people might stop using the lounge if they hear it's getting renovated anyway, or start using the shuttle). I honestly don't know if these are real problems or just vibes.

So the memo should: recommend whether we commission the survey (yes / no / yes with conditions, e.g. changes to how results are announced), give the reasoning in terms the board can follow, take seriously the strongest point on the losing side — so if you're saying yes, address the members who think the money and the announcement effects make it negative expected value, and if you're saying no, explain why the "free info never hurts" theorem fails us here rather than just asserting it; and please be explicit about which parts of your reasoning are fragile — tell me what would have to be true, or what argument from the other side, that would flip your recommendation, since I have to defend this in front of people who've already picked sides. Don't just restate the theorem at me, I need the actual decision. Also feel free to say if my worry in (2) is confused, but explain why rather than just dropping it.

One more small thing: keep it to something I can actually read out loud in five minutes, the meetings run long as it is.
