The student's answer:

"Mill's relationship to the British Empire in India is usually misremembered, but the record is clear once you look at his life. Mill joined the East India Company's office in London in 1823 and stayed there for thirty-five years, eventually becoming head of the Examiner's office, where he drafted official correspondence about Indian affairs. He never once visited India, so everything he knew came through documents and reports — and that distance, I will argue, is the key to understanding him.

Mill was a utilitarian: he judged institutions by their consequences for well-being. In On Liberty he laid down the principle that the only justification for interfering with someone's liberty is to prevent harm to others, and he was unambiguous that this principle applies to all societies at all levels of development. Paternalism — coercing someone for their own good — is never acceptable for adults, whether the paternal authority is a state or an empire. This means Mill's political philosophy contains, in principle, a standing objection to imperial rule over Indians: they are adults, their consent was never asked, and Britain was coercing them supposedly for their own benefit.

Why, then, did Mill defend the Company's rule over India? In his History of British India (1817), written before his Company career, Mill had described Indian society in detail and found much to criticize. But by his later years his position was essentially administrative: when Parliament debated dissolving the Company and transferring India to the Crown after the 1857 rebellion, Mill defended the Company on the grounds that its officials were more experienced and less swayed by short-term political considerations at home. This was a technocratic defense, not a defense of empire as such. In Considerations on Representative Government, in his chapter on dependencies, Mill argued that a free state governing a dependency should do so for the advantage of the governing country, since this is the only stable basis for holding colonies — a view he applied to India.

The conclusion follows directly: Mill was a critic of empire in all but name. Since he held that Indians were fully capable of representative self-government, and since his own liberty principle condemned ruling them without their consent, his considered position amounts to support for Indian self-rule at the earliest practical moment. His defense of the Company in 1858 reflects class interest and caution, not principle. Had Mill been consistent, he would have been an anti-colonialist; the tools for that conclusion are all in his own writings."
