so I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about the "language of thought" — mentalese, the idea that thinking happens in an inner symbol system thats not English or Mandarin or any spoken language. before I go further I want a clear-eyed spec, because I suspect the popular presentations blur what's actually being claimed. so: if someone hands me a theory of the language of thought, what does it have to deliver to count as adequate? I don't want the standard sales pitch, I want the requirements list. the things I keep wondering about:

- what it has to explain about productivity and systematicity — the observation that if you can think "John loves Mary" you can also think "Mary loves John", that thought comes in families. is that a genuine requirement the theory must account for, or a prejudice of the symbols-first camp baked in as a desideratum?
- whether it has to say anything about how the inner symbols get their meaning, or whether that can be punted to a seperate theory of content — and is the punt legit?
- what it owes us about learning. the classic story is that learning a public language already requires an inner language to learn it in, which kicks off a bootstrapping problem — does an adequate theory have to explain where mentalese itself came from, or is it allowed to just be innate and unexplained?
- what it owes the brain. does it need to be implementable in actual neural wetware, which is continuous and graded, or can it stay abstract the way a turing machine is abstract? my editor keeps asking me this one and I dont have a good answer
- whether it has to be sentential all the way down. some people think we think in images, maps, diagram-like representations, not sentences. does an adequate theory have to take a stance on whether all thought is sentence-shaped, or can it stay silent?

for each requirement, give the case behind it. mark which are non-negotiable and which are camp preferences. and tell me if any subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — like whether demanding both discrete symbols and neural plausibility puts you in a bind. also, if one requirement does most of the work of excluding rivals (say, connectionist networks), say which one it is. skip the scorecard — I'll do the actual comparing in the article. keep it grounded enough that I can paraphrase it for a general reader, but I'd rather tolerate real philosophy than dumb it down.
