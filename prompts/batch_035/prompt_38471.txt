For thirty-eight years I worked in comparative neurobiology, first on electric fish and later on crustacean nervous systems; since retiring I have been reading philosophy of mind, and I have grown impatient with the habit in that literature of answering hard questions by refusing to answer them. I want numbers. I am aware that any number I ask for will be an educated construction rather than a measurement — that is precisely what interests me: how a disciplined reasoner distributes finite credence when the underlying fact is inaccessible.

Below is a list of ten taxa, deliberately strung across the conventional break points: the vertebrate/invertebrate divide, the centralized/diffuse nervous system divide, and the with/without a brain divide.

1. Human (include as a calibration anchor; assign it 1.0 by stipulation)
2. Chimpanzee
3. Common raven
4. Common octopus
5. Laboratory mouse
6. Brown trout
7. Shore crab
8. Honeybee
9. Earthworm
10. Sea anemone

For each taxon, except the anchor, please supply:

(a) A point estimate, on a 0–1 scale, of the probability that a typical individual of that species is capable of valenced experience — states that feel good or bad from the inside, not mere nociception or discriminative processing;

(b) A 90% credible interval around that estimate;

(c) A decomposition of the point estimate into two shares that sum to 1: the portion of your credence that rests on phylogenetic proximity to humans and the structural continuities inherited from a common ancestor, versus the portion that rests on convergent, independent evidence — the sort of evidence that would still move you if the species had been discovered on another planet, with no phylogeny available.

I then impose four further requirements, meant to force the exercise beyond reciting familiar talking points:

First, no ties. Equal numbers are permitted only where you explicitly argue that the tie itself is the finding, and you may do this at most once.

Second, at least one inversion: somewhere in your ranking, a taxon with fewer neurons (or a smaller brain) must sit above a taxon with more, and you must justify the inversion rather than bury it.

Third, after the table, articulate the single organizing principle that best rationalizes your own numbers — say, centralized integration, or behavioural flexibility, or something else your list actually tracks — and then name the one taxon that this principle handles worst. State honestly whether you adjusted that taxon's number to fit the principle or left the anomaly standing.

Fourth, tell me which taxon has the widest divergence between columns (a) and (c): the creature for which phylogeny and convergent evidence point in opposite directions most sharply, and what that divergence does to your confidence.

Please present the results as a table followed by your analysis; I would rather see a defensible number I disagree with than a paragraph of caution that commits to nothing.
