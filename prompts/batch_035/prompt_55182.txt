I'm a commercial contracts lawyer — fifteen years of drafting and fighting over M&A agreements, supply contracts, and the occasional nasty indemnity dispute. A law-school friend who went into philosophy keeps telling me that everything I do all day is applied Grice: that when a court asks what a "reasonable person" would understand a clause to mean, it is really asking what the drafter implicated but did not say, and that the interpretive canons I reach for reflexively are just the maxims of conversation in court dress. My knowledge of Grice is entirely secondhand: I know he distinguished what a sentence means from what a speaker means in uttering it, and that speakers can get across more than they strictly say. But I don't know the machinery, and I can't tell whether my friend is flattering my discipline or dissolving it. What set this off was a deal last spring where the judge found a limitation-of-liability clause "clear and unambiguous" while both sides' negotiators testified, credibly, about completely different things they had intended it to accomplish — and neither lawyer at the table found that result strange.

Restate Grice's account of conversation and speaker meaning in the vocabulary I actually work in: express terms versus terms implied in fact and in law, the objective theory of contract, the parol evidence rule, contra proferentem, the surplusage canon and noscitur a sociis, course of dealing and trade usage, the plain-meaning and ambiguity doctrines, the implied covenant of good faith. Map the cooperative principle and the four maxims onto the interpretive canons as precisely as you can, and tell me where each mapping bends. Does the maxim of Quantity really become the rule against surplusage, and if so, what do we do with the cases where courts decline to read a term as surplusage anyway? Does the conventional-versus-conversational implicature distinction track the line between express and implied terms, or does it track something else that contract law doesn't separate cleanly? Does implicature cancellation have a counterpart in the way a carefully drafted merger clause is supposed to work?

Then give me the residue report, which is what I actually care about. Which parts of Grice's picture have no counterpart in contract interpretation — I suspect the parol evidence rule is where the translation breaks, since it deliberately excludes evidence of what the parties actually meant, and speaker meaning seems to be exactly the thing Grice built his theory around while contract law worked so hard to look away from it? Does the residue point at something contract law is missing, at something Grice's framework cannot say about institutions that must interpret utterances by dead drafters years after the cooperative context has evaporated, or at a genuine disagreement between the two practices about whose understanding counts? I want to come away knowing whether my friend is right that I've been doing philosophy without a license, or whether the two practices come apart in a way that makes the comparison seductive but misleading.
