I am a twelfth-grade student and the host of my school's philosophy society podcast. Next week's guest is a philosopher who has spent much of her career defending the Gödelian anti-mechanist argument: the line of reasoning, going back to J. R. Lucas's 1961 article "Minds, Machines and Gödel" and elaborated in Roger Penrose's books The Emperor's New Mind and Shadows of the Mind, according to which Gödel's incompleteness theorems show that human mathematical understanding cannot be the output of any algorithm or Turing machine. Please draft nine interview questions for me, subject to the following constraints.

First, my listeners are classmates who have never studied formal logic. Each question must be phrased so that they can follow it; wherever a technical distinction is unavoidable — soundness versus consistency, being a theorem versus being true, formal provability versus seeing that something is so — the question should ask her to explain the distinction in the course of answering rather than presupposing it.

Second, the questions must engage the structure of the argument rather than ask her to restate it. I have read that defenders of this argument are pressed hardest at the points below, and I want at least one question on each: (a) the gap between the Gödel sentence's being true and our being able to know it — what exactly does the mathematician know, and can she know that she knows it, given that her knowledge is supposed to rest on seeing that the machine is sound, something she cannot prove any more than the machine can; (b) the mechanist's reply that since she cannot know which machine she is, she can never write down her own Gödel sentence to begin with; (c) the reply that a machine faithful to human fallibility might be inconsistent, or might already prove its own Gödel sentence, so that the disanalogy holds only for an idealized, error-free mathematician — and then whether the argument is about us at all; (d) the iterability worry: each time she outstrips a machine, the mechanist hands her a slightly strengthened one, so that she seems a step ahead in an endless race rather than outside the race; (e) the charge that the argument refutes only a caricature of computationalism — artificial intelligence as explicit theorem-proving — and leaves untouched the thought that mathematical insight might be implemented by processes that never surface as formal derivations; (f) the leap from the non-computability of mathematical understanding to claims about physics — why the non-computability must manifest as new physics rather than as some feature of how a computable system is trained or embedded; and (g) why evolution, which ordinarily builds serviceable but cheap faculties, would select for a capacity that outstrips every algorithm.

Third, one question should present a concrete scenario the audience can picture: design a hypothetical theorem-proving system that says something Gödelian about itself, and ask her whether our reaction to it displays a kind of understanding the system itself could not have. Invent the scenario yourself; do not simply describe Gödel's construction.

Fourth, attach to each question a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give as a defender of the view, and make the follow-ups progressively harder to deflect, so that the sequence cannot be survived with a stock phrase like claiming that the objection misses the argument's actual target.

Fifth, include one question on whether Penrose's later reformulation of the argument genuinely escapes the truth-versus-knowledge equivocation that critics allege in Lucas's original version.

Sixth, no generic questions — nothing about how she came to the view or what philosophy means to her. The final question should ask her for something observable: given her view, should we expect present-day AI systems to hit a permanent ceiling in genuine mathematical discovery, or is the argument simply silent about them — and what would it take, in practice, for her to concede that the argument has been answered?
