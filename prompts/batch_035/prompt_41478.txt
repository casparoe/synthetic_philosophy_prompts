Paraphrasing closely, it says: "Epiphenomenalism holds that conscious experiences are real but completely inert — the brain produces them, like smoke from an engine, but they do no causal work of any kind, not even on each other. This makes the view close to self-refuting: if experiences truly did nothing, then my sincere report 'I am seeing red' has causes entirely independent of the experience itself, so I could be having an entirely different experience, or none at all, while confidently describing this one — and I would never know. Chalmers called this the paradox of phenomenal judgment and used it to argue that epiphenomenalism collapses: the mere fact that we can accurately describe our experiences proves those experiences must be doing causal work in the brain after all."

My hunch is that the book is being too hard on the view, because I vaguely remember that Chalmers himself is some kind of epiphenomenalist, so it would be odd for him to have "used" the paradox to refute his own position.
