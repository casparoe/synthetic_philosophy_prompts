Please argue for a particular resolution of a dispute about how to measure whether the world is becoming less violent over the long run. To set up the dispute, imagine a century in which the human population quadruples while the per-capita rate of violent death is cut in half, so that the absolute number of people dying violently each year doubles. Someone who measures violence epidemically, as a toll on the population, will say the world has become more violent; someone who measures it actuarially, as the risk facing a randomly chosen individual, will say it has become less. Your task is to argue that the actuarial view is correct — that the later world is genuinely the less violent one — while also insisting that this verdict rests on a substantive evaluative commitment rather than a neutral statistical convention, and that the dispute nevertheless has a fact of the matter rather than dissolving into a mere choice of measure. In the course of the argument, address two objections: first, that per-capita normalization quietly presupposes an individualist picture of moral concern, on which the unit that matters is a person's prospect rather than the aggregate of victims; second, that absolute counts deserve priority because the dead are whole individuals, not fractions of a population, and a doubling of the annual toll cannot be offset by a larger denominator. Please also say, in your own terms, what a fact of the matter about a violence trend could consist in if the choice of denominator is itself value-laden. Write your answer in American English.
