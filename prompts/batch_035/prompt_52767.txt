I'd like a poem about Gödel's rotating universe and the argument he built on it.

By way of background, so the poem can be judged against the physics: in 1949, for the volume of essays honoring Einstein, Gödel exhibited a solution to Einstein's field equations — a stationary, non-expanding universe of pressureless dust, everywhere rotating, with a negative cosmological constant — in which closed timelike curves pass through every point. A traveler with a sufficiently powerful rocket could, after a finite amount of their own proper time, arrive at any event in that world's past, including the event of their own departure. Gödel then drew a philosophical moral: a world in which the ordering of events into past, present, and future could be circumvented by such a journey cannot possess that ordering as an objective feature; so the passage of time, the "now" sweeping forward, is an artifact of our standpoint, and the world in itself is a timeless block. The argument is subtler than it first appears — even granting this, Gödel needed the further step that if such journeys are impossible in our universe, that impossibility is an accident of initial conditions rather than something the laws enforce, and so cannot ground time's objectivity either — and the poem may register that subtlety without becoming a lecture.

As for form: the structure should carry the physics rather than illustrate it. Please write roughly sixteen lines in rhymed quatrains in which the final quatrain repeats the first one verbatim — not as a decorative circle, but because the speaker is aboard a rocket on a closed timelike curve, and the "arrival" is numerically the same event as the departure. Around line nine, functioning as a volta, the speaker should register Gödel's actual inferential step: the realization that the mere possibility in principle of this journey is what shows the "now" they believed they were leaving behind was never a feature of the world. The poem should be in Gödel's own voice — exact, spare, faintly melancholy — and it should succeed on both registers at once: someone versed in relativity should find the geometry correct (rotating, stationary, no expansion; the loop closed at finite proper time), while someone who has never heard of the solution should still find it a good poem about a journey that returns to the place it never left.
