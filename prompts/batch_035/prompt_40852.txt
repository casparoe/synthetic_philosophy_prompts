Last term I wrote a paper arguing that self-deception is possible, and my advisor sent it back with one comment: that the argument, if it works, proves too much. I can see the problem but not the exit. I'd like to know which step to give up and what giving it up costs.

The argument:

(1) Self-deception, where it happens, is genuine deception: one and the same person is deceiver and deceived.
(2) Deceiving someone about p requires knowing that p while bringing it about that they believe not-p.
(3) A single, undivided mind cannot at once know p and believe not-p.
(4) So either self-deception never happens, or the mind can partition into parts that do not fully share what they know.
(5) Self-deception happens — everyone has met the person whose marriage is failing and who is somehow the last to know, and not by accident.
(6) So the mind sometimes partitions: a part that knows p coexists with a part that believes not-p.
(7) Once so partitioned, the knowing part can work on the believing part through the ordinary channels — where attention goes, which comparisons get made, how high the burden of proof is set — and because the parts don't share what they know, this is deception in the full sense, not mere motivated bias.

That was my case for possibility. My advisor's objection: by exactly the same reasoning, every case of acting against one's better judgment becomes self-deception. Take the smoker. A part of him knows the cigarettes will kill him; a part lights the cigarette anyway; by (7), the knowing part is deceiving the acting part. So akrasia collapses into self-deception: the weak-willed and the self-deceived become the same phenomenon, and "I know it's bad for me but I do it anyway" stops being a confession of weakness and becomes, automatically, a symptom. I can't accept that — not because I'm certain the line is sharp, but because the distinction seems to do real work: the self-deceived can't find the truth, while the akratic has it and ignores it.

Here is what I can't sort out. If I repair (7) by requiring that the knowing part intend to deceive, the intention must be invisible to the believing part or the trick is seen through — but the partition already guarantees invisibility, so the repair seems to cost nothing and explain nothing: either I am reintroducing the paradox I partitioned the mind to escape, or I am labeling as intentional whatever I was already disposed to call deception. And if I instead deny that the smoker's case is relevantly like the spouse's, I owe an account of the difference, and the only candidate I can see is that the smoker can still say what he knows — but the self-deceived also say things, just the wrong ones, so I can't tell whether that difference is real or just reflects the cases I started from.

Which step should I give up, and what does giving it up cost? For what it's worth, I am committed to (5): self-deception happens. What I most want to save is the distinction between it and ordinary weakness of will — but if that can't be saved, I would rather learn that the collapse is the truth of the matter than rescue the distinction by fiat.
