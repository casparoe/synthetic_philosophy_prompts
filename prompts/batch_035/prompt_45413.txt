I'm someone who has thought about this on my own for a while, without any academic training, so please treat me as an intelligent layperson and not assume I know the literature.

Here is my position. I believe that what a person really wants is not what they currently say they want, but what they would want if they were fully informed, had unlimited time to think things through, were free of biases and momentary mood, and maybe knew themselves better than they do. Call this their "reflective preferences" as opposed to their surface preferences. I've come to think that when a person's surface and reflective preferences conflict, the reflective ones are the ones that should win -- that they have a stronger claim to being the person's actual will. I've also noticed that this idea seems to underwrite a lot of talk about choosing for other people "for their own good" (paternalism, nudging, deciding for children or for people who are temporarily not themselves) and about what humanity should collectively aim for.

I'd like you to act as a careful philosopher of preferences and decision theory and think through this as rigorously as you can. What would count against my position? I don't mean just the obvious worry that the "fully informed, fully reflective" version of a person might be a fiction we construct. I mean: which specific observations, thought experiments, or arguments should genuinely move me? For instance, would it count against the view if reflection reliably changed people's values in different directions depending on their starting point, so that "extrapolation" converges on nothing? What if some of what we value seems essentially tied to our limitations -- partiality toward our own people, not wanting to know certain things, or caring about outcomes precisely because they're uncertain? What if people, after deep reflection, say they wish their earlier, less reflective selves had been able to keep certain commitments that the reflective self wants to abandon? Please tell me which of these actually threaten the view and which are merely awkward.

I also want you to say where the view risks becoming unfalsifiable: if I'm always allowed to say that the person's "real" preferences are the ones they'd have after even more reflection, or that any stable preference is by definition the reflective one, is there anything left for the view to be wrong about? If it has been insulated that way, tell me whether that's a fatal problem for this kind of view or just the nature of interpretive claims about persons.

Please be self-critical and appropriately hedged: don't present one contested stance on this as the settled view, and where you give your own assessment, flag it as yours and note what people who know this area actually disagree about. Rank the defeaters from those that would make me abandon the position to those I could reasonably live with, and say which of them a committed defender of the view would concede is a real cost. Please start by brainstorming the possible lines of attack before evaluating them.
