A chapter on infinity in a popular economics book I've been reading covers the Pasadena paradox, and it has left me more confused than informed. Here is the gist of what it says. In Daniel Bernoulli's St. Petersburg game, a fair coin is flipped until it lands heads, and if heads first appears on toss number n, you win 2 to the power n dollars, so the expected value comes out infinite — which is supposed to explain why no sane person would pay a fortune to play. The book then says that two theorists, Nover and Hájek, modified this into the Pasadena game: the payoffs now alternate in sign, so that if heads first appears on toss n you win 2 to the power n when n is odd and pay 2 to the power n when n is even. This makes the expected value the series 1 − 1 + 1 − 1 + …, which, the book says, mathematicians have shown sums to 1/2. But then comes the punchline: by a classical theorem of Riemann's, the terms of an infinite series can be reordered to produce any total you like, so the value of the Pasadena game is whatever the order of bookkeeping happens to be, and the reordering can make the game look as attractive or as ruinous as you please. The book concludes, first, that this shows expected values in all such unbounded games are undefined rather than infinite — including the original St. Petersburg game — and, second, that Nover and Hájek took their construction to refute expected utility theory outright, leaving rational agents with nothing to go on but intuition in cases like this.

I am inclined to accept the punchline about ordering, since the book illustrates it convincingly with a reorganized payoff table, but a friend who studied mathematics tells me the 1/2 figure is dodgy and that the Riemann point is being misused, and I can't quite reconcile the two. Could you go through this summary more or less sentence by sentence and tell me which claims a decision theorist would accept, which are oversimplifications they would wince at, and which are simply mistaken? I would especially like to know (i) whether the game as described actually yields the series quoted, and whether that series genuinely sums to 1/2 or whether that number is a convention of some kind; (ii) whether the appeal to Riemann's rearrangement theorem is correct as stated, or whether that theorem applies only to a narrower class of series; and (iii) whether the two conclusions drawn at the end follow from whatever is accurate in the summary — in particular whether the Pasadena result really says anything about the original St. Petersburg game's value, and whether Nover and Hájek's own view of what their game shows was that expected utility theory is refuted, or something more modest.
