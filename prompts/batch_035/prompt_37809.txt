My sister, a microbiologist, and I have been arguing for months about what counts as alive, and the problem is that my gut verdicts on particular cases refuse to assemble into a principle. I'd like help extracting one — or a straight account of which verdicts I ought to surrender. So, my verdicts as I actually hold them. A worker honeybee that will never lay an egg is alive, and so is a mule, though neither will ever reproduce. An influenza virion sitting on a park bench is not alive, and on that I don't waver. A viable seed that has been dried and stored for decades, with no detectable metabolism, is alive; a tardigrade desiccated for thirty years, which revives when wetted, was alive throughout. Yet a mature red blood cell, which is metabolising the whole time, somehow strikes me as not itself alive — merely part of a living thing — while a HeLa cell in a dish seems alive to me, perhaps worryingly so. A self-replicating pattern in a cellular automaton, or a computer worm copying itself through memory, is clearly not alive. A prion converting its neighbours is not alive. A brain-dead body on a ventilator, whose every cell is metabolising, is, I want to insist, dead. And the case I keep going back and forth on: a vesicle built in a laboratory, which copies a short stretch of DNA using an enzyme its designers supplied, splits in two when the strand is copied, and stops — dies? — when the feedstock runs out.

The obvious candidates — metabolism, reproduction, responsiveness — each seem to be refuted by one pair of my verdicts, which makes me suspect the truth is something about lineages or histories rather than present-tense properties. But I can't state that in a way that doesn't either let the virus in (its ancestors were manufactured inside living cells) or lock the door after the first protocell, before which there was no lineage at all. So: is there a single principle that delivers all of my verdicts, or close? If a simpler principle would require me to drop one of them, I'd rather know which, and why that one rather than another. Please set out the principle, or a small set of candidates, precisely; tie each clause to the case that motivates it; say what it predicts on cases I haven't listed — a slime mould, a coral colony, a giant fungus, a frozen embryo, or something self-replicating found on another planet; and be honest about which of my verdicts your preferred principle cannot save. And if your conclusion is instead that 'alive' is not a natural kind and my verdicts are tracking something else — our parochial interests, perhaps — then say that plainly, but explain what my verdicts are in fact tracking, since they don't feel arbitrary from the inside. Please write your answer in British English.
