My friend thinks a lot about how epic and legendary traditions relate to history, and over the years she has given me fairly firm verdicts. The Trojan War: yes, some historical core — she takes it there were destructive conflicts around the Late Bronze Age settlement at Hisarlik that the epic tradition ultimately reflects, whatever the Iliad's gods and plot owe to poetry. King Arthur: no historical core, a legendary accretion around a dimly remembered post-Roman military figure. The Exodus as narrated: no departure from Egypt on anything like the biblical scale. Ragnar Lothbrok: no. Gilgamesh, by contrast: yes, she thinks a real Early Dynastic king probably stands behind the name — partly on the strength of his appearance in ancient king lists and early non-epic references. William Tell: no. Now she's agreed to hear three new cases over dinner: Robin Hood; Moses considered strictly as an individual leader, set aside from the question of the Exodus narrative's scale; and Etzel in the Nibelungenlied. Predict her verdict on each. I'm less interested in the answers than in the rule she seems to be running: several rules fit her track record equally well — for instance, one keyed to independent corroboration of the central event or site, and one keyed to whether the name attaches to a person attested early in non-epic sources — and they come apart on at least one of these cases. Identify the candidate rules, say what each predicts, give your best guess with reasons, and name a further case that would best tell the rules apart. One more request: please keep separate what is actually settled among people who work on this — for example, regarding the archaeology of Hisarlik and the later tradition around Arthur — and what is genuinely contested, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus.
