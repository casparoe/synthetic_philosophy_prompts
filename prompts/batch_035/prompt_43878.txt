So next month I'm giving a talk at this small interdisciplinary workshop on explanation in the historical sciences, and I want to be ready for the Q&A because I expect it to be rough. My thesis, roughly: hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy (the Taleb-flavored point that once you have a story, the story feels inevitable and you can't see it any other way) are real effects, but the debiasing literature has way overreached with them, turning them into a blanket suspicion of retrospective explanation as such. My positive claim is that retrospective explanation can be methodologically respectable anyway, because a good historical narrative has what I want to call dynamic constraints: it commits to counterfactuals (what could and could not have gone differently, which actors had real room to move), and it rules out whole classes of finding, so that even though nobody pre-registered the explanation, it could still have been refuted by the archive. Prediction is not the only currency of epistemic respectability — explanation that constrains counts for something too.

The room, as far as I can tell from the attendee list: maybe 40% academic historians, who will bristle at any suggestion that their craft is systematically biased and mostly think the psychologists have never actually worked in an archive; maybe 40% behavioral-decision people — Fischhoff-style hindsight bias researchers and Tetlock-style forecasting people, including at least two who've run in forecasting tournaments and think scoring rules are the measure of all things; and the rest a grab bag of philosophers of science plus one data journalist who wrote a whole book about how every historical account is just hindsight bias with footnotes.
