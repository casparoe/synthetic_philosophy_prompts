I lead the philosophy club at my high school — about fourteen students from grades nine through twelve, meeting for one hour after school — and in two weeks it's my turn to run the session. The topic I've been assigned is whether self-deception is even possible, and I keep getting stuck on the version of the problem I found while preparing: if I believe something mainly because I want it to be true, it seems I must somehow already know it's false, and it's hard to see how I can then still believe it. A friend suggested this framing misses the point — that self-deception might be less a paradoxical state of simultaneously knowing and not-knowing, and more an ongoing process of managing which evidence about yourself comes in and which never does — and that distinction between the state picture and the process picture is what I want the group to feel rather than be told about.

Please design the activity for me. My vague idea is that participants should first make predictions about themselves (something like how they'll score on a short quiz), then be offered the chance to check exactly how they actually did — with checking being free in points but carrying some other cost, maybe just having to ask for it out loud — and that the interesting cases will be the people who decline to check and why. Feel free to scrap this and propose something better if you see a cleaner setup. Whatever you come up with should use only materials available in a school building (paper, printed sheets, a whiteboard; no phones), should have a scoring system that pays off in something other than money, and should arrange things so that participants run into the problem themselves before anyone, including me, names it — so no mini-lecture from me at the start. Afterwards I need a debrief plan that maps particular moments from the activity onto the two pictures of self-deception, a list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure (I have never facilitated anything before), and an honest note on which feature of real self-deception no classroom game can reproduce, so I can at least say so out loud before the session ends.
