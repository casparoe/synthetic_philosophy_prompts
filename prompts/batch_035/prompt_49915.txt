In my school's philosophy club we have spent two months on Sartre, and the debate has frozen in a way I would like help diagnosing rather than settling. The position, roughly, is that we are radically free: there is no fixed human nature constraining what we can make of ourselves, and consciousness is not a thing with a determined set of properties but a kind of ongoing choosing. From this, Sartre gets two further ideas. First, that we are responsible for what we make of ourselves, since nothing outside us settles it. Second, that bad faith is possible and blameworthy: at any moment I could acknowledge my situation and my projects honestly, but instead I can pretend to myself that I have no choice — that I am just a waiter being a waiter, or a coward by constitution — and this pretending is itself something I am doing, not something that happens to me.

The two club members who keep arguing have settled into fixed posts. One of them, who wants to study neuroscience, holds that radical freedom is an empirical claim and a losing one: in the classic experiments on voluntary movement, the brain activity that predicts a person's action is detectable before the person reports any awareness of deciding to act, which looks like the choice being settled before the "I" arrives on the scene. On her reading, if the body is already moving before consciousness is consulted, the "ongoing choosing" is a story the brain tells afterward, and both the responsibility and the blame for bad faith lose their footing. The other member, who is the club's Sartre partisan, answers that radical freedom was never a claim about which physical events cause which — it is a description of how things stand from the inside, for the one deliberating, where I cannot find my next move already written anywhere and where my situation only has the meaning, including the constraining force, that my project gives it. Neuroscience, he says, describes persons from the outside, and Sartre is describing them from where only the person can stand, so the experiments don't even touch the thesis.

Neither has moved an inch in six weeks, and I have come to suspect the argument is not about Sartre at all. My working hypothesis is that it is a disagreement about what a claim has to do to count as an explanation or a contribution to it — one of them wants every assertion about human beings to compete with other assertions about human beings on the same terrain, and the other thinks some assertions are answers to a different question, one that arises only from within deliberation. But I am not confident, and there is a wrinkle I have not heard either of them address, which is why I am asking here. It seems to me that the experiments, if granted, do not threaten the two sides equally: the outside view that settles my movements before I am aware of them would make bad faith even stranger than freedom, since the person in bad faith is supposed to be actively not-acknowledging something, and if actions, including noticings and ignorings, are prepared before awareness, the "actively" looks like post-hoc narration, and I can no longer tell whether self-deception is something I do or something my brain does while I watch. Yet the neuroscience-minded member likes the bad faith concept and the Sartre member rarely uses it, so the wrinkle cuts across their positions and neither wants it on the table.

So here is what I would like, please. Diagnose the disagreement for me using what each of them actually says, not a survey of Sartre scholarship: is it substantive, and if so what fact or finding would settle it; is it verbal, turning on the word "freedom" doing causal work for one of them and phenomenological work for the other; or are they answering different questions under one heading, one about the causal authorship of actions and one about the standing from which a person deliberates? In your diagnosis, say whether you think my hypothesis about explanation is right, and say honestly what the wrinkle about bad faith does to each side's position — whether it gives one of them an unexpected liability or an unexpected resource. Finally, tell me what, if anything, would count as progress here: a distinction, a reformulated question, a further finding, or an argument that the impasse should stand and why. I would rather have a clear diagnosis I disagree with than a balanced summary of both sides, which I can write myself.
