I write novels about people who decide on behalf of others. My last book concerned a wartime minister who must order a man's death to save a town; earlier ones concerned a hospital triage officer, a film editor cutting a collaborator's scene, and a mother dividing scarce attention between two children. While sketching the next novel I noticed an argument assembling itself out of my own characters, and it appears to prove far too much. I would be grateful for a careful diagnosis.

(1) To have dirty hands is to do something that is, in one respect, wrong while being, all things considered, the right thing to do in the circumstances. The minister who orders the killing is the paradigm: what he does is rightly done, and yet he has done something that may not be done — the literature calls this the problem of dirty hands.

(2) What makes his act wrong in that one respect is not its outward shape but the fact that it sets back a legitimate claim someone holds against him — the claim of the man not to be killed.

(3) But nearly every serious choice anyone makes sets back some legitimate claim, whichever option is chosen. The triage officer, the editor, the mother: each leaves someone with a valid complaint, however fairly the choice is made. Where claims compete and resources are scarce, someone's claim loses.

(4) So in all such cases the agent does wrong in the relevant respect even while doing the best thing available.

(5) If having dirty hands is simply this — wronging in one respect while doing right all things considered — then dirty hands is everywhere. My editor has dirty hands every time she cuts a scene; the mother has them every morning.

(6) Yet the concept was supposed to mark something distinctive about public, responsibility-bearing office — the situation in which someone must do wrong so that others need not. If it applies to the editor exactly as it applies to the minister, I have lost the distinction I wanted my novel to turn on.

I cannot accept (5), but I also cannot locate the fault. Each premise still looks defensible to me when I read it on its own, which makes me fear the trouble is an equivocation I am reading straight past — something in how "wrong" and "claim" shift their weight between (1) and (4). Tell me which step to reject; explain why that step looked innocent when I first wrote it down; and then price the alternatives honestly, including what each rejection costs the others. If I must choose, I would rather preserve (6) than preserve (1) exactly as I have phrased it, but I want to know what I am buying before I decide.
