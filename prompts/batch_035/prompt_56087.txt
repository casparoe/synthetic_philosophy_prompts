I run a small bioethics reading group here in my department, mostly faculty plus a couple of grad students, and this week it dissolved into a genuinely good argument about the definition of death that I could not settle to anyone's satisfaction. I'd like you to referee it. Please take on the role of a professional philosopher of medicine and bioethicist, think carefully, maybe even start with a quick brainstorm of the moving parts before adjudicating, because the exchange covers a lot of ground. The topic: whether death should be defined as brain death (permanent loss of all brain function) or as circulatory death (permanent cessation of heart and lung function), and whether there even is a pre-theoretical fact of the matter here.

The two disputants, both of whom I'd call strong philosophers, went roughly like this. (1) My colleague Marcus argued that the traditional concept of death was keyed to circulation and respiration for all of human history, and that whole-brain death is best understood as a legal fiction adopted in the late 1960s largely to unlock organ transplantation — a noble lie of sorts. On his view, a brain-dead patient on a ventilator is a living organism that we have agreed to treat as dead for pragmatic reasons, and calling this "death" is like redefining a mile to make a marathon easier. (2) My colleague Elena replied that Marcus is confusing the traditional *signs* of death with the underlying phenomenon: circulation was how we *detected* death, not what death *is*. She argued the concept should track the irreversible loss of the body's capacity for integrated functioning as a single organism, and that the brain's role in integrating and regulating the organism makes whole-brain death a perfectly good gloss on that — the ventilator just masks the signs. (3) Marcus came back hard: brain-dead patients maintained on ventilators can, sometimes for months, regulate temperature, fight infections, heal wounds, and in a handful of documented cases even gestate a fetus to viability — all of which look like integrated organismic functioning to him, so the "integrated functioning" criterion doesn't actually track brain death, it tracks something like the brainstem plus machinery. (4) Elena answered that these residual functions are fragmentary and dependent on external support in a way an intact organism's aren't — the patient doesn't breathe, will never regain consciousness, and the "integration" is happening partly in the machine — and that anyway Marcus has now smuggled in a criterion (roughly: loss of the capacity for consciousness) that points toward a higher-brain standard, not the circulatory standard he started with. (5) Marcus's final move was that this just proved his original point: if the debate slides this easily between death of the organism, death of the brain, and death of the person, then the concept has been destabilized by the transplant lobby, and there's no fact of the matter left to discover — only a stipulation to negotiate.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move — for each of the five numbered moves, say whether it's basically right, basically wrong, or right-but-undercut-by-something-the-other-side-said, and justify the rating. Then tell me who comes out more right overall, and what if anything both of them are missing (I have my own thoughts about the difference between a definition of death, diagnostic criteria for it, and clinical tests for those criteria, but I want to see if you get there independently). Please don't pull in literature or cite sources — I want your reasoning, not a bibliography, since I'll be reading this offline.
