I'm the instructor for an undergraduate course on the history of analytic ethics, and I need help grading a midterm. The exam question was:

"G. E. Moore claimed that what is most valuable in the world — the closest thing we can get to an ideal — is largely constituted by two kinds of human goods. Identify the two, explain what exactly has intrinsic value on his account (and what does not), and show how his doctrine of organic unities bears on the value of these wholes. Finally, explain what role this part of his theory plays in his opposition to hedonism."

One student submitted the following answer, reproduced verbatim:

"Moore begins by arguing that 'good' is a simple, non-natural property that cannot be defined in any other terms, and that questions about what things are good cannot be settled by observation. Our best evidence about intrinsic value comes from reflecting on what we judge valuable for its own sake. He then identifies the two goods that make up his ideal: the contemplation or appreciation of beauty, and personal affection, meaning the love of persons. Importantly, what has intrinsic value is the whole state of consciousness — the appreciating or the loving — not the beautiful object or the loved person considered on their own. This follows from Moore's doctrine of organic unities, which says that the value of a whole is simply the sum of the values of its parts. So once we know how much intrinsic value the beautiful object has by itself, and how much the appreciative consciousness contributes, we can compute the value of the whole. Since a beautiful object has enormous intrinsic value whether or not anyone contemplates it, a world of unseen natural beauty would be nearly as valuable as one in which it is actually appreciated. On affection, Moore holds that love is good only when its object is good: if the beloved person has little worth, the affection is correspondingly worthless, since otherwise we could make the world better simply by admiring anything whatsoever. Finally, both aesthetic appreciation and personal affection are, on Moore's account, states of consciousness that are pleasant to be in, so his ideal is best seen as a refinement of classical hedonism: he differs from Bentham only in holding that pleasures differ in quality, some being higher than others."
