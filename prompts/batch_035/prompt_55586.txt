I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've recently gone down a rabbit hole that started with a code-review argument. A colleague invoked Chesterton's fence — the idea that you shouldn't remove a fence until you understand why it was put there — to defend not deleting some gnarly legacy code, and I wanted to know whether the principle has any deeper grounding than it sounds. That led me to discover that the kind of thinking behind Chesterton's fence is often traced back to Edmund Burke's Reflections on the Revolution in France (1790), so I found a copy online and tried to read it directly. Most of it was over my head, but one stretch near the end stuck out to me as exactly the thing I was looking for — and also exactly the thing I can't fully parse.

I don't have the text in front of me with page numbers, so I'll summarize the passage from my notes rather than quote it, and I'm hoping the content is recognizable enough that you can tell me what I'm looking at. As I understand it, Burke is arguing there that a state that has no way to change also has no way to preserve itself, because a political order that can't adapt at all will eventually be broken rather than reformed. He goes on to say that the combination of a disposition to preserve and an ability to improve would be his standard of a statesman, and he laments that this combination is rare — that the people who love change for its own sake and the people who resist all change tend to be, in his eyes, mirror images of each other, and that states have often been lost by those who were drunk with the spirit of innovation just as much as by those who clung blindly to what existed. If I'm remembering the drift correctly, he also suggests that the habits and settled arrangements of a nation embody accumulated wisdom that no single generation or committee of rational reformers could reason out from first principles.

Here are the things I'd like help understanding:

1. What is Burke actually claiming in this passage, stated as precisely as possible? Is it a claim about the epistemics of reform — that existing institutions encode information that reformers lack — or a claim about stability — that even a well-informed reform introduces risks that accumulate — or something else? I can see how it could be either and I don't know which reading Burke intends.

2. How does the passage relate to Chesterton's fence? They clearly rhyme, but Chesterton's version sounds like a near-absolute veto on change ("understand first, then remove"), while Burke's version seems to require a statesman who is good at changing things. Is Chesterton just a flattened, aphoristic version of Burke, or do they actually come apart somewhere?

3. The case my code-review argument actually hinged on, which neither version seems to answer: what should the reformer do when they *do* understand the original reason for the fence, and believe — maybe correctly — that the reason no longer applies? Does Burke have anything to say about how much certainty is appropriate before overturning a settled arrangement, or how to weigh the possibility that you've misunderstood the reason even when you think you understand it? Put in engineering terms: what does Burkeian prudence look like for a refactor that seems justified?

4. And a more basic question I feel embarrassed asking: is Burke giving an *argument* here at all, or just describing a temperament and attaching value judgments to it? When I read it, I can't find anything that would convince a skeptical progressive of the time that cautious, preservative reform is actually better — it reads more like a claim about what kind of person to trust. If that's right, is the epistemic defense of the position (institutions as compressed, load-bearing history) something later writers added to Burke, or is it latent in passages like this one?
