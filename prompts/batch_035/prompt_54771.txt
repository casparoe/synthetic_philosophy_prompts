Quick question that's been bugging me for a while and I can't find the right word for. Is there an actual name for this thing?

I've spent the last few years working between the Netherlands and Japan, so I've gotten a front-row seat to two opposite styles of saying no. My Dutch colleagues will just tell you the plan is bad. My Japanese counterparts will say something like, that might be a little difficult, and what they mean is no, and crucially everyone in the room knows that's what it means. When I hear it now, I don't start thinking about which difficulty I could help remove -- I hear "no," full stop. So you have an utterance that is false or at least misleading taken at face value, which nonetheless produces exactly the correct belief in anyone competent in the convention. Nobody is deceived. It's clearly not a lie in the normal sense, since the whole point of a lie is to create a false belief and this creates the true one. But it's also not just an idiom like "kick the bucket," because if you ask my coworker directly, so that's a no? she'll cheerfully confirm, and the words still carry their literal meaning in other contexts.

The same goes for the social stuff in English. "We should totally get dinner sometime" said at the end of a party, where both parties know it means goodbye forever. "It was so great to meet you!" delivered with the exact warmth that means it wasn't. Nobody is fooled, and yet nobody says the sincere version ("I don't want to have dinner with you, but no hard feelings"), because the sincere version would be a social catastrophe.

So: does this configuration have an established name? And if it does, or even if you have to coin one, I want the carving, not just the label. How is it different from a genuine white lie (where the target maybe IS fooled), from an idiom, from simple exaggeration for effect? Where are the edges -- e.g. what happens with someone like me circa my first year in Japan, who half-knows the code? Am I being lied to then, and if so, what changed -- the utterance or the listener?

The reason I actually care: I keep running into people who debate whether white lies are wrong, and both sides seem to assume the harm/benefit is about the hearer getting the wrong picture. But this category is insincere utterances that do their job BY being insincere -- if you made them sincere they'd fail. Doesn't that break the usual framing? Curious whether the philosphy of language people or the ethics people have a settled account of this, and how the two literatures connect, if at all.
