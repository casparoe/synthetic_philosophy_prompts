The theory wants their preferences to satisfy the independence axiom, but real clients violate it constantly and aren't shy about it, and I currently have no principled way to respond -- half the time I just quietly average things out. Write me a procedure I can actually run inside a 90-minute elicitation session with an executive who has zero patience for math: which choice pairs to present and in what order, how to tell a genuine independence violation from noise or from someone just misreading a lottery, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, when to re-present the choice, when to restructure the decision so the violation stops mattering, and where I have to stop and admit the question is about their values and not mine to overrule. Then run it on two cases from my last month: a CEO who reliably picks the sure $1M over a 50/50 shot at $2.5M when choosing between single projects, but picks the gamble once both options are wrapped inside a coin flip that decides whether either project even happens -- same final outcomes -- and she insists she's being perfectly consistant, not irrational. And a risk manager who shows the common-ratio pattern backwards from Allais's original subjects, refusing even very favorable long shots and then suddenly accepting terrible odds once probabilities get above some threshold he can't articulate. Include at least one point where the procedure gives an answer I won't like, and be blunt about where it just fails. Under 800 words.
