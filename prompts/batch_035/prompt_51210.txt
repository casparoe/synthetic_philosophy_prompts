I'm teaching a first-year seminar called paradoxes and puzzles — 16 students, one 80-minute session. Next week is the surprise exam paradox, and I don't want to open with the argument. Design an in-class game where they run into the backward induction themselves before I put anything on the board.

Roughly what I want: compress a school week into the session — five "days" played as rounds, with a night before each one where they have to commit. At the start I announce that exactly one of the five days will have a quiz, and that it will be a surprise: they won't be able to predict, the night before, that it's coming that day. Then we play it out with real-ish stakes — points counting toward something they care about, but no actual money. They should have to make public predictions each "night," and the payoff structure should reward actually pinning down the day if they can, so it's worth trying to be clever. Ideally someone notices they can't coherently bet on the last day, and then the elimination unravels backwards from there, and then the quiz actually happens on some day and IS surprising, and they're annoyed about it. That annoyance is the thing I want.

Second phase if you can make it work: run it again, but this time the announcement is made privately to one designated student instead of to the whole class. I have a hunch this changes what people can coherently conclude, and I want the debrief to draw that out — something about what the announcement does once it isn't common knowledge anymore. But I've never tried running that version so tell me if it's a mess in practice.

So, the deliverables: rules, materials, a timeline that fits 80 minutes, the payoff scheme, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the parts of the argument — the base case, the induction step, what exactly "surprise" has to mean for the elimination to even get started. Also tell me what will predictibly go wrong the first time I run this (students refusing to play along, someone guessing the day by dumb luck, the designated-student phase just confusing everyone) and how to handle each. And be honest about which feature of the paradox a game played in one session just cannot reproduce — what I'll have to supply myself in the debrief, and where the game might actively mislead them.
