I'm a program officer at a small private foundation that disburses about $2M a year across roughly thirty proposals per cycle — global health, chronic pain treatment, mental health, factory-farm animal welfare, and, awkwardly, a community grants stream that funds things like youth orchestras and public gardens. Our founding charter, written decades ago by the donor, commits us to the relief and prevention of suffering and states that where trade-offs arise, the prevention of intense suffering takes precedence over the production of positive experiences. It says nothing more specific than that. My co-officer reads it as a strict lexical rule: no amount of happiness, however distributed, can outweigh a fixed quantity of severe suffering, so the suffering-relief proposals win automatically and the community grants are effectively decorative. I don't buy that when the stakes are tiny — I'm not willing to let one person's mild headache trump a million people's clearly worthwhile afternoon — but I do feel its pull when the suffering is genuinely severe. Each funding round we end up arguing this from scratch, and our board (three retired professionals, zero philosophers) is tired of it.

What I want from you is a decision procedure we can actually run in our review meetings, which last about forty minutes per proposal. It should translate the charter's principle into an ordered set of questions a non-philosopher can answer from a grant application: how to classify a proposal's harms and benefits (including whether "absence of suffering" counts as a benefit or just as the absence of a harm), when aggregation across many beneficiaries is permitted, and when a severity threshold overrides the arithmetic — including some operational way of locating the threshold, or at least of recognizing that a case sits near it. Say what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, where the procedure terminates in a clear no, and where it must hand the decision back to us as a value judgment rather than pretending to have settled it. Please be explicit about the places where the procedure is a judgment call wearing a uniform — especially around measuring the intensity of suffering from a written application, comparing suffering across different beings (a burn victim, a cataract patient, a hen in a battery cage), and handling uncertainty about whether the projected benefits materialize at all.

Then run the procedure on three cases from our last cycle. First, a proposal to fund cataract surgeries for two thousand people in a low-income region, against a competing proposal to expand a youth orchestra serving five thousand children. Second, a proposal to treat chronic, debilitating back pain in a few thousand patients versus a proposal to eliminate a milder but very widespread ailment — headaches, say — affecting half a million, where the total aggregation on almost any cardinal scale favors the headache program. Third, a case where the procedure yields an answer I confess I don't like: it should either force us to fund the pain program even though the headache program is our board's sentimental favorite, or block the orchestra funding on grounds the board will find baffling — pick whichever falls out honestly, and defend it or concede the procedure needs an amendment, saying exactly which step fails. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do at all — the systematic blind spots a rule like this inevitably has when it meets a grant portfolio rather than a philosophy seminar.
