I'm a software engineer working on a targeting-support system for a defense contractor, and I've gotten interested in a philosopher my brother keeps recommending — Elizabeth Anscombe, the one who protested Oxford giving Truman an honorary degree in 1956. I have no philosophy background, but I've pieced together her track record on wartime killing, and I'd like a prediction of what she would say about the system my team is building. As I understand her verdicts: Hiroshima and Nagasaki were murder of the innocent, full stop, and the usual lives-saved defense was an exercise in corrupting everyone who accepted it; the Allied area bombing of German and Japanese cities was likewise murder; but a pilot who attacks a munitions factory in a residential district, foreseeing but not intending some civilian deaths, is not guilty of murder — that's her use of double effect; a private man forced by a sadistic aggressor to kill one innocent person under threat to his family has not committed murder; and she went on to condemn nuclear deterrence itself, on the ground that forming the intention to do what would be murder to do is already wrong, even if it's never done.

Here's the new case. Our system ingests surveillance data and produces ranked strike recommendations. For each candidate target it computes an estimated number of civilian deaths, and the commander sets a threshold: below it, the recommendation goes onto a queue for a human "engagement authority" to approve, who by doctrine is supposed to review each one, though in practice they see high-level summaries and have perhaps a minute per strike during a surge. The system doesn't aim at civilians, and in most strikes the human could not aim at anything more specific than the machine's selected aim point. The wrinkle that bothers me: our adversary embeds military assets in residential neighborhoods, so a meaningful fraction of the queue consists of apartment buildings and homes where the "military objective" is a mid-level officer who lives there, struck at night when he is most likely present. Our compliance division says this is fine because the deaths are foreseen side effects of attacks on legitimate military objectives, and no human intends them — the threshold, the ranking function, and the approval click each distribute the intent so thinly that no one "chooses" the deaths at all.

So: what would Anscombe say about deploying this system? I'd like you to treat her verdicts as a track record and figure out what rule she's actually running. The obvious candidate is strict double effect — foreseen-not-intended civilian deaths are permissible when attacking a genuine military objective — but I suspect that's too fast, because it can't easily explain why she condemned deterrence: nobody intended the deaths there either, they only conditionally threatened them. A second candidate is that what matters is whether the thing attacked is described as a military object or as a population — which would make night strikes on officers' homes look like the area bombing she called murder, not like the munitions-factory pilot. A third is something like her deterrence logic extended forward: that programming a threshold and pre-committing to kill up to N civilians per strike is itself forming the conditional intention she already condemned. Which of these rules best fits her whole track record, what does each predict about my system, and — since you'll have to pick a best guess — what hypothetical further case, one I could describe to our compliance people, would most sharply separate the candidate rules from each other? I should add that I don't need the policy argument for or against the program, only her considered verdict and the reasoning behind it.
