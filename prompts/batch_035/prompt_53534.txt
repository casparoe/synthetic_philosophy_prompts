I teach an upper-level PPE seminar on collective choice. My sixteen students have already worked through majority cycling and the Condorcet paradox over options, but they have not yet encountered judgment aggregation. I have one 90-minute session and want a staged activity in which they run into the discursive dilemma — a case where a majority of the committee endorses each premise of a decision rule while a majority rejects the conclusion the rule licenses — before anyone has a name for it.

The scenario I want them immersed in: parallel appointments committees deciding whether to recommend a job offer for a final-round candidate, governed by a written rule the committee itself ratified at an earlier (fictional) meeting — an offer goes forward if and only if the candidate clears both a research bar and a teaching bar. Individual ballots should record each member's judgment on each premise and on the offer itself. Divide the class into three committees reviewing identical candidate files but using three different aggregation procedures: one that votes premise-by-premise and then applies the rule mechanically; one that votes only on the bottom-line question; one where the chair decides alone after a non-binding straw poll. You will need to construct the judgment profiles — how many members accept or reject each premise and the offer — so that the conflict actually fires and the committees reach different outcomes on the same evidence. Give me the profiles and the reasoning behind those numbers, including at least one member whose pattern of judgments makes the tension vivid when it is revealed.

Additional elements to design: roles for students who are not on a committee (chair for each panel, a tally-keeper, and one designated dissenter whose scripted job is to raise a procedural objection at a set moment); a prediction pool among the observers, paid in tokens rather than money, on which committees will recommend the offer; and a revision round in one committee only, run after the premise tallies are posted there, so members can change their conclusion votes — I want the debrief to be able to distinguish people changing their minds from people changing their votes.

The plenary comparison and debrief should map what happened onto the distinction between aggregating premises and aggregating conclusions, and should explicitly contrast it with the cycling they already know: inconsistent collective judgments across interconnected propositions are not the same phenomenon as cyclic preferences over fixed options, and I want them to see why. The term discursive dilemma should not appear until the final minutes.

Then the practical notes. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — including that sharp students will spot the inconsistency mid-round and try to renegotiate the decision rule, that some will vote insincerely once they see where the tallies point, and that a committee may converge on a compromise the profiles were not built to produce — and how I should handle each case without shutting down the very friction the activity exists to create. Finally, an honest paragraph on which feature of the real result the activity cannot reproduce: they will experience one hand-built conflict under three procedures in one session, and I need to know exactly what I must supply in the debrief to gesture at the general impossibility results for aggregation on interconnected propositions without being in a position to prove anything to them.
