Last night a friend and I had a four-hour argument about the white horse discourse — the Warring States text in which Gongsun Long defends the claim that a white horse is not a horse — and we ended it in an unsatisfying place: each of us convinced the other was answering a different question. She takes what I will call the dissolution reading: the argument is a disputation exercise that trades on an ambiguity, since denying that a white horse is a horse equivocates between denying identity and denying that the compound designation picks out the same thing the general name does, and the point of the exercise is to expose or exploit that slippage. I take what I will call the theory reading: Gongsun Long is a serious theorist of names, and the claim is literally true on his semantics — each name singles out exactly one item (a whatness), horse and white horse are different names, so they single out different whatnesses, which coheres with the companion arguments about hardness and whiteness being separable and about how names relate to what they pick out. Since we could not even settle where our disagreement was substantive rather than verbal, I want the document we should have produced instead.

Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between these two camps. The collaboration is between interpreters who have argued for years, not between people reconstructing a historical debate — so the agreed claims, the cruxes, and the predictions should all be about how to read the texts and what the texts show, not about which side of the ancient argument was right. Please structure it as follows:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — things a committed dissolutionist and a committed theorist could both sign. Include at minimum what they can jointly say about the transmitted text's late and uncertain compilation; about which steps of the white horse dialectic are formally valid under modern predicate-logical reconstruction and which are not; and about the limits of what any single translation can establish, given that translators of this corpus routinely import the very semantic commitments the dispute is about.

2. A disagreements table: each residual disagreement stated in wording its own side would accept — formulations neither slanted toward the sophistry charge nor toward the ontology, and neither so neutral as to be vacuous. For each entry, flag whether it is substantive or, in your judgment, verbal, and if verbal, say what the two sides are using the key term to mean.

3. A cruxes section. Two I suspect belong there, though you may revise the list: (a) whether the negative in the master claim functions as denial of identity, denial of predication, or denial that one name singles out what the other does — and whether the dialogue's exchanges give any evidence that the author kept these apart; and (b) whether the chapters on hardness-and-whiteness and on how names pick out things form a unified theory with the white horse discourse or are later strands of the school stitched together by the compilers, such that treating them as one author's system is an interpretive artifact.

4. For each crux, at least one argument or test that both camps would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Favor constraints and conditionals over appeals to authority: for instance, both camps may accept as a constraint that a paraphrase of the master claim under which the dialogue's steps become formally valid is evidence about what the argument needs to be doing, and then disagree about what that shows. Neither camp may settle anything by citing a translator's preference; where respected translations diverge, the divergence itself must be treated as data about the text's indeterminacy, not as a verdict.

5. A closing section stating, as explicit conditional predictions rather than a wish list, what each camp would expect from hypothetical new evidence — say, a newly excavated early manuscript containing a fuller version of the discourse, or a contemporary commentary discussing how the argument was received — and whether there is any finding both camps agree would move at least one of them, and which.

I want the drafting to do real work: the agreed-claims section should contain claims it would actually be an achievement to get both parties to sign, and the disagreement formulations should be ones each side would recognize as fairly stating its own position without conceding anything it holds. Please do not resolve the interpretive dispute or award it to either camp, and do not supply a bibliography — the statement should stand on its formulations alone.
