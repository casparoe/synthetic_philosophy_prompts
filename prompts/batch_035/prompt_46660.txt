I've been recording my gut verdicts on gambles and would like to know what decision rule, if any, they add up to. Throughout, assume my utility for money is linear across every amount mentioned, so any risk attitudes must show up in how probabilities are weighted, not in the utility function. Expected utility theory ranks a gamble by its probability-weighted average of dollar outcomes. Risk-weighted expected utility (REU) theory instead scores a gamble whose dollar outcomes are x1 ≤ x2 ≤ ... ≤ xn, with Pi the probability of an outcome at least as good as xi, as x1 + r(P2)(x2 − x1) + r(P3)(x3 − x2) + ... + r(Pn)(xn − x(n−1)), for some nondecreasing function r with r(0) = 0 and r(1) = 1; r(p) < p expresses a kind of risk aversion that the utility function cannot. My verdicts:

1. I take $50 for sure over a coin flip between $0 and $100.
2. I take $500,000 for sure over a gamble paying $2,500,000 with probability 10/100, $500,000 with probability 89/100, and $0 with probability 1/100.
3. But between a gamble paying $2,500,000 with probability 10/100 and $0 otherwise, and one paying $500,000 with probability 11/100 and $0 otherwise, I take the first.
4. I refuse a coin flip costing $100 or paying $110, yet I would pay $5 for a 1-in-10-million ticket on $40,000,000, though that is negative in expectation.
5. I consent to an operation described as offering 90% survival; when the same operation is redescribed to me as 10% mortality, I find myself inclined to refuse, and on reflection I want to count that inclination as a verdict about the very same gamble.
6. The case I keep going back and forth on: offered one hundred coin flips, each costing $100 or paying $110, taken sequentially with the option to stop, I would refuse the first and so never reach the rest; but if I had to commit in advance to all hundred, I would accept.

Is there a single r that captures all six? If not, I'd like you to work through which verdicts have to go, whether what survives is still REU or some other theory, and what that surviving theory predicts about a case I haven't listed — say, a single coin flip costing $1,000 or paying $1,100.
