I cover atrocity and international law stories for a general readership, and something about the way we're all forced to name these events keeps nagging at me, so I want to understand what the naming system actually assumes. The setup everybody works with: the 1948 Genocide Convention defines the crime as certain acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such. So courts spend years litigating whether a perpetrator had that special intent to destroy a group as a group, versus just wanting them gone from a territory or crushed as a political threat. Governments, meanwhile, treat the word itself as radioactive — the State Department back in '94 famously twisted itself in knots during Rwanda to avoid saying it, and there was that whole era of "ethnic cleansing" as a conveniently legal-sounding euphemism that obligates nobody to do anything. And then you have Darfur in 2004, where the US actually did make a formal genocide determination and then said, more or less, that nothing new followed from it.

Here's what I can't square. The whole scheme seems to presuppose that destroying a group as a group is somehow the worst thing, worse than killing the exact same number of people for territory or political power — which is why killing a million people for their political allegiance lands as "mere" crimes against humanity while killing far fewer because of their ethnicity can be the crime of crimes. What theory of value does that presuppose? That groups themselves are bearers of moral value with their own standing as victims, or just that individuals are harmed in a special way when attacked qua group membership? And note the doctrine quietly rules out political groups entirely — which I gather was a Cold War compromise — and it also excludes destroying a group's culture, language, heritage, unless that's tied to physical destruction, even though Lemkin himself, the guy who invented the concept, was obsessed with exactly that cultural dimension. So the law operationalizes a version of Lemkin that Lemkin arguably didn't hold. Then there's the intent requirement: a campaign that foreseeably annihilates a group but is officially framed as counterinsurgency doesn't count, which seems to presuppose that intended group destruction is categorically worse than foreseen group destruction.

And the political layer seems to presuppose something in flat contradiction with the legal layer: officials avoid the word precisely because they treat uttering it as triggering some obligation, but the legal practice says the word triggers nothing automatic (Darfur), and the obligation under the Convention is to prevent and punish, which you'd think applies to the atrocities themselves however labeled. So my question, I guess: what's the implicit theory of what makes mass atrocity distinctive that this whole edifice — the category boundaries, the intent obsession, the euphemism dance — rests on? Do the legal and political parts presuppose consistent things or is the incoherence real? Is any of this underdetermined, i.e., compatible with several different underlying theories? And if we rebuilt the practice around, say, crimes against humanity as the headline category — which some human rights lawyers I've interviewed basically advocate — what exactly would change about which atrocities count as worst, and would that be more honest or would it lose something Lemkin was right about? I'd love a serious reconstruction rather than the usual "naming matters" op-ed take.
