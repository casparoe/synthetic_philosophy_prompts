i teach history and philosophy of science, and every spring i do a lecture on kepler's defense of astrology, because students find it baffling that the guy who gave us elliptical orbits was also casting horoscopes for a living and complaining about clients. the way i present his defense (mostly out of Tertius Interveniens and bits of the Harmonice, and i'm paraphrasing throughout, not quoting) is that it runs on two analogies stacked on top of each other, and i've started to suspect i've been letting them do more work than they can carry. i want an audit.

analogy one: the astrologer is like a weather forecaster. kepler's line, as i read him, is that astrological prediction is fallible but not therefore worthless -- the aspects nudge sublunar stuff the way the sky's condition nudges the weather, the forecaster gets it wrong alot of the time, and we don't conclude meteorology is fraud. the misses don't refute the enterprise because the influence is a weak tendency, not a compulsion. so the student who says "astrology predicted X and X didn't happen, therefore astrology is bunk" is making the same mistake as someone who cancels the weather bureau after a bad forecast.

analogy two: the heavens are like a clockwork. kepler writes to herwart in 1605 that the celestial machine is not a divine living being but something like a clockwork, all the motions driven by a single simple force the way a clock's motions are driven by a weight. now on its face this was aimed at the animists -- no world soul, no intelligent movers, just geometry and force. but kepler keeps the astrology! and the way he keeps it, as best i can tell, is by quietly relocating the magic into the human soul: the soul has an innate sensitivity to geometrical ratios (the aspects), so the configuration up there doesn't *push* events down here so much as *display* something the soul reads, the way you read a dial rather than being shoved by a hand.

here's what bugs me and what i want the audit to adress. the clock analogy was kepler's weapon against the animists, and the weather analogy was his weapon against the skeptics, and it's not obvious you can fire both without the shots crossing. a clock doesn't causally *do* anything downstream with the time it shows, and weather isn't a *display* of anything -- it's brute push and shove. so which is it? does the configuration of the heavens influence sublunar events the way a front influences a barometer needle, or does it merely indicate, with the soul supplying the causal punch by responding to the geometry? if the second, isn't that just... the animism back in through the side door, only now the ensouled thing is us instead of the heavens? and if the first, then the misses aren't excusable forecaster-style error, because a deterministic clock train that only *sometimes* delivers is a broken clock.

so, please: lay out both analogies with the correspondence made explicit (a table is fine -- what plays the role of the barometer, the clock spring, the tendency, the reading). then say where each mapping breaks, and -- this is the part my students never manage -- separate the disanalogies that matter for kepler's actual use from the ones that don't. a forensic weather forecaster has feedback and calibration; the astrologer mostly has vague postdictions and a client who remembers the hits. say whether that's a defect in the analogy or just a contingent shortfall of 17th-century practice. then tell me straight: once the disanalogies are on the table, does kepler's cleaned-up, non-fatalistic, geometry-based astrology survive as an epistemic enterprise he was entitled to keep, or was he propping it up with two analogies that each quietly import what the other one removed? and if neither analogy can carry it, is there a better one -- something that is a genuine display-plus-response system, where an upstream configuration carries information without force and a downstream system with structure of its own picks it up -- or does every candidate analogy smuggle the animism back in?

(i'd rather you not turn this into a reading list. argue it out from the analogies themselves.)
