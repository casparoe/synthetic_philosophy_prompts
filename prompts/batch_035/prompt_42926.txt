I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, so please write for a lay reader; technical terminology is fine so long as you define it when you introduce it.

Here is my situation. I work at a company that hires engineers through a two-stage pipeline: an initial screen run by a recruiter team, then a technical loop. The recruiter team is headed by a man I'll call R. Over the past year I've sat on enough hiring debriefs to be confident that R. systematically misrepresents the job to candidates: the tech stack they'll actually work on, the on-call burden, the travel, the team's real roadmap. Each misrepresentation is individually modest — nobody is being defrauded of money — but candidates make life decisions on the basis of what he tells them: several have relocated across the country, and one couple postponed adopting because of promises about parental leave scheduling. By my count there are four or five active deceptions in progress right now.

The problem is that I can't prove any of it. Debriefs aren't recorded, and R. is careful never to put the misrepresentations in writing. Two engineers have already complained informally and were brushed off; another undocumented complaint would mostly teach R. to be more careful. There is one reliable way to document him: a sympathetic manager has offered to slip a fake candidate into his pipeline, and she has asked me to be that candidate — to sit through a screening call while pretending to be a job-seeker, recording everything. To do this I would misrepresent myself, deliberately and at some length, to a colleague.

I have a hard personal rule against this. It is not that I think my impersonation would be worse than what R. does — by my own lights, each of his deceptions is at least as bad as mine would be, and there are several of them. It is that I hold an unqualified rule against my own deliberate misrepresentation, and I can see no principled line below which I would permit myself to break it. So refusing to help appears to mean that more of exactly the thing my rule is against will happen, through R.'s actions rather than mine.

My partner says this is straightforwardly irrational: if what makes deception bad is what it does to the person deceived, then declining to do a smaller amount of it to stop a larger amount just protects my self-image at everyone else's expense. When I try to answer, I find I don't actually have an argument — only the conviction that there is a real difference between lying and letting lies happen, and that the difference isn't erased by counting.

What I would like from you:

1. A genuine adjudication of whether my refusal is defensible, not a pep talk for either side. In particular: does my position survive if the number of deceptions I would prevent grows — if there were fifty instead of five? Identify where, if anywhere, it has to give.
2. Whatever terminology exists for this dispute, so that I can at least name what I'm standing in the middle of.
3. Finally, I don't know how much of this is settled among people who study it and how much is one school's opinion. Please distinguish the parts of your answer that reflect broad agreement from the parts that are genuinely contested, and do not present contested positions as consensus. If the honest answer is that informed people have always disagreed about the core question, I would rather know that than receive a tidy verdict.
