I keep bumping into people who cite the Condorcet jury theorem as a reason to trust big juries: if each juror is even a hair better than a coin flip at getting the truth, then 500 heads voting together are basically infallible, so a conviction by a jury of 500 must have been near-certainly correct. I want you to stress-test that move on the most famous jury verdict we have: the trial of Socrates.

Facts so we're on the same page (these are all standard): Athens, 399 BCE, a jury of roughly 500 citizens (usually counted as 501), charges of impiety and corrupting the youth, no judge and no professional lawyers, and jurors who had spent decades on the recieving end of Socrates' public interrogations in the agora. The vote for guilt was narrow — by the usual reading of Plato's Apology, a swing of about thirty votes would have acquitted him — and the later vote for death came back with a much bigger majority.

Do the analysis in both directions.

Theory to episode: map the theorem onto the trial properly. What would the competence parameter have to be, and with 500 jurors at that competence, how confident does the theorem say the majority verdict was? Then say which assumptions the episode actually breaks: independence (they all sat through the same arguments in the same place and voted together right after), shared evidence and common priors, the fact that the error here looks like decades of accumulated personal grievance rather than noise around a true signal, and the fact that at least part of what they were voting on — whether he corrupted the youth — is normative rather than factual, so it's not clear the theorem even purports to apply. Also tell me about deliberation specifically: by the theorem's lights, is jurors talking to each other before voting a feature or a bug? Is there any way to restate the theorem so that pooling information doesn't just correlate everyone's errors?

Episode to theory — this is the part I actually care about. The obvious reply is that the theorem never claimed the verdict was right, only that IF jurors are competent and independent THEN the majority is probably right, and Socrates' jurors presumably weren't, so there's no anomaly. I find that reply suspicious and want you to say whether it's legitimate or whether it hollows the theorem out. When we're looking back at an actual historical jury, is there any non-question-begging way to estimate the competence parameter — one that doesn't already presuppose we know what the right verdict was? If not, what is the theorem actually good for: a statement of the conditions under which majorities track truth, or a design tool for institutions (jury size, majority thresholds — and note the ugly flip side, where competence below one half makes bigger juries strictly worse) that can never be deployed after the fact? And please be honest about what the theorem is silent on here rather than forcing it to speak.

Skip the binomial math beyond the one competence calculation I asked for — I know the standard derivation. What I want is disciplined mapping and a verdict on whether the Socrates case is a counterexample to how the theorem gets used in conversation, or just a case that falls neatly outside it.
