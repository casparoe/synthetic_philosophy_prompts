I run operations at a 400-person engineering consultancy and we're rolling out AI tooling fast enough that we've started a standing monthly meeting to decide which tasks get handed over next. My CFO just wants the business case, but I've been reading philosophy of work in the evenings and I don't want to wake up in five years having hollowed out everyone's jobs and then act surprised when retention and workmanship collapse. The bits that stuck with me, roughly: one camp says the value of work is all in the product, so if the product stays the same or gets better, nothing worth caring about is lost. Another camp says the process is constitutive — that achievements require difficulty, so if you automate the hard parts of someone's job, what's left is like winning a chess game where a machine played all your moves: same trophy, no achievement. There's also the idea that people get self-respect from exercising complex capacities (something like Rawls's Aristotelian principle, I think?), and separately the thought that even burdensome work can be valuable *as* burden, which I confess I don't fully get.

Write me a decision procedure the comittee can actually run on a batch of proposed automations: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a "don't automate," and where it hands the decision back to humans because the question is about values rather than facts. Constraints: about 15-20 tasks per meeting, ten minutes each, and the committee is ops managers plus one HR person — no philosophers — so the theory has to be buried inside the decision criteria, not lectured at the top of the page.

Then run it on three live items: (1) auto-generating first drafts of the site reports our junior engineers spend half their week on — they complain about the drafting but also say it's how they learn the job; (2) automating the final visual QC pass that our senior technicians take real pride in, even though it pays no differently than the rest of their week; and (3) automating the timesheet and compliance paperwork that literally nobody will defend — tell me honestly if that one's a trap (skill atrophy? people losing the ambient picture of what colleagues actually do?).

Two things I want you to be upfront about. First, I want the QC pass automated and I want the juniors protected — if the procedure can't deliver both, say so rather than fudging. Second, where does the procedure misfire — e.g. how do I tell whether someone reporting that their work is meaningful is reporting a fact about the work, or a fact about how they've made peace with work they'd dump tomorrow if they could?
