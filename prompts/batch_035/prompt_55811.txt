In discussions of mechanistic anomaly detection for language models — the idea that we might catch dangerous capabilities by noticing inputs that get processed by a different mechanism than the ones that accounted for the training-time behaviors we signed off on — I keep encountering a borrowed biological analogy. Field biologists distinguish homologous traits from analogous ones: a bird's wing and a bat's wing do the same job, but only one pair derives from a shared ancestral structure, while a bat's wing and a whale's flipper look nothing alike yet count as the same arrangement modified by descent. The analogy invites us to treat mechanism-identity in a network the same way: two refusal behaviors might be produced by one circuit reused across contexts (homology) or by two circuits that independently converged on refusing (analogy), and the safety proposal is that the dangerous cases are precisely the ones where an input is handled by a differently-assembled mechanism rather than the one that explained the benign evidence. A second analogy competes with it: mechanisms as subroutines in ordinary software, which are either called or not called, so that sameness of mechanism collapses into sameness of code path.

Please audit the biological analogy in particular. Lay out the correspondence table — what plays the role of lineage, of common ancestor, of descent-with-modification, of the fossil record — and be specific about what in a trained network could ground mechanism-identity in the way shared ancestry grounds homology (the training run? gradient descent as a selection pressure? the pretraining corpus as a gene pool?). Then say where the mapping breaks: polysemantic components that let two "different" mechanisms share parts in ways organs cannot; mechanisms distributed across layers with no discrete boundary; the grain problem of whether the relevant unit is a feature, a circuit, or a whole behavioral disposition; and whether within-network convergence is common enough that the homology/analogy line loses its bite. Finally, judge whether the conclusion the analogy is being used to license — that there is a well-defined detection task here, a fact of the matter about same-mechanism-or-not that a monitor could check — survives the disanalogies, or whether the cruder subroutine analogy, with all its faults, does a better job of sustaining it.
