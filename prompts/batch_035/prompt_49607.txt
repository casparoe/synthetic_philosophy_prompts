I'm teaching a class that pairs Kierkegaard's The Present Age (the 1846 bit of Two Ages) with films about media, and next week is Network, the 1976 Sidney Lumet / Paddy Chayefsky one. Help me read the film through Kierkegaard's critique of the press and "the public." His claim, roughly, is that the press conjures up an abstract phantom public whose whole existence is as a spectator, that it levels everything — passion, authority, individuals — into reflection and chatter, and that envy and anonymity replace actual engagement. On the surface Network is a perfect illustration: the network takes Howard Beale's genuine on-air breakdown and metabolizes it into a show, his mad-as-hell rant becomes a cathartic ritual the audience performs and consumes, and when he finally tells people to turn their televisions off, they stop watching until Arthur Jensen takes him to the boardroom and converts him into a preacher of the corporate cosmos — and when even that tanks, they have him shot on the air as a programming decision. But here's what nags me: Kierkegaard says the press age is an age *without* passion, and Beale is nothing but passion — the whole movie is shouting. So does the film confirm Kierkegaard or refute him? Is the show's absorption of Beale's rage exactly the leveling he feared (passion metabolized into entertainment until it isn't passion anymore), or is Chayefsky making a different point about corporate power and ratings that Kierkegaard's categories can't reach? I want a reading that takes both the film and Kierkegaard seriously, tells me honestly where the fit breaks down, and maybe says whether the Jensen scene — Beale switching prophets without much resistance — is the best test case or whether the mad-as-hell montage is. Also, if there's room: does the film give me anything to work with for the last seminar question, namely whether Kierkegaard would see anything genuinely new in social media or just the press at higher speed? Please don't just collect scenes that echo Kierkegaard — the interesting part is where the film resists the reading.
