I have been reading, purely as an amateur, about the ancient sentence that says of itself that it is not true, and about the various things philosophers have proposed to do about it. A recurring pattern keeps striking me, and I cannot find a settled name for it. Perhaps you can tell me whether one exists.

The pattern is this: whenever someone offers a resolution of the original sentence — it fails to express a proposition; it is neither true nor false; the word 'true' behaves differently depending on where it is used — it seems one can immediately construct a fresh sentence that recreates the problem using nothing but the resolution's own resources. If the proposal is that the original sentence is undefined or lacks a proposition, the new sentence says of itself that it is not true, and not false either, and not undefined, and not any of the other statuses the proposal allows. If the proposal assigns the word different roles at different levels, the new sentence somehow reaches for the role it itself occupies. Each resolution appears to arrive with a nastier successor already attached, and the successor is tailor-made for that resolution and no other; it is not a fixed paradox but a family of them, each generated on demand from whatever vocabulary the latest solution introduced.

My questions, then, are these. Does this cycle — the generation of successor paradoxes phrased in a proposed solution's own terms — have an established name in the literature on truth, and if so, what exactly does the name cover and what does it exclude? If it does have a name, I would still like you to locate its edges for me, because the label alone will not satisfy me. Specifically: is this just the observation that particular solutions happen to be incomplete, repairable case by case, or is it something structural about the concept of truth — a reason to expect that any account whatever, however sophisticated, will be vulnerable to its own successor? How, if at all, does it differ from the older, blunter point that some sentence or other will always slip through whatever net is cast? And how does it bear on the view that 'true' is not a weighty property at all but a mere device of endorsement — does that view escape the cycle, quietly restrict what it applies to, or simply meet a successor built from its own notions?

If, on inspection, no standard term covers precisely this configuration, please say so plainly, coin a term, define it carefully, distinguish it from the nearest neighbouring ideas (such as the general incompleteness of particular fixes, or the thought that the concept of truth cannot coherently be applied to everything, including statements about truth itself), and give a couple of boundary cases that show where the coined concept stops applying.

Please write your answer in British English. I am not after a reading list — only a precise map of the conceptual terrain.
