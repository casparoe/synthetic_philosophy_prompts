I got into a long argument with my brother-in-law over the holidays about whether it can ever be a mistake to find something out. He's a "knowledge is always good" guy, and I kept coming up with what felt like counterexamples — you learn bad news early and stress longer, you find out a coworker hates you, whatever. He waved all of them away with the same move: either the info cost you something to get, or the bad part was just in your head, or other people found out too. Then he pulled up something he half remembered from a stats class: an old theorem, by some guy named Good I think, that free information can never make you worse off *in expectation*, as long as you get to choose what to do after seeing it and only the facts plus your choice determine how things turn out. When he laid it out I couldn't poke a hole in it, and that bugs me, because my gut still says sometimes you're better off not knowing.

So here's what I want you to build me, and please follow the specs exactly, because I've thought about this a lot and the easy versions are all wrong. Scenario A: a case where the information is genuinely sharp — it can even be perfectly accurate about the relevant fact — and yet its value is exactly zero, not just small. It has to come out zero because of the decision situation, not because the test is weak or noisy. Scenario B: a case where information has strictly negative value, but with the cheap moves banned — no cost to obtain it, no emotional harm from knowing, nobody else finds out, and the fact itself won't hurt you whether you know it or not. The harm has to come from something structural about how the deciding works, if thats even possible; my brother-in-law bets it isn't, and honestly if you can't do it, tell me that straight and explain why. Scenario C: whichever counterexample you think is the most tempting one people reach for (the genetic test in the sealed envelope, airplane anxiety, whatever), rebuilt so that once you actually run the numbers the info turns out to be worth having after all.

Constraints on all three: ordinary-life settings — a kitchen, a job offer, a doctor's office — no sci-fi, no perfect predictors, no demons, one paragraph each. After each scenario do the check the way he does it: the prior, what the signal changes it to, what you'd do before seeing it versus after, and the expected value both ways, with rough numbers I can follow. Then tell me which bit of the theorem's fine print is doing the work in that case, and what happens to the value of the information if you patch that bit. Keep the whole thing under 800 words. I don't care about proper philosophy vocabulary, I care about being able to win the next time he brings this up.
