I'm in a little reading group that's been doing ethics-adjacent topics this semester, and last week a two-person argument broke out about whether the "better than" relation is transitive -- i.e., whether A better than B and B better than C forces A better than C. I mostly just listened at the time but I've been chewing on it since, and I'd love some outside help. Please answer as if you're a professional philosopher who works on axiology and formal ethics, and think carefully / maybe brainstorm a bit before committing to verdicts, because I suspect they're partly talking past each other.

Here's roughly how it went. Wes (econ PhD student, very sure of himself) opened by saying transitivity isn't an empirical hypothesis but part of the logic of comparatives themselves: "taller than" is transitive, "better than" is just the evaluative member of the same family, so denying it is like denying basic logic. He added that if betterness weren't transitive, you could construct a money pump -- someone who trades A for B at a cost, B for C at a cost, then C for A at a cost, ending up exactly where they started minus their money, so the view is literally incoherant. Ilana (philosophy MA) answered that the pump is about preferences and choices, not betterness: you only get pumped by what you *choose*, and the assumption that you'd always choose the better option is an extra normative bridge she doesn't have to grant -- betterness could be nontransitive while every rational choice remains fine. Then she gave what she called a spectrum argument. Take one person suffering an excruciating year-long headache. Isn't it better if instead two people each have a year-long headache that is imperceptibly milder? Repeat: four people with imperceptibly milder ones, eight, and so on. Each step looks like a clear improvement, so transitivity says a huge number of people with year-long, almost-mild headaches is better than the single excruciating one. But somewhere down the chain it seems obvious that a million people with year-long mild headaches is *worse* than one person having a bad year. So either transitivity fails or one of the "obvious" pairwise judgments fails.

Wes's counter was that the spectrum argument doesn't refute transitivity, it just shows one of the pairwise judgments must be wrong, which is how paradoxes always get resolved: "barely less intense but burdening twice as many people" isn't automatically better, the tiny intensity gain doesn't obviously outweigh the doubling of suffering, so it's just false that each step is an improvement -- transitivity is doing its job of forcing us to revise the shaky intuition, since a coherent betterness ranking can't cycle. He also warned that without transitivity, "choose the best option" has no determinate meaning when cycles appear, which would be a disaster for ethics as a guide to action. Ilana replied that this proves too much: her pairwise judgments seem *at least* as secure as the transitivity intuition itself -- she can just see that slightly milder pain is better than slightly worse pain -- and if Wes gets to throw out intuitions that conflict with transitivity, he owes her a reason why transitivity gets privileged status. (At some point they also drifted into whether tiny improvements compose over billions of steps, like grains of sand forming a heap, but neither of them resolved whether that's the same phenomenon or a distraction.)

Can you go through the exchange move by move and rate each step -- the logic-of-comparatives claim, the money pump and Ilana's bridge-claim response, the spectrum argument's premises, Wes's "revise the pairwise judgment" move, the privileged-status challenge, and the action-guidance worry? Also please say whether the Sorites-flavored drift point actually matters or just muddies things, and whether the disagreement partly turns on conflating "better than" with "rationally preferable" or something like that. I don't want a diplomatic both-sides-have-merits answer -- give real verdicts on each move, flag where each of them is saying something true but beside the point, and tell me who comes out more right overall.
