I teach philosophy of physics to undergraduates, and this term a popular book has been making the rounds among my students claiming that the Everett (many-worlds) interpretation makes quantum probability easy. The thought, as they recite it back to me, runs: when a measurement splits the world, every outcome occurs somewhere, so the probability you should assign to finding yourself with a given outcome is simply the fraction of your future copies that find it. Probability on this telling is nothing deep — it is population statistics about your own descendants, the same reasoning by which a randomly sampled voter's credence that she voted for the winner tracks the winner's share of the vote. Before my students harden this into a conviction, I want the analogy audited properly.

Please lay out the mapping explicitly: in the analogy, what plays the role of the population, the random draw, the reference class? Then show where the mapping breaks, attending especially (though not exclusively) to two points. First, the fraction-of-copies rule appears to commit us to counting branches, and branch count is not a stable quantity: nothing in the formalism fixes how finely worlds split, and a great deal of arguably irrelevant downstream decoherence — the detector's display, the lab notebook, environmental scattering — multiplies copies on one side without altering any physics. Consider a measurement with two equal-amplitude outcomes whose second outcome is then fed into a further device that splits it again: is the probability of the first outcome one-half or one-third? The Born weights say one-half throughout; counting seems to say it depends on where we place the splits, which is precisely the sort of thing that should not matter. Second, amplitudes: the Schrödinger equation assigns each branch a modulus-squared weight, and those weights are exactly what matches observed frequencies — yet they are invisible to the counting picture, which returns one-half for a 50/50 amplitude split and equally one-half for a 70/30 one.

Finally, tell me whether the popular conclusion survives the disanalogies. If naive counting fails, is there a repaired version of the population-share idea — one that weights copies by amplitude and then asks why a rational agent should care about those weights in deciding how to bet before the split, or one that treats pre-measurement credence as self-locating uncertainty over branches, on the model of the Sleeping Beauty problem? And say whether the Sleeping Beauty comparison ultimately helps or hurts: the halfer and the thirder at least argue over a reference class fixed by the experiment's setup, whereas if the fraction-of-copies rule yields different answers depending on how we carve the branching, it looks to be in worse shape than either. I would like a concluding judgment on the question my students are really asking: does the Everettian need anything beyond the bare formalism to recover the Born rule — and if the population-share analogy cannot carry that weight, what, if anything, can?
