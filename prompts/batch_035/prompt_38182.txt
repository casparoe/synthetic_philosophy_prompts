I'm taking a philosophy of language course and we just finished Kripke and Putnam on natural kinds and the necessary a posteriori, and I keep coming back to the Pluto mess because it fits the material in an uncomfortable way. Quick facts so we're on the same page: Pluto was discovered in 1930, called the ninth planet for 76 years, and then in 2006 the International Astronomical Union held a vote at its Prague general assembly — a few hundred astronomers out of a membership of thousands, with plenty of the absentees grumbling afterwards that the whole thing was decided by a small clique — and adopted a definition of 'planet' that requires, roughly, orbiting the sun, being round under your own gravity, and gravitationally dominating your orbital zone. Pluto fails the last one, so it's now a 'dwarf planet'. The trigger was that in the mid-2000s astronomers identified Eris, an object roughly Pluto's size, and everyone could see a slippery slope coming. And apparently this already happened once before: Ceres and a few others were called planets for decades in the early 1800s, until enough similar objects piled up and they quietly got demoted to asteroids, and nobody rioted then — with Pluto, half the internet did.

Here's what I want. Take the 2006 reclassification and run it through rigid designation and the necessary a posteriori, in both directions. One direction: was 'planet' functioning as a natural-kind term whose hidden structure was under investigation — like 'water' turning out to be H2O, or 'whale' turning out not to be a fish — such that pre-2006 speakers' beliefs about Pluto were tracking a structure the term's meaning was already pointed at? Or had 'planet' never really picked out a kind at all (my TA mumbled something about jade actually being two unrelated minerals, jadeite and nephrite), in which case 'Pluto is a planet' was never a candidate for a necessary truth or necessary falsehood in the first place? And what does the vote itself do on each reading — is it the linguistic community's experts refining reference through their causal contact with the world, which is what Putnam's division of linguistic labor seems to promise, or is it plain legislation, a stipulated new meaning by fiat? If it's legislation, then externalism looks weirdly silent on who counts as the expert class and by what procedure, and I'd like you to say whether that's a real problem for the view or just me overreading.

The other direction, which is the part I actually can't get straight: work the necessity story through carefully. If some pre-2006 statement like "Pluto is the ninth planet" was true, was it neccessary a posteriori in Kripke's sense — and does its going false afterwards show it was never necessary, or that the term shifted meaning, or that astronomical kind terms just aren't in the business of supporting necessities the way chemical ones are? Take an actual stance on whether the criteria in the 2006 definition were discovering the meaning or replacing it, and say whether the episode is ultimately a point for externalism, a point against it, or neutral.
