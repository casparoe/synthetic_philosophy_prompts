I've been keeping a list of my gut reactions to how people treat characters in video games, and I'd like to know whether the verdicts add up to a principle or whether I'm just being inconsistent. Watching a friend spend an hour in a fantasy RPG repeatedly setting a scripted villager on fire to hear the screams: fine, it's just pixels and a sound file. A classmate who plays a life-simulation game and deliberately builds a doorless room to let a simulated family starve: also fine, but somehow I don't want to watch, and it makes me like him a little less. Someone who plays a western game where the horse is rendered and animated with unusual realism, and who shoots his own horse after each mission just to see it: not fine in my gut, even though I'd say the horse is no more conscious than the villager. A player who exploits a glitch to skip the mission where a beloved companion character is scripted to die, so that in her save file the companion lives on: I find this sweet rather than silly. And someone in an online game who kills another player's tamed pet purely to upset that player: clearly wrong, but the wrongness seems to be entirely about the griefing, not about the pet, which confuses me, since the pet "dies" either way.

The principle I keep half-forming is something like: nothing matters for the character's own sake, because the character feels nothing, but our treatment of them can matter for our sake, because of what it rehearses or reveals in us. That handles the villager and maybe the doorless room, but it struggles with the horse, since shooting a realistic horse and burning a cartoonish villager rehearse pretty similar things in me as an observer, yet my verdicts differ. And I'm not sure what it says about a newer kind of game I've read about, where a companion character is driven by machine learning rather than pure scripting, so that it remembers what you've done across sessions and reacts in ways the developers didn't directly write. My instinct is that being cruel to that kind of character is worse than being cruel to a scripted one, but I can't tell whether that's a real difference or just my being fooled.

Could you try to state a principle, or a small set of candidates, that captures as many of my verdicts as possible? Please be explicit about which of my verdicts any principle has to overturn, and why those rather than the others. I'd also like to hear what your best principle predicts about a case I haven't decided on: a single-player game that randomly generates a procedurally simulated creature each playthrough, which visibly struggles and suffers when the player mistreats it in ways that no developer specifically coded, but which is deleted entirely whenever the game closes. And if the honest answer is that my verdicts track nothing principled — that the horse case is sentimentality and the machine-learning case is anthropomorphizing — I'd genuinely rather know that than be handed a principle built to flatter me.
