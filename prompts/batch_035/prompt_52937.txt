I'm a type designer, and my studio runs a small reading group on the conceptual side of letterforms. Our next session is on Hofstadter's Letter Spirit project, which tried to model, computationally, how a letter can be recognized and creatively re-rendered across wildly different guises. I'd like to close the evening with a poem, and I'd like that poem to be a sestina.

Here is the thought it should carry. Consider how different the physical marks are that we all read as 'a': the monumental capital A of a Roman inscription, the double-story 'a' of a book face, the single-story 'a' of a geometric sans-serif and of most people's handwriting, a blackletter a, a child's wobbly copybook attempt, the red embroidered A in The Scarlet Letter. No two share a decisive set of visual features; some pairs share almost none. Yet we identify them instantly, and we can even make finer judgments — this one and that one belong to the same typeface, that one is the same letterform at a larger size, this one is a bad 'a' wearing the wrong clothes. The thought from the reading group is that the essence of a letter is not a template or a shared feature set but a role in a self-defining system: an 'a' is whatever competently plays the role of 'a' against the backdrop of the other twenty-five letters, and a typeface is a coherent manner of filling all those roles at once — the category tells you which letter you are seeing, the style tells you whose hand or foundry made it.

Make the form do the argument. The six end-words should be drawn from the anatomy of the letter 'a' itself — I suggest bowl, stem, counter, aperture, terminal, and ear, though you may swap in others (finial, spur, shoulder) if they serve better. These recurring words are the fixed roles: the same words return in every stanza, but each time in a different syntactic and semantic guise, so that the poem enacts the very thesis it describes — sameness of role, endless variation of manifestation. You may take one liberty with the scheme, such as letting a word return inside a compound or a pun, but only one.

Across the six sestets, visit six different 'a's, roughly in this order: an inscription capital; the double-story 'a' of a book face; a single-story 'a'; a blackletter a; a child's first attempt; and the red embroidered A. The envoi should bring two of these 'a's face to face and register instant recognition — the kind that precedes any explanation — without explaining anything.

Constraints: do not use the words 'category' or 'style' (or any of the reading group's other jargon) in the poem itself; a type designer should not wince at the anatomical vocabulary; and someone who has thought about these questions should be able to see the thesis in the structure rather than being told it. If you can work in, quietly and without making it a punchline, the fact that 'a' is also the indefinite article — the mark of any-one-member-of-a-class — that would be a welcome grace note.
