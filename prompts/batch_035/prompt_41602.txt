I'm taking an introductory philosophy of mind course this semester, and last week we spent a whole session on the knowledge argument. The lecture went by fast, so I picked up a popular book on consciousness that a friend recommended — I'd rather not name it, since I'm not trying to trash it — and its chapter on the thought experiment left me more confused than the lecture did. The book summarizes things like this: "Frank Jackson asked us to imagine Mary, a vision scientist who knows every last physical fact about color — wavelengths, retinal transduction, the wiring of the visual cortex, everything neuroscience could ever discover. She has been raised in a strictly black-and-white room and has never seen any color at all. One day she walks out and sees a red tomato. Clearly she learns something new: what red looks like. So there must be facts that are not physical facts, and physicalism is false. In his original paper Jackson went further and argued that since qualia are nonphysical, they must be epiphenomenal — they don't cause anything, which is the price of accepting the argument. The ability hypothesis — that Mary only gains new abilities like recognizing and imagining red, not knowledge of a new fact — was later accepted by Jackson himself, which is why he abandoned the argument and returned to physicalism."

I'm inclined to think physicalism is true, so I went in hoping the book would show me where the argument goes wrong, but I came away unsure whether the summary is even reliable. Some of it matches what my professor said, but the parts about Jackson's own later views especially confused me, and the lecture treated the "what did Mary actually gain?" question as an open debate rather than something settled one way. Could you go through this claim by claim and tell me which parts a philosopher of mind would accept, which parts are simplifications they'd tolerate with a sigh, and which are genuine misrepresentations — especially anything about what follows from the argument versus what was a separate commitment of Jackson's, and about how and why Jackson's own position actually changed? And a second question: the chapter closes by saying that the knowledge argument shows consciousness will always lie beyond the reach of neuroscience, so a complete science of the brain is impossible in principle. Does that follow from whichever parts of the summary survive correction?
