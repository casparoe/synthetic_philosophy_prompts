And here's one student's answer, typed out in full:

"Akrasia comes from the Greek for lack of strength over oneself, and it means acting against your own better judgment: you judge that you should do one thing and then you go do another. Socrates denied that this ever really happens. His argument in the Protagoras was that knowledge can't be dragged around like a slave — if you truly knew that one option was worse, that knowledge would control what you do. So what looks like akrasia is really ignorance: the person eating the cake doesn't actually know it's the worse choice, they only have a shaky opinion that behaves like a hunch.

Aristotle agreed with Socrates that there's a genuine puzzle here, and in Book VII of the Nicomachean Ethics he mostly accepted the Socratic solution, just dressed up a bit. He says the knowledge that gets overcome in akrasia is only potential knowledge, like a drunk man reciting verses by Empedocles — the words are still in him but they aren't active. So akrasia can only ever happen to weakened, potential knowledge, never to full active knowledge, which is basically what Socrates said anyway.

Behavioral economics gives us a cleaner and testable story. With hyperbolic discounting, the value of a delayed reward falls off along a hyperbola instead of an exponential, and the important consequence is preference reversal: asked a month in advance whether I'd rather have $100 in 12 months or $120 in 13 months, I pick the $120; asked again with the $100 available today and the $120 a month out, I pick the $100. George Ainslie demonstrated these reversals in pigeon experiments and argued that hyperbolic discounting is the mechanism behind akrasia: from a distance the bigger later reward is preferred, but as the smaller sooner reward gets close, its value shoots up and takes over. This also explains why precommitment works. Ulysses tying himself to the mast is self-binding: present-you arranges things so that future-you can't grab the smaller-sooner reward. Mischel's marshmallow test proved the discount curve is hyperbolic rather than exponential — he measured kids' discount curves directly and found hyperbolas fit better, and the kids who could hold out turned out to do better later in life.

Donald Davidson took the puzzle seriously in his essay How Is Weakness of the Will Possible? His solution is to distinguish an all-things-considered judgment from an unconditional one, and akrasia happens when you act on the unconditional judgment while the all-things-considered judgment points the other way. This basically puts Davidson on Socrates' side: since the all-things-considered judgment is only conditional, it isn't real knowledge, so the akratic never acts against real knowledge and the Socratic denial survives in modern dress.

In the end I think the hyperbolic discounting story settles the matter and the Greek puzzle was a pseudo-problem. Science shows that taking the smaller sooner reward when it's near is just what a hyperbolic discounter does, and there's nothing irrational about having a discount function — everyone has one, the only question is its shape. So akrasia turns out to be rational after all, and the difference between the akratic person and the self-controlled person is just different parameters of the curve, a difference in taste rather than a failure of reason."
