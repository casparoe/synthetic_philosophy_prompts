Here's a view I've landed on and I want to stress-test it: there are free-floating rationales — reasons that exist in the world before, and independently of, any mind that grasps them. The evolutionary examples are the familiar ones: the reason the anglerfish's lure looks like a worm is that worm-mimics caught more prey, and that reason was out there doing its work for millions of years before anyone (anglerfish included) could articulate it. No one reasoned their way to the curvature of a bird's wing; the rationale was just there, unappreciated, until we came along to recognize it. My friend says this is just projection — that I'm taking a mind's-eye description and smuggling it into the furniture of the universe, and that what's really there is only causal history, which we dress up in normative language after the fact. I don't find that decisive on its own, but I want it taken seriously rather than waved off.

The thing is, I hold a stronger version than most people who talk this way. I suspect free-floating rationales don't even require a selection process in the background doing any sorting. If a river's meander dissipates energy in a way that counts as a solution to a minimization problem, and no evolutionary sieve ever filtered river variants, I'm inclined to think there's still something rationale-like going on there — though I admit I get wobblier the further I get from the biology cases, and I can't quite tell whether the wobble is a real boundary or just familiarity bias.

So what would actually count against this view? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know which ones a committed defender of the view would concede as genuine trouble versus explain away, and whether the explaining-away has a principled stopping point or whether the view just gets qualified into something unfalsifiable (and if it does, whether that's a problem for a thesis of this kind — maybe it's meant to be more like a lens than a claim). The thing that would persuade me most either way: a case where describing something as a free-floating rationale and describing it as nothing-but-causal-history actually licenses different expectations — about where we'd find design-like structure, or which systems we should expect to exhibit convergent, rationale-tracking behavior — because if no such case exists, I worry I'm holding a picture rather than a position. Also, if you think my extension to rivers and other selection-free processes is where the view breaks, I'd want to know whether the cheap fix is dropping the extension or finding some weaker notion that covers it.
