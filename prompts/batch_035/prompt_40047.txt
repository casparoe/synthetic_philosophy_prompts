I'm a software engineer at a mid-sized company, and for the past year I've been part of a small internal prediction market where engineers and managers bet play-money on things like whether a release will slip, whether a given service will have a page-worthy incident in a quarter, and which of three candidate designs will get picked. The thing that fascinates and slightly disturbs me is that the market is nearly dead: after the first couple of months, volume collapsed to almost zero, and the current prices just sit there. One colleague, who took an economics elective once, says this is exactly what theory predicts — that if everyone is rational and shares a common prior, you should never trade purely because you have different information, since the very act of someone wanting to trade reveals their information and prices adjust until no one wants to trade anymore. He cites what he calls the no-trade theorem and the related result that perfectly informative prices would leave no incentive to gather information in the first place, and concludes our market is pointless and should be shut down. Another colleague says that's nonsense, since financial markets in the real world obviously have enormous volume and clearly do aggregate dispersed information, so the theorems must be saying something much narrower than my first colleague thinks.

I have no formal philosophy or economics training, but I've read enough to know the disagreement turns on things like whether priors are really common, whether people trade for reasons other than information (hedging, liquidity needs, risk aversion), and whether the no-trade result is best read as a knife-edge idealization or as a genuine insight about when speculative trade is a mistake. I'd like an essay on this controversy: what the no-trade results actually establish, what assumptions do the heavy lifting, and whether a market like ours — small, play-money, participants who know each other and talk to each other constantly — sits closer to the theorem's assumptions or further from them than a real financial market does. Reach a verdict: does the theory say our internal market is a waste of effort, or does it explain its silence in some other way (for instance, that the price has already converged and the market has quietly done its job), and should we try to revive trading or accept it? Don't end on a shrug; commit to a position.

Two constraints on the essay. First, since I'm an outsider to the literature, keep the reasoning self-contained: any theorem or assumption you invoke, state it informally and explain why it matters rather than gesturing at a literature I can't check. Second, I'd like an explicit section at the end on what would change your mind: which assumption, if it failed in a specific way, would flip your verdict about our market, and where in the argument you think you're most likely to have gone wrong. If your verdict depends on an empirical claim about how people like my colleagues actually behave — something no theorem can settle — say so plainly rather than smuggling it in as if it followed from the math.
