I'm a graduate teaching assistant running the discussion section for a mid-level ethical theory course (about 20 students, one 75-minute session). The lecture this week covered consequentialism and its critics in the abstract; next week we turn to aggregation — whether and how the number of people at stake should figure in whom we rescue — and I want the students to arrive at that problem from the inside rather than from the reading. Design an in-class activity in which students have to allocate a single rescue resource (one helicopter, one dose of antidote, whichever framing works best) across a series of escalating cases, arranged so that they first feel the pull of counting heads, then feel the pull of refusing to count heads, and finally cannot settle which pull to trust.

My rough sketch, which you should feel free to improve on: early rounds pit one victim against one victim, where the only difference is personal detail, revealed in stages, so the class can see how much the detail is doing; a later round pits one against several, with the details of the several kept deliberately thin; then a round in which a fair lottery over everyone is offered but carries a cost, so students must decide whether equal chances are worth lives forgone; and a final round in which the class must vote as a rescue committee while one student is designated as the individual who will be saved or lost and has to sit there, silent, while the committee deliberates about them. No real money or payoffs; use cards, sealed envelopes, a timer, and role sheets. Participants should not hear the name of any author or position during the activity — they should construct the positions themselves, and only in the debrief should the facilitator attach labels to what they said, including the view that the numbers simply don't count in themselves and that the right response to one-versus-many may be to give each person an equal chance rather than to save the greater number.

For the debrief, I want a mapping from specific moments in the activity to specific claims: the round where personal detail flipped votes, the round where the lottery was rejected because it felt like wasting lives, the moment the designated student watched a committee deliberate about their fate. Then tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — strategic voting, students refusing to choose at all, the designated student playing it for laughs — and how to handle each. Finally, be frank about which feature of the underlying problem the activity structurally cannot reproduce: in particular, whether anything staged in a classroom can mimic the fact that the real choice falls on a single agent who must live with it, rather than on a committee that can share the weight.
