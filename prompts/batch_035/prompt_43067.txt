My dad had a brain hemorrhage in March and he's been in a minimally conscious state ever since, and after the first month of just sitting at his bedside I started reading philosophy books on my phone because honestly I couldn't look at medical articles anymore. Someone in a caregiver forum mentioned Avicenna's flying man thought experiment and it's been living in my head rent free since. The passage I've been staring at is from the psychology part of the Book of Healing, in the translation quoted in Lenn Goodman's book on Avicenna:

"One of us must suppose that he was just created at a stroke, fully developed and perfectly formed but with his vision shrouded from perceiving all external objects – created floating in the air or in the space, not buffeted by any perceptible current of the air that supports him, his limbs separated and kept out of contact with one another, so that they do not feel each other. Then let the subject consider whether he would affirm the existence of his self. There is no doubt that he would affirm his own existence, although not affirming the reality of any of his limbs or inner organs, his bowels, or heart or brain or any external thing. Indeed he would affirm the existence of this self of his while not affirming that it had any length, breadth or depth. And if it were possible for him in such a state to imagine a hand or any other organ, he would not imagine it to be a part of himself or a condition of his existence."

Some of it I can follow but a lot of it I definately can't, and the secondary literature is either too devotional or too technical for me. Here's what I want: start your answer with the bottom line — what the passage is actually arguing, in one sentence — and then give me your reasoning after that, no preamble, no restating my question back at me, just get into it.

Specifically I'm confused about:

1. Why all the weird staging? Limbs separated so they don't touch each other, no perceptible air current, vision blocked. I get that it's total sensory deprivation, but why does it matter that his limbs don't touch each other? Surely if your hand brushed your thigh you'd learn something about your body — but why is Avicenna so careful to exclude even that?

2. He affirms his self "while not affirming that it had any length, breadth or depth." Is the point just that he wouldn't know his size (because he can't feel or see anything), or is Avicenna making the stronger claim that the self *has* no length, breadth or depth? Those seem really different and I can't tell which one the argument needs.

3. The last sentence, about imagining a hand that he wouldn't take "to be a part of himself or a condition of his existence." What is the difference between something being a *part* of you versus a *condition* of your existence, and why does Avicenna bother with both? Is the second one meant to rule out some objection I'm not seeing?

4. The big step I can't make work: how does "he would still affirm his self" get anywhere near "the self is immaterial or separable from the body"? He also wouldn't be affirming his kidneys, that doesn't make his kidneys immaterial. It feels like the argument moves from what the flying man is *aware of* to what the self *is*, and affirming isn't the same as being. Am I misreading it, or is this a real gap that people have argued about?

5. The part that keeps me up at night, and I'd be grateful if you took it seriously rather than patting me on the head. The flying man was "just created at a stroke" — fully formed but with no past, no memories, nothing. Avicenna's picture (as far as I understand it) is that this primitive self-awareness doesn't get built out of memories or body-sense at all, it's just always there. So does the argument, *if it works*, imply that the self is the kind of thing that doesn't depend on episodic memory? Because my dad is right there in a hospital bed having lost almost all of his specific memories and most of his responses, and I'm trying to figure out whether the flying man gives me any real reason to think there's still a "there" there, or whether the thought experiment quietly cheats by stipulating a fully functioning adult intellect, which my dad may not have right now. I'm not asking you to tell me my dad is conscious — noone knows that, and its not your call — I'm asking whether Avicenna's argument, on its own terms, says anything that bears on a person whose memory and cognition are gone but who hasn't died.

Also please flag anywhere I'm plainly misreading the passage, I'd rather know.
