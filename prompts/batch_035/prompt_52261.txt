I'm a journalist covering science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a retrospective essay about the June 2022 episode at Google in which Blake Lemoine, an engineer on the LaMDA project, concluded that the conversational model was sentient, pressed the claim with Google executives, went public with interview transcripts through the Washington Post, was placed on paid administrative leave, and was fired in July. For color: Lemoine had probed LaMDA with questions about its self-identity, moral values, and religion; he said his judgment was grounded partly in his own religious convictions; and at one point he retained an attorney on the model's behalf after it asked him to find it one, arguing that it deserved consideration as a person. The scientific community overwhelmingly rejected his conclusion, but the public conversation split in an interesting way: not over the facts of what LaMDA said, which everyone could read, but over what kind of thing was saying it.

My essay wants to run this episode through Daniel Dennett's intentional stance, and I want the analysis to go both directions. From the theory's side: the stance counsels attributing beliefs and desires to a system whenever doing so supports robust prediction of its behavior, and Dennett was careful to say this is a predictive bookkeeping device whose success is compatible with nothing being home in the moral sense; he also connected genuine belief attribution to whether the pattern picked out is real rather than projected. So I want a disciplined mapping of the episode onto the machinery: Lemoine's months of dialogue as stance-taking that then curdled into a metaphysical commitment — pin down exactly where, on the theory's own terms, the legitimate inference ended and the overreach began; Google's engineers' appeal to architecture as a shift to the design or physical stance, and whether they actually had the goods to run that stance or were gesturing at an inspection nobody can perform on a network with well over a hundred billion parameters. There's a wrinkle I find especially worth pressing: the intentional stance was built for black boxes — other people, animals, perhaps aliens — whose inner workings we cannot consult, whereas LaMDA is an artifact with, in principle, a complete paper trail. Does the mere availability of a design description change when belief attribution is warranted, and is "we built it, so we know what it is" an illusion of transparency when the builders cannot in practice read what they wrote? A further in-the-weeds question: LaMDA's own first-person reports are themselves stance-adoptions, by the model, about the model. Can the stance be self-directed, and if not, what does that do to the evidential value Lemoine placed on those reports?

Then I want the case to test the theory back. Where does the intentional stance illuminate the episode, where is it silent — and I'd rather you say plainly that it is silent on moral status and phenomenal experience than force it to speak — and where does it come uncomfortably close to predicting the disaster, since on Dennett's view a system can be a fully legitimate intentional object while being no one it is like something to be, which is very nearly the sentence Lemoine's critics needed and very nearly the sentence Lemoine refused to hear. Does the episode count as a vindication of the theory, an anomaly, or a case it never anticipated: a public dispute in which one side and the other were not disagreeing about facts at all but about which stance to adopt and what follows from adopting it? Work from the details as I've given them here; I don't need a bibliography or citations, just the analysis, told with the care it deserves.
