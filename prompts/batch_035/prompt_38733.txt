I chair the five-member appeals panel of my university's academic integrity system. Last week we heard a student's appeal of a plagiarism finding, and our regulations direct us to uphold the finding only if the panel concludes both that (i) the contested passages were copied from the identified sources and that (ii) the student understood that she was presenting another's work as her own. On the first question we split 3–2 for copying: the majority found the textual overlap too extensive for coincidence, while the minority thought the overlapping strings were mostly unremarkable factual phrasing. On the second question we also split 3–2, but with a different majority: the same three members found that the student, who drafted the paper in her second language and had never been taught citation conventions, did not understand what the norms required, while the other two found she plainly knew what she was doing. Every member then voted on the bottom line exactly as her two findings dictated, so the finding was rejected 1–4 — even though a majority of us affirmed each element required to uphold it. Our charter says appeals are decided by majority vote of the panel and is silent on whether that means a vote on the bottom line, votes on the elements, or something else; it also gives the chair discretion over how contested issues are framed. Before we issue a decision, two colleagues are circulating a proposed standing rule that all future appeals be decided by majority vote on the elements, since that is how a court would instruct a jury. Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before our Thursday meeting: recommend how we should decide this case and whether to adopt the elements rule, a bottom-line rule, or something else — and if your recommendation is that we deliberate to consensus rather than vote, say what we should do when deliberation deadlocks, as ours effectively did. Engage seriously with the strongest consideration on whichever side you come out against. In particular, whichever rule we choose, someone — an accused student, an accuser, or the office that frames the charge — can profit from how the issues get sliced up, and I want the memo to say something honest about that rather than pretend any rule is neutral.
