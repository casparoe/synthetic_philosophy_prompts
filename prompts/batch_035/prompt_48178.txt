A friend and I spent most of a dinner arguing about underdetermination, and the whole dispute kept circling back to a picture he was leaning on: evidence is like a scatter of data points, a theory is like a curve drawn through them, and since infinitely many curves pass through any finite set of points, the data can never decide between theories. From this he concluded that theory choice is ultimately down to taste, fashion, or ideology, and that scientists who talk about evidence "forcing" a conclusion are fooling themselves. I want the analogy audited properly. First, the correspondence table: what plays the role of the points, the curve, the requirement that the curve pass through every point — and is there anything in the picture at all for background assumptions, measurement error, or the fact that real observations never sit exactly on a theory's predictions the way points sit on an interpolating curve? Second, where the picture breaks: can it represent the difference between a principled theory and an ad hoc one that snakes through the points, the extra credit we give to predictions that were risky when made, or Duhem's point that it's a theory-plus-auxiliaries package that faces the tribunal? And which disanalogies actually matter for his conclusion, as opposed to being quibbles? Finally, tell me whether the anything-goes conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was doing all the work — and if the picture is hopeless, what would be a better analogy for the genuine predicament a scientist faces when the evidence underdetermines the theory.
