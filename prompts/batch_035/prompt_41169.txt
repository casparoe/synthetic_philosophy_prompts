I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a seminar on collective decision-making, and I also serve, as the student representative with full voting rights, on my department's small graduate fellowship committee. Our guidelines state that the committee awards the Whitfield Fellowship to a nominee only if it judges that the nominee (a) shows outstanding research promise, (b) is making satisfactory progress through the program, and (c) has presented a coherent plan for the fellowship year — the award is supposed to be withheld if any one of these conditions fails. Last week we had a single nominee, and we took a recorded vote on each condition separately. Here is how it came out:

Condition (a): two yes, one no.
Condition (b): two yes, one no.
Condition (c): two yes, one no.

The complication — the reason I'm writing — is the pattern behind those tallies. The member who voted no on (a) voted yes on (b) and (c); the member who voted no on (b) voted yes on (a) and (c); and the member who voted no on (c) voted yes on (a) and (b). So every one of the three of us judged the nominee to fail at least one condition, and none of us supports giving her the fellowship. Yet a majority of the committee endorsed each individual condition. Our chair now argues that the committee, majority by majority, has judged the nominee qualified on all three counts, and that the award should simply follow from our own recorded votes. Another member says that's a fiction: the committee never voted for the award, no member supports it, and deriving it from the premise votes would saddle us with a decision none of us made. The guidelines are silent — they list the three conditions but never say whether the final award is to be decided by a direct vote on the award itself or derived from the votes on the conditions. The chair has asked me to circulate a short written opinion before our follow-up meeting next Tuesday recommending a course of action.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher specializing in social choice theory and the rationality of groups, and think carefully before writing. Begin with some brainstorming: set out the possible readings of our guidelines and the possible aggregation rules available to us — deciding by a direct majority vote on the award, deriving the award from the majority votes on the conditions, requiring unanimity or a supermajority somewhere, or something else entirely — and work through what each rule would yield in our case and why anyone might favor it. Then draft the memo itself: a concrete recommendation for Tuesday's meeting, reasons my colleagues could actually engage with rather than dismiss, and an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side. I'd especially like you to address whether my chair's position requires conceiving of the committee as a single collective agent with judgments of its own — a committee-level mind that can endorse a conclusion no individual member endorses — and whether that idea can be defended, or whether the committee is nothing over and above three individuals, only individuals can judge anything, and the premise votes are just three separate opinions that happen to overlap. If your recommendation also has implications for how we should structure our voting in future years, please include them.
