I'm a senior editor at a popular history magazine and our line editors keep having to referee pieces built around non-human causes — a plague that "redrew the map of Europe," a drought behind a revolution, a volcanic winter that topples a dynasty. Some of these pieces are great, others quietly become "the germs did it" storytelling where nobody in the past actually makes a decision. My editors aren't historians and get about half an hour per draft, so vibes-based pushback isn't cutting it.

Write me a procedure they can actually run on a draft: the questions to ask in order (I'm imagining stuff like: is the volcano doing the causal work or just setting the price of grain?, would the writer make the same claim if a human agent were substituted in?), what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure terminates in a "rewrite this paragraph," and where it has to kick the draft back to me because the real issue is a value judgment about which story of the past we're telling, not a factual one. Be honest about the known failure modes — e.g. that the procedure will over-flag good environmental history, and what a cheap second-look pass for that looks like.

Then run it on two drafts: one arguing a decade of cold, wet summers after a major eruption pushed a grain-short empire into civil war, and one arguing plague-driven labor scarcity forced elites to rewrite wage law — where the humans are visibly responding rather than being swept along. I'd also like one worked case where the procedure tells the writer to kill the thesis sentence, since that's usually the sentence the whole pitch was sold on and they'll fight us on it.
