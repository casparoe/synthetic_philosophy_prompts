I teach a graduate seminar on the philosophy of history, and our next unit turns on individual agency versus structural explanation — whether history is driven by persons or by forces, the old question of whether a Hitler-less Germany would still have produced something like the Third Reich. For the seminar's fiction session I've assigned Philip K. Dick's The Man in the High Castle, and I want a careful reading of it rather than a gesture in its direction, because I suspect the novel does something with counterfactuals that neither side of that debate straightforwardly owns.

On its face, the novel's premise is pure Great Man contingency: Giuseppe Zangara's shot kills President-elect Roosevelt in Miami in 1933, there is no New Deal, America stays depressed and isolationist, and the Axis wins the war — a single death, and world history pivots. Yet nearly everything else in the book complicates that. The Grasshopper Lies Heavy, the forbidden novel-within-the-novel, imagines an Allied victory won largely through structural facts — Soviet depth and endurance, Anglo-American industrial capacity — of the sort structural historians cite to argue the Axis was probably doomed anyway; and that inner history is not ours either, so the reader is handed three pasts (ours, the novel's, Abendsen's) with no stable actual against which any of them counts as the deviation. Then there is the oracle: at the end, the I Ching tells Juliana that the inner book is the true one — so within the fiction, our world is the counterfactual, and the counterfactual is the world.

I'd like a reading that takes both the novel and the philosophical debate seriously, and that addresses, roughly: (1) whether Dick's causal chain from one bullet to Axis victory is the kind of chain structural explanation is meant to rule out — the claim that preconditions, not persons, do the world-historical work — or whether the fact that the divergence requires a whole cascade (depression without recovery, sustained isolationism) quietly concedes the structuralist point; (2) what the layering of counterfactuals does to the debate — if there is no fact about which history is the real one, is the question of whether a Nazi Germany without Hitler is even well-posed, or does the novel dramatize how its answer depends on which background you hold fixed?; (3) how to read the scenes that resist the framework, above all Tagomi's slip, via the Edfrank triangle, into a U.S.-governed San Francisco where he is the one treated with racist disdain — a scene that seems to say the deep structures of racism and empire survive either victory, as though the structural facts are precisely what the counterfactual cannot touch; and (4) whether the oracle-and-fate apparatus — Juliana, the I Ching, and the revelation about how Abendsen actually wrote his book — amounts to a third position, contingency inside a frame of destiny, or is simply indifferent to the historiographical question. I want the reading to say what the novel contributes to the debate that argument alone cannot — and to say honestly if the contribution turns out to be not about explanation at all, but about which world we take ourselves to be in.
