A friend of mine has become an enthusiast for lottocracy — filling parliaments by lottery instead of elections — and the case he makes rests on two comparisons he uses as if they settled the matter. I have no academic background; I've just been chewing on this for a while, and I want to know how much weight each comparison can actually bear.

The first is the jury comparison. He points out that we already trust twelve randomly selected strangers to decide whether a person spends decades in prison, which is at least as grave as most legislation. On his telling, a chamber of, say, five hundred randomly chosen citizens setting policy is the same institution with bigger questions and a larger panel — and if twelve is enough for a murder verdict, five hundred is generous.

The second is the sampling comparison. A poll of a thousand people tracks the mood of a whole country because a random sample mirrors the population; so, he says, a selected chamber is a standing poll of the nation, except that it gets to hear evidence and deliberate before answering. The chamber is the country in miniature, and it speaks with the country's own voice rather than a politician's.

Three things about this bother me, and I'd like them dealt with head-on rather than around.

First, the mirroring assumes people show up. I've read that response rates to ordinary polls have collapsed because most people no longer answer, and pollsters now have to correct for exactly who bothers. If a third of those summoned to the chamber declined, we'd have a sample of people willing to serve, not of the nation. Does his answer — pay members well, treat refusal the way we treat skipping jury duty — actually patch this, or does the sample quietly become self-selected in a way elections, for all their faults, are not?

Second, he seems to want it both ways on deliberation. The chamber is legitimate because it mirrors the nation; but it votes after weeks of hearing evidence and argument, which changes people — and the rest of the nation got no such seminar. So the verdict is the nation's view after an education only the sample received. Hasn't the polling comparison broken at exactly the point where he thinks the chamber is most valuable? Is there a version of the mirroring claim that survives deliberation, or must he choose between "it represents us as we are" and "it represents us as we'd be if we were better informed" — two quite different claims?

Third, the jury comparison. Where I live, a jury finds facts — did the thing happen — while a judge steers it toward a verdict the law has already fixed. A policy chamber seems to be choosing ends, not checking whether something occurred. Is that a difference in kind that kills the comparison, or is it less clean than it sounds, given that jurors also bring values into the room?

So set out, for each comparison, what corresponds to what; mark where each mapping breaks; and distinguish the breaks that matter for the lottocracy case from those that don't. Then tell me whether his conclusion survives once the breaks are on the table, or whether the comparisons were carrying it all along. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning — no preamble, and no restating of my question.
