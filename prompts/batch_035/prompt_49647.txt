I serve as a volunteer trustee of a small regional animal welfare charity — my background is in logistics, not philosophy — and I've found myself in the middle of a budget dispute that, I've slowly realized, turns on an idea I've been chewing over privately for years without having a name for it. Our charity has roughly two hundred thousand dollars to allocate next year, and the board is split between two uses. The first is our adoption and enrichment program: play yards, toys, foster support, adoption events. It touches thousands of animals each year and reliably makes each of them somewhat better off, though none of them starts out in terrible shape. The second is a proposal to fund emergency veterinary pain management for the worst cases we encounter — animals with injuries or illnesses our vet frankly calls excruciating. That program is expensive per animal, helps perhaps fifty animals a year, and mostly returns them to ordinary, decent lives rather than joyful ones. One faction on the board argues the arithmetic is simple: total welfare is what matters, thousands of modest improvements outweigh dozens of severe ones, and the money should go to enrichment. The other faction — and privately I'm pulled this way — has been pressing a principle I've long believed on my own: that there's a level of suffering so bad that no quantity of added happiness or comfort elsewhere can balance against it, so our first obligation is to get every animal above that level even if the number helped is small. But I've also started to wonder whether the principle is as workable as it feels. If the worst suffering really does outweigh everything else combined, wouldn't the logical endpoint be that nearly the whole budget belongs with the worst cases — and is a principle that demands that something a board can actually follow? And if the line where pain becomes untradeable is set even slightly too high or too low, does the whole view quietly collapse back into ordinary cost-benefit arithmetic wearing a dramatic label? I've been asked to circulate a written opinion before our November meeting recommending a specific split of the funds between the two programs. Please draft that opinion for me. It should recommend a concrete allocation with the reasoning behind it, take the majority faction's arithmetic seriously rather than dismissing it, and be written plainly enough that trustees with no philosophical background — and a certain impatience with abstraction — can engage with it and respond. Write it as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables: a page or two, the way a thoughtful letter reads.
