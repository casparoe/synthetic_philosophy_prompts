I write historical novels — mostly 19th century stuff, wars and famines, that genre — and I keep hitting the same problem in every single book, usualy about two thirds of the way through, at 11pm, when my editor is already asking for pages.

The problem: every big event I depict has too many causes, and I have to pick which one gets narrated as THE cause. Take the outbreak of WWI. My narrator gets maybe two sentences to say why the war happened. Was it the assassination? The alliance system? The mobilization schedules that turned a crisis into a countdown nobody could stop? If I cram all of it in, it reads like a textbook; if I pick wrong, reviewers either call me a determinist or accuse me of turning something complicated into a story with one villain. Same issue at smaller scales. In the famine book I want the government's policy response to be the villain, but honestly the counterfactual seems to run through the blight itself. And in my current draft the heroine delays a departure by a day, and I want the reader to feel that's why they got caught in the storm — even though I suspect that storm was coming for them no matter what she did.

So: please write a procedure I can run per scene, in a few minutes at my desk, grounded in whatever philosophy of causation and history has actually worked out on this — necessary vs. sufficient causes, Mackie's INUS conditions, Lewis on overdetermination and preemption, the historians' old habit of dividing "causes" from mere "background conditions." I need the questions to ask in order, what counts as an answer good enough that I can write the sentence, where the procedure stops and hands the choice back to me as a judgment call — and when it hands it back, I want it to say what I'm really choosing between (like, whose story it is, not which fact is true). Then run it on my three cases above, including the heroine one — I have a feeling the procedure will give me an answer I don't like there, and I'd rather know now than from a review.

Last thing: tell me where this whole procedure is most likely to misfire on me, and what would change your mind about the verdicts — i.e., which step, if some philosopher or historian turned out to be right about something I'm glossing over, would collapse the whole thing.
