I convene a weekly seminar for graduate students in logic and epistemology, and this term's running theme has been the knowability paradox — the result, first published by Fitch in 1963 and now known to descend from Church's 1945 referee report, that the knowability principle ("every truth is possibly knowable") entails its own collapse into "every truth is actually known." For our final session I want a decision tree that sorts participants by which response to the paradox they actually hold, as revealed by what they are prepared to say about cases, rather than by which response they profess to favor. Please build it under the following constraints.

The tree begins with a respondent who accepts some version of the knowability principle — someone drawn to the thought that truth cannot outrun possible knowledge — and routes them to a leaf naming their position. Every internal node must be a question the respondent can answer without knowing which leaf it leads to; the questions should be concrete claims or cases on which the positions genuinely divide, never questions about labels or terminology. The tree must at minimum contain branches corresponding to the following decision points, though you may add others:

1. Whether the respondent reads the principle's modal and negation vocabulary classically or intuitionistically — whether they accept the classical inferential steps the derivation uses, or regard the anti-realist's commitment as formulated in intuitionistic logic so that the derivation never gets traction. The leaf here is the Dummett-style position that semantic anti-realism was never committed to the classical reading of the principle.

2. Whether the principle applies to every truth whatsoever or only to some restricted class, with sub-branches for the two main restrictions in the literature: Tennant's restriction to so-called Cartesian propositions (those whose negations are not already known), and the indexical reformulation associated with Edgington, on which what is knowable is not the bare proposition p but p as indexed to the present situation. The diagnostic question here should be a concrete case — for instance, whether the conjunction of some unnoticed truth with the claim that it is never known could itself ever be known — rather than a question about which restriction the respondent finds natural.

3. Whether knowledge distributes over conjunction: whether someone who knows that p and q thereby knows that p. A leaf is needed for the respondent who denies this, together with one sentence on what the denial costs them outside the paradox.

4. A leaf for respondents who simply accept the conclusion that all truths are already known. Please state honestly the most awkward commitment this saddles them with, and whether it can be made to look like anything other than a reductio.

5. Somewhere, the question of what "knowable by" means: knowable by some possible individual, by the community at some time, or only at the limit of inquiry. The theorem's conclusion changes strength across these readings, and I want the tree to route people accordingly rather than letting respondents who differ on this point land at the same leaf for different reasons.

Leaves should carry the name of the position where it has a defender in the literature, and "unnamed" where the combination of answers has no defender, with one sentence diagnosing whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Ten to fourteen node questions in total. Finally, please flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate: I suspect the Dummett-style intuitionist and the Edgington-style indexer will be hard to pull apart with case questions, and I would rather be told what question, if any, splits them — or that none does — than have the difficulty papered over. Under no circumstances should any node ask anything of the form "are you a verificationist?"
