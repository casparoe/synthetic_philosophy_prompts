My partner and I have been arguing about the socialist calculation debate, and I want to know where our disagreement actually lives before I take it any further. The proposition, stated so it has a truth value: in a hypothetical planned economy whose planners are perfectly benevolent (no principal-agent problems, no falsified statistics, no careerism, no nomenklatura privileges) and which has effectively unlimited computing power, a central plan could allocate capital goods across a decade at least as efficiently as a competitive market would have done in the same initial conditions. I put the probability of that at about 0.4; she puts it around 0.15, mostly because she thinks the twentieth century already ran the experiment. I think she's smuggling the incentive problem back into a question I've stipulated it out of.

Please give me your own credence, but what I really want is the decomposition, with a rough probability and one line of justification for each term. At minimum I'd want you to take a stance on: whether Hayek's dispersed-knowledge argument is about information that exists but is costly to collect, or about tacit knowledge that cannot be articulated even in principle; whether an iterative procedure of the kind Lange proposed, or a modern demand-forecasting system of the kind large retailers actually use to coordinate continent-spanning supply chains, could in principle converge on something close to market-clearing prices; and whether the problem is ultimately innovation rather than calculation — that is, whether planning for products that do not yet exist is not merely hard but ill-posed, since there is no demand data for the not-yet-invented. Add any other terms you think belong, and note explicitly where you think my stipulations are doing illegitimate work — for instance, whether benevolence without local knowledge is even coherent, and whether abstracting away incentives makes the question philosophically clean or just vacuous.

Then tell me two further things: which single term the final number is most sensitive to, and what evidence or argument would move that term the most — say what would count, even roughly, as the question resolving one way rather than the other, whether by simulation, natural experiment, or something else. Finally, given your decomposition, say whether my 0.4 or her 0.15 is better explained by a disagreement about the facts and mechanisms, or by a disagreement about what the stipulations permit us to ignore. Keep the whole answer under 450 words, numbers first, justifications compressed to their single best line.
