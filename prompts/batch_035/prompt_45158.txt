You are a philosopher of history with deep expertise in twentieth-century German historiographical debates, in particular the Historikerstreit and the wider literature on the uniqueness and comparability of the Holocaust, and you have a sideline in designing diagnostic instruments for public humanities education.

I run training for new docents at a Holocaust memorial museum. Our docents constantly field visitor questions along the lines of: Wasn't Stalin worse? Isn't every genocide unique in its own way, so calling the Holocaust unique is just special pleading? Haven't human beings always done this sort of thing? Is it even possible to explain the Holocaust without somehow excusing it? These questions are rarely asked in bad faith, but the same visitor will often give answers that point toward incompatible underlying positions without noticing. Before we teach the content, I want the docents to be able to figure out where a visitor's questions are actually coming from.

Please write me a diagnostic quiz, titled something like Which view of the Holocaust do you actually hold? The quiz should have eight to ten questions, each presenting a concrete case or claim — a proposed museum panel text, an exam question a teacher might write, a comparative statement a visitor might make — with two or three answer options apiece. No question may simply ask the visitor to pick a label or self-identify; every question must be answerable by someone who has never heard of any of the positions or the Historikerstreit.

Crucially, I want the quiz built from the logical space, not from a list of famous names. Please first construct the space out of four separating questions, roughly: (1) Is the Holocaust unique in kind, or only in degree, or not even that? (2) Is comparison with other atrocities enlightening, relativizing, or contextually fine depending on intent and frame? (3) Is the causal center of gravity in specifically German history, in general features of modernity like bureaucracy and instrumental rationality, in a specific ideology, or in something like recurring features of human nature? (4) Does explaining the crime risk normalizing or excusing it? Then locate the actual positions in that space as the quiz's scoring-key leaves: a Nolte-style causal-nexus position, a Habermas-style view on which the Holocaust's uniqueness grounds rather than undermines a postnational identity, Bauman's modernity thesis, the strict uniqueness-in-kind view associated with Katz and Fackenheim, Arendt's banality-of-evil reading, a functionalist-structuralist position, and a comparative-genocide position that treats the Holocaust as one instance of a recurring phenomenon.

For each leaf, give one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Flag explicitly which pairs of leaves your questions cannot reliably separate — for instance, the Habermas leaf and the Katz leaf both say unique, and if your questions only probe uniqueness they will blur. Also note any combination of answers that comes out logically coherent but corresponds to no defended position, and say whether you think that combination is unoccupied because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Please think carefully before writing. Start by brainstorming candidate diagnostic questions, discard the ones that fail the label-blindness requirement, and only then assemble the final quiz and scoring key. Keep the tone measured and precise: this will be used with lay audiences in a sensitive setting, so every question must be phrased so that no answer option is baited to look monstrous.
