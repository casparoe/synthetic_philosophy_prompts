I'm a novelist, and for the last two years I've been writing a book about two old friends who can't stop arguing: a mathematician who insists that when he proves something about the primes he's discovering facts that were already there, waiting, before anyone existed, and a former poet turned editor who thinks mathematics is the most successful game of make-believe humans have ever invented — useful fictions, like characters in a novel, that happen to run the world. I gave the poet the usual lines (we made the symbols up, different notations yield the same facts, dinosaurs didn't do arithmetic), and I gave the mathematician his (nobody chose that there are infinitely many primes, wildly different cultures converge on the same results, and the feeling of resistence — you can't just make a theorem come out how you'd like, the way I can make my characters do anything).

Here's my problem. I originally planned to let the argument resolve — one of them wins, the other concedes; it's a novel, I get to decide the ending. But the more I've read around, the more it looks like in real life nobody has moved an inch in roughly a century. The platonists apparently have to explain how we meat-and-causal creatures could ever get in touch with things that, on their own account, sit outside space and time and can't nudge our brains — I gather someone called Benacerraf put this as a dilemma back in the seventies and it's still the standard stick. The nominalists meanwhile have to explain how physics gets done at all if you refuse to take the numbers literally, and the one serious attempt I heard of — rewriting science without numbers, by someone called Field — apparently only covered Newtonian gravity and left the rest of physics untouched. Both sides seem happy to concede that the other's problem is real, publish another reply, and carry on exactly as before.

So what I want from you is not a survey and not a verdict on who's right — I can invent the ending myself either way. What I want is a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, because I suspect it determines how my novel is allowed to end, or whether it can end at all. Is this a substantive dispute about what exists, or is it partly verbal — the two sides meaning different things by 'exists' or by 'object'? Is there a hidden empirical question underneath (if physics ever finished its job, would the dispute dissolve?), or is the real difference about what each side counts as needing an explanation — the platonist demanding an account of mathematical objectivity, the nominalist demanding an account of causal contact? Given whatever diagnosis you land on, tell me honestly whether anything would count as progress — a theorem, a completed nominalisation of physics, some new conceptual distinction — or whether I should accept that the argument between my two characters is structurally endless and write the ending that way instead. And keep in mind I can't go check sources, so if you bring in names like Benacerraf or Field, I only need the shape of the point, not bibliographic detail.
