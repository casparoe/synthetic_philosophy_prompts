I'm a software engineer with no philosophy background, and over fifteen years of writing code -- especially the stretch where I was getting a formal verification toolchain to actually pass on a distributed-systems codebase -- I've drifted into mathematical platonism: the integers, groups, and the theorems about them seem to be out there waiting, the way an unsurveyed mountain pass is there before anyone crosses it. Refactoring a legacy codebase feels like archaeology, and when two teams independently converge on the same data structure under the same constraints, it feels like discovery rather than parallel invention. I also put weight on the fact that the same theorem keeps surfacing in wildly different formalisms, which strikes me as evidence that formalisms are just notation for something independent of us. What would count against this view? I want the observations, arguments, intuitions, and formal results that should move me, drawn from philosophy but also from the actual practice of mathematics and software, sorted from merely awkward to decisive -- plus an honest account of which of these a committed platonist would concede as counting against the view and which they would wave away, and whether a view like this has been qualified into unfalsifiability and whether that's a problem for it. What I'd personally find most persuasive is a concrete picture of what mathematics would look like if it were invented rather than discovered -- for instance, whether alien mathematicians with very different cognitive hardware could have a genuinely different but equally successful mathematics -- so tell me what, if anything, my view has at risk on that question.
