I'm an undergrad in a decision theory / rational choice seminar, and in two weeks I have to present the position that the sunk cost fallacy isn't always a fallacy — that there are real situations where honoring sunk costs is rational, or at least not irrational. The audience is mostly econ majors who've already taken behavioral economics (so they've seen the sunk cost stuff a hundred times and think it's settled), plus the professor, who is a pretty hardcore Bayesian and keeps asking everyone to write their arguments as decision trees, and one grad TA who loves the classic experiment with the discounted theater season tickets and always brings it up like it decides everything.

My sketch of the argument so far, so the objections have something to target: (1) past investment is sometimes genuine evidence about future payoffs you have no other access to — if I've sunk five years into a PhD that's information about my fit for it even if it came through a sunk cost channel; (2) predictable regret counts in expected utility, and quitting right after big investments predictably produces worse regret than quitting earlier, so timing matters even when the investment itself is unrecoverable; (3) self-trust — I treat my future selves as an audience, and bailing whenever things get expensive trains me into someone whose commitments nobody, including me, should believe; (4) relatedly, the standard advice ("treat sunk costs as sunk") evaluates each choice one-off, but a policy of never honoring sunk costs is itself a policy you can evaluate, and it might be a bad one. Yes I know some of these slide into each other, that's part of why I'm nervous.

So what will they actually throw at me? The econ kids will say none of this touches the basic opportunity cost argument, the prof will demand I formalize the self-trust story as a tree (I genuinely don't know if I can), and someone always brings up the Concorde like it's a mic drop. Prepare me like it's a Q&A prep sheet: the objections in roughly the order I'll get them, phrased the way this specific audience would phrase them, a short reply to each, what I should just concede up front so I don't look evasive, and where each reply is soft. Include the one question I'm hoping nobody asks. Also — and the TA will pounce on this if I don't — please be honest about which parts of my argument are basically settled versus genuinely contested among people who actually work on this stuff. I don't want to pass off a contested claim as consensus in front of a room that knows the litterature better than I do.
