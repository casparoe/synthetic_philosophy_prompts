I've never studied philosophy — I drive a delivery van and do most of my thinking between stops — but last winter I picked up a cheap second-hand copy of Pascal's Pensées, and one of his ideas has been rattling around in my head ever since. Pascal seems to think God deliberately stays out of plain sight: that God hides from people who are full of themselves but lets himself be found by those who earnestly search for him, and that our wretchedness without God is itself part of the message. I gather from later reading that after Pascal's time, in the 1990s, a philosopher named Schellenberg turned this whole territory into a serious argument against God's existence — the so-called argument from divine hiddenness. As I understand it, the crux is that a perfectly loving God would make sure no one ever ended up in a position where they genuinely wanted a relationship with him but, through no fault or stubbornness of their own, simply couldn't believe he exists. And yet there seem to be plenty of such people — honest seekers who just never get there. Their nonbelief is, as I've seen it put, nonresistant.

What I'd like from you is a prediction of how Pascal himself would have taken this argument had someone set it before him. He obviously can't have known it in its modern form, so I'm asking you to reason from what he did write: the idea that there's enough light for those who wish to see and enough obscurity for those with the opposite disposition, the famous wager about betting on God's existence even under uncertainty, and his general picture of God as a hidden God who reveals and conceals at will. Would Pascal have said the sincere-but-unable nonbelievers haven't truly searched, or that their pride is subtler than they realise? Would he have leaned on the wager, effectively telling them to act as if God exists and let belief catch up? Or is there something in his thought that would make him actually sympathetic to the complaint — the sense that God's hiding is hard on honest people — even if he wouldn't follow the argument to its conclusion? I'd also like you to flag where you're extrapolating beyond anything Pascal actually said, so I can tell the difference between his views and your reconstruction of them.

Please keep the whole answer under 500 words, and treat it as your best-informed guess rather than settled fact — I'd rather have one careful, hedged reconstruction than a confident list of claims dressed up as his.
