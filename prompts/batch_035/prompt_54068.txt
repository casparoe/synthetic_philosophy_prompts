I write novels for a living (midlist fantasy, nothing you've read), and last month at a writer's retreat two friends of mine — Dev, who's been building the same secondary world for eleven years, and Mara, who edits other people's doorstoppers for a big imprint — had an argument I can't stop chewing on, and they finally told me to settle it. Fat chance, but maybe you can.

It started with worldbuilding. Dev's thing is that every detail in his world has to have a reason: the moon is red because of the dust in the upper air, the dust is there because the gods corpse is decomposing, the corpse is there because of the war in heaven, and so on. Mara said that's a road to nowhere, because reasons bottom out eventually and the "bottom" is always just something the author declares — the war in heaven just IS, at some point you're simply asserting. So the real test, she said, isn't whether each detail stands on a deeper one, but whether the whole thing hangs together: a detail earns its place if the rest of the world implies it and it implies the rest. The reader never sees foundations anyways, only the fit.

Dev said that's circular, and he had a concrete jab ready: in Mara's own book (the one she swears she'll finish), the sea-clans fear the forest because the forest takes their children, the forest takes children because the clans burned it in the old war, and they burned it because they feared it — he said her world is a ring holding itself up, and if nothing in it ever touches the ground, nothing in it is held up at all. Mara didn't flinch. She said a circle is only vicious if it's small — the reader checks each detail against its neighbors, never the whole world at once — and honestly, even in the real world, courts and history books and science are mostly mutual support: nobody has ever actually seen one of Dev's bedrock facts, people just get tired of asking why and stop there.

Then Dev did something unexpected. Instead of defending bedrock, he gave it up and said fine, maybe the answer is neither: you just never stop. Every fact has a reason and that reason has a reason, and the regress doesn't need to end because no reader can walk it to the end anyway. He'd had wine and called it iceberg worldbuilding: the surface is justified by the layer below, which is justified by the layer below that, and you keep going until no reader could ever reach the floor. Mara said that's a con: an iceberg still has a bottom, and a promise that there's always one more layer is not the same as there being one — you can't actually write infinite backstory, so his position is just one of ours with the ugly part hidden, and worse, it's unfakeable-proof: there's no world deep enough that you couldn't fake the depth cues, so what's he even claiming.

Dev's last word was that Mara's bedrock is worse than his ladder, because at least his regress hands the reader a reason at every step they actually take, whereas her fiat facts and tight circles give reasons only until you notice the shape of the whole thing. Mara's last word was something like: the shape IS the reason, and demanding a floor under a story is like demanding a floor under the ocean.

They asked me to referee on the grounds that I supposedly understand how stories work, and I honestly bounced off all of it. Can you go through their exchange and tell me who's right at each step — including whether Dev's pivot mid-argument counts for or against him — and whether there's something both of them are missing entirely?
