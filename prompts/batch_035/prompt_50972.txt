I'm a graduate teaching assistant for a large introductory evolution course, and last weekend I got into a long argument with my friend Marcus about how to explain organismal traits. Marcus is a committed adaptationist in the everyday sense: ask him why human blood is red, why we get fevers, why deer have spots as fawns, and he will immediately give you a just-so story about what each feature is for, and when I pushed back he'd just refine the story rather than question the approach. I kept invoking Gould and Lewontin's famous 1979 spandrels paper, pointing out that some traits are byproducts of other adaptations rather than adaptations in their own right, but our argument kept sliding past each other because we were using the word adaptationist to mean at least three different things: a claim that most traits are in fact adaptations, a claim that adaptive explanation is the most useful default research strategy, and a personal habit of reaching for a function story first. By the end I realized neither of us could cleanly say which of these we actually disagreed about, which is exactly the confusion I see in my students' discussion sections.

So I'd like you to help me build a one-page taxonomy for my students, structured as a decision tree about a single focal question: why does this trait exist in the form it has? Please build the tree from the logical space first rather than from a list of famous names. That is, first decide what the branching questions are, in an order where each one can be answered by a student who has never heard the technical terms — something like whether the trait would still be roughly there if selection on it were somehow switched off, or whether the thing the trait now does is the same thing that originally got selected, or whether the trait's form follows from some structural or developmental constraint on the organism rather than from anything that happened to it historically. Then locate the leaves in that space. The leaves I need represented are: a direct adaptation; an exaptation, where the structure was built by selection for one job and later co-opted for another; a spandrel or byproduct, where the trait is a side effect of selection on something else; a product of genetic drift, where the form reflects sampling accident rather than selection; and a product of constraint, where the form reflects the body plan or allometric relationships the lineage was stuck with. If your tree generates other logical combinations, include them and note whether anyone actually defends them or whether they're unoccupied — and say briefly whether you think that's because they're incoherent, empirically empty, or just unexplored.

Two specific requirements. First, flag the pairs of leaves your tree genuinely cannot separate: I'm worried in particular that exaptation versus direct adaptation with a shifted function will collapse together, that weak selection and drift may be indistinguishable at most branch points, and that a byproduct of an adaptation and a byproduct of a constraint can look identical from the trait's own perspective. Tell me honestly where the instrument fails rather than papering over it. Second, add a short closing note for the handout distinguishing, for each leaf, whether accepting it is an empirical claim about what happened in a lineage or a methodological recommendation about where a researcher should start looking — the thing Marcus and I failed to keep apart. Assume the audience is first-year students with no philosophy background, so every branch question should be answerable from ordinary reasoning about a concrete case, not from knowing the jargon. Please do not pad the handout with reading lists or citations; I just want the tree, the leaf notes, and the closing paragraph.
