A book I picked up on the philosophy of language summarizes David Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals like this: "In the 1970s, Lewis proved that ordinary-language conditionals cannot have truth conditions. The argument runs as follows. Start with a principle Lewis himself had proposed: the probability of the conditional 'if A, then B' equals the conditional probability of B given A. Lewis showed that if this principle holds across a probability space rich enough to contain a few independent propositions, then every proposition in that space must have probability 0 or 1 — which is absurd for anyone with partial beliefs. Since conditionals therefore cannot be propositions, they cannot be true or false. And the same argument applies to counterfactual conditionals: Lewis took his result to show that his own possible-worlds semantics for counterfactuals had rested on a mistake." The ending strikes me as suspicious, since I thought Lewis was famous for defending possible-worlds semantics, but I don't know the literature well enough to audit the rest. Please go through the summary claim by claim: which parts would a logician working on conditionals accept as correct, which are simplifications of what Lewis actually proved, and which are outright misattributions or errors? In particular, I'd like to know whether the conclusion that conditionals lack truth values follows from anything Lewis himself established, or whether that is a further move that others made in response to his results.
