I'm a program officer at a small regional foundation whose charter commits us to funding "scientific research and public understanding of science." We review perhaps forty proposals a quarter, most from independent researchers, citizen-science groups, and small nonprofits, and we have no scientific advisory board — just me and three trustees, none of whom have a philosophy of science background. A recurring problem is that we receive proposals that occupy the gray zone: rigorous-looking studies of questions with a pseudoscientific reputation, poorly designed studies of respectable questions, and theoretical programs that never seem to make contact with evidence. The trustees have asked me for a defensible way to draw the line, and I'd rather ground it in the actual demarcation literature than in my instincts.

Please write a decision procedure we could actually run on an incoming proposal: the questions to ask in a fixed order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, at which points the procedure terminates with a recommendation to decline, and — critically — which steps are genuinely mechanical versus which are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, so that when a trustee asks me to justify a decision I can say honestly where I exercised discretion. The procedure should be built around what falsificationism actually says — risky predictions, prohibitions, the response to refutation — but it should also incorporate what that view gets wrong or leaves open: the way auxiliary assumptions let a theory absorb failed predictions, the difference between a research program that is merely unproductive and one that is unscientific, and the fact that some clearly legitimate sciences (historical sciences, early-stage theory) don't fit the falsificationist template well. Where the philosophy is genuinely contested, say so and give us a default rule anyway.

Then run the procedure on three proposals of the kind we actually see. First, a properly designed, preregistered, randomized controlled trial of a homeopathic remedy for a self-limiting condition, submitted by a licensed clinician with an adequate sample size calculation. Second, a request for three years of funding for a small theoretical physics group whose program has produced mathematically sophisticated results but no experimentally testable predictions in a decade. Third, a citizen-science project proposing to test whether pet dogs can anticipate their owners' epileptic seizures, run by volunteers with mentorship from a university statistics department. For each, show the procedure's output step by step, and identify any case where the procedure gives an answer I or the trustees are likely to find unwelcome, explaining whether that discomfort reveals a flaw in the procedure or a bias in us.

Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do — the cases it will misclassify or leave unresolved, and where I simply have to make a value judgment and own it in the memo to the trustees. Keep the whole thing usable by someone with no philosophy background; the trustees will read only the procedure itself, not the theory behind it.
