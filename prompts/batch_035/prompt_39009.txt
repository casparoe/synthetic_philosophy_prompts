I write novels, and I have come to hold a view that a philosopher friend assures me is a metaphysical howler: there is exactly one thing on the plinth, not two. The statue simply is the clay, shaped; speaking of the statue and the clay it is made of is a manner of talking, not a report of two objects. My reasons are partly craft. In my current manuscript a sculptor reworks a failed bust into a torso, and I have found that the narration gets cleaner and, I want to say, truer if I never pretend there were two things there at any point. The view also sits comfortably with how I think about my own characters, which I take to be made things: if the statue were a second object over and above the clay, conjured into existence by the sculptor's act, I would owe an account of what a character is made of, and I suspect the honest answer is that it is made of nothing — which rather embarrasses the analogy I lean on. But I am aware that almost no professional metaphysician will let me have this. The standard argument is that the clay can survive being squashed into a ball while the statue cannot, so the two cannot be identical; and there is the cases where one thing is shaped into a statue while allegedly remaining, alongside it, a distinct lump. Tell me what would genuinely count against my one-object view, and treat it as a live position rather than a beginner's error. I want the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward: which cases from the studio, which arguments, and which broader commitments about persistence and modality the view stands or falls with. Say which of these a defender of my view would concede as telling and which they would dismiss, and be frank about whether the standard qualification strategies — treating the statue as a phase or a way of counting rather than a further thing — amount to insulating the view from any possible refutation, and, if so, whether that is a vice or simply what an austere ontology looks like. Please write in British English, and answer in continuous prose only, as in a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables.
