At a family dinner last week, my cousin — a committed nationalist in the philosophical rather than the political-party sense — defended the claim that compatriots have special claims on one another by analogy: the nation is a family writ large. Feeding your own children before strangers' children is not partiality in any vicious sense; it is simply what it is to be a parent. Likewise, he argued, giving priority to compatriots in welfare spending — even when distant strangers are far worse off — is not bias to be apologized for but the ordinary shape of a special relationship. He leans on three features of the analogy in particular: family obligations are not consented to (one does not sign a contract with one's siblings), they are not conditional on the recipients' merit (one does not audit one's relatives' deservingness before helping them), and they involve a shared budget that is ours rather than yours or mine. He then runs the analogy in both directions: the first two features are meant to defeat the cosmopolitan's demand that obligations be either consented to or owed to humanity at large, while the third is meant to recast foreign aid as generosity rather than duty. I would like a careful audit of this analogy. Please set out the correspondence table — what is meant to play the roles of the parent, the siblings, the household budget, and the front door — and then identify where the mapping breaks, separating the disanalogies that matter for his intended uses (justifying priority to compatriots, and downgrading duties to outsiders) from those that do not. Along the way, consider whether the analogy proves too much, licensing priority to compatriots even in trivial cases where we would not accept such priority within a family; whether it can explain why compatriots who despise each other, or share nothing but a passport, still owe each other anything; and whether it leaves the cosmopolitan with any account of why the rich owe the distant poor anything at all beyond optional charity. Finally, once the disanalogies are on the table, does his conclusion survive? Can the special claims of compatriots be defended on some other footing — say, shared institutions, cooperative schemes, or a common culture — that the analogy was never needed to supply? If a better analogy is available for his position, state what it is and whether it carries the conclusion more securely than the family did.
