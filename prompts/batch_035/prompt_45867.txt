I am a novelist by trade, and I sit on the interpretation committee of our provincial history museum, which is preparing a permanent gallery on the famine that struck this region in the late 1890s. I have been asked to circulate a decision memo before our meeting next month, and I would like you to draft it.

The committee is deadlocked over a single sentence of wall text. Draft A, favored by the chair and the government liaison, reads: "Between 1896 and 1898, drought brought famine to the province; tens of thousands died before the rains returned." Draft B, written by two of our younger curators, reads: "Between 1896 and 1898, famine policy failed the province's poor; grain continued to leave by rail while wages collapsed, and tens of thousands died preventable deaths." The trustees who back A say it is factual and restrained; they also fear, though they have not said so bluntly, that B points an accusing finger at the colonial administration and, by extension, at families like our principal donors', one of whose ancestors was a district relief officer. The curators who back B say that A quietly absolves everyone and that our own archive will not support it.

I should tell you what the archive contains, since the memo must reason from it. The rainfall figures show a real but moderate deficit in the worst-hit districts — poor harvests, not annihilated ones. Provincial grain exports continued throughout, though not at famine-bounty levels. Rural wages fell much faster and earlier than grain prices rose, which means laborers' capacity to buy food collapsed even where food was visibly present in the markets. Relief works opened late, and their stated rationale — that wage rates must stay below market wages to discourage dependence — kept the weakest applicants from reaching them at all. Mortality concentrated among landless laborers and itinerant artisans, not among landholding cultivators.

Here is my own bias, which you should know and may discount: I have spent twenty years deciding when a sentence assigns agency, and the difference between our two drafts is almost entirely grammatical. In A, drought is the subject and the verb is intransitive; in B, policy is the subject and the verb is transitive. I keep thinking of the argument Amartya Sen made about Bengal in 1943 — that famines are failures of people's entitlement to food rather than simple shortages of it — and I want the memo to test that idea against our archive rather than merely invoke his name.

Please draft the memo, no more than nine hundred words, recommending a concrete decision: adopt A, adopt B, or propose a third text you actually supply. The reasoning should face the entitlement question squarely — was this, on the evidence above, a food-availability failure, an entitlement failure, or something the distinction does not capture? It should give the strongest fair treatment to the losing side, including the trustees' legitimate worry that a gallery can become a courtroom. And it should close with criteria our curators can apply to the remaining two hundred labels in the gallery, since whatever we do with this sentence, the problem of who gets to be the subject of the verb will come up again before lunch.
