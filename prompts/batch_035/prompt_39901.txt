You are an experienced examiner in formal epistemology, and I'd like your help constructing the centerpiece question for my seminar's final exam. The seminar is an upper-level course on self-locating belief and anthropic reasoning (we've covered conditionalization, Dutch book arguments, van Fraassen's Reflection principle, and the halfer/thirder debate over Sleeping Beauty as it has run since Adam Elga's original paper and David Lewis's reply). The exam is 90 minutes, and this question carries 25 marks. I'm deliberately not asking for a "compare and evaluate the halfer and thirder positions" question, because my students can recite that in their sleep. Here is the material I want the question built around, in three parts. First, my own variant of the setup, which I want stated in full in the question so it is self-contained: alongside the standard Sleeping Beauty protocol (fair coin Sunday night; Heads means one awakening Monday, Tails means awakenings Monday and Tuesday with amnesia in between), there is an outside observer, Bookie, who never sees the coin. After the experiment ends, Bookie is shown a video of exactly one awakening, chosen uniformly at random from all the awakenings that occurred, and is asked for his credence in Heads. Second, the settlement asymmetry: a bet on Heads can be offered to Beauty under two regimes — settled once per experiment, or placed and settled once per awakening (so a Tails run means two payoffs). Third, the two diagnostic problems: the thirder's apparent violation of Reflection (Sunday-Beauty, knowing she will wake with credence 1/3, must apparently expect her own future self to "irrationally" diverge from a credence of 1/2 today), and the halfer's problem with being told the day (the move to the double-halfer position, holding credence 1/2 on waking but 2/3 conditional on learning it is Monday, which requires treating temporal indexical evidence as somehow non-standard when conditionalized upon).

Please think carefully before writing, and brainstorm a few possible framings before committing to one — I want the framing that best forces engagement with my material rather than with the textbook debate. Then deliver three things.
