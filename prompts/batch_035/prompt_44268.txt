As a science journalist I'm writing a long piece on whether modern physics supports the idea that the future already exists. The popular physics book I'm drawing on explains special relativity with a loaf of bread: spacetime is the whole loaf, and observers moving relative to one another slice it at different angles, so that an event lying in the future of one slicer lies in the present of another. The book then concludes — following what philosophers of physics call the Rietdijk–Putnam argument — that past, present, and future are all equally real, a "block universe" in which becoming is an illusion. A physicist I interviewed for the piece objects that the angled slicing is merely an artifact of coordinates, like the way a map's lines of longitude converge toward the poles without anything on the ground being squashed, and that nothing in relativity forces the block conclusion. I'd like a careful audit of the loaf analogy. First, the correspondence table: what plays the role of the loaf, the knife, the angle, the observer. Then, where the mapping breaks — which features of an actual loaf it quietly imports that spacetime lacks, and which disanalogies matter for the block-universe conclusion versus which are incidental. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion the analogy was deployed to support survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether, as my physicist source claims, the knife was doing all the work — and if a better analogy for relativistic relativity of simultaneity is available, offer it. Please write your response in American English.
