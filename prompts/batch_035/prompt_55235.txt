There's a wrinkle from the seminar I'd especially like you to address. My professor suggested that the paradox is not fundamentally about infinity at all, but about what we might call expectations dominated by events of vanishing probability: even in a game with a huge but finite expected value carried almost entirely by outcomes you're near-certain never to see, the same tension between expectation and rational choice shows up, just weaker. Relatedly, I know there's a variant — I've seen it called the super St. Petersburg game — where the payoffs grow fast enough that even Bernoulli's logarithmic utility leaves the expectation divergent, so the marginal-utility fix can't be the whole story, and I've heard that truncating at the bankroll's actual resources has its own awkward feature: the fair price then tracks the house's wealth rather than anything about the gamble's structure, which seems to get something backwards.

Throughout, I need you to keep a clear running distinction between what is broadly settled among decision theorists and what remains genuinely contested — for instance, I take it the claim that rational utilities must be bounded is still disputed, and I don't want a contested position smuggled in as consensus.
