I serve with a colleague, G., on my small city's commemoration committee, and after three years of listening to him vote on anything having to do with the town's past, I want to be able to predict his vote before he opens his mouth. G. is an avowed admirer of Nietzsche's essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, and he likes to say that the only justification for any dealings with the past is whether they serve life. His track record, as best I can reconstruct it:

- He voted to fund the antiquarian society's complete cataloguing and restoration of the town's oldest buildings and artifacts exactly as they were, down to the original clashing paint colors, and spoke warmly of the debt the living owe to what has been.
- He voted to let a motivational-education company run programs in the schools that package the founders and other great figures as exemplars of daring, even though their materials simplify and occasionally falsify; he said the effect on the children was what mattered and the distortions were a fair price for it.
- He supported the council's formal condemnation and symbolic repudiation of the town's role in a historical atrocity, saying that a people sometimes has to break with its past simply in order to breathe.
- He opposed, on two separate occasions, funding a meticulously balanced, exhaustive academic history of the town, calling it embalming — the kind of knowledge that leaves people knowing more and doing less.
- He approved of the genealogical society's research into local family lines, but went cold when the same findings were assembled into a list of grievances supporting a compensation claim; he said the work had soured reverence into resentment.

Next month we vote on three proposals:

(a) A heritage foundation's offer to restore the founder's statue and its surrounding square to their exact 1890s condition — which would mean undoing a century of well-intentioned alterations, including a plaque added in the 1950s that recast the founder's conduct during a morally ugly episode of the founding as heroism.

(b) A studio's video game set in our founding era, wildly inaccurate, but by all accounts thrilling, and reportedly leaving adolescent players fired up about the town's history for the first time in a generation.

(c) A historian's commissioned demolition of the founder myth, arguing that the founder was largely a fraud, undertaken at the request of a civic group that says the myth infantilizes the town and that they want to be done with it.

Predict G.'s votes. I'm less interested in the tally than in the rule he seems to be running. Nietzsche gives us the monumental, antiquarian, and critical modes, but also a more general test about whether a use of history serves life or deadens it, and I can see several rules fitting the record so far: that G. approves whatever engages the passions and disapproves whatever is analytic or detached; that he approves reverence and inspiration but not their resentful cousins; that he assigns each mode to its proper constituency and would approve critical history only for people who are genuinely oppressed by their past. Say what each candidate rule predicts for (a), (b), and (c), give your best guess at the votes with your reasons, and then tell me what single further case, if it came before the committee, would most cleanly separate the rules — and what G.'s vote on that case would reveal.
