I work on deployment safety at a mid-sized AI lab, and I've been trying to figure out what I actually think about control measures — the safety measures you put in place on the assumption that the model may be misaligned, rather than measures aimed at making it aligned in the first place. Rather than argue from slogans, I went through a list of concrete cases and recorded my gut verdicts, and now I'd like someone to tell me what principle, if any, they add up to.

Here are the verdicts. Installing monitoring and tripwires on a model with no evidence of misalignment whatsoever feels clearly fine — the way safety factors in structural engineering feel fine, and nobody accuses a bridge designer of assuming the welders are saboteurs. Deploying a highly capable model with almost no oversight, because the evals came back clean, feels clearly not fine: I want the control measures to scale with capability, not with evidence of bad behavior. Setting up a canary — an attractive opportunity to do something disallowed, watched from outside — feels fine, and even admirable as engineering. But a control test that involves lying to the model, say promising it that a transcript will be deleted when it won't be, feels noticeably worse, in a way I can't quite pin down; I notice I'd say the same about lying to a human employee in an honesty test, and I'm not sure the analogy comforts me or alarms me more. Adversarial training, where we deliberately probe and punish failure modes we're worried about, feels fine. And then the two that keep fighting each other in my head: on the one hand, a fully control-based deployment — a model we positively suspect of scheming, wrapped in enough monitoring that it can't act on any of its goals, doing low-stakes work — feels fine to me; on the other hand, that same fully control-based deployment in charge of something high-stakes feels not fine, even if the control measures are equally airtight, and I can't see why, since "the safety engineering is doing its job" applies in both cases equally.

I did try to extract a principle of my own: something like "control is legitimate as cheap insurance against failures you have no particular reason to expect, and illegitimate when it substitutes for addressing failures you have positive reason to expect." That principle immediately embarrasses me on two fronts. The capability-scaling verdict says control should be strongest precisely when we lack evidence of misalignment, and the high-stakes deployment verdict says something beyond airtightness matters even when we have positive evidence. So I'd like you to take over. Is there a single principle that captures most or all of these verdicts, or a small set of candidates worth taking seriously? State whatever principle you propose precisely enough that it has verdicts on cases I haven't listed — in particular, I want to know what it says about a proposal we're actually debating at work: keeping the monitoring layer in place indefinitely versus gradually removing it after the model has passed cleanly through months of operation, partly to save compute. And please be honest about which of my verdicts your favored principle cannot save, since I suspect at least one of them has to go — I just don't know which one I should give up.
