In the secondary literature I've read, Adam Smith's impartial spectator is routinely characterized as a kind of internalized conscience: an inner voice built up from the reactions of the people around us, which the agent consults when judging his own conduct. If that characterization is correct, though, I don't see how the spectator could ever do anything more than echo the norms of the society a person happens to inhabit. The spectator would be constructed out of local reactions, so it would presumably reflect local conventions: someone raised in one country would end up with a spectator endorsing that country's mores, and had he been raised elsewhere, he would have ended up with a spectator endorsing those instead. That would make Smith, in effect, a conventionalist about moral judgment, with the impartial spectator serving as a relay for whatever the surrounding community is disposed to applaud. What confuses me is that Smith is simultaneously credited with explaining how individuals can come to judge their own society harshly, seeing through widely accepted practices and prejudices rather than simply absorbing them, and I cannot see how that celebrated capacity follows from the spectator-as-echo picture. So my question is: is the standard characterization itself misleading, such that the impartial spectator is not merely a mirror of prevailing opinion but has genuine resources for rising above it, and if so, what exactly are those resources in Smith's account? Or is the tension real, and does Smith simply leave it unresolved?
