I have been keeping a list of my own verdicts about the principle of indifference — the rule that one should assign equal probabilities to hypotheses when one has no reason to prefer one over another — and I would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

1. A fair-looking coin from a trustworthy mint: assigning one half to each face is not merely convenient but correct, and I do not think I should be embarrassed that this came from indifference over a two-way partition.
2. A circle in which a chord is drawn by an explicit procedure: a spinner selects two points on the circumference, and the chord joins them. Here I am confident there is a determinate right answer, and that applying indifference along the procedure — the two points uniform on the rim — delivers it.
3. The same circle, with the question posed bare: a chord is drawn at random, and I am asked for the probability that it exceeds the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. My verdict here is that there is no fact of the matter: the three classical answers — one third, one half, one quarter — are each correct relative to a procedure, and the procedure-free question is ill-posed.
4. Yet in another case with no procedure available — a machine produces cubes whose side length lies somewhere between 0 and 2 cm, and I am asked for a prior over the side length — I have the stubborn feeling that uniformity over the side length is genuinely more natural than uniformity over the volume or the surface area, and not merely an artifact of which variable I was given first. I cannot reconcile this with verdict 3.
5. A mixture of water and wine, totaling at most one unit and containing at least a drop of each: indifference over the quantity of wine and indifference over the ratio of wine to water each appear to satisfy the principle, and they yield different probabilities for the same event. Verdict: the principle, stated at that level of generality, is broken.
6. A coin from an erratic mint, known only that its bias lies somewhere in the unit interval: I go back and forth on whether uniformity over the bias is a sensible default or precisely the mistake verdict 5 exposes, with uniformity over the log-odds equally defensible.
7. I am told the erratic mint makes exactly three kinds of coin — fair, two-headed, and two-tailed — and indifference over those three alternatives feels right to me. But this cannot be squared with any credence the prior from verdict 6 would induce, and more generally I hold that a hypothesis's probability should not depend on how finely I carve up the alternatives: splitting one hypothesis into two should not lower it. Indifference seems to violate this constantly, and yet I stand by verdict 1.

Is there a single principle — perhaps one demanding an actual symmetry or transformation group that leaves the problem's description fixed, or one about exchangeability, or one that distinguishes physical randomization from epistemic ignorance — that captures all or nearly all of these? State it precisely, tell me which verdict each of its clauses is there to save, and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, since I suspect verdict 4 and possibly verdict 6 will have to go. Then apply it to a case I have not listed: a spinner of unknown design has landed on red in six of its first nine spins, and I want to know whether your principle yields any credence at all that it lands on red next, or whether it must refuse to answer — as my verdict 3 would insist it should when no generating procedure is specified.
