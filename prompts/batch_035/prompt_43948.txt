For context: I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy training, and this question has been nagging me since it came up, in miniature, in my actual work. At my company I had to choose between two performance fixes: one adds a single millisecond of latency to every request our service handles, and we handle a few billion requests a day; the other adds an hour of complete unavailability for a few thousand users. I recommended the milliseconds without a second thought, and my reasoning was pure arithmetic: a few billion milliseconds of distributed annoyance against a few thousand user-hours of concentrated failure. That got me thinking about how small harms add up, and I went back to a famous thought experiment I first saw on a blog years ago, which I finally have concrete opinions about. Here they are.

One person is tortured for fifty years versus some absurdly large number of people — the kind of number you need stacked exponent notation to write — each getting a single dust speck in their eye, one moment of mild irritation that they forget a second later. I side with the dust specks, and I don't feel remotely torn. In the same spirit, if I could either give a billion people each one restless night of sleep, or torture one person for a week, I think the restless nights are worse, and again I'm fairly settled about it. And within a single life the compounding seems obvious too: one person who gets a dust speck in their eye every waking second for the rest of their life is, I'd say, worse off than someone tortured for a year — the trivial harms pile into something grave, no question.

But now the cases that don't fit. A billion people each losing one cent versus one person losing ten million dollars — and note the amounts are exactly equal, which is partly why I chose them — the single catastrophic loss seems to me clearly worse, and my gut says the cents don't really aggregate at all: nobody registers losing a cent, nobody could do anything with a cent, no life is different in any way it could notice. So apparently harms below some threshold just drop out. But then why doesn't the dust speck drop out? A moment of irritation you forget a second later also falls below anything that could matter to a life; it seems exactly as sub-threshold as the penny. I have one verdict where sub-threshold harms add up without limit across people, and another where they don't add up at all, and I can't see what distinguishes them. A colleague suggested the difference is that money is fungible and transferable while experience isn't, but that seems to cut the wrong way: it would make the pennies aggregate and the specks not.

My tentative repair is something like: harms aggregate when each instance is an experience, however small, but not when each instance is below the granularity at which it could touch anyone's life at all. But I don't trust it. It's not clear the millisecond of latency in my work case is an experience in the relevant sense — nobody feels a single millisecond — and yet I'm confident the milliseconds are a real cost at scale. And I can't shake the thought that my speck verdicts are an artifact of numbers so large my intuitions just fail, since I'd hesitate if it were a trillion specks instead of the stacked-exponent number, and I don't like a principle that flips because the exponent got big.

So here is what I'm asking for. Tell me whether there is a single principle behind all my verdicts, and if so state it precisely enough that I could implement it. If there isn't one, give me the two or three best candidate principles and say, for each, which of my verdicts it forces me to give up and which clause of it is motivated by which case. Then apply your preferred principle to the cases I haven't decided: the trillion specks, the work trade-off if the latency were ten milliseconds per request instead of one, and one person suffering a headache twice as bad as the headaches suffered by each of two people. Finally, tell me honestly which of my verdicts you think I should simply abandon, because I'd rather overturn a comfortable intuition than carry two principles that contradict each other. Please answer in continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way you'd write a long email.
