I'm writing a term paper on the conditional analysis of dispositions, the idea that to call something fragile is to say something like: if it were struck, it would break. My seminar has been marching through the counterexample literature — finks, where the disposition is present but the conditional comes out false because something stands ready to remove the disposition before the stimulus can do its work (the kind of case Martin's device for making a fragile vase momentarily cease to be fragile while it is being struck illustrates); mimics or reverse finks, where the disposition is absent but the conditional comes out true because something would produce the disposition at the last moment (the dead-wire sort of case: touch it and you get a shock, but only because a mechanism would connect it); and masks, where the disposition is present and the stimulus occurs but the manifestation is prevented by some interfering factor, as styrofoam packing prevents a fragile glass from shattering though it remains fragile. I've noticed that papers in this area each enforce their own private standard of adequacy, and before I catalogue any further counterexamples I want the standard stated explicitly.

So: write me the desiderata — the requirements any analysis of disposition ascriptions must meet to count as adequate. Include the cases it must get right (plain unfinked, unmasked dispositions; finked dispositions; mimics; masked ones), the distinctions it must respect (having a disposition versus manifesting it; a disposition versus its single manifestation; one-track versus multi-track dispositions), and the truisms it cannot contradict — for instance, that not everything is disposed to everything (the analysis must not trivialize, so the stimulus and background conditions cannot be left completely open), and that many dispositions at least appear to be intrinsic properties of their bearers. Give the motivating case or consideration behind each requirement, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are merely the prejudice of one camp (I suspect the demand for strict intrinsicness is in the second category, but argue it either way). Most important: flag any pair of desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied — if the list itself contains a trade-off, that is the result I most want to see, since it would explain why the counterexample literature never converges. Then, and only briefly, run two contenders against the list: the simple conditional analysis, and Lewis's revision that builds in an intrinsic property and a requirement about how soon after the stimulus the response must occur. Keep that scorecard to a short paragraph — the criteria are where I want the thinking, not the bookkeeping.

Please hold the whole thing to a strict limit of 900 words; ruthless compression is better than padding, and a table for the desiderata is welcome if it saves space.
