"In *I Am a Strange Loop*, Douglas Hofstadter describes a thought experiment he calls the Careenium: a billiard table mounted on a wheeled cart, with a bunch of balls ('simmballs') rolling around inside it. Some simmballs settle into a stable, self-maintaining pattern that Hofstadter treats as playing the role of a symbol — in the analogy, a self-representation. The individual simmballs are constantly colliding with the walls of the table, and the momentum from those collisions pushes the whole cart around the room. What is the thought experiment supposed to show about whether symbols or symbol-level patterns can 'push the substrate around'? Does it succeed in answering the epiphenomenalist worry — the worry that if the mental is nothing over and above patterns in physical stuff, then it can't cause anything? Defend your answer."

And here is the student's answer, unedited:

"Hofstadter's Careenium is meant to show that the symbol level has genuine causal power and that epiphenomenalism is false. The setup is a billiard table on a wheeled cart with balls rolling around inside. Some of the balls form a stable, self-maintaining pattern, which in the analogy is a self-representation. When individual simmballs collide with the walls, momentum is transferred and the whole cart gets pushed around the room. So the motion of the 'body' is produced by what's happening at the level of ideas.

The key point is that nothing spooky is going on. All of the causation is ordinary momentum transfer between balls and walls; there are no extra forces in the picture. What makes the symbol level causally real is that the pattern-level description and the ball-level description pick out the very same events. So the symbol level doesn't compete with the physical level — there is just one set of causal transactions, described two ways, and the coarse-grained description is often the more illuminating one.

This answers the epiphenomenalist. In fact I think it also refutes Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument. Kim says that if the physical level fully determines behavior, then the mental level is excluded from causal relevance. But the Careenium shows that the symbol level causes the cart's motion over and above what the ball-level collisions do — the pattern pushes the substrate around in its own right. So higher-level properties have a causal surplus: they contribute something the microphysics doesn't already contain, which is exactly what the exclusion argument denies.

One might object that the cart's motion is then overdetermined by two independent levels, which would be a problem for causal explanations. But since the two descriptions refer to the same events, this is benign overdetermination, the kind we accept everywhere in science, so the objection fails. Overall the Careenium establishes that symbols can literally move the substrate, and by extension that mental states, as patterns in the brain, can move the body."
