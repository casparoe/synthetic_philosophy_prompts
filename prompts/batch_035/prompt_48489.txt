I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm preparing a long piece on what behavioral genetics can and cannot tell us. The occasion: at a press briefing last month, a researcher promoting a forthcoming popular book offered a capsule summary of heritability that has been nagging at me since, and my editor wants me to fact-check it properly before we run anything adjacent to it. The summary, as I transcribed it, went roughly like this: "Heritability is a statistic that tells you how much of a trait is caused by genes as opposed to environment. For height, heritability in well-nourished modern populations is around 0.8, so 80 percent of a person's height is genetic, and IQ shows comparable figures in adulthood. Strictly speaking, heritability is a property of a population rather than of an individual — but the implication is the same: since IQ is that heritable, differences in average scores between groups must be overwhelmingly genetic as well. It also explains why interventions like enriched early-childhood programs show such disappointing long-run effects — when a trait is highly heritable, the environment simply doesn't have much room left to move it."

I suspect some of this is right and some of it quietly slides between claims, but I couldn't say precisely where on the spot, which is not a position I enjoy being in professionally. Could you give me a claim-by-claim audit? I want to know which sentences a working behavioral geneticist would accept as stated, which they'd accept only with qualifications, and which are outright conflations — and for the conflations, what the correct version is and where exactly the slippage happens. I'm particularly interested in the move from "this trait is highly heritable in this population" to "so the differences between groups must be genetic," and in the further move from "highly heritable" to "interventions can't do much": both feel like they might trade on the same ambiguity in the phrase "how much of a trait is genes," but I want to be sure I can explain the distinction cleanly rather than just gesture at it.

Two things my editor specifically asked me to nail down. First, whether the practical conclusions the speaker drew — about group differences and about the futility of interventions — actually follow from whatever portions of the summary are true, or whether they collapse once the conflations are cleaned up. Second, whether there's a vivid contrast case we could use for readers, of the form "trait X is highly heritable and yet changed enormously across a generation or two," and whether height over the twentieth century genuinely works as that case, or whether I'd be overreaching by using it. Please be candid about which parts of the summary are defensible simplifications a scientist might make to laypeople and which parts a geneticist would wince at even in a casual setting.
