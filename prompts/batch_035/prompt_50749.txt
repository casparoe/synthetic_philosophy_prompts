I am a journalist covering science and ideas, and I am preparing a long-form essay on what the Whig interpretation of history means for the way science gets narrated to the public. My working worry is this: the standard critique of Whig history, going back to Herbert Butterfield's 1931 pamphlet, condemns writing the past as the story of how the present was dutifully achieved — Protestants and progressives cast as the ancestors of liberal modernity. But my beat constantly commits a version of this: we celebrate Democritus as a man who anticipated the atom, run anniversary pieces presenting Mendel as a genius ahead of his time, and frame every superseded theory as a step on the staircase to the current textbook. And I suspect the standard cure — reconstruct the past in its own terms, on its own questions — is simply not available to a working science journalist, because readers have no reason to care about, say, caloric theory except as a wrong turn on the way to thermodynamics.

Write the essay around this tension, and reach a verdict rather than a pox-on-both-houses. In particular, I would like you to weigh one candidate middle position I have half-formed: a distinction between judging the past by the present's standards (which I take to be the real vice) and merely using the present's questions to select what to narrate (which may be unavoidable and harmless, or even an honest disclosure of why the reader is here). Consider whether science, unlike political history, actually licenses retrospective judgment — a refuted theory is simply wrong, whereas a defunct constitution was not a botched attempt at ours — or whether that thought is itself the Whiggism I am trying to escape, since what counted as success was often not settled when the work was done. Apply whatever you conclude to two concrete editorial choices: whether to call Democritus an early atomist, and whether to structure an anniversary piece as a story of discovery finally recognized. No citations or reading lists; I will track down sources myself. Reach a definite conclusion, state how confident you are in each of your main claims, and flag explicitly the point in the argument you are least sure of.
