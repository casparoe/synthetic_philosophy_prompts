I teach a discussion section for an undergraduate course on decision theory, and next week we begin a unit on dynamic choice, planning, and commitment. As a warm-up before the formal treatment, I want to hand the students a parody document and have them work through it clause by clause, identifying which regulations are genuinely decision-theoretically correct and which quietly smuggle in something dubious — so the humor has to come from getting the underlying paradox right, not from generic jokes about forgetfulness. The piece I'd like you to write: an appendix to a state driver's manual, in the flat bureaucratic voice of a real vehicle code (numbered regulations, a short Frequently Asked Questions section, and one or two sample written-exam questions), governing drivers with a medically certified condition that erases all memory between intersections and, awkwardly, renders the two highway exits on their route literally indistinguishable from the inside — same signage, same odometer reading, same everything. The state, paternalistic but scrupulously rational, has published utility stakes for the three outcomes on a laminated glovebox card: exiting at the first intersection yields payoff 0; exiting at the second yields 4; continuing past both and taking the long way home yields 1.

The appendix must legislate the best achievable policy for such drivers — never the plan-optimal one — and every regulation should be traceable to the actual structure of the problem: why the randomizing coin must be flipped exactly once, at home, before the trip begins, rather than at an intersection; why a driver who reasons "I am probably at the second intersection, so I should exit now" is generating trouble for himself at the other intersection too; why the Department refuses to condition any advice on which intersection the driver is currently at; and what the Department's official position is on drivers who attempt, illegally, to leave themselves a note. Where the manual mandates a randomization probability, it must be the arithmetically correct one, since part of the students' assignment is checking the Department's math. Please also include at least one FAQ answer whose reasoning is sound only if the driver's absent-mindedness really is perfect, one clause that quietly takes a stand on whether the driver at the first intersection and the driver at the second should be regarded as the same agent, and one sample exam question whose "correct" answer is strictly worse than what a driver with functioning memory would choose.

Keep the whole appendix deadpan — the state treats all of this as settled road-safety policy, never as a paradox worth remarking on — but legible, so that a careful reader could reconstruct the problem from the regulations alone. The total length, heading and FAQ included, must not exceed 500 words.
