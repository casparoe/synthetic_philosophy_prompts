I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, working on a team that builds an interactive assistant which adapts to user feedback. The system continuously learns from several kinds of signals: explicit ratings, the edits users make to the assistant's suggestions, whether users accept or reject its recommendations, and free-text complaints. A product decision now hinges on something that turns out to be philosophically loaded: when the learned model of "what the user wants" conflicts with the user's own later, more considered statements, we need a policy for which signal to treat as the user's real preference. A colleague with a philosophy background told me that moral philosophers have long faced an analogous problem: distinguishing preferences that reflect a person's values from states that merely look like preferences but are better described as biases or distortions — for example, adaptive preferences formed under unjust constraints, preferences that track a person's worst moods rather than their reflective judgment, and preferences that a person explicitly disavows while still acting on them. She also mentioned a standing disagreement about whether "what a person would endorse after ideal reflection" is even a well-defined target, given that the process of reflection is itself subject to bias, and that people can reasonably want things they would not endorse in a calm moment.

Please describe the most important implications of this preference-versus-bias problem for how my team should design the system's treatment of user feedback. Cover at least the following: (1) which parts of our feedback stream can plausibly be treated as evidence of stable preferences, and which cannot, together with what that implies for how the system should weight the different signals against one another; (2) what follows for cases where a user's immediately expressed preference and their more considered judgment appear to conflict, including whether the system should ever decline to follow an explicit current instruction on the grounds that it seems unreflective; and (3) which concerns that philosophers raise in this area are overrated for our purposes — for instance, ones that would only matter if the system had far more autonomy or scope for action than it actually has. For each implication, briefly explain why it follows from the underlying difficulty, and what feature of our setting would make it not follow. Please answer in American English. I want the reasoning itself, not a survey of positions, so please do not include reading lists, bibliographies, or citations.
