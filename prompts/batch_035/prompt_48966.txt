I've just taken over a four-person analytics unit inside a regional transportation authority. Our job is to attach probability estimates to decisions that are irreducibly singular: whether a specific bridge retrofit finishes before the viaduct closure permit expires, whether ridership on a new ferry route hits the business case threshold in year two, whether the named contractor on a tunneling job defaults mid-project. Whenever a number goes out, we fight about which past projects to average over — the ones of similar size, similar geology, similar procurement type, or simply everything the authority has ever built. Two of my analysts say the fight is arbitrary and we should average over everything and let conditioning on the specific facts do the work; one refuses to average at all and insists each project is unique enough that the exercise is theater. I gather philosophers call this the reference class problem. Please turn whatever in that literature is actually usable into a procedure my team can run in an afternoon on a single estimate: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, when the procedure is permitted to output a range instead of a point, where it stops and hands the decision to a named human because the question is one of judgment rather than method, and where it should say outright that no defensible class exists and we report that instead of a number. Then run it on two cases: the bridge retrofit against its deadline, where the closest matches are few but favorable, and the contractor default question, where the class we would want to use contains almost no members. Include at least one case where the procedure yields an answer management will not want to hear — for instance, if it shows the business case number came from a cherry-picked class and the honest range straddles the go/no-go threshold, sending the decision back to the politicians. Be explicit about what the procedure cannot fix, especially the fact that whoever chooses the class largely chooses the number.
