I'm building a little teaching module on epistemic akrasia (you know, believing one thing while thinking your evidence supports something else) and I want my students to see it's not always cut and dried. Below are six characters, each with a first-order attitude plus some higher-order attitude or higher-order evidence about it. Can you rank them from most to least epistemically blameworthy, with a sentence or two of reasoning for each? It's fine to have ties, and the whole ranking should work even if someone disagrees with you about how bad akrasia is in the first place.

Alice is 90% confident a coin will land heads even though she knows for a fact the coin is fair. Bob is 60% confident he aced his job interview, but he's read the psych literature on how people inflate these self-assessments, so he thinks the evidence really only supports 45%, and he stays at 60. Carol also read that literature, also thinks her evidence supports 45%, but actually lowers her credence to 45 while still feeling like it went great. Dave thinks his proof of a math conjecture is airtight and he's fully aware that he's the kind of overconfident person who always thinks that, and he says so out loud, but he keeps his credence at 95 anyway. Erin's three epistemic peers, who she knows are just as smart and informed as her, disagree with her conclusion, so she halved her credence even though she still can't find a flaw in her own reasoning. Frank just never has views about whether his beliefs are well-supported, only the beliefs themselves, so nothing about him ever conflicts.

One more thing: please write your whole answer as continuous prose, like an email to a colleague, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no headings, no bold or tables. So the ranking has to come through in the sentences themselves, in order.
