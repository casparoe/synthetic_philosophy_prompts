A friend of mine who does decision theory walked me into this argument at a party and I still can't get out of it. The problem is it seems to prove way too much. Here's the setup:

(1) Utility is just a way of numbering options so the numbers respect preference, and any positive rescaling of my utilities (multiply everything by 2, add 5) represents my preferences exactly as well as any other.
(2) So my utilities are only defined up to an arbitrary rescaling -- there's no fact about whether my morning coffee is "really" worth 2 utils or 400.
(3) Whether my total utility exceeds yours depends on which equally-good rescaling you pick for each of us, and there's no fact about that.
(4) So there's no fact about who'd benefit more from the last concert ticket, and no fact about the utilitarian sum over a group. Utilitarianism isn't false; it's undefined.

That far it's basically the classic ordinalist point from the 1930s and I was happy to acept it, since I was never that attached to utility sums anyway. Then he extended it:

(5) My preferences at 25 and my preferences at 65 are also two different orderings, and neither can claim authority over the other without an argument.
(6) So each time-slice's utilities are also only defined up to its own arbitrary rescaling.
(7) Deciding how much to save for retirement, whether to keep dieting, whether to study tonight instead of slacking off -- all of these are tradeoffs between utilities on incommensurable scales.
(8) So prudence is exactly as undefined as utilitarian aggregation.

I want to reject this somewhere between (4) and (8). I can live without utilitarian sums, but I can't believe there's no fact about whether my retirement savings is worth the sacrifice now. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? And here's the part I actually care about: does whatever I say to save prudence hand the utilitarian the same repair job for interpersonal comparisons, or is there a principled point where the two cases come apart? (Someone told me you can link the scales across time by saying a whole life is one preference ordering over sequences of experiences, but I don't see how that survives my preferences actually changing over time.)
