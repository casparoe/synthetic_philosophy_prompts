I sit on the safety standards committee of a consortium of frontier AI developers — nine labs, plus observer seats for two government agencies. At next month's meeting we will vote on whether to adopt a "safe Pareto improvement" (SPI) protocol, and I've been assigned to circulate a written recommendation beforehand. Here is the proposal on the table. In certain recurring disputes between members — most prominently, simultaneous independent discovery of a capability-relevant technique where both parties claim priority, and contested cross-hiring of research staff under non-competes — the default resolution is expensive adversarial contention: litigation, racing to publish, retaliatory recruiting. The protocol would instead bind signatories in advance to a specified surrogate game: in priority disputes, for instance, a fair lottery over which lab's name leads the joint publication, plus a pre-agreed licensing split, in place of the race to file. The design idea, borrowed from the game-theory literature on SPIs, is that because the surrogate game is fixed before the dispute arises and improves both parties' expected payoff relative to the default conflict, rational signatories can adopt it without trusting each other's goodwill — the same logic by which duellists might have preferred to settle the matter with a coin rather than a gunshot, had they been able to commit.

The vote is not a formality, and I want the memo to reflect that. One faction, led by our competition-authority observer, argues the protocol is a cartel device dressed in decision-theoretic clothing: the Pareto calculation covers only the two disputants, and a pre-agreed split of a disputed discovery is not obviously an improvement for anyone outside the table — if both labs will share the asset either way, each has less reason to independently stress-test the other's safety claims before deployment. Our counsel raises a second objection: SPIs are supposed to be robust to pure self-interest, but ours would run on private information. Each lab knows its own valuation of the disputed asset, the ex ante Pareto-improvement claim depends on those valuations, and the committee has no way to audit the numbers a lab reports when it signs. A delegate from one of the smaller labs adds a repeated-game worry I find hard to dismiss: if disputes become cheap and painless to resolve, the prospect of costly conflict stops disciplining anyone's behavior, and members may drift into more of the very confrontations the protocol was meant to head off.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate: a concrete recommendation — adopt as drafted, adopt with conditions (naming them specifically), or reject — together with the reasoning, and an honest treatment of the strongest point on whichever side loses. It should be a document the committee could actually vote on after a single reading, not a survey of the game-theory literature: assume members will tolerate roughly as much formalism as I've used above, but no more, and write for the ones who skipped it.
