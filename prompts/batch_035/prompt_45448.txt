I'm drafting the framework chapter of a thesis on self-deception, and I've come to suspect the literature keeps stalling because the participants are implicitly working with different adequacy standards: the empirically minded have a theory of how belief formation goes wrong under motivation, while the philosophers have a theory of how the phenomenon is even possible. Before I engage any particular account, I want the specification the disagreement is really about. Please write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of self-deception has to deliver — which phenomena it must explain (the static paradox of somehow believing p while possessing one's own evidence against p, the dynamic paradox of setting out to acquire a belief one must already in some sense have, twisted cases like the hypochondriac or the jealous spouse who believe what they hope is false, and self-deprecation where the belief is flattering only in a distorted way), which distinctions it must respect (self-deception versus wishful thinking versus ordinary motivated cognition, and — a case I've become fixated on — evidence-avoidance, as when someone unsubscribes from their lab's email list or steers conversation away from a symptom: does a theory owe us a line between self-deception and merely managing one's future evidence, or is that line a prejudice?), and what it must stay consistent with from cognitive and social psychology without populating the mind with a committee of homunculi who deceive one another. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it. Then flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied: my own hunch is that making the phenomenon agential enough to ground the responsibility verdicts we actually render — we blame the self-deceived in ways we do not blame the merely mistaken or the merely hopeful — conflicts with keeping it psychologically realistic, but I want that suspicion tested and possibly rejected, not endorsed. Note that the theory does not need to be adequate for everything at once: its primary job is to settle questions of culpability, such as whether the executive who 'believed' the safety reports or the partner who 'never suspected' anything is responsible for the beliefs they ended up with. So mark which desiderata are non-negotiable for that purpose and which are artifacts of wanting a theory for other purposes, such as therapy or self-knowledge. Finally, in no more than a few sentences each, say how three broad contenders fare against the list: intentionalist views on which the self-deceiver runs a strategy that presupposes a divided mind; deflationary views on which self-deception is just motivationally biased gathering, weighting, or interpreting of evidence; and views on which self-deception is fundamentally a failure to avow, or spell out to oneself, what one already knows. Keep the weight on the requirements, not the scorecard — the scoring I will redo myself in the chapter.
