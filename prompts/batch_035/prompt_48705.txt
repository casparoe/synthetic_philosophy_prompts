I've been following the fight over non-empirical confirmation in fundamental physics for a while now, and I can't shake the feeling that it's completely stuck. On one side you've got people like the philosopher who argues that string theory is confirmed to some degree even without new experimental data, because of things like the absence of alternatives, the unexpected explanatory coherence of the theory, and the fact that research programs with similar track records of unexplained successes eventually delivered in the past. On the other side you've got the people who think this is basically wishful thinking dressed up in Bayesian language -- that you can't get evidence for a theory about what happens at the Planck scale from sociological facts about how the community of string theorists behaves, and that trusting "no one found an alternative in forty years" is just an appeal to ignorance about a space of theories nobody knows how to map anyway.

The thing that gets me is that both sides seem to accept the same formal machinery -- nobody in this fight is anti-Bayesian as far as I can tell -- and yet they've been at a standstill for well over a decade. Same community, same history of physics, same examples (the Higgs prediction gets brought up constantly by both sides, as I understand it, one side as proof that long-shot theoretical bets can pay off, the other as proof that what mattered was that it was eventually testable). My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not about confirmation theory at all but about priors over a domain that is empirically silent: whether you treat "the theory could have been a mess but isn't" as a signal depends on stuff nobody can check, so both camps are really just disclosing how much indirect evidence they were willing to count before the debate even started, and the meta-arguments (the no-alternatives argument in particular) just launder those priors through impressive-sounding language. But maybe that's too uncharitable to the confirmation side, and there's a genuine question about what epistemic agents should do when their field's questions outrun their experiments -- a question that applies way beyond physics.

So: diagnose this for me. Is it a substantive disagreement about the nature of evidence, a verbal one where "confirmation" is being used in two different senses (something like "raises the probability of being true" versus "increases the credence of the community" or something else), or two groups answering different questions under one heading -- one group doing epistemology of science and the other doing a kind of decision theory for research investment? And where does the multiverse/landscape stuff fit in -- does the fact that some people think the landscape makes the whole thing untestable-in-principle change the diagnosis, or is that a separate fight? I want your actual reasoning about the structure of the disagreement, not a survey of the positions, and I want you to tell me honestly what, if anything, would count as progress here -- a new formal result, an actual experimental surprise from collider physics or cosmology, some conceptual distinction nobody has made yet -- or whether we should just accept that this one is unresolvable in principle, and why.

Also, and this matters to me: tell me where your own diagnosis is most likely to go wrong. What argument or consideration, if someone pressed it hard enough, would flip you to a different reading of the disagreement? If your answer is that nothing could move you, say so and defend it. And keep in mind I'm a physics grad student, not a philosopher -- don't assume I know the jargon, define your terms when they matter. Sorry for the rant, this debate just genuinely irritates me and I want to know if the irritation is rational.
