I am a retired statistical mechanic — forty years spent on phase transitions, equipartition, and the dreary business of getting grants renewed — who has lately been auditing a graduate seminar in formal epistemology, and I have become stuck on what the economists call the common prior assumption. As I understand it, Aumann proved that agents whose posteriors are common knowledge and who started from a common prior cannot agree to disagree, and Milgrom and Stokey pressed the point into market theory: purely speculative trade among common-prior agents is impossible, since the very willingness to trade functions as evidence, revealing information until no advantageous trade remains. Harsanyi, if the seminar discussion represented him fairly, seems to have regarded the common prior as a normative requirement: if two ideally rational agents assign different probabilities to the same proposition, someone somewhere has reasoned badly. Yet I also gather that others in the field treat the assumption as no more than a modeling convention — a bookkeeping device that relocates all disagreement into the information structure, so that "a difference in priors" becomes merely a drawer in which the modeler files whatever he does not care to explain.

The question I want settled, in a proper essay with a conclusion I can lean on, is which of these the common prior assumption really is: an empirical hypothesis, a normative constraint on rationality, or a convention. An analogy from my own field keeps suggesting itself, and I would like you to take it seriously, though not uncritically. In mechanics, initial conditions are arbitrary but factual: the dynamics is silent about them, yet they are not conventions, and two systems may legitimately begin in different states. In equilibrium statistical mechanics, by contrast, the distribution over microstates is derived rather than assumed, and assigning different priors to two samples of the same gas at the same temperature would simply be an error. The common prior assumption appears to sit between these poles. Bayesian conditioning is a dynamics that never says where the prior comes from, which makes the prior look like an initial condition; but the agreeing-to-disagree and no-trade results make the common prior behave like an equilibrium assumption, under which residual disagreement is not a fact about the world but a defect in the agents. So I want an essay that weighs the case for treating the common prior as a normative ideal against the case for treating differences of prior as rationally permissible fault lines in Bayesianism, says explicitly what would follow for the rationality of disagreement — whether two people who understand each other perfectly and still disagree must count at least one of themselves irrational — and closes by defending a verdict rather than offering a survey of positions. Write it as continuous prose only, in the manner of a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
