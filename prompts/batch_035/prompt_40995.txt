I'm plotting a novel set across the 1780s-90s -- the Little Ice Age's nasty final act, the Laki fissure eruption in Iceland, the famine years in France, etc. I wrote up my framing notes below and would love comments, especially on whether the central argument is coherent or whether I've smuggled in a contradiction. As a novelist I care more about the moral texture than the historiography, but if I'm being naive about the history I want to know that too.

THE ARGUMENT (rough notes):

The common move in environmental history -- treating germs, climate shifts, volcanic winters as "historical actors" -- has always struck me as a rhetorical trick with a real insight buried in it. Yes, calling the Tambora eruption an "actor" makes you attend to causal chains you'd otherwise skip (ash cloud --> failed harvests --> grain prices --> bread riots --> political crisis). But actors in the full sense have to be responsive to reasons, and a volcano isn't. So I want to say: the volcano is not an actor but it functions like one in the archive, because its effects are always mediated by human institutions that ARE actors -- grain markets, poor laws, monarchies deciding whether to hoard or release stores. The ash doesn't cause famine; the ash plus a particular institutional response causes famine. Same eruption with different institutions is a bad harvest, not a catastrophe.

From this I want to derive a rule for the novel itself: the volcano gets chapters, but never interiority. The nonhuman world is treated with what I'm calling "narrative respect without narrative voice" -- close attention to its causal weight, refusal to give it a perspective. My characters get interiority but their choices are always shown against the backdrop of this huge indifferent pressure, and I want the reader to feel that their responses were genuinely open -- that a worse or better institution, family, or king could have responded otherwise. That openness is, I think, what saves the book from determinism: if the environment fully determined the famine then my characters are just puppets and there's no story, only weather.

Where I start to doubt myself: (a) is the actor/cause distinction actually doing work here, or is "mediated causation" just how all causation in history works -- the Black Death no more needs "institutions" as a middle term than a bullet does? (b) some environmental historians DO want to grant a kind of agency to nonhumans, not as metaphor but metaphysics, and I'm not sure my position engages theirs at all or just talks past it; (c) I worry the "characters could have chosen otherwise" thing is my novelist's wishful thinking overriding what the actual record supports -- maybe in a real famine most actors really were trapped.

So: does the central argument hang together? Is the volcano-without-interiority rule actually a meaningful distinction or a bit of authorial handwaving? And is there a version of (c) I should take more seriously as a matter of historical fact rather than as a threat to my plot? Also happy to hear if you think the notes are just wrong-headed and the "historical actor" framing should be ditched entirely.
