Please analyze contemporary hustle culture — the online celebration of grinding, the 5 a.m. routines and cold showers, productivity influencers, the side hustle stacked on the day job, rest reframed as recovery to be scheduled and optimized — as a case study for Weber's Protestant ethic thesis, and then tell me where the mapping breaks.

I want the mapping done carefully rather than decoratively: what, if anything, plays the role of the calling; whether there is any functional equivalent of salvation anxiety, or whether the psychology runs on something else (fear of falling behind, status competition, the gamified metrics of platforms); and whether visible productivity and wealth function as outward signs of some inner state, and of which state — since for Weber the sign pointed to election, a fact about the person that no amount of striving could change, whereas hustle culture typically promises that striving itself produces the outcome.

Then run the analysis in both directions. If the ethic persists long after the theology has evaporated, does that confirm Weber's own suggestion that capitalism's spirit would eventually dispense with its religious foundations and compel work from everyone regardless, or does it instead support his critics — that the causal weight was always carried by economic and institutional pressure, with the culture merely reflecting rather than producing it? Note that hustle culture is not spontaneous folk asceticism: it is monetized, taught in courses, and amplified by platforms that profit from the anxiety it both diagnoses and feeds. Does that commercial organization count against a Weberian reading, or is it continuous with Weber's observation that the ascetic life became methodically organized and, in effect, professionally served? Be explicit about what the framework is silent on here — class, geography, the gendered division of the second shift — rather than forcing the theory to speak. At most 550 words.
