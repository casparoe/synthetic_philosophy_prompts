In arguments about the paradox of deontology — the complaint that deontological constraints are irrational because they make an agent allow five rights violations rather than cause one — I keep seeing people reach for a card-game analogy in the tradition of Dworkin's rights-as-trumps: rights or constraints are trumps. If the opponent leads a utility card, you simply play your trump and win the trick; the five lives saved don't beat the one right not to be killed, and no further story about why you wouldn't violate it is needed — you hold the trump. The analogy is doing real work here: it's used to make the refusal to kill one to save five look not just permissible but rationally unproblematic, dissolving the paradox as an artifact of consequentialist arithmetic that the deontologist never accepted.

Audit the analogy. Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the suit, the trick, the hand, the player, and the point of the game — then the places the mapping breaks, then a verdict on which disanalogies matter for the intended use and which are harmless. In particular, tell me whether the conclusion — that holding a constraint rationally requires no further explanation — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the trump picture quietly smuggles back in exactly the agent-neutral, aggregate-ranking structure the deontologist was trying to reject (a trump wins by ranking above other cards within a single ordering; it's not obvious constraints work that way, especially when the trade-off is between rights I violate and rights I merely allow others to violate). If you think there is a better analogy for what deontological constraints are doing, say what it is and why it carries the load better.
