My grandfather died last month at 88. He was a cognitive scientist who spent basically his whole career on Dennett's idea of the self as a center of narrative gravity -- the self isn't a little thing inside you, it's an abstraction generated by all the storytelling your brain does, the way a cloud's center of mass or a novel's protagonist is real as a pattern but isn't a piece of stuff anywhere. He believed this completely and cheerfully, and it did real work in how he lived: he liked to say you never actually meet a person, you meet a body and go away with a character. He left instructions for the funeral: no comforting falsehoods, no soul, no him watching over us -- but he also would've hated the flat "he's just gone" version, because to him a center of narrative gravity isn't nothing, its a real pattern even though it's not an object. I'm giving the eulogy. The room is maybe a third psychologists and philosophers who knew his work, and the rest is family who will take whatever I say about death literally. Write it as exactly five points I can speak from, in the order I should say them, each a sentence or two, no intro and no conclusion -- just the five points. It has to comfort my grandmother without saying anything he'd have considered false, and the third of the room that knew his stuff should be able to hear that the eulogy is doing real philosophical work rather than just being sweet. He also had this line about how we only ever write each other's later chapters -- if you can use that without mangling it, do.
