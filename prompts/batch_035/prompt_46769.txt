My friend and I have been fighting for a week about a game he invented, and the argument we've built appears to prove two incompatible things about it. I can't find the false step, and I'm starting to worry there isn't one.

The game: a dealer runs an infinite lottery. For n = 0, 1, 2, ..., he selects n with probability (1/3)(2/3)^n, writes 2^n on one slip and 2^{n+1} on another, seals them in two envelopes, and hands them out by fair coin toss. You open yours and see the amount a. One envelope always holds twice the other.

The argument:

(1) Suppose you observe a = 2^k with k at least 1. This happens either because the dealer drew k and the coin gave you the smaller slip — in which case the other envelope is double — or because he drew k−1 and the coin gave you the larger — in which case the other is half. The two possibilities have probabilities proportional to (2/3)^k and (2/3)^(k−1), so they stand in ratio 2:3.
(2) Given that you see a, the other envelope holds 2a with probability 2/5 and a/2 with probability 3/5. (At the minimum, a = 1, the other envelope is certainly double, which only strengthens what follows.)
(3) So the expected gain from switching, conditional on seeing a, is (2/5)a − (3/5)(a/2) = a/10 > 0.
(4) This holds for every possible observation. So you should switch whatever you see — and if you would switch whatever you see, you should switch without looking at all.
(5) I also tried to compute the unconditional expected gain from switching: sum over k of the probability you see 2^k, times 2^k/10. The kth term grows roughly like (4/3)^k, so the sum diverges. The argument seems to show that switching, before looking, has infinite expected value.
(6) But the setup is perfectly symmetric between the two envelopes. Both holders cannot come out ahead; swapping labels cannot change what the game is worth. The expected gain must be 0 if it is anything at all.

So the argument proves both that switching is worth +∞ and that it is worth 0.

My friend now says the flaw is in the step from (3) to (4): the inference from "positive conditional gain at every observation" to "switch" is invalid here, something about the gain failing to be integrable. I don't see how a theorem can just fail. Averaging a quantity that is positive at every point, weighted by the probabilities of the points, gives something positive — that is the law of total expectation, not a heuristic. He says that law is exactly what breaks, and can't explain it to me in a way that doesn't sound like special pleading for this case.

There is also a wrinkle that keeps me from dismissing the whole thing as an artifact of infinity. Truncate the dealer's distribution at some maximum n = N. Everything is now finite, all expectations exist, and the law of total expectation works. Yet switching still has positive conditional expected gain at every observation except the very top one, where the other envelope is certainly half — and that observation is vanishingly rare as N grows. So even in a perfectly well-behaved version of the game, "look, then switch" is right at almost every information state, and the rare top loss quietly balances the books to make the overall expected gain exactly zero. My friend's diagnosis, whatever it is, has to cover that case too, and I can't see how it does.

Tell me which step to reject, and what rejecting it costs. I would rather keep (3), which I verified by direct computation, and I would also like to keep the symmetry in (6), because giving it up seems to amount to saying that renaming the envelopes changes what they're worth. If you think my friend is pointing at the real flaw but misdescribing it, say so and fix the description. And please keep two registers apart for me: what is actually settled among people who have worked on the two-envelope problem, versus what is a live dispute — for instance, what a decision theory should do when expectations are undefined or infinite, or how much of the puzzle is the mathematics versus the decision principle. I can accept a diagnosis as settled; I don't want to be told that some particular repair is the consensus view when it is one faction's proposal among several.
