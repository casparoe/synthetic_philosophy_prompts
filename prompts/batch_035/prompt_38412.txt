My older brother and I have kept up an argument for years, mostly at Sunday dinner, about the word "for." The question is when it is literally true that some part of a thing is for a purpose, and when that is just loose talk. I once said a river's meanders are for slowing the water down and he nearly choked, so now I keep a running list of his rulings, and I have noticed he is remarkably consistent. Here is the list so far. A mousetrap is for catching mice. A heart is for pumping blood, even though nobody designed it and no one is trying to achieve anything by it. A spider's web is for catching flies. The sweet flesh around a fruit's seeds is for getting animals to swallow and carry off the seeds. A mousetrap with a broken spring is still for catching mice, even though it can't. Clouds are not for raining, even though raining is what clouds characteristically do and everything depends on it. And my blackened lungs, he says, are not for filtering soot, even though they happen to do that every day.

So he clearly isn't running the rule that a part is for whatever it in fact does, and he clearly isn't requiring a designer, since hearts and webs get functions with nobody behind them. But I can't pin down the rule he is using, and it matters for a few cases we haven't settled.

First, the appendix. The current thinking seems to be that it harbors a reserve of the bacteria we need in our guts, so that after a bad bout of diarrhea it can help repopulate the intestine — a genuinely useful job. But the appendix also looks like a shrunken leftover of the large organ that our distant leaf-eating ancestors used for digesting plant matter, an organ that has mostly been whittled away over evolutionary time rather than built up for anything. Is my appendix for storing bacteria?

Second, a penguin's flippers. Penguins' remote ancestors had wings that served for flying, and the flippers were originally put in place by all of that; but a penguin's flippers cannot support flight at all, and they are what the penguin uses, superbly, for swimming. Are a penguin's flippers for flying, for swimming, or neither?

Third, goosebumps. When we get cold or frightened, tiny muscles at the base of our hairs pull them upright. In furry animals that does real work — it traps a layer of warm air and makes the animal look bigger to a rival. In us, with our sparse hair, it accomplishes essentially nothing. Are our goosebumps for keeping us warm?

What do you think he will say about each of the three, and why? What is the rule he seems to be running, what other rules fit his rulings on my list just as well, and which of the three cases would separate those rules most cleanly? My own guess is that he will deny the goosebumps are for warming us, since he denied my lungs were for filtering soot, but I am much less sure about the appendix and the penguin, because there something useful really is getting done, and his heart ruling makes me think he allows functions without anyone intending them. Finally, if his list of rulings is consistent with more than one rule, tell me what single new case I could bring up next Sunday that would force him to reveal which rule it is.
