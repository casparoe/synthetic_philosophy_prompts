My two roomates got into a huge argument at dinner the other night and I got totally lost trying to referee, so I'm hoping you can do it. What they were actually disagreeing about was pretty dumb — whether our neighbor's cat, which has been missing for a week, is ever coming back (Alecs says 75% chance it's gone for good, Jordan says 40%) — but the fight itself turned into this whole thing about rational disagreement.

Alecs had just read about some theorem (Aumann's agreement theorem, I think?) saying that two rational people with the same prior can't agree to disagree — if their posterior beliefs are common knowledge, they have to be identical. So Alecs said: we've now told each other everything we know about this cat, and we still disagree, so one of us is being irrational. (1)

Jordan said no — the theorem only kicks in if you share a common prior, and nobody actually has common priors. She grew up around cats that wandered off and came back; Alecs's whole view is shaped by a childhood cat that vanished forever. Different priors, persistent disagreement, no irrationality anywhere. (2)

Alecs came back with: your prior differing from mine is itself evidence about the world. If I'm rational, I should treat the fact that your prior came out different as something to update on, and you should do the same with mine, so the disagreement ought to wash out once we notice it. (3)

Jordan said that's not how it works — the theorem needs our posteriors to be common knowledge, and her saying "I'm at 40%" once doesn't make it common knowledge (she could be lying, misreporting, whatever). And anyway, she said, even if the two of them took turns announcing their credences back and forth until the numbers stopped moving, what you converge to is some compromise number, not necessarily what you'd believe if you actually pooled everything you know. (4)

Alecs conceded the common knowledge point but said it doesn't rescue her, because if we just keep announcing credences until they stabilize, that stability is the practically relevant thing — the theorem's idealization doesn't matter for real people doing their best. (5)

Jordan replied that real disagreements mostly come from different priors and different background models, not from failures to share information, so the theorem is close to useless as a guide to actual fights like theirs — the rational response to disagreement is to get curious about the other person's prior, not to accuse them of irrationality, which is exactly what Alecs opened with. (6)

They asked me to referee and I honestly could not follow the last half. Can you go through the exchange move by move — numbered 1 through 6 like I did — and say who's right about what? I'd especially like to know whether Jordan's claim in (4) is actually true, that trading credences back and forth until they stabilize doesn't get you to the pooled-information answer, since that seems like the crux. And please end with an overall verdict on who came out ahead.
