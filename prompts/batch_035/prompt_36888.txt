A popular science book I picked up summarizes the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis like this: "Sapir and Whorf jointly proposed that language determines thought: if your language has no word for a concept, you cannot think it. Their centerpiece was Hopi, which they claimed has no way at all of referring to time — a claim demolished when later fieldwork documented Hopi's tense system and its extensive vocabulary for days, seasons, and durations. Color perception research then administered the coup de grâce: speakers of languages with only a few basic color terms turn out to see colors exactly as everyone else does, so language doesn't shape even the most basic perception. The hypothesis was abandoned for decades, until it was recently revived in a much weaker form: Russian, which has distinct basic terms for light and dark blue, makes its speakers slightly faster at telling those shades apart. But that only shows language affects attention and memory — not thought itself. So the interesting version of the hypothesis is dead, and only a trivial version survives."

How much of this should I trust? Go through it claim by claim: which parts would a linguist accept as stated, which are overstatements or conflations of positions Sapir and Whorf themselves distinguished, and which are accurate reports of research pressed into the wrong service? I'm particularly unsure whether the Hopi story and the color studies show what the author says they show, and whether the closing distinction between "attention and memory" and "thought itself" is a principled line or a way of rescuing the conclusion. Don't leave me with a survey of possible readings — give me your definite verdict on whether the summary is fair, where precisely it goes wrong if it does, and whether the author's conclusion survives once the errors are corrected.
