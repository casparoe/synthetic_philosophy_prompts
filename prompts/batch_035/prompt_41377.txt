I edit the satirical back-page of a graduate philosophy magazine, and for the upcoming issue I want a mock page from a consumer review-aggregation site — think Yelp or TripAdvisor — for an establishment called Omega's Clearing House, the business at the bottom of Newcomb's problem. The setup, spelled out on the page the way a business description would be: you arrive, the opaque box has already been filled or left empty by the house's predictor, whose published accuracy record is essentially unblemished; the transparent box always contains $1,000; you take one or both and leave. The page should consist of six to eight reviews with star ratings, at least one reply from management, one or two of those 'X people found this review helpful' tallies used as a punchline, and perhaps a flagged review or a listing for a discontinued service — around 700 to 900 words in total.

The humor has to come from the decision theory rather than from the premise alone, and I'd like the reviews to track the actual fault lines. A two-boxer leaving one star and demanding a refund on grounds of dominance, since the contents were fixed before he walked in and the extra $1,000 is simply his. A smug one-boxer writing five stars about her million and a little condescending advice to the other reviewers. Someone who reasons evidentially about what kind of customer the establishment tends to have. A disappointed customer asking why the house doesn't offer a pre-commitment product — pay a deposit now, be locked in to one box later — with a management reply explaining, in perfectly courteous and perfectly empty corporate prose, why that offering was never going to work for people who needed it. Ideally also one review that wanders off into a medical variant (a doctor who can apparently read your future habits from your pharmacy records) and at least one reviewer who compares the box to Schrödinger's cat and gets quietly demolished in the replies. I want the whole page to still read as a funny set of vendor reviews — entitled customers, an unnervingly consistent small business, a suspiciously well-calibrated manager — for someone who has never heard of Newcomb.

Alongside the page itself, please append a short note, outside the fiction, on where the piece takes liberties with the ideas: anything that turns on a contested reading of what the predictor's track record amounts to, anything that treats causal and evidential decision theory as personality types rather than theories, and any place where the joke quietly begs the question against one side. In particular, tell me whether the page as you've written it stacks the deck — I suspect a page of reviews naturally flatters the winners — and what specifically you would have to change to make a committed two-boxer concede the satire is fair. If some gag only works by making the losing side stupider than its actual argument, name it.
