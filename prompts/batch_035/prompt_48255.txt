I spent forty years as an analytical chemist, and most of that time reporting measurements with finite precision: a length of 5.3 cm, a mass of 2.47 g. In that setting, indeterminacy is always a matter of measurement error. There is a fact of the matter about the true value; the fuzziness lives entirely in my instrument and my rounding conventions, never in the sample. Now that I've started reading philosophy, the sorites puzzle strikes me as involving something like this analogy: for any given number of hairs, there is presumably a sharp fact of the matter about whether the person is bald, and what we call vagueness is just our inability to locate a boundary that is really there — the way I can't tell you whether the true length was 5.31 or 5.32 cm. I understand this is roughly the epistemicist view, and that supervaluationists instead deny there is any missing fact: a borderline sentence is neither true nor false because it comes out different on different admissible ways of sharpening the predicate, while classical logic is preserved by quantifying over those sharpenings. Please audit my analogy rather than my reading of the literature. First, the correspondence table: what in the sorites case plays the role of the true value, the instrument, the tolerance interval, and the rounding convention? Second, where does the mapping break? My own suspicion is that it breaks at the edges: when I report 5.3 cm, my tolerance interval has perfectly sharp boundaries, and I can state them, whereas for 'bald' the penumbra's edges seem as blurred as the penumbra itself — and I'd want to know whether that higher-order blurriness is something the epistemicist can explain, or merely something they must deny, and whether supervaluationism actually fares any better given that its precisifications are themselves stipulated to be perfectly sharp. Finally, the question that matters to me: if the analogy does break down in this way, does the epistemicist conclusion (a real but unknowable cutoff) survive on independent grounds, or was the measurement picture quietly carrying the argument? And is there a better physical analogy available — one drawn from, say, coarse-graining or noise — that either side could actually use?
