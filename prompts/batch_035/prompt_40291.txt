I'm the sole full-time employee of a small county historical museum, which means I write most of the exhibit labels myself. A recurring fight, maybe three or four times a year, is when what the community remembers about a local event doesn't line up with what our archives say, and I have to decide what the label actually claims. It just came up with a panel about our town founder: local tradition (loudly maintained by the family, two of whom sit on my board) is that he bought the land from the Native people who lived here fair and square, while the deed in our own files shows a payment of blankets and flour, witnessed by the founders brother-in-law. Another one: everyone in town remembers the 1937 flood as the dam company's negligence, but the engineering report we hold is honestly ambiguous and points at least partly at rainfall nobody had ever seen before. And a third case I'm dreading: a beloved teacher who ran the Black school under segregation is remembered by her former students, who still live here, as devoted to those kids — which she was — while the record also shows her enforcing the segregation rules enthusiastically, in her own writing. I trust archives more than nostalgia, personally, but that's not an argument that works on my board, and honestly I'm not sure it's always right — the archive was usually written by the same people who had the power.

So what I want is a procedure I can actually run whenever a label like this comes up: the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each one, where it lands on just go with the record or just go with the memory, and where it hands the decision to the board because it's a values question I shouldn't settle alone. Ground it in whatever historians and philosophers of collective memory actually know — that community memory does work a record can't, that records are themselves written by interested parties, that a museum label is somehow doing both jobs at once — but keep it usable by someone with no theory background, no reading list, and about a week per label. Then run it on the three cases above, including the one where it gives me an answer I won't like — I suspect it's the teacher case, where my instinct to correct the record might not survive the fact that her students' memories are the only record anyone has of what that school actually felt like from inside. And please be straight with me about what the procedure can't do: I assume there's a version of every one of these where two reasonable people run the same checklist and land in different places, and I'd much rather know that up front than pretend the checklist settles it.
