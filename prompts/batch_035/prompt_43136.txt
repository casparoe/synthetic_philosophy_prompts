I am a novelist, some twenty years into the craft, and fluent in the technical vocabulary of narrative: point of view and its limits, free indirect discourse, reliable and unreliable narrators, dramatic irony, the implied author, the difference between what a text establishes and what a character within it believes, and the working habit of testing a scene by imagining near variants of the manuscript — the draft in which I change one fact and ask which lines of narration would have to change along with it. I have lately been reading epistemology in the evenings, and I cannot shake the feeling that Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge is something I already do at my desk every day. As I understand it: to know that p, your belief that p must be sensitive — in the nearest worlds where p is false, you would not believe it — and adherent — in the nearest worlds where p is true, you would believe it; and Nozick accepts that the price of this is denying that knowledge is closed under known implication, on the strength of cases like Dretske's zebra (you know the animal is a zebra, but do you know it is not a mule cleverly painted to look like one?) and the grandmother who knows her grandson is well because she has seen him, even though, had he been injured, the family would have sent her a reassuring letter all the same. Please translate the tracking theory and the closure denial into my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Treat my characters as the believers and the finished novel as the world their beliefs must answer to; treat sensitivity as something like narrator reliability assessed across variant drafts; and work out what knowledge-under-entailment becomes when the reader, unlike the grandmother, can hold together facts established through different channels of the text. Then give me the residue report: which parts of the debate survive the translation cleanly, which survive only with distortion, and what, if anything, the philosophers are arguing about that has no counterpart in narrative craft at all. In particular I want your judgment on a suspicion I cannot dislodge: that the closure denial is an artifact of treating a single belief as a single line of narration, whereas any working novelist manages a point-of-view character's entitlements holistically, and would say the grandmother's problem is not that she fails to know some disjunction she is entitled to infer, but that she is an unreliable informant about exactly one class of worlds. Is that residue present because narratology is missing a distinction epistemology genuinely needs, or because the epistemologists' distinction is less clear than it looks?
