A decision-theory reading group I sit in on has been deadlocked across three meetings on a single scenario, and since I was the bystander asked to keep minutes, I can report the exchange in detail — but I cannot referee it, which is what I'd like you to do. First the scenario, as they stipulated it. A fair coin was flipped at some point in the past, and you were never told the outcome. Before the flip, a predictor ran a perfect simulation of your decision procedure and inspected what that procedure would do in each branch. If the coin landed heads, the predictor is now standing in front of you asking you to hand over $1,000; if you pay, nothing further happens, and if you refuse, you simply keep your $1,000. If the coin landed tails, you are never approached at all, and $1,000,000 has meanwhile been deposited into your bank account if and only if the simulation paid in the heads branch. You have just been approached. The question: do you pay?

Here is the exchange as it unfolded.

First, Marcus argued for refusing. The deposit either exists or it does not; handing over $1,000 now cannot causally affect it; and the coin is now known to have landed heads, so the tails world is ruled out. Paying, he said, is destroying money to honor the memory of a contract that was never signed — the same structural mistake as honoring sunk costs, except that the past expenditure here is purely hypothetical.

Elena replied that Marcus is evaluating an act when he should be evaluating the policy that produces it. The act on the heads branch and the deposit in the tails world are joint effects of a single earlier cause — the decision procedure the predictor simulated — and that procedure is present in full whenever he deliberates, so the choice of procedure is not behind him: what he does now is the procedure's output, and so is what the simulation did. Assessed from before the flip, the paying policy is worth roughly +$499,500 (a half chance of losing $1,000 against a half chance of gaining $1,000,000), while the refusing policy is worth nothing; and, she insisted, the deliberation before the flip and the deliberation on the heads branch are not two decisions but one decision about one object, made at different distances.

Marcus answered that "assessed from before the flip" is where the trick lives. Refusing to condition on what you have observed is not a virtue; it is refusing to do the one thing agents exist to do, and it is like insisting on betting on yesterday's weather by averaging over worlds where it rained. Worse, he continued, the pre-flip moment is not a privileged standpoint but an arbitrary one: why evaluate from just before this coin flip rather than before the predictor chose its game, or before you existed? And that arbitrariness is not merely academic, because adversaries can exploit it. Imagine a predictor who commits to flipping a coin and paying you $1,000,000 on heads if and only if its simulation of you pays $2,000 when asked on tails, having chosen the numbers so that the paying policy looks profitable from the pre-flip standpoint — and who offers such deals selectively, precisely because it knows updateless agents will honor them. A population of agents who count counterfactual branches' payoffs as reasons can be farmed this way. The ex ante perspective, Marcus concluded, doesn't just permit weird conclusions; it licenses being exploited.

Elena granted more than I expected here. The weather comparison fails, she said, because betting on yesterday's weather changes nothing regardless of your policy, whereas the deposit in the tails world was fixed by which procedure you run, and the procedure is exactly what you are choosing now — so the counterfactual branch is not causally inert with respect to your current options. But she conceded that the choice of standpoint is a genuine degree of freedom and that the farming scenarios turn on it. Her line was this: the correct updateless stance is not "honor whatever hypothetical deal looks positive from any veil," but "do what your best prior commitment would have committed to," where the commitment point must precede the adversary's selection of the deal — evaluated from that deeper standpoint, the farming arrangement is unprofitable for the adversary and the updateless agent refuses. Updatelessness, on this view, is not a license to pay every counterfactual toll; it is a rule whose correct application requires fixing the right partition, and she admitted that saying precisely what fixes it is unfinished business.

Marcus took the concession and ran. Then updatelessness is not a decision theory but a parameter, he said: in the original scenario he refuses, in Elena's farming cases Elena's own position refuses once the deeper standpoint is chosen, and in every hard case the recommendation swings with an unformalized judgment about where the decision point belongs. Causal decision theory, whatever its embarrassments elsewhere, delivers an answer everywhere from one fixed rule; the updateless approach delivers Elena's intuitions, paid for with a free variable. Elena's closing move was to point back at Marcus himself: if offered, before the flip, a pill that would make him pay in the heads branch, he would take it — he would eagerly become the agent who walks away with $1,000,000 in the tails world — and an agent who would take the pill but refuses to pay is committed to regretting his own rationality. Paying now, she said, is just the pill administered at a distance; the predictor has already made him the agent he would have chosen to be, and he is now deciding whether to defect against him.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: for each of the six moves, say whether it is right, confused, or right about something smaller than it claims, and where either party concedes too much or too little. Where a move depends on a distinction that itself needs defending — whether choosing a policy now is a real choice when the policy's other outputs lie in ruled-out worlds, whether the pre-flip standpoint is privileged or arbitrary, whether the farming objection refutes updatelessness or merely relocates its hard problem — please make that dependency explicit rather than letting it pass inside a premise. Finally, give an overall verdict on who is more right, and say what you take the disagreement to finally turn on.
