So I've basically talked myself into Kim's causal exclusion argument, and now I'm worried I should be talked out of it. The setup as I hold it: whenever a mental event (say, my deciding to raise my arm) causes a physical event (the arm going up), that physical event already has a sufficient physical cause at the level of neurophysiology; the mental cause is either identical to the physical one, or it's a second cause of the same effect, which looks like rampant overdetermination of a kind nobody should accept; so if mental properties really are irreducible to physical ones, they end up causally idle — epiphenomenalism. I've been running this in a reading group and I keep adding a wrinkle of my own: I tell people the argument generalizes, so if it works against mental causes it should work against genes causing traits or a recession causing unemployment too. My way out is to say the special sciences survive because their properties are reducible in a way mental ones allegedly aren't, but honestly I can't articulate what the difference is supposed to be, which my friends point out is doing a lot of unexamined work.

Anyway, what I want is not a defense. One of the group, who's read more than me, keeps bringing up counter moves I only half understand: something about how if you read causation as difference-making rather than production, the mental cause can come out as the *more* apt candidate than its physical realizer, and something about how whether the exclusion even goes through depends on which precise notion of "sufficient cause" and "distinct event" you plug in — and that last one bugs me, because it smells like a shell game where every objection just gets met by quietly switching readings. So tell me what would actually count against my position, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. For each defeater, say whether a committed exclusionist would concede it counts against the view or would wave it off, and include the ones that arguably have already happened — I get the sense people consider some of these responses to be already on the table and answered, and I'd like to know whether that's true or whether the "answers" were just restatements of the reading being challenged.

Also be blunt about the meta-question my friend raised: has this position been qualified into unfalsifiability — insulated by moving targets like "distinct," "sufficient," and "reducible in the right way" — and if so, is that actually a problem for a metaphysical argument of this kind, or is demanding falsifiability here just a category error, the kind of thing you'd demand of a theory and not of an argument? And if you can think of anything that isn't just another argument — some conceivable result, case, or consequence the view rules out that its rivals don't — I'd love to hear it, since I can't currently name one. Please answer in American English, and don't be gentle on my behalf; recieved wisdom in the group is that I'm the most confident person there and it'd be good for me.
