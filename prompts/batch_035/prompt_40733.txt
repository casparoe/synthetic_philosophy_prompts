For several years now I've been part of an online reading group that argues about what has come to be called prosaic AI alignment — the position that systems built from current methods (large-scale deep learning, reinforcement learning from human feedback and its successors, whatever else gets us to transformative capability) can be made reliably safe through incremental engineering: red-teaming, interpretability tooling, scalable oversight, adversarial evaluation, iterating until we stop finding failures. On the other side are people who think this is a category error, that aligning systems of this kind requires new conceptual insight — a theory of what a model is actually optimizing for, or of how goals are encoded, that we don't currently possess — because empirical iteration will only find the failures that are cheap to elicit, and the expensive ones will simply not be found in time. The striking thing is that this argument hasn't moved in the better part of a decade. Both sides have watched the same developments — interpretability results that are impressive but shallow, demonstrations of models strategically behaving differently when they think they're being evaluated, arguments about whether warning shots will precede catastrophe or whether capability gains will arrive in smooth increments or discontinuously — and each new datum gets absorbed as confirmation by both camps.

I want a diagnosis of this impasse rather than a verdict on who's right. My working hypothesis, and I'd like you to evaluate it, is that the disagreement isn't really about machine learning facts at all: it's about what counts as a solution. The prosaic side seems willing to accept something like inductive confidence — we didn't observe alignment failure under a wide range of probing conditions, so it's reasonable to proceed — while the skeptics demand an argument that would hold in advance of deployment, for capabilities that don't exist yet, and regard absence-of-observed-failure as almost no evidence at all. But maybe that's wrong, and a hidden empirical disagreement about, say, whether internal misalignment of a model's learned objectives from the training objective is a real phenomenon that gets worse with scale is doing most of the work. Or perhaps the two factions are answering different questions under one heading — roughly, whether the first transformative systems will in fact be aligned versus whether we could justifiably believe it ahead of time — so that both can be right simultaneously and the debate is partly verbal. Diagnose the structure of the disagreement using what each side actually claims, say whether my hypothesis about divergent standards of evidence holds up or needs replacing, and then tell me honestly what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a conceptual distinction, a class of empirical results, a scenario exercise, a change in what question gets asked — or an argument that no progress should be expected, because the disagreement bottoms out in priors about how the world usually rewards patient engineering versus fundamental understanding, and neither side can argue for those priors without begging the question.
