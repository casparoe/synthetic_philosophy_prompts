I've been reading a popular philosophy-of-social-science book that introduces the discursive dilemma, and I want to check how much of its presentation to trust before I use it in a seminar discussion. The book's summary, as near as I can reproduce it, goes like this: "Imagine a three-judge court deciding a civil case, where liability requires both that the defendant breached a duty and that the breach caused the harm. Judge One accepts both premises and votes liable; Judges Two and Three each accept exactly one premise and vote not liable. So a majority accepts each premise, yet a majority votes not liable. This is the discursive dilemma, first discovered by Condorcet and later rediscovered by legal scholars. It reveals that majority voting is internally inconsistent: if the court votes on the premises, it must find liability; if it votes on the conclusion, it must reject it. Since any collective decision procedure faces this problem, groups cannot literally have beliefs or intentions of their own — talk of 'what the committee decided' is really shorthand for what its members decided. Generalized, this becomes an impossibility theorem exactly like Arrow's: no aggregation procedure can respect a handful of obvious rationality conditions without collapsing into dictatorship. The moral is that majoritarian committee decision-making, and deliberative democracy more generally, rests on a contradiction."
