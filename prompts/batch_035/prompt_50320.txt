I'm taking a seminar on medieval political thought, and for my term paper I'm reconstructing Aquinas's account of tyrannicide from two texts that my seminar partner, Costanza, insists are in tension. The first is Summa Theologiae II-II, question 42, article 2, where Aquinas argues that sedition is always a mortal sin because it is contrary to the unity of the multitude. In his reply to the third objection — the one that asks how deposing a tyrant can avoid sedition, since the multitude will be divided between those who want to keep him and those who want to remove him — he says, in substance, that a tyrannical government is not just, because it is directed not to the common good but to the ruler's private good, and that consequently there is no sedition in disturbing such a government. The second text is De Regno (On Kingship), chapter 6, where Aquinas concedes that if some men plot together to shake off a tyrant's yoke, the attempt might endanger the multitude, with the result that "if there be no other recourse and if the whole commonwealth would be in danger, it seems that the slaying of the tyrant is not murder." Yet only a few lines earlier, in the same chapter, he writes that "it is not praiseworthy to deliver a multitude from a tyrant if the deliverance itself should entail some danger to the multitude," and he grounds this in the example of David, who, though he had Saul in his power, merely cut off the hem of his garment and then was stricken with remorse for even that.

Costanza's reading is developmental: on her view, the Summa is the early, more permissive Aquinas, while De Regno is the mature Aquinas who has thought better of it and quietly retracts the sedition doctrine — or at least buries it under prudential hedges. My own hunch is that this is wrong and that the two texts state a single doctrine, but I can't yet make the mechanics explicit, and that is what I want your help with. Please construct the decisive syllogism: an argument in which Aquinas's conclusion in De Regno — that the tyrant's killing is not murder, but only in the desperate case, only when there is no other recourse — follows from the premise that disturbing an unjust, private-good-directed government is not sedition. I want you to make explicit exactly which auxiliary premise does the real work in that inference. Is it a general thesis that private individuals may never intentionally kill (so that only an act ordered to the common good, i.e., an act of the community's public authority rather than a private act of killing, escapes the definition of murder)? Or is it the weaker claim that only the public authority is competent to judge when a particular act falls under public order rather than private vengeance — so that the private individual who kills the tyrant is doing the right kind of act but lacks the standing to do it? And please explain how the thesis that sedition is always a mortal sin survives: is the thought that the tyrant's supporters, being the ones who are seditious, are those who disturb the common good, so that there is an asymmetry between the two factions even when the community is visibly split?

Costanza also has a fallback objection that I want you to confront head-on rather than sidestep: she grants that the syllogism is formally valid but charges that it is only vacuously so, because the condition "when there is no other recourse" is satisfied by definition in the very case Aquinas is adjudicating — the desperate case — so the syllogism licenses the conclusion only because the conclusion has already been smuggled into the triggering condition. Please assess whether that charge sticks, and if it does not, say precisely why not. Finally, a request about the epistemic status of your answer: at the end, state what would change your mind about the single-doctrine reading — for instance, what kind of further passage, in either text or elsewhere in the Thomistic corpus, would push you toward Costanza's developmental reading or toward the view that De Regno's apparently conditional permission is really a prohibition in disguise — and also flag the step in your reconstruction where you think your reasoning is most likely to fail, so that I know which part of the argument to shore up before submitting the paper.
