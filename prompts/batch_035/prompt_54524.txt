I am a recently retired spectroscopist — thirty-six years in a physical chemistry group at a government laboratory — and since retiring I have taken up two new habits: reading my way along the decision-theory shelf, and hosting a weekly games evening for my family. For the grandchildren I designed a simple outguessing game. Each round, the Guard secretly writes "left door" or "right door" on a slip; simultaneously, the Raider writes one of his cards. If he plays LEFT and the guard is at the right door, the guard pays him ten tokens; if the guard is at the left door, the Raider gets nothing that round — and symmetrically for RIGHT. There is also a cautious card, BOTH, meaning "send half your force to each door," which takes exactly four tokens from the guard whichever door he picked; and in the longer version we play on holidays there is a fourth card, NONE, meaning "stand down, but collect a token as a toll," which takes exactly one token regardless. The slips are revealed together, so nobody moves first.

At last week's session my son, who took an economics course in college, proposed to simplify the teaching chart by crossing out the "bad" cards, and here our household divided. He crossed out NONE immediately ("always worse than BOTH — no one will miss it"), and then, looking at the reduced chart, pronounced BOTH a bad card as well, on the ground that flipping a fair coin between LEFT and RIGHT would net five tokens against either guard — better than BOTH's four. My granddaughter objected twice over. First, the coin is not a card: our rules put nothing in the deck but the four cards, and she doubts one can flip a mental coin anyway. On that point she insisted we test it, so in good laboratory fashion I had the grandchildren write out a hundred "random" left-or-right choices at the kitchen table; they alternated far too often — the runs were too short, and the sequences would have failed a runs test handily. Second, she noted that my son's final chart — only LEFT and RIGHT for the Raider — leaves the guard facing exactly the same guessing contest, at which point he cheerfully advises the guard to "go 50-50 between the doors" in her head. So his whole analysis demands that everybody do the very thing we had just shown nobody can do, and the two of them ended the evening holding different simplified charts: his with two Raider cards, hers with three, because she refused to let a coin that is neither in the deck nor in our heads count as "something better to play instead."

My question, then, is in three parts, and I would be grateful for a considered answer rather than a ruling. First, was the procedure sound? Specifically, was my son right that BOTH is a bad card in the sense that matters, or was my granddaughter right that the judgment quietly replaces a card with an action that our rules do not permit and our natures do not allow — and does the answer change depending on which standard of "worse than something else" one adopts, given that the two standards here reached different verdicts? Second, if reasonable standards of pruning can disagree in this way, is there a principled way to choose between them, or must a group simply stipulate one before the chart can be simplified — and if so, which would you stipulate to a family that wants to teach its youngest member something true? Third, and this is what keeps me at the kitchen table after the others have gone to bed: when my son tells the guard to go 50-50 in her head, what is he actually advising her to do? If none of us can execute it, the advice appears empty; yet it also seems to be getting at something real, perhaps about not being exploitable, or about how a long evening of play ought to look in the aggregate. I have my spectroscopist's instinct that the coin talk is a claim about populations rather than persons, but I cannot make that precise, and I would like to explain it correctly to the grandchildren before we play again on Sunday.
