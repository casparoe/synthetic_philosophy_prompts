I am a novelist — three books published, a fourth nearly finished — and I want to ask whether a pattern I keep running into in my own working life has a name in philosophy of mind. My training is in fiction, not philosophy; I have read Searle's Chinese Room argument and a scattering of essays on consciousness, but I do not know the literature on what I am about to describe, and I may be reinventing a wheel that has been machined to a much finer tolerance elsewhere. If so, please tell me what the wheel is called and how the professionals carve it. I should also say up front that a bare label will not satisfy me: I want to know whether the established concepts genuinely cover what I am describing, where they fail to, and what the edges of the thing are.

The pattern has three parts, which I first thought were unrelated and now suspect are one phenomenon.

First, the obvious part. The marks on my manuscript pages are about a woman named Vivian — they describe her childhood, they attribute beliefs to her, some sentences state what she wants. But the ink itself is not about anything; it is pigment. The aboutness seems to be something I lend to the page, and the page then lends to readers, whose beliefs about Vivian are presumably also about her in some way — though she does not exist, which raises questions I will set aside. There is a chain of lending: me, the text, the reader. I take it no one thinks the page originates the aboutness; the page is a borrower.

Second, the curious part. During drafting, Vivian began to behave in ways I had not planned, and I found myself writing scenes to find out what she would do, in something like the frame of mind of an observer rather than a creator. When interviewers ask why I made her leave her husband in the way she did, I say that she left him, and I do not experience this as a charming evasion — I experience it as an accurate report. And it is not only that I am surprised; it is that I now take instructions from her. Her fictional circumstances, which I invented and which are therefore borrowed from me, now determine what I believe a fitting next chapter is, what I count as in or out of character, what I would find a betrayal of her. Something derived from my own states has become a standard against which my own states are assessed and corrected. The borrower has begun issuing instructions to the lender.

Third, the part that makes me doubt the whole lending metaphor. Vivian has readers, some of whom write fiction about her — continuations, counterfactuals, one rather ambitious prequel. Their claims about her are presumably derived from my text. But my Vivian is derived too: she is assembled from habits of speech I absorbed from two or three earlier novelists, from a woman I sat across from on a train for forty minutes, from a photograph I misremembered. If I follow any chain of aboutness far enough upstream, I never seem to reach anyone who was not, in the same sense, borrowing. Which makes me wonder whether the distinction between aboutness that originates and aboutness that is passed along — a distinction I am confident someone has drawn, since I stumbled into it without trying — can survive the observation that the originators are all downstream of something.

So my questions, stated as precisely as an amateur can manage. Is there an established concept or distinction for the difference between mental content that originates somewhere and mental content that is inherited from elsewhere, and what is the standard account of how the inheritance works? Does that account have anything to say about my second observation — the loop, where content that was admittedly derived from an author's states comes to govern those same states — or is the loop usually treated as a loose metaphor, a writers' superstition that dissolves under analysis? And does anyone take seriously the third observation, that if derivation is traced far enough the distinction between the original and the derived may be a distinction between roles in a chain rather than between two kinds of thing? I can see that Searle's Chinese Room is somewhere in the neighborhood — the worry about a system manipulating symbols that are about something only because someone outside made them so — but the Chinese Room seems to be about whether a system can understand, whereas what I want to understand is the direction and the origin of the lending, and whether it can curl back on itself. Please tell me what the nearest concepts are, how each of them fails to fit which of my three observations, and — if nothing fits all three — say so plainly and give me a way of carving the phenomenon that does, edges and boundary cases included. I am not asking for a reading list; I am asking for the shape of the territory.
