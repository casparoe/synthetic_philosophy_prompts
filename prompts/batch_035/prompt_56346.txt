Take Arendt's distinction between the old-fashioned liar -- someone who knows perfectly well what the truth is and hides it for advantage -- and the organized, image-making kind of lying she worried about in her essays on the Pentagon Papers, where whole bureaucracies build a picture that doesn't just cover reality but gradually replaces it, and the people doing it half-believe their own image. Now take the 2003 Iraq WMD affair and analyze it through that lens. Map it properly: who was actually making the image, who came to believe it, and did the facts eventually take their revenge the way Arendt says they tend to when the image-makers still have to act in the real world? Then tell me where the lens misdescribes the case -- a lot of the people involved genuinely believed the intelligence, some key claims traced back to a defector nobody in the policy rooms had been able to interview directly, and much of the public knew the image was an image and went along with the war anyway, which is not really something Arendt's picture anticipates. For contrast, think about how the Watergate cover-up looks under the same distinction, since that one really does look like old-fashioned lying about a known crime. Does the Iraq case confirm her framework, strain it, or show a gap in it? Be honest about where the theory is silent instead of forcing it to speak.
