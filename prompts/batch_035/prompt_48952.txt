In the philosophy-of-economics seminar I co-teach with a colleague from the economics department, we just finished Milton Friedman's 1953 essay on the methodology of positive economics, together with Alan Musgrave's later article untwisting what he called the F-twist. My problem is the sentence every economist in the room knows by heart: Friedman's remark that truly important hypotheses will be found to rest on assumptions that are wildly inaccurate as descriptive representations of reality, and that, in general, the more significant the theory, the more unrealistic the assumptions. That sentence is deployed in my seminar roughly once a meeting, most recently to wave away a student's complaint that a perfect-competition model assumes away everything interesting (perfect knowledge, homogeneous goods, no strategic interaction). The economists treat it as settled methodology; the philosophers treat it as an obvious howler; and I suspect both camps are reacting to different sentences hidden inside the one.

Before our next meeting I want a handout that does the disambiguation work properly, so that when someone invokes the line we at least know which thesis is being invoked. Please lay out the distinct precise claims it could be expressing. I have some candidate readings in mind, but don't feel bound by them: that significance is a matter of abstraction, so a theory that explains much from few premises ipso facto omits many actual influences, making the line something close to a truism; that the line states a methodological injunction about what to test — that assumptions are never to be checked directly, only predictive consequences, in the spirit of the as-if treatment Friedman gives the expert billiard player; and the stronger instrumentalist reading on which the assumptions are not even approximately true and this simply doesn't matter. There is presumably also a self-serving reading lurking in the essay's actual dialectic — the one Friedman needed against the questionnaire studies of businessmen's pricing practices, where firms did not report reasoning their way to marginalist conclusions — and I would like to know whether that reading is the same as the as-if reading or distinct from it. For each reading in your menu, say whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or straightforwardly false, and — this is the part I most want — whether it would actually license the use my seminar put it to, namely dismissing a complaint about the descriptive realism of a model's assumptions without engaging the complaint's content.

I would also like a closing paragraph on where Musgrave's distinction between negligibility, domain, and heuristic assumptions leaves all this. My instinct is that the tripartite apparatus rescues a modest reading of Friedman — unreal assumptions of the negligibility sort are harmless, domain assumptions restrict scope rather than assert falsehood — while leaving the seminar's deployment untouched, since the student's complaint wasn't obviously about either category. But I want you to check that against your menu rather than just confirming my hunch: which of the readings, if any, does Musgrave's untwisting actually defuse, and which survive it intact? Keep the handout to something my students can absorb in twenty minutes; they will be the ones wielding the sentence afterward, so clarity about which thesis they are wielding is the whole point.
