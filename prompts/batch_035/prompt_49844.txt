I teach an upper-level seminar on moral disagreement and political ethics, fourteen students, one ninety-minute session. A colleague of mine is fond of insisting that principled compromise is essentially a myth: whenever two sides with genuinely different moral commitments split the difference, at least one side is betraying its principles, and usually both are, so "moral compromise" is just a polite name for moral dilution. I think he's right about some compromises and badly wrong about others, and I want my students to feel exactly where the line falls rather than be told about it. My working intuition is that some compromises are structurally like trade — each side concedes on something it weights lightly and gains on something it weights heavily, so both walk away closer to what it values than it would under either unilateral outcome — while other compromises are structurally like contamination, where the joint action does a bit of what each side independently regards as wrong, and no amount of clever packaging changes that. Students have read the standard theory and can recite it; what they can't do yet is recognize which situation they're in while it's happening to them.

Design an in-class game or structured negotiation that makes this distinction show up in the participants' own choices rather than in anything I say. Set it up so that at least some genuinely trade-like gains from moral compromise are available and some deals on the table are contaminating, but don't label which is which — I want factions to discover, mid-negotiation, that some concessions cost them almost nothing by their own lights while others feel like betrayal, and to start arguing internally about why. Please specify the factions and what each holds (you can use simplified but non-cartoonish value systems — e.g., a strictly consequentialist group, a group with strong side-constraints, a virtue- or integrity-centered group — or stakeholder roles in a concrete policy dispute if you think that works better), the rules of negotiation, a payoff or point scheme that doesn't involve real money, a time structure, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the trade/contamination distinction and onto my colleague's thesis. Include an honest note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — dominant strategies, factions that merge ideologically, deals that are too easy — and how I should handle each. Also be frank about which feature of the philosophical question the game structurally cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply in the debrief: I suspect, for instance, that any point-based payoff scheme quietly presupposes that all moral costs are commensurable, which is itself part of what's at issue.

One more request: where you make design choices that turn on contested assumptions — about whether moral values are commensurable, about whether principled concessions are possible at all, about whether contamination is worse than unilateral loss — flag them explicitly rather than burying them in the rules. Tell me which argument or consideration would lead you to substantially redesign the activity or to conclude that no game can teach this distinction, and identify the point in your reasoning about how students will behave where you're least confident and most likely to be wrong.
