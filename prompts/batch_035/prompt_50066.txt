I'm a software engineer with no philosophy background, and after falling down a rabbit hole on the argument from illusion I've ended up sympathetic to disjunctivism — the idea that a successful perception and a subjectively identical hallucination don't share a common mental state, that in the good case you're literally in contact with the world itself and the hallucination case is just a different kind of thing. My credence that disjunctivism (or at least naive realism about the good case) is roughly right is maybe 0.6. Give me your own number, but what I actually want is the decomposition: probability that subjectively indistinguishable states must share a common mental kind, probability that intentionalism (experience = representing the world, accurate or not) can capture everything the disjunctivist cares about, probability that the "highest common factor" framing of the argument from illusion even makes sense, plus whatever other terms you think belong — one line of justification each, then the combination.

Here's the thing nagging me from my day job: when an LLM hallucinates, the internals look like the same kind of process as when it's accurate — same forward pass, same tokens, no internal flag saying "this one's made up." That feels like evidence for the common-factor side. Does the disjunctivist have a real reply to that, or is it just an analogy that breaks the moment you poke it? Fold that in where it belongs.

Tell me which term you're least confident in, and which single argument, if it landed, would move your number the most. And please keep a clean line between what's actually settled among people who study perception and what's a live fight — I've definately seen intentionalism presented as just "the standard view" when I gather half the field pushes back.
