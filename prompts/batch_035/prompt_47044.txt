I'm a second-year PhD student with a prospectus deadline in about six weeks, and my supervisor's verdict on my current idea is that it's promising but is really two ideas tangled together — I need help untangling them and turning the result into a workable dissertation proposal before the committee meeting.

Here is the tangle. In the literature responding to Bernard Williams, his integrity objection and his complaint that impartial moral theories demand one thought too many are almost always treated as two symptoms of a single underlying worry: that agent-neutral moral theories alienate agents from their projects and relationships by subordinating everything to what impartiality recommends. My hunch is that these are actually two complaints with different structures, and that the difference matters. The integrity objection, as I read it, is pitched at the level of theory and assessment — it concerns what a moral theory permits or requires with respect to an agent's ground projects, regardless of what the agent thinks at the time. The one-thought-too-many complaint, in the famous case of the man who can save only one of two people and saves his wife, seems instead to be about the architecture of deliberation: about what may or must actually pass through an agent's mind at the moment of action, and perhaps only about what the theory's defenders say agents should think, not about what the theory itself implies. If the complaints are dissociable, there should be possible cases where integrity is at stake but no thought is too many (an agent deciding in a heartbeat, with the theory's verdict never surfacing in deliberation) and cases where the thought is too many but integrity is not (an agent with nothing like a ground project invested in the outcome, yet something still seems off about reaching for the impartial justification). And some standard replies to Williams — for instance, that consequentialism can license a fact about the agent's wife as a justification without requiring it as a deliberative step — may answer one complaint while silently changing the subject on the other.

Write the prospectus, roughly two pages, with the following: a precise statement of the question, framed so that it has a determinate answer rather than licensing an essay that just says the complaints are sometimes separable; an explanation of why the existing positions in the debate leave the question open; a method section saying what I would actually do, chapter by chapter — including how I plan to construct and test the dissociation cases, what makes a case a fair test rather than a rigged one, and how I'll handle the objection that the too-many-thoughts complaint is just loose talk about psychology that philosophy of action, not ethics, should adjudicate; a statement of what would count as the project failing (e.g., if it turns out the two complaints provably coincide, or if the deliberative/justificatory distinction can't be made sharp enough to do the work); and an honest assessment of scope — whether this is a dissertation or really a long article, and if the latter, what a defensible dissertation-shaped version would add. Don't include a bibliography or citations; I'll fill those in myself, and you should feel free to refer to positions and argument-types generically rather than by chapter and verse. Please also make sure the framing doesn't quietly assume Williams is right about everything — the prospectus should be as attractive to a supervisor who thinks the objection has been answered as to one who doesn't.
