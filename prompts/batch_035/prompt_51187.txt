The question, worth 20 points, read as follows:

"Question 4 (20 points). Russell introduced the theory of descriptions partly to handle sentences containing definite descriptions that denote nothing, such as 'The present King of France is bald.' (a) Give the logical form Russell assigns to a sentence of the form 'The F is G,' decomposing it into its clauses. (b) State the truth value of 'The present King of France is bald' on Russell's analysis, and explain why. (c) Explain how the theory resolves the puzzle about the law of excluded middle that Russell raises for sentences of this kind. (d) Explain how the same analysis accounts for the informativeness of 'Scott is the author of Waverley,' the puzzle Russell illustrates through George IV's curiosity about that identity."

The student's answer is reproduced verbatim below.

"According to Russell, a sentence of the form 'The F is G' is really a conjunction of three claims: (1) at least one thing is F; (2) at most one thing is F; (3) that thing is G. So 'The present King of France is bald' says that there is at least one present King of France, that there is at most one, and that he is bald. Since France presently has no king, the first clause fails, so the conjunction is false; the sentence is therefore false rather than lacking a truth value. Here Russell improves on Frege, who treated such sentences as having no truth value: like Frege, Russell holds that the description 'the present King of France' has a sense but no referent, and it is this failure of reference that makes the whole sentence false.

As for the law of excluded middle, Russell can uphold it once scope is made explicit. On the wide-scope reading, 'The present King of France is not bald' is true, since it is just the negation of the false existential claim; on the narrow-scope reading, where the negation attaches only to 'is bald,' the sentence is false. Either way, every reading receives a truth value, so excluded middle is preserved.

The same machinery explains why 'Scott is the author of Waverley' was informative. As Frege showed, 'Scott' and 'the author of Waverley' differ in sense while denoting the same man, and Russell accepts this account: the identity statement is informative at the level of sense, while at the level of logical form, where the description has been analyzed away, it reduces to the trivial truth 'Scott is Scott.'

Finally, note that since the existence clause fails whenever a definite description is empty, every sentence containing a non-denoting definite description comes out false on Russell's analysis."
