Here is the student's answer, verbatim:

"A multipolar trap is a situation where there are many actors and none of them can afford to unilaterally do the right thing, because whoever does it first gets outcompeted by the rest. So each actor follows its individually rational interest, and the collective result is bad for everyone. Essentially it's a zero-sum game at scale: each firm's gain in market share is exactly balanced by another firm's loss, so total welfare stays constant, but everyone has to spend more and more just to stay in the same place. Classic examples are an arms race between countries, firms spending ever more on advertising, and academics publishing more and more papers of lower and lower quality just to keep their jobs. In each case everyone would prefer that everyone stop, but nobody can afford to be the one who stops first.

Races to the bottom are a special case of this. When firms compete on cost, they keep lowering standards — wages, safety, environmental protection — because any firm that doesn't will be undercut by those that do, and the standard falls until it hits bottom. Really a race to the bottom is just the tragedy of the commons with extra steps: each herder gains by adding another cow while the cost is spread across everyone, and likewise each firm gains by shaving another dollar while the cost (pollution, unsafe products) is spread across everyone. Overfishing is a good example: each company benefits from taking more fish, and the depletion of the fishery is shared by all.

Moloch is the name Scott Alexander gives to the intelligence behind these traps. Moloch is a rational actor who benefits from keeping us divided, and he deliberately arranges competitions so that virtue is punished and vice rewarded. Since Moloch wants us competing, the only real escape is a single world government that outlaws the competition everywhere at once, since by definition the trapped actors can't free themselves. Some people object that competition isn't always destructive — firms competing on safety features, or countries trying to outdo each other on environmental rules — but these are exceptions; in general competition inevitably becomes a race to the bottom."
