Payoffs: exiting at the first intersection gets you 1 (a motel), exiting at the second gets you 4 (home), and continuing past both gets you 0 (you end up lost in the bad part of town). So always exiting is worth 1, always continuing is worth 0.

Here's his argument. (1) Ex ante, the best plan is: at each intersection, continue with probability 3/8. That's worth 25/16 ≈ 1.56, which beats both pure plans, so planning-optimal rationality endorses this specific randomization. (3) Suppose you're at an intersection and you know you're implementing the 3/8 plan. You reach the first intersection with probability 1 and the second with probability 3/8, so conditional on being at an intersection at all, it's 8/11 the first one and 3/11 the second. (4) Exiting now is worth (8/11)(1) + (3/11)(4) = 20/11; continuing now is worth (8/11)[(5/8)(4)] = 20/11. You're exactly indifferent. (5) So at the point of action the plan's randomization does no rational work — you have no reason to continue rather than exit. (6) An agent with no reason to follow a plan's instruction at the point of action can permissibly do anything, including resolving right now to always exit. (7) But always exiting is worth 1 ex ante, which is worse than 1.56, so the "optimal" plan is self-undermining: adopt it, and it rationally permits behavior that defeats it. (8) The same goes for any mixed plan in any problem, so randomization is never really rationally binding and ex ante plan-optimality can't be the standard of rational action.
