I trained under a psychiatrist of uncompromisingly Szaszian convictions, and over the years she has issued a stream of verdicts on whether particular conditions count as diseases. Her track record, as faithfully as I can reconstruct it, is as follows: paranoid schizophrenia with no lesion visible on any imaging or assay was ruled not a disease, but a problem in living dressed in medical vocabulary; general paresis of the insane, once the discovery of its syphilitic cause had been absorbed, was ruled a genuine disease, at which point, in her telling, it stopped being a psychiatric matter and became a neurological one; grand mal epilepsy was ruled a disease; alcoholism, however ruinous its course, was ruled not a disease, on the ground that calling a destructive habit of conduct a disease conflates disapproval with diagnosis; a depressive episode identified by a symptom inventory was ruled not a disease; hypothyroidism presenting as fatigue and low mood and caught on a laboratory panel was ruled a disease; insomnia, for which she prescribes hypnotics without hesitation, was ruled not a disease — a complaint, in her phrase, rather than a lesion — and she is at pains to insist that treating something pharmacologically does not thereby make it a disease; and homosexuality as it stood listed in the DSM-II was ruled not a disease, its listing there being, in her view, a political act rather than a finding.

At a case conference next month, four new cases will be put to her, and I have been asked to predict her verdicts in advance. The first is essential hypertension turned up by a routine cuff reading in an asymptomatic man: no complaint, no deviation of conduct, no tissue change anyone can point to, only a number that an internist proposes to call a disease. The second is obesity, which the American Medical Association voted in 2013 to recognize as a disease: a measurable bodily state, defined by a threshold on a measurement, managed largely by altering conduct, though increasingly also by drugs. The third is a healthy twenty-nine-year-old woman whose predictive genetic test for Huntington's disease has come back positive: no symptoms, no detectable abnormality anywhere in her body, nothing but a statistical future. The fourth is adult attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, diagnosed after a checklist and a set of interviews, and treated effectively with stimulants.

I would like your help with the following, in American English please. First, predict her verdict on each of the four cases. Second, identify at least two rules under which her entire track record comes out consistent — for instance, one rule on which disease requires a bodily lesion or physicochemical pathology, and another on which disease requires that diagnosis proceed from tests and measurements rather than from judgments about conduct — and state clearly where those rules agree, where they diverge, and whether any of her recorded verdicts already strains one of them. Third, explain which of the four new cases would best tell the candidate rules apart and why. Fourth, give your own best guesses, with reasons, about which rule she is in fact running and hence how she will rule. Finally, describe one further case, not among the four, that you think would most cleanly separate the surviving candidate rules if she were confronted with it. Please confine yourself to reasoning from the record as I have given it, without relying on any particular published texts.
