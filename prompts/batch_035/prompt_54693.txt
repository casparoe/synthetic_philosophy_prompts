I have been assigned a fifty-minute section of a first-year jurisprudence course on the Hart–Fuller debate. The students will have read only short excerpts — the two 1958 Harvard Law Review essays, and a few pages of Fuller's The Morality of Law covering the fable of King Rex and his eight ways of failing to make law — so they will know the material exists without having any grip on the shape of the disagreement. Every one of them has already encountered the grudge informer case in their criminal law course, so I do not want the session carried by Nazi Germany at all: the debate is about the concept of law, not about whether the Nazis were bad, and I want the sequencing to establish that before any wartime example can hijack the room.

What I need from you is a plan for the explanation, not the explanation itself. Specifically:

1. Dependency order. Which pieces must come before which, and why. For instance, must the class first grasp what positivism is actually a claim about — the separation of law and morals as a conceptual thesis, not a moral endorsement of whatever the powerful happen to say — before Fuller's internal morality can be presented as an answer to it rather than as a list of sensible administrative habits? Where in the sequence should it become explicit that the entire debate is a dispute over the criteria for calling something law?

2. The carrying example. I am inclined to build the session around King Rex, but used against the grain: the students' unassisted reading of Rex will be that his eight failures are just lessons in effective governance. So the plan must show me where and how to force the question of whether the desiderata are genuinely moral standards or merely conditions of efficacy — the challenge Hart pressed when he reviewed the book, that a craft can have an exacting internal discipline (his example, as I recall it, was something like the poisoner's art) without thereby becoming moral. If you think Rex cannot carry that weight and something else should — an ordinary, morally neutral instrument such as a badly promulgated local ordinance or retroactive tax rules — say so, and specify exactly what the substitute must show and what it must not show.

3. The misconception map. I can predict three: that positivism means law has no moral content; that natural law means judges should follow conscience over statute; and that Fuller's principles are just good drafting advice. For each of these, and for at least two more I have not listed, tell me the point in the sequence where it should be headed off — not left for question time at the end — and what precisely heads it off. One complication I want handled with particular care: the students will naturally import Hart's rule of recognition from The Concept of Law into the 1958 exchange, where it had not yet appeared, and specifically the fact that a rule of recognition can make moral criteria legally decisive without the theory ceasing to be positivist — a concession that defuses the first misconception but is anachronistic as a reading of the debate itself. Decide whether the plan should deploy that concession explicitly as a later lens held up to the exchange, or avoid it entirely, and justify the choice.

4. The check. One closing question, and what a correct answer would have to contain, that would distinguish a student who has understood the debate's actual subject from one who has merely acquired a firmer opinion about obedience, or about the Nazis.

5. Refusals. What I should openly decline to cover in fifty minutes and say so. I am thinking of the later taxonomy of inclusive versus exclusive positivism, Dworkin, and the intricacies of the postwar German prosecutions, but I would welcome additions, each with a sentence on why declining it is pedagogically honest rather than a cop-out.

Please be concrete about timing and transitions: where each block begins, what it presupposes, and what it must leave in place for the next block to work.
