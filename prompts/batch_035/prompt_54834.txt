So here's my situation: I write novels, which means my native vocabulary for talking about communication is craft talk — POV, free indirect style, subtext, unreliable narrators, showing vs. telling. The one thing every novelist knows is that readers build most of the story themselves: the words on the page are maybe a fifth of it, the rest is inferences from what I select and what I leave out. And we exploit that on purpose — the character whose mother is never once mentioned, the scene framed so the crucial thing sits just outside the shot.

What I want is for you to take the philosophers' debate about whether lying is morally worse than merely misleading, and translate it into that vocabulary as honestly as you can, without smuggling in any philosophy dressed up as translation. On one side there's roughly the view that lying is special because you assert something you believe false, while saying something technically true that you've engineered so the hearer draws the false conclusion themselves is somehow cleaner — Grice's implicated-content machinery usually shows up here, and sometimes the Kantian case about the murderer at the door, where the thought is that a misleading-but-true answer might be permissible where a lie isn't. On the other side there's the view that whoever set up the inference owns the false belief either way, and the distinction is hair-splitting.

So map it out for me: asserted content vs. implicated content — is that the words on the page vs. what the reader constructs in the gaps? Where does that mapping hold, and where does it strain? And then the part I actually care about, the residue report: what in the philosophical debate has no counterpart at all in craft-talk? Because here's the thing — in my world, engineering a false conclusion in the reader's head without ever stating a falsehood is not a moral stain, it's the whole art, and we get *praised* for it. So is the residue there because my vocabulary is just missing the moral dimension, or because the philosophers' distinction is doing less work than they think once you see how communication actually runs? Be honest about which it is, and tell me if anything on the philosophy side genuinely can't be said in my terms.
