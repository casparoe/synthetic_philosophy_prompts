My politics and religion seminar keeps circling back to something and I can't tell if its actually a philosophical puzzle or just lawyers being pragmatic. In the US, courts basically refuse to strike down "In God We Trust" on the currency or "under God" in the Pledge, on the ground that these phrases are merely ceremonial — they've been repeated so often, the reasoning goes, that they've lost any real religious content. Yet the same courts are pretty strict about, say, a school principal delivering an actual prayer over the loudspeaker, even once. So what view of how language works does the first practice presuppose — that meaning drains out of a phrase through sheer repetition? And whose meaning matters: the state's intention, or how a reasonable (or a devout!) hearer takes the words? Can that really sit consistently with the second practice, where one fresh utterance counts as establishment of religion? I'd like a reconstruction of the implicit theory of meaning and speech acts here (is the state even the kind of thing that can "speak"?), an account of where the two practices pull apart, and a note on what would change if we replaced the "rote repetition secularizes" idea with the view that conventional meaning is fixed by what religious speakers and hearers still take the words to mean.
