So I've been reading LessWrong since about 2014, long enough to remember when commitment races were mostly a fun abstract worry about superintelligions in acausal trade and not something I'd ever have to apply myself. Well, the day has come. My cofounder and I are deadlocked over whether our startup pivots to enterprise sales. She knows me well enough to predict that if she credibly refuses to discuss the pivot any further, I'll cave and keep slogging away on the consumer product rather than blow the company up. And I'm sorely tempted to beat her to the punch — set up something that makes my refusal basically irreversible, like signing an LOI with an enterprise customer or announcing the pivot publicly, so that she's the one facing the choice between capitulating and mutual destruction. The classic race: whoever commits first grabs the surplus, and the other side's options collapse to "fold or burn everything."

Assuming you buy the standard rationalist framing (Newcomb-ish reasoning, your decisions being evidence about correlated agents, TDT/UDT-style updatelessness and all that), what are the most important implications of the commitment races literature for how I should actually handle this? Not the game-theory-textbook version and not the superintelligence version — what does it say to someone in a two-player standoff with real but bounded stakes, where we'll have to keep cooperating closely after the standoff resolves? I specifically want you to address whether the "commit early to grab bargaining power" logic even survives the fact that she's roughly as smart as I am and can run the same playbook, and what happens to the relationship equilibrium after one of us "wins" this way and the other knows they were beaten by a trick rather than persuaded by an argument.

A few requests. Answer in British English please. And since I've seen enough confident rationalist takes go sideways over the years, tell me explicitly what would change your verdict — which consideration, if it turned out to be wrong, would flip the advice — and where your reasoning is most likely to fail (my guess is somewhere around whether a human who knows she'll feel terrible about it later can actually make a "credible" refusal, but you tell me). Also don't just give me the reflexive "early commitments are fragile, ergo don't" line — actually engage with the asymmetry between us, e.g. that her threat is self-enforcing because it's just an accurate prediction of what she'll do, while mine would need costly external scaffolding to be worth anything.
