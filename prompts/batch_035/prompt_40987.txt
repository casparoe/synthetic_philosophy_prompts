The obvious case study is the whole Jane chain in season 2: Walt doesn't exactly kill Jane, he stands there and watches her choke after a decision (or non-decision) that takes maybe three seconds, and then via her grieving father, an air traffic controller, and a mid-air collision over Albuquerque, a hundred-plus strangers end up dead with Walt causally upstream of all of it. The show barely dwells on the crash victims -- it's like the writers dropped a nuke on the city and then moved on. Is that just bad writing or is it the point?

Here's my pet theory and I want you to stress-test it: in the early seasons the writers systematically *bank* luck in Walt's favor. His early crimes basically produce no visible victims -- he cooks meth and nobody we see dies of it, he lies to Skyler and it costs nothing, Tuco and the crazies who do die seem to "deserve" it -- so the audience is grading Walt's choices under lucky outcomes, which is exactly the condition where our blame comes out soft. Then season by season the writers collect on the debt, and by the end we're retroactively re-grading the same man. If my reading is right, the audience's shifting verdict on Walt is itself a demonstration of resultant moral luck: we judged his intentions partly by how the outcomes landed, and the writers control the outcomes. Does the show deliberately alternate "lucky" and "engineered" harms so you can never settle into either a pure-choice or pure-luck interpretation?
