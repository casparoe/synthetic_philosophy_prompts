I got into an argument at a pub quiz afterparty (of all places) about whether majorities can literally be trusted to track the truth. My friend is a big fan of the Condorcet jury theorem — the idea that if each voter is even slightly better than a coin flip at picking the right answer, and votes independently, then as the group gets bigger the majority's chance of being right goes to 1. He wants to use this to argue that we should defer to majorities on factual/political questions basically everywhere. I pushed back with what I take to be the obvious problem: real voters aren't independent, they read the same news, talk to each other, get moved by the same demagogues, and a lot of them vote expressively rather than sincerely. He said the theorem is "asymptotically robust" to small violations and that I'm nitpicking.

So here's what I want from you: give me your actual credence, as a number, that the Condorcet-style argument succeeds in vindicating epistemic deference to actual large-electorate majorities (say, national referenda on technically complex issues) — not in the idealized math, which I grant, but as applied to real voters. And decompose it: something like, the probability that the average voter is genuinely above 50% competent on referendum-style questions (and what "competence" even means when the question isn't a fact with a knowable answer), the probability that whatever correlation structure real voters have stays weak enough that the competence edge still compounds rather than washing out, the probability that sincere-vs-strategic voting doesn't break it, and any other terms you think belong (I keep going back and forth on whether non-binary option spaces — ranked choices, multi-candidate elections — should count as a separate term or just a failure of applicability). Give me a rough probability and one line of justification for each, then tell me how you combine them.

Two extra things. First, to make it concrete: suppose 10,000 voters, each 51% likely to vote correctly, but with a pairwise correlation of 0.1 in their errors (shared media environment, say). Does the majority still converge to truth as you scale n, or does correlated error cap the benefit? Walk me through the intuition, not just the verdict — my friend claims it doesn't matter, and I want to know if he's right before I concede. Second, tell me which single term in your decomposition you'd be least confident in and what evidence would actually move that number — like, would a study showing referendum outcomes flipping with question wording count, or is that evidence about competence rather than independence? I suspect our whole argument lives in exactly one of these terms, so if you can tell me which one, that'd settle the pub debate.
