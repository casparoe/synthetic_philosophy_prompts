So I stumbled on Turing's 1950 paper the other week, and the bit that's stuck with me isn't the imitation game at all -- it's right near the end, where he suddenly starts talking about education. His picture is roughly: instead of programming a machine to be intelligent, you make a "child machine" and teach it, the way you'd teach a human child, through something like training -- Turing even talks about rewards and punishments, with punishment meaning the teacher goes in and tweaks the machine's internals when it's on the wrong track. And there's this genuinely odd couple of sentences about how the teacher of such a machine will typically be quite ignorant of what's actually going on inside it, even though he can predict its behaviour well, because he's watched its whole education. Turing is apparently fine with this -- he treats the teacher's ignorance of the mechanism as no obstacle to the teaching being real.

And it hit me that this is pretty much how we actually run state education, and have for two centuries, give or take. A teacher with no knowledge of a pupil's brain -- no access whatsoever to what's happening inside -- delivers a fixed curriculum, largely through verbal instruction, to a room of children who are expected to learn mostly by listening and practising, with success measured by written exams that sample a fraction of what the pupil can do. Nobody thinks this is strange. But if you take Turing's learning-machine framing seriously, it raises the question of what this whole arrangement presupposes about human learning, and I can't find a clean answer anywhere.

So that's what I want you to do: reverse-engineer the implicit theory of learning that mass schooling rests on. Some things I'd like you to actually address: does the practice presuppose that learning is some kind of update procedure that verbal instruction can drive directly, a la the child machine, or does it presuppose something richer where the internal reorganisation only gets triggered by the instruction and could in principle have gone many ways? Does it presuppose a shared learning architecture across pupils -- which is presumably why one teacher lecturing thirty children at once seems sensible at all -- and what does that commitment say about the children who the method visibly fails, since we just... keep the method and label the pupils? Is there a story about why examinations are treated as adequate evidence of the internal state, given that Turing's teacher at least had access to the entire history of his pupil's training, whereas an examiner sees only the outputs at one moment? And the part I most want: work out which bits of the practice are strictly required by the presupposed theory and which bits would survive under rival theories of learning -- say, if learning turned out to be much more dependent on the internal state than the practice assumes, what would schooling have to change, and would it look more like apprenticeship, more like tutoring, or something we don't have a name for?

Start with your bottom line in the first sentence -- your one-paragraph reconstruction of what mass education presupposes about learning -- and then give the reasoning. No preamble and don't restate my question back at me.
