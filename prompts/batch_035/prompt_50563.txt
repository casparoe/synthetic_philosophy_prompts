my meditation group has been reading the dialogue where the monk Nagasena talks to King Milinda (the greek king, milinda of menander) and argues that "Nagasena" is just a conventional label for a bundle of processes. to make the point he asks the king how he arrived, and it was by chariot, and then he takes the chariot apart in effect: is the pole the chariot? the axle? the wheels? the yoke? is it some thing over and above the parts? the king has to say no each time, and Nagasena says the person is like that — no self to be found in or apart from the five aggregates, just a designation we use for the collection.

everyone in the group now leans on this as an argument, but for different things. one guy says: if the chariot picture is right then personal identity is just a convention, there's no further fact over and above the aggregates, so the "me" who will suffer tomorrow is basically a different conventional person, and worrying about your future suffering is like worrying about a stranger's. another guy pushes back: chariots have a designer and a unifying function that holds the parts together for a purpose, whereas nobody designed the aggregates; the chariot's parts sit still while the chariot exists but the aggregates are supposedly arising and passing moment to moment; and also nobody claims a chariot gets reborn, so the analogy is silent on exactly the bit that makes no-self hard (who, if anyone, inherits the karma, who attains liberation).

i want an audit of this analogy. specifically:

- start with the correspondence table — what plays the role of the parts, the assembly, the label, the user who needs to point at the thing
- then the disanalogies, and please sort them into ones that actually matter for what people are using the analogy for and ones that dont (i suspect the designer point is less fatal than it sounds but not sure)
- then what the analogy quietly imports — e.g. does the fact that a chariot is an artifact with observer-relative boundaries do any sneaky work?
- then the verdict on both uses: does it support the "dont care specially about future you" conclusion, and does it leave the rebirth/karma question in better or worse shape than before
- finish with whether there's a better analogy on offer for the no-self view — river, flame, flow of traffic, whatever — or whether all of them fail in the same way

no need for sources or citations, just the reasoning. sections in that order, a couple of paragraphs each is fine except the table obviously. also could you keep it in british english (behaviour, characterised etc) since the group is mostly in the uk and someone will moan otherwise. one more thing: dont just conclude "analogies are imperfect" — i know theyre imperfect, i want to know which imperfections bite and which ones dont, and what the chariot can still legitimately carry.
