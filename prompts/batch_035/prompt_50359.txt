I teach political philosophy at a British university, and this term a seminar on comparative conceptions of the person has been repeatedly obstructed by a single proverb: 'the nail that sticks out gets hammered down.' It first came up when a visiting colleague from East Asian studies used it to characterise the relation between person and community in societies she described as Confucian-inflected; a fortnight later a student seized on it to argue that such societies are structurally hostile to individuality as such — that the proverb, taken literally, describes a normative order in which distinctiveness is punished wherever it appears, so that anything resembling the Western ideal of self-realisation is simply unavailable there. I suspect the proverb is being made to carry weight it cannot bear, but I would prefer the matter laid out carefully rather than waved away.

Please construct the menu of precise claims the proverb could be expressing, ordered from the most deflationary to the most radical. I want, at minimum, the following candidates on the list:

1. A sociological generalisation: in certain societies, deviation from prevailing practice attracts sanction, and the proverb is an empirical observation about how such communities in fact behave.
2. A piece of prudential advice: whoever you are, if you stand out you will incur costs — a prediction addressed to the individual, which entails nothing about whether the community is right to impose them.
3. An outright endorsement: deviation ought to be suppressed, and the proverb expresses a norm of conformity rather than merely reporting one.
4. A constitutive thesis about the self: on some conceptions, a person just is a node in a web of role-defined relations, so that 'standing out' is not suppressed by such communities but conceptually unavailable to them — the proverb as a compressed metaphysics of personhood.
5. A reading confined to behaviour, contrasted with a stronger reading on which belief, aspiration, and inner life must also not protrude.

For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or false; and tell me whether it would actually support the student's inference to structural hostility to individuality, or whether his argument requires a stronger reading than anyone would defend.

Two further matters, since I suspect they are where the real work is being done. First, the student's argument appears to run together two distinct axes: a value axis (does the group's welfare outweigh the individual's?) and a constitutive axis (is the self constituted by its relations rather than prior to them?). A society could conceivably be collectivist on the first axis and robustly individualist on the second, or the reverse. Say where on this grid each reading of the proverb places East Asian societies, and whether the proverb itself determines any placement at all. Second, the cultures in question visibly celebrate certain forms of standing out — the exemplar, the master craftsman, the person who distinguishes himself within an assigned role. The proverb may therefore encode a distinction between standing out by excellence within a role and standing out by exiting or contesting the role. If so, that distinction, and not any general hostility to distinctiveness, is plausibly what the hammer is for; say whether this is so, and what follows for the student's argument. For the sake of the exercise, also suppose it were true that the proverb circulates more widely in Anglophone commentary about East Asia than in everyday speech within the societies it describes: what, if anything, would follow about which reading is really being deployed when it is used in a Western seminar room?

Deliver the readings as a numbered list I can bring to the next seminar, each with its verdict and its bearing on the student's conclusion, and close with a short statement of which precise reading his argument actually required and whether it is one anyone would defend.
