The following is a grading task for an exam in a philosophy of law course. You are the instructor.

**Exam question (40 points):** In their 1958 exchange in the Harvard Law Review, H.L.A. Hart and Lon L. Fuller debated the relationship between law and morality. (a) Explain the separability thesis Hart defended, and explain how the postwar West German grudge informer prosecution figured in his argument, including the two options Hart said were honestly available to the postwar court and which one he favored. (b) Explain Fuller's claim that law has an inner morality: its procedural character, its principal requirements, and why Fuller regarded the Nazi legal system's failures as failures of legality rather than merely of moral content. (c) Explain Hart's objection that a regime pursuing evil ends could nonetheless observe the principles of legality, and assess how well Fuller's position withstands that objection. (d) Finally, assess whether the disagreement between Hart and Fuller was substantive, concerning the nature of law itself, or largely verbal.

**Student's answer:**

Hart and Fuller disagreed about the connection between law and morality. Hart's legal positivism rests on the separability thesis: whether a norm counts as law is one question, and whether it is morally acceptable is another, and the first does not depend on the second. Fuller, a natural law theorist, holds instead that law and morality are necessarily connected, so that a sufficiently immoral norm is not law at all.

The grudge informer case illustrates why this matters. In 1944, a German woman, wanting to be rid of her husband, reported him to the Nazi authorities for derogatory remarks he had made about Hitler while home on leave; he was sentenced to death by a tribunal, and after the war the wife was prosecuted for the unlawful deprivation of his liberty. The postwar court held her guilty, reasoning that the Nazi statutes under which she had acted were contrary to the sound conscience and sense of justice of decent people and therefore not law. Hart praised this outcome: since the Nazi statutes were too wicked to count as law, the court did the honest thing in treating them as void from the beginning, whereas a retroactive statute would have been the dishonest option, punishing conduct that was lawful when performed. Hart conceded in the same article that any legal system requires a minimum content of natural law — prohibitions of violence and theft — which shows his position was closer to Fuller's than the labels suggest.

Fuller's answer to Hart is the inner morality of law, sometimes called procedural natural law. Its requirements are the familiar principles of legality: rules must be general, published, prospective, clear, non-contradictory, possible to comply with, reasonably stable, and administered as announced. Fuller argued that the Nazi system departed from these principles so severely that it lost the character of law — the failure was one of legality, not merely of moral content. But Fuller's view is not purely procedural: among the requirements of the inner morality is that the law must not be grossly unjust, since a rule of that kind cannot guide conduct in Fuller's sense. At the margins, then, procedural morality merges with ordinary morality.

Hart objected that the principles of legality are compatible with the pursuit of evil ends: a tyranny can operate through general, published, clear, prospective rules, just as a poisoner can operate efficiently. So the inner morality of law is not really a morality at all, but only a set of craft rules for effective governance. Fuller's best reply is that this understates things: sustained compliance with the principles of legality constrains abuse over time. Both sides are partly right. In the end, the two positions come to the same thing: both allow that courts may decline to apply wicked law, and both treat legality as a matter of degree. So the disagreement between them was largely verbal — a dispute about what deserves the name of law, not about what officials should do.
