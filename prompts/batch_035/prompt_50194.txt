ok so last night my friend and I had this argument that went on for like two hours about Buddhist no-self and i'm still annoyed about how it ended. she's been reading early Buddhist material, the dialogue with the monk Nagasena and king Milinda, and she kept reaching for the same two analogies. one: the self is like a chariot — when the king keeps asking what exactly the monk is, Nagasena points out there's no chariot over and above the wheels, the axle, the yoke etc., "chariot" is just a convenient label for those parts in an arrangement, and same for "Nagasena", it's just a label over the aggregates. two: rebirth without a self is like one flame lighting another candle — nothing travels across, the second flame is causally continuous with the first but neither identical to it nor completely unrelated, and that's supposedly how you get reborn with no soul doing the rebirthing.

she wasn't using these just as teaching devices, she was drawing a pretty strong conclusion: that once you see the self is a label, the whole question of personal identity becomes verbal. "will i survive death?" is like asking "is the chariot in the shed" — there's just parts, continuity, and causal chains, and any line you draw between survives and doesn't-survives is a convention we choose, not a fact we discover. so she thinks the entire debate (teleporters, fission, Parfit, all of it) is people arguing about where to put a word.

i pushed back that the analogies might be doing the work her conclusion can't do by itself. like, fine, no chariot over and above the parts — but chariots don't anticipate being disassembled. the parts-and-arrangement story seems to leave out exactly the feature (experience, anticipation, mattering-to-someone) that makes identity a question worth answering rather than a labeling decision. and with the flame, nobody lies awake worrying about the second candle; we care about our own continuation in a way the analogy just quietly drops. but i also wasn't sure how far my pushback actually gets me — maybe experience can also be redescribed as just more parts in an arrangement, and i couldn't say why not on the spot.

so can you audit this for me? i want the full correspondence for each analogy — what plays the role of the parts, the label, the arranging, the second flame, the gap between candles — then where each mapping genuinely breaks, and then the real question: does her conclusion (it's all convention, no fact of the matter about survival) follow from what's left of the analogies, or do they only carry her partway and she'd need something further that she hasn't given me? don't do the "some philosophers say X, others say Y" thing, that's exactly the move that made the argument go in circles. take a side: if you think she's right that it's all convention all the way down, commit to that and defend it; if you think the analogies stop somewhere, say exactly where they stop and what she'd need beyond them. and if you think i'm wrong about the anticipation point, tell me that too, plainly.
