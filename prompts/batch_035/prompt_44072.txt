I'm in a small reading group that's mostly game theory and AI strategy stuff (we've been going through bargaining, credible threats, that kind of thing), and last week one of the guys -- ex-quant, loves this stuff -- walked us through an argument on the whiteboard about what happens when agents get really good at committing to things. The conclusion seemed way too strong to me but none of us could find the hole, and it's been bugging me since. Here's the argument as we wrote it down, roughly:

(1) In a bargaining situation, whichever side has a credible, irrevocable commitment already in place when bargaining starts gets to set the terms: if I've genuinely locked in "I walk unless I get at least 60% of the surplus," and you haven't locked anything in yet, then your options are give me 60% or get nothing, and you take the 40%. (2) Commitments made earlier beat commitments made later, since if we can both commit, whoever does it first establishes the fait accompli. (3) The earliest possible moment to commit is, in the limit, before you know anything about the situation -- before you know who you're dealing with, what's at stake, whether the counterpart is friendly. Every piece of information you wait for is a window in which the other side commits first. (4) So between two otherwise similar agents, the one willing to lock in demands early, before learning, wins the bargains. (5) Agents who lock in before learning will sometimes have locked in to terrible terms -- threats to torch cooperative relationships over trivia, red lines against counterparts they'd have gotten along with great. (6) But by (4), agents that pause to learn lose bargains to agents that don't, so whether through competition or through agents upgrading themselves toward whatever policy wins, the pausing gets squeezed out. (7) So the winners are agents holding commitments they made blind, and the whole landscape fills up with deals much worse than what patient agents could have struck -- win-win cooperation collapses because everyone pre-committed to incompatible demands before they ever met. Basically the argument concludes that the better agents get at commitment, the more rationality selects for blindness, and there's no principled stop anywhere short of everyone having locked in maximally aggressive stands as their first act.

I can't tell whether the right response is "the argument is valid and (7) is just true, welcome to hell" or whether something sneakier is going on. Something about (3) felt off to one of the other people in the group but she couldn't articulate it either. Please act as a decision theorist who's seen every version of this kind of argument, think carefully, and maybe brainstorm a couple of candidate diagnoses before committing to one (ha). Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked fine to a bunch of smart people staring at a whiteboard, and what rejecting it costs -- if there's a version of the argument that survives your fix, I want to know that too.
