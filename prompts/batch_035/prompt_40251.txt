Write a dialogue between two reasonably smart friends, Maya and Dev, about whether artifacts can have politics baked into them. Maya is basically on Langdon Winner's side of the "do artifacts have politics" debate — designs can settle political questions without anyone passing a law. Dev is more of a social-construction guy: all the politics lives in decisions and uses, the artifact itself is politically inert, and calling a thing political is just a way of not blaming the people who chose it.

Don't have them rehash the famous examples everyone trots out (the low bridges, nuclear power vs. solar). Give them a fresh case to fight over: a mid-sized city that replaces every physical bus farebox with a smartphone app — no cash option, account required, card on file, and the app quietly logs where each passenger boards. The official justification was purely budgetary: fareboxes were expensive to service and fare evasion was eating revenue. Nobody in the decision chain was hostile to poor riders; it's politics without villains, which is exactly what Maya thinks is the interesting part.

Ground rules for the dialogue: Dev should NOT deny that unbanked and older riders get hurt — that'd be a strawman. He should insist the harm is done by the transit authority's decision and the maintenance contract, and that the app is just an instrument that would be harmless under different rules (cities could add cash kiosks tomorrow without changing the artifact). Maya should press Winner-style points: the mandatory account, the bank card requirement, and the boarding-location logging each quietly select who can ride, and they keep doing their selecting even when no official is currently doing anything bad. She should also use the fact that the logging feature outlived the budget problem it was installed for — the fare-evasion panic is over, the surveillance is still there, and noone has to renew that decision.

Make them actually respond to each other. Atleast twice, a character should change or refine their position under pressure rather than just repeating their opening speech. At some point have them notice they might be talking past each other about what "having politics" even means — the artifact settling a political question vs. the artifact being a political actor — and have them try to pin that distinction down explicitly. End unresolved but not as a tie: each should have conceded something real and specific, and the reader should be able to say what moved.
