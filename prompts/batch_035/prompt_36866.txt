I'm a novelist — three mid-list books, a past residency that got me onto its advisory board — and I now sit on the governing board of an international literary translation residency that brings writers from smaller-language literatures together for a season each year. We are chronically underfunded, and we have just been offered a five-year endowment by the cultural foundation of a foreign state. The state in question is a prosperous, orderly one-party country whose government is not shy about defending itself in terms of GDP growth, infrastructure, safety, and delivery; its cultural foundation funds libraries, translation prizes, and residencies abroad, and, as far as anyone can tell, attaches no editorial strings to any of it. Our board is split. One faction, led by our treasurer (a retired editor of great pragmatism), argues that the money translates books that would otherwise never be translated, that we can find no evidence of interference in any of the foundation's other grantees, and that the foundation's home government, whatever its politics, has plainly delivered a level of prosperity and stability its citizens seem content with — so that the "legitimacy through performance" defense of such governments, the one constantly rehearsed in debates about China and Singapore, does at least some real work, and taking its money is not so different from buying its products. The other faction, which includes me, worries that a residency's whole value is its reputation for independence, and that our name on the foundation's list of grantees is itself the purchase: it functions as an endorsement, a small brick in the wall by which such regimes argue that prosperity and order can substitute for consent, and that liberal hand-wringing about elections is a luxury of countries that can afford to be poorly governed.

Please take the role of a political philosopher who works on state legitimacy and on the ethics of institutional engagement with nondemocratic states — think of yourself as equally unsympathetic to autocratic apologetics and to moral grandstanding that costs nothing to the person doing it. Before writing, think carefully and sketch out, for yourself, the different things "performance legitimacy" can mean: deliverance of goods as a claim that satisfies the population, as an external observer's criterion for withholding criticism, as an exchange in which order is purchased at the price of political voice, and so on — the argument our treasurer is making only works on some of these readings, and I'd like the memo to show which. Then draft the decision memo I will circulate before our June meeting. It should have a clear, actionable bottom line — accept outright, reject, or accept with specific, enforceable conditions — and it must engage the treasurer's position seriously rather than restating that engagement with authoritarian states is disqualifying as such; give the strongest version of his side its full weight before explaining where it fails or where it applies. Please also say something concrete about exit: what would trigger our giving the money back, and how we would know. Write the memo in American English, and keep it short enough that board members will actually read it — say, under a thousand words.
