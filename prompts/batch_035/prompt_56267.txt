Retired physical chemist here, spent most of my career on phase transitions and melting/crystallization in confined systems, and lately I've gotten hooked on philosophy of language. Reading about the sorites paradox (the heap stuff) I came across the epistemicist position, which honestly shocked me: the claim that there IS a sharp cutoff -- some exact number of grains where it stops being a heap -- we just can't ever know it. My philosophy friend is on the other side, she's a supervaluationist, says borderline sentences are neither true nor false, or true only when they come out true on every way of sharpening the word.

Here's my question. When my friend tried to talk me out of epistemicism, I found myself defending it with an analogy from my own field, and I want to know if it actually holds up or whether I'm fooling myself. The analogy: "tall" is like "frozen." Water doesn't go gradually ice-ish. At -0.3°C it's ice, full stop, and the boundary is perfectly sharp even though our everyday word "cold" is vague and even though no human could tell you the exact microsecond a particular fluctuation solidifies. So the epistemicist says: there's a fact about grain #n, we're just too coarse to ever locate it, same as we're too coarse to see the exact freezing event without instruments. Seems to me that if you accept sharp-but-unobservable boundaries in thermodynamics, you should accept them in language.

But I know at least two things nag at me, and there may be more I haven't thought of. First, the sharpness of a first-order phase transition is a thermodynamic-limit phenomenon -- it only becomes exactly sharp as particle number goes to infinity. A finite droplet (I've measured these!) melts over a range of temperatures, with hysteresis on top of that. So in any real, finite system the boundary is fuzzy in a perfectly physical way. Language communities are very finite. Does that break the analogy, or does the epistemicist get to say the limit is an idealization that applies anyway? Second, the freezing point at 0°C is only sharp relative to a pile of stipulations -- one atmosphere, pure water, equilibrium -- and it's anchored by operational definitions we can actually run. The heap cutoff is supposed to be sharp with no convention behind it at all, which is a weirder kind of sharpness than anything in my lab experience.

So here's what I want from you: audit the analogy properly. Give me a correspondence table -- what plays the role of the order parameter, the thermodynamic limit, the external pressure, the instrument resolution, etc. Then tell me which disanalogies actually matter for the philosophical fight between epistemicism and supervaluationism and which ones I'm overthinking. Then the two verdicts I care about: (a) does the phase-transition picture support epistemicism over supervaluationism, or does it maybe fit supervaluationism better once you take the finite-size rounding seriously -- borderline cases as the "rounded" regime? and (b) which of my two worries, if either, is the real problem, and what would the epistemicist have to say to patch it. If the analogy fails but you think some other analogy from physical science would serve epistemicism better, sketch it in a sentence or two.

Please put your final answer in a fixed form at the very end: a last line reading "Verdict:" followed by a short phrase saying whether the analogy survives, breaks, or supports something weaker than full epistemicism. Keep everything above that line as the reasoning. And go easy on the jargon where you can -- I've read Williamson's name enough times to recognize it but not enough to paraphrase him reliably, so argue from the ideas, not from the literature.
