Last night a friend and I argued for hours about the problem of warning people ten thousand years in the future away from buried nuclear waste, and we ended at a stalemate I suspect can only be broken with some actual theory. The episode is real: beginning with the Human Interference Task Force in 1981 and continuing through a 1993 Sandia National Laboratories report for the Waste Isolation Pilot Plant in New Mexico, panels of semioticians, linguists, and engineers tried to design markers for waste repositories that would stay legible for ten millennia. Their proposals ranged from hostile landscapes — huge spikes bursting from the earth at all angles, blackened fields of house-sized stone blocks, earthworks shaped like lightning bolts — to layered inscriptions in every UN language plus Navajo, arranged so that successive levels of the site would convey, first, that something man-made is here; then that something man-made and dangerous is here; and finally the detailed explanation of what, why, and when. The report even drafted a model text meant to be evoked in any future language, opening with lines like "This place is a message... and part of a system of messages... pay attention to it!" and insisting that the danger is still present, in the reader's time, as it was in ours. The semiotician Thomas Sebeok went furthest of all, proposing in the early 1980s a folkloric relay: a body of legends about the sites, renewed every few generations by something like an initiated "atomic priesthood," so that the warning would be carried by continuous retelling rather than by any artifact at all.

My friend's position is that the whole enterprise is futile because meaning lives in the interpreter, not in the marks: whatever we build, future people will read into it whatever their culture disposes them to read, and a field of thorns may attract as easily as repel. My own position — half-formed, I admit — is that Hofstadter's account of messages in the chapter of Gödel, Escher, Bach he calls "The Location of Meaning" gives the problem its right structure: every message carries a frame message (this is a message; decode me), an outer message (here is how to decode me), and an inner message (the content proper), and the hard part is always the outer layer, which cannot be stated in the very language it is trying to teach.

What I want is a careful analysis of the marker episode through that three-layer scheme. Do the mapping rigorously: what plays the role of the frame message — the sheer anomaly of the landscape, its obvious artificialness? Where do the layered inscriptions sit, and is the Sandia report's tiered structure best understood as a bootstrapping ladder for the outer message, an attempt to build a Rosetta chain with no reader's language guaranteed in advance? Is the pictographic level really an outer message, or an effort to bypass the outer layer entirely by recruiting bodily responses — wariness of spikes, aversion to blasted land — that Hofstadter's theory assigns no role? Then tell me where the model breaks: Sebeok's relay shifts the outer message from the artifact to social transmission across generations, which may not even count as a message in Hofstadter's sense; does that expose a channel the theory never anticipated, or can the theory absorb it with a small extension? Finally, adjudicate our argument: does the episode confirm Hofstadter's picture that the burden of meaning is shared between message and decoder — with the decoders of 12,000 AD being the great unknown — or does it vindicate my friend's interpreter-side pessimism? I already know the standard worries (languages die, symbols invert their meanings, a skull and crossbones might someday promise treasure); what I want is a disciplined mapping and an honest account of what the frame/outer/inner distinction explains here, what it misdescribes, and where it is simply silent.
