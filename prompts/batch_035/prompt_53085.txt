Some years ago I taught myself Portuguese almost entirely from written material — novels, news sites, grammar books, message boards. I never heard it spoken and never met a Brazilian. I got genuinely good at it: I could read Machado de Assis, argue in comment sections, write letters. At some point I noticed something odd about my vocabulary. Every Portuguese word I knew was connected only to other words. My word for pineapple connected to the English word, to descriptions, to sentences the word appeared in — but I had never seen, smelled, or tasted one. Then I finally visited São Paulo, and in a market I held a pineapple and ate a piece of it, and I experienced what I can only describe as a click: a whole cluster of vocabulary reorganizing itself around a referent. Afterward my relation to that word felt anchored in a way it hadn't before.

Not all my words have had their click. I know the word saudade only through paraphrases, contexts, and its neighboring words, and I notice that my grip on saudade still feels qualitatively different from my grip on the pineapple word — more like knowing a route through a city than like knowing the city. So my questions are these. First, is there an established term in philosophy or cognitive science for the distinction between these two kinds of knowing what a word means — the kind that runs word-to-word and the kind that terminates in something that isn't a word? Second, was the click real, in the sense that my pre-trip word and my post-trip word are different states, or did the experience just deposit more word-like material — the memory of the taste being itself something I can describe to you in words? Third, and this is the part I keep going back and forth on: I also notice that I have never seen an electron either, nor has anyone, in the way I saw the pineapple, and yet I'd resist saying I don't know what "electron" means. So a large part of every competent speaker's vocabulary seems to be of the word-to-word kind, mine included. That makes me wonder whether the second kind of connection is really necessary for meaning, or whether it is a private sensation that happens to accompany some words for some people some of the time.

The reason I ask now is that I tinker with large language models, and I find myself assuming they have only the first kind of connection, permanently, because they learned roughly the way I learned before the trip. But I'm not sure that assumption follows from anything. Maybe the first kind of connection, at sufficient scale, just is meaning, and my "click" was decorative. Maybe the click was the acquisition of a new kind of connection I can't fully describe because it isn't made of words.

I've heard of the Chinese Room, but as I understand it, that's an argument that manipulating symbols by rules can't amount to understanding, whereas my question is different: I'm fairly confident I understood Portuguese before the trip — I could read novels, after all — so what I want to know is what the trip added, and whether it added anything that matters. If there is an established concept for exactly this, I'd like its name; but more than the name, I want the edges: what the concept covers, what it doesn't, how it relates to the arguments I've half-heard of, and whether my three questions about it are actually three questions or one.
