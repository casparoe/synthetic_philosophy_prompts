my friend and I had a three-hour argument over dinner about her ADHD diagnosis, of all things, and it ended with her telling me my views on psychiatric classification are "a pile of contradictions wearing a trenchcoat." I've been mulling it over since and honestly I want a second opinion on whether she's right. here's roughly where I stand after thinking about it (some of this comes from a course I took on philosophy of science, we read some Hacking on "making up people" and looping effects):

(1) psychiatric categories are not natural kinds like gold or hydrogen — the criteria for ADHD or major depression partly track institutional needs, insurance billing, school policy, whatever, and I'm fine with that. (2) looping effects are real and powerful: when you classify people, the people change — support groups form, people reshape their self-understanding to fit the label, clinicians' diagnostic habits shift, and the population being classified becomes a moving target. (3) and yet I still think there's a fact of the matter about whether someone has schizophrenia, independent of any classificatory practice — the dysfunction is there in the brain whether or not anyone has a word for it. (4) I think the DSM dropping homosexuality back in '73 was obviously right, and more than that, it shows a virtue of the whole enterprise: a good classification system should keep evolving in response to how the classified population and the surrounding society react to it, a system that never moved would be worse, not better. (5) I think psychiatry has made genuine epistemic progress — current categories carve closer to the real underlying dysfunctions than the ones from the 1950s did, even if there's a long way to go. and (6) a case verdict that came up in the argument: after multiple personality disorder got its footing in the DSM in the 80s, diagnoses exploded, and I think both of these are true — most of those patients genuinely experienced the symptoms, and the symptoms were largely produced by the diagnostic practice itself (the therapy culture, the media, the whole apparatus). I'm comfortable holding both halves of that.

her main attacks were: that (2) and (3) flatly contradict each other, that (1) and (3) can't both be true, and that (5) is impossible if the target keeps moving the way (2) says it does. she may be right about some of these but I don't think she's right about all of them, and I can't fully tell which ones. so — go through my list and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly, which only look like they're in tension, and for the real ones what the cheapest fix would be and what it costs me elsewhere on the list. please rank the tensions by severity rather than just flagging everything equally, and be specific about which commitment is actually generating the problem in each case. also curious whether (6) is a harmless side note or whether it quietly drags something else on the list down with it.
