a bit of a weird request but here goes. I'm a software engineer, zero philosophy background, and a few weeks ago a close friend of mine died pretty suddenly (34, heart thing nobody saw coming). I'm not doing great, and I'm the kind of person who copes by thinking stuff through, so I keep running into this old argument — I think it goes back to Epicurus, or Lucretius, one of those ancient guys. The idea is that death can't be bad *for you*, because once you're dead there's no you left for it to be bad for. The complaint has nowhere to land — kind of like derefencing a null pointer, there's just no object there to throw the exception. And there's a second version of it, the mirror thing: the stretch of time before you were born is a huge amount of not-existing, and nobody lies awake at night regretting that, so why dread the mirror image after you die. That all sounds really clean to me at 2am. Then at 3am the counterargument shows up: death is bad not because it hurts but because it *deprives* you — you lose all the good stuff you would have had, decades of it, and that looks like a genuine loss even if there's nobody around afterwards to feel the loss. And these two thoughts just chase each other around my head all night. Neither one ever quite addresses the other.

So here's what I want: turn that into a villanelle. The two refrains should be the two positions — one refrain line carrying the Epicurean thought (no subject, no harm; when death is present, you are not) and the other carrying the deprivation thought (all of it lost, everything you would have had). They need to be clearly distinguishable, so that someone who knows the philosophy could point at any line and say which camp it belongs to. Let the form do the real work: the whole gimmick of a villanelle is that the same two lines keep coming back in shifting contexts, never touching, which is *exactly* how these two arguments feel to me — each one returns intact, neither ever answers the other.

One more thing though — I don't want it to end in a shrug. In the last stanza the two refrains finally have to appear together as the closing couplet (that's built into the form anyway), and I'd like you to use that meeting to actually settle it. My gut says the mirror/symmetry argument is the strongest reply to the deprivation worry — that regretting your post-death nonexistence makes no more sense than regretting your pre-birth nonexistence — so lean that way if you can, but it has to feel earned inside the poem, not bolted on. Nineteen lines, standard villanelle rhyme scheme. Please don't make it a eulogy and don't put my friend in it by name — keep it about the argument, I need something I can read without falling apart. And it should still work as an actual poem for someone who's never heard of Epicurus.
