Context first, then the ask. I teach political philosophy at a state school, and through a colleague I got roped onto my city's Climate Advisory Commission — we're an unelected body that writes recommendations to the city council, which then does whatever it wants, which is usually nothing. In November we owe the council a recommendation on how the city should hit its 2030 emissions target, and the commission has split into three camps. Because I'm the one who wouldn't shut up about distributive justice at the last meeting, I've been tasked with drafting the memo the whole commission will fight over, so I want a strong working draft.

The three camps:

1. The price camp: a municipal fee on natural gas and gasoline, starting modest and rising on a pre-announced schedule, with revenue returned as a flat per-household dividend. Their story is the standard one: emissions are an externality, price the externality, and let millions of households find their own substitutions instead of the council guessing which ones to mandate. They add, correctly I think, that the dividend version is actually progressive on net, since low-income households spend less on carbon-intensive stuff than the dividend returns.

2. The cap camp: prices don't guarantee any quantity, only a cap does — and the atmosphere cares about quantities, not willingness-to-pay. They want equal per-person carbon allowances (they keep calling this "cap and share"), tradeable, so the total is fixed by construction and everyone starts with the same claim on it. They also have a moral point they hammer: with a tax, if you're rich enough you just keep burning, which means the tax treats the carbon budget as something you can buy your way through.

The price camp's rebuttal to that is one I find annoyingly good: every law prices violations — speeding fines, parking fines — so a carbon tax is just a fine for emitting, and nobody says parking fines make illegal parking legal. The cap people answer that fines price *exceeding* a prohibition, whereas a tax is payment for the act itself, so their instruments aren't parallel at all. I want the memo to actually engage that exchange rather than wave at it.

3. The degrowth minority (two members): both of the above are growth-compatible instruments for a growth problem. They say the 2030 target is unreachable under any plan that assumes the city keeps growing its output and population, so the memo should recommend non-marginal demand reduction — shrinking rather than expanding the airport, ending the sprawl-permitting pipeline — and should say plainly that pricing schemes just buy time for a system that can't decarbonize fast enough. They concede the political cost ("council will laugh us out of the room") but argue the commission's job is to say the true thing. The rest of us worry a memo council laughs at is a memo with zero emissions impact, and being right on paper while the city does nothing is its own kind of failure — though I notice that argument proves too much, since it would have us endorse literally whatever is popular.

You're a professional philosopher with a solid environmental economics background. Please think carefully before writing — do a bit of brainstorming about what's really at stake here (I suspect the fight isn't really about instruments, it's about whether prices can do moral work, but maybe that's too cute). Then draft the memo itself: a clear recommendation to council (a package or a conditional recommendation is fine if you think that's honest), reasons a council member could act on, a serious treatment of the strongest argument from whichever camp(s) you're not endorsing, and concrete next steps with a rough timeline before our November meeting. Keep the memo under ~900 words. No bibliography and no citations of papers — I can't check them from here, and I want arguments, not a lit review. I'll owe you a coffee.
