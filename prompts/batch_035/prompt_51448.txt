After a career in experimental physics — mostly neutron scattering and a great deal of curve-fitting to spectra I no longer believe in — I have spent my retirement reading philosophy of science and formal epistemology, and I have arrived at a position I will try to state precisely. Occam's razor, in its modern dress, is not a methodological prejudice or a fact about human psychology but something close to a theorem. The thought is this: assign prior probability to hypotheses in inverse proportion to their Kolmogorov complexity, as in Solomonoff induction, and your total predictive error relative to the best computable predictor is bounded by a term proportional to that predictor's complexity. Simplicity-preferring priors are then not merely convenient for finite minds; they are the priors of successful prediction, and the success of science is explained rather than assumed. I used to treat parsimony in model selection as a budgeting rule — don't spend free parameters you can't afford — and I have come to think it is a substantive claim about the world, or at least about our epistemic relation to it. What I want from you is a map of what would count against this position, sorted from fatal to merely awkward.

The defeaters I have either encountered or half-formed myself, and would like your assessment of each:

First, the universal prior is defined only relative to a choice of universal Turing machine, and the invariance theorem guarantees only that switching machines changes hypothesis complexities by an additive constant. In the limit that constant is invisible, but for any finite data set it can swamp the evidence: a description system natural for one machine can make arbitrarily complicated what another machine treats as primitive. Does this show that simplicity is relative to our descriptive conventions rather than a feature of nature, or is the defender entitled to treat constant-level disagreement as noise, and on what grounds — a reference class of naturally arising machines, a claim about which description systems the actual world has so far rewarded? This worry bites me personally: in my scattering days, whether a fit was parsimonious depended on which parameterization we wrote down, and I never had a principled answer to a postdoc who re-expressed our five-parameter model as a one-parameter one by absorbing the rest into a bespoke function.

Second, Solomonoff induction is uncomputable, and everything actually usable — minimum description length, bounded-complexity priors, whatever a real agent runs — is a truncation. How much of the philosophical work does the uncomputable ideal do? If only the approximations exist, are we entitled to the claim that the razor is principled rather than pragmatic, or does the argument quietly become the old pragmatic one wearing new notation?

Third, the guarantee concerns prediction, not truth. The convergence result holds whether or not the true hypothesis is anywhere near the front of the queue; if the generating process of the world is computable but astronomically complex — the laws simple, the initial conditions a monstrous digit string — or if the world is uncomputable in whatever sense a physicist is entitled to intend, then the razor's purchase on reality, as opposed to our predictions, needs a separate argument. A philosopher of my acquaintance puts it this way: the theorem vindicates Occam's razor for forecasting, but the razor as scientists use it licenses inference to explanation — choosing between empirically equivalent theories because one is simpler — and that abductive step is precisely what the theorem does not underwrite. Is that a distinction with a difference, or does the predictor who converges to the truth have everything the realist wanted?

Fourth, no-free-lunch-style worries: are there families of environments in which simplicity-weighted priors systematically lose, and does their existence show merely that the guarantee is relative to a class of worlds, or something that should actually trouble someone with my view — namely, that the razor is an a priori epistemology smuggled in as a result about the world?

Finally, and most importantly, I want an honest audit of whether my position has been qualified into unfalsifiability. When pressed on the machine-dependence, I find myself retreating from a claim about the world to a claim about agents: agents that predict well behave as if complexity-weighted priors, and the rest is bookkeeping about constants and idealizations. That retreat feels to me like principled humility, but it has the troubling feature that the resulting position seems to be true by construction — no observation could ever disconfirm it, since any predictor's success counts for it and any failure gets blamed on the approximation rather than the ideal. Distinguish the qualifications a serious defender of the view would themselves concede from those that amount to capitulation, rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say plainly what would genuinely move you, were you me — including, if this is the right verdict, that the view survives everything and that such survival is a symptom rather than a strength.
