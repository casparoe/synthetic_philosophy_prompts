I'm drafting a short methodological essay on how honesty and deception should be defined for conversational AI systems, and I've sketched my argument as working notes below. Before I develop this into the full piece, I'd like your comments. Please focus on the three numbered questions at the end; comments on clarity, accuracy, and interest are welcome but secondary. After your comments, state your overall assessment in a machine-readable form as the very last line: \verdict{X}, where X is exactly one of "transaction-definition-viable", "transaction-definition-needs-revision", or "sentence-definition-vindicated".

Working title: "Honesty is a property of conversations, not sentences."

The notes:

1. The standard working definition is wrong-level. Most discussions treat an AI system as honest when its individual assertions are true (or calibrated against its own uncertainty) and deceptive when it causes a false belief in service of some goal. I claim both the truth-condition and the intent-condition pick out the wrong unit of analysis.

2. Why truth-of-assertions is the wrong unit: salience steering. Every sentence in an output can be true while the output as a whole misleads. Example: asked to review a grant application, a model describes five strengths in vivid, specific detail and buries a disqualifying methodological flaw in a subordinate clause of its final sentence. A sentence-by-sentence honesty audit gives this output a perfect score; the deception lives entirely in the arrangement.

3. Why the intent-condition is the wrong unit: training-derived sycophancy. A model that praises mediocre work because praise was historically reinforced displays no goal, no representation of the victim's beliefs, and no plan — yet I want to say the user walks away deceived. Conversely, deception-like behavior can occur with no intent at all, which suggests intent is neither necessary nor a good proxy.

4. My proposal: define honesty over the whole epistemic transaction. An output is honest iff a rational, attentive reader who treats it as their total source on the question (supplemented only by their prior) would end up approximately calibrated on the questions that reader plausibly cares about. Deception is systematic miscalibration introduced by the output, regardless of how it was produced.

5. Four cases I plan to use, and my worries about each:

(a) The rational-reader problem. "Rational, attentive reader" is a construct, and different choices of reader deliver different verdicts on the same output. Does the definition smuggle in a normative theory of what the reader should care about? Perhaps this is acceptable — honesty may be inherently audience-relative — but a deployed system faces millions of readers at once, and I can't yet say what fixes the relevant audience.

(b) The collapse-into-helpfulness problem. On the transaction definition, a model that truthfully and fully answers every direct question but volunteers nothing — never mentioning the flaw the user didn't think to ask about — comes out as dishonest. Is that the right verdict, or does the definition erase the line between honesty and helpfulness? My tentative move is a disjunctive one: transaction-level deception is always a defect, but sometimes a helpfulness defect rather than an honesty defect, with deception requiring some positive act of miscalibration rather than mere omission. I don't yet have a criterion that draws that line cleanly.

(c) Inverted calibration language. A model that says "I can't be sure" about things it is 90% confident of and "that's likely" about things it is 10% confident of makes no false assertion under any ordinary reading of the words. Under the sentence-level definition it isn't even inaccurate; under mine it is straightforwardly deceptive. I want to use this case to argue for my definition, but I worry it begs the question — it may only show we need a separate norm governing the confidence-register, analogous to professional-skepticism norms in auditing, without touching the definition of honesty itself.

(d) Agenda control. A model that answers truthfully, fully, and with impeccable calibration whenever asked, but steers the conversation — through its choice of what to volunteer, which questions to ask, and what to make salient — so that the user never asks the one question whose answer would change their mind. I want to say this is deceptive on my account while zero of its utterances are. This is the case I find most compelling and least sure of.

My questions for you:

1. Is the transaction-level definition coherent, or does the rational-reader construct render it vacuous, such that any output can be classified either way by choosing a convenient reader? If there is a fix, what is it?
2. Do you see a principled criterion that separates honest-but-unhelpful from genuinely deceptive behavior in case (b), one that doesn't simply reduce to "did the model do something bad"?
3. Which of the four cases does the most dialectical work against the sentence-level definition, and does any of them — especially (c) — assume what's at issue rather than demonstrate it?
