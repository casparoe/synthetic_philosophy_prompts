My background is in machine learning, but I've been drawn into a long-running argument with a friend who studies affective neuroscience. She complains that popular talk of reinforcement learning agents "seeking reward" quietly assumes the reward signal plays, inside the agent, the role that pleasure and pain play in animals. But in the systems we actually build, the reward signal is a scalar supplied by the environment or by a reward model and consumed by the learning algorithm for credit assignment; once learning has shaped the policy, nothing in the deployed system consults that scalar again. Her analogy is dopamine: once popularly treated as a pleasure signal, it now looks more like a prediction-error signal used for learning. If that is the right picture even in animals, she says, then reward was never the same thing as valence there either, and a reinforcement learning agent has, a fortiori, even less claim on suffering. I find this persuasive but not conclusive, and I want an outside view with actual numbers rather than our two priors talking past each other.

So I don't want the usual dodge that it all depends on your theory of consciousness. Give me your credence that at least one reinforcement learning system in existence today — say, an RL-trained policy deployed in games, robotics, or content recommendation — has episodes of valenced experience in a morally relevant sense: that there is something it is like to be it, and that this includes states of negative or positive valence. Then decompose the number into its terms: (a) the probability that some broadly functionalist account of valence is correct, on which valence is fixed by what a state does within a system rather than by the substrate it is implemented in; (b) conditional on that, the probability that current RL architectures satisfy the relevant functional conditions — for instance, a persistent agent that monitors and reacts to its own internal states, a unified valuation signal that is consulted online during behavior rather than only during training, and motivational states that outlast the learning update; and any further terms you think genuinely belong in the decomposition, each with a number and one line of justification. Combine them, tell me which term the final credence is most sensitive to, and what evidence or argument would move that term the most.

Please commit: pick a number and stand behind the decomposition, rather than laying out the live positions and leaving the choice to me. If the answer turns on a specific account of valence or of moral relevance, name the account and say why you weight it as you do — and if you think my friend's dopamine analogy is doing more or less work than she supposes, say that directly too.
