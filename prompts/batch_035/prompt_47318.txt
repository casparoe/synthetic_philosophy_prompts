My roommate, a philosophy grad student, and her friend from a statistics programme got into a genuinely long argument at dinner last week about Buridan's ass, and I've been asked to referee — but I honestly can't. For context in case it helps: the seminar I'm auditing covered the medieval puzzle of the donkey placed exactly midway between two identical bales of hay which, having no reason whatsoever to prefer either, is said to starve. (1) My roommate's opening move was that if the donkey's preferences are perfectly symmetric, a perfectly rational donkey is doomed; so the idealization that rational agents always act on the stronger preference is incoherent, and rational agents need something like a tie-breaking rule — for instance, randomizing between the options. This, she said, shows that choice and preference can come apart: you can rationally choose without preferring. (2) The statistician replied that exact ties are a fiction: any two real bales differ somehow, and an agent genuinely responsive to all reasons will register the difference — one bale marginally greener, one walk marginally shorter — and that suffices. The paradox, he said, only threatens an agent idealized as having infinitely precise yet exactly tied preferences, which is like threatening a frictionless plane with wear and tear. (3) She pressed back: if any difference, however minuscule, can rationally settle the choice, then rational choice becomes hostage to noise — let a speck of dust land on one bale and the 'preference' flips — and we lose the idea that what you prefer should track what matters. Better, she said, to allow indifference-tolerant choice than to pretend every tie is secretly broken. (4) He conceded that ties might be real but denied that randomization is any solution: flipping a coin is itself a choice made for no reason, and if you're permitted that, you may as well let the donkey simply amble to whichever bale catches its eye for no reason at all — so her 'choice without preference' either smuggles in a covert preference for the lottery's fairness properties or is just a confession that rationality runs out. (5) Her last word was that coin-flipping is not irrational but a policy that guarantees the expected value of the options whenever you are indifferent — kidney exchanges and school-placement lotteries already work this way — and that calling it 'choosing for no reason' conflates reasons for adopting a policy with reasons for its particular outcome.

It would help me if you were explicit about which moves are clearly right, which are clearly mistaken, and which are defensible only under a particular reading.
