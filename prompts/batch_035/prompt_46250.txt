Each spring our engineering team publishes a satirical piece on the internal blog — normally we skewer some framework or cloud vendor, but this year I'd like to aim at an argument instead of a product. The argument is the one my friends and I keep circling: that a single person's contribution to a diffuse harm makes no difference, so there's no real point in any one of us recycling, skipping meat, flying less, or voting. I build distributed systems for a living, and that's probably why the argument gets under my skin: an outage in a fleet of a thousand nodes is never "caused" by any single request, yet the outage happens and the postmortem still assigns ownership. I've come to think the individual-makes-no-difference reasoning is stronger and stranger than it sounds, and I want the satire to respect that rather than sneer at it.

Please write an Onion-style news article — headline plus roughly 450 words — about a local man who has adopted this reasoning with total, unnerving consistency. He doesn't vote, doesn't sort his recycling, idles his engine in the driveway; the trash truck comes whether he recycles or not, and the flight departs whether he's on it. Give him deflection maneuvers that are actually good: when a neighbor asks what would happen if everyone thought this way, he calmly points out that not everyone does, and that his changing his mind about it doesn't change theirs. Include at least one moment where a reporter pushes him toward the hard cases — whether he'd feel differently if his candidate had lost by exactly one vote (a retroactive threshold problem), or what he'd say about the members of a firing squad, none of whose bullets was individually necessary. And give him one visible asymmetry the reader can catch: he does buy lottery tickets, reasoning that a tiny chance of a huge payoff is worth five dollars, while insisting a tiny chance of a decisive vote is worth nothing.

Some constraints on the piece. Keep it deadpan — no winking narrator, and the man should be sincere, articulate, even likable. The jokes must come from the real structure of the reasoning (threshold effects, overdetermination, expected value, the coordination problem) and not from strawmanning him, so that a philosopher who finds the argument troubling laughs in recognition rather than irritation. Outside the deliberate lottery asymmetry, don't let him contradict his own principle — his consistency should be part of what unsettles the reader. It's fine, even good, if he does direct one-on-one good deeds (handing his lunch to a homeless man, say), since on his view clearly-causal actions register even when diffuse ones don't. And no comeuppance ending: close on him at rest, untroubled, with the reader left holding the genuine question of whether he's wrong about any of it, and if so, where exactly the reasoning breaks.
