For my school's mathematics club I have to submit a short written statement of my views on how probabilities should be assigned when we have no special evidence one way or the other, so the other members can challenge them at the next meeting. Before I submit it, I would like an outside check on whether my positions are consistent with one another. Here is my current set of views:

(1) The principle of indifference is the correct starting point: when the evidence treats a set of possibilities symmetrically, we should assign them equal probabilities.

(2) Indifference can only ever be applied to some particular variable — endpoints, radii, midpoints, angles — and there is no further, description-independent fact about which variable is the natural one. So in Bertrand's chord problem, which asks for the probability that a chord drawn at random in a circle is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, the three classical answers are each correct relative to their own choice of variable: 1/3 for choosing one endpoint on the circumference and the second endpoint uniformly along the circumference; 1/2 for choosing a radius and then a point uniformly along that radius, drawing the chord through the point perpendicular to the radius; and 1/4 for choosing the chord's midpoint uniformly over the area of the disk. On my view the problem simply has no single correct answer — probability assignments of this kind are relative to how we choose to describe the setup.

(3) At the same time, I find Jaynes's invariance argument very convincing. His point, as I understand it from his 1973 paper "The Well-Posed Problem," is that the statement of Bertrand's problem specifies no position or size for the circle, so any objective answer must be unchanged under translations, rotations, and rescalings; and when one solves the invariance conditions directly, they determine a unique distribution, which yields 1/2. This seems to show that at least some problems of this kind do have objective answers after all, fixed by the symmetries of the information we possess.

(4) The correct general method for assigning probabilities is the maximum entropy principle: among all distributions compatible with what we know, choose the one with the greatest entropy, with indifference as the special case where we know nothing. I take this to give the unique rational probability assignment whenever it applies.

(5) Probabilities measure an agent's state of information rather than any physical randomness in the world; two observers with different knowledge can correctly assign different probabilities to the same event.

I have tried to make these fit together, but I worry I am holding onto things that cannot all be true. Please tell me which of my positions genuinely conflict, which only appear to conflict, and what the least costly revision would be for each real conflict. Rather than listing every possible friction as though they were equally serious, rank the tensions by severity, and identify the single change that would clean up the most.
