I teach a philosophy of mind course for cognitive science majors — about 16 students, one 90-minute session. Design an in-class game that makes them feel the force of Harnad's symbol grounding problem: the point at which a system can manipulate symbols with flawless internal consistency — sort them, transform them, answer questions, even catch its own errors — while nothing anywhere in the system fixes what any of it is about.

The engine of the game should be a closed symbol economy: tokens whose values are fixed only by other tokens, the way exchange rates are defined against each other or sorting rules are defined by prior sortings, with a strict ban on explaining anything in natural language. I want two conditions — one where the tokens are quietly grounded by ostension to real objects or colors in the room, one where they never touch the world at all — and the crucial design constraint is that students in both conditions should end up equally fluent, so fluency by itself cannot tell them which condition they were in. The reveal should then turn on some reference test that the grounded group passes and the ungrounded group can meet only by luck.

Afterwards, a debrief that maps specific moments onto parts of the problem: the difference between sorting tokens correctly and knowing which tokens pick out what; the difference between grounding a symbol and merely inferring its role in the economy; and where the students, in the ungrounded condition, stood relative to a large language model trained only on text. No lecture up front — they should not hear the name of the problem until the debrief.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I expect the English ban to leak, and someone to believe they have grounded the symbols when they have only reverse-engineered them — and be candid about the one feature no version of this activity can reproduce: my students arrive already grounded, so their background semantics will smuggle meaning into the game no matter how I set it up. Tell me how to handle that in the debrief rather than pretending it away.
