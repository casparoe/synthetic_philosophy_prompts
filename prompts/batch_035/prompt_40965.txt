My closest friend and I spent most of last Saturday arguing about what utility is, and we ended the evening nowhere — which convinces me that at least part of the time we were talking past each other. I hold something like the revealed-preference line: utility is whatever choice behavior, suitably cleaned up, represents, and there is no further fact about how well someone's life is going that stands behind their choices and could contradict them. She holds the opposite: utility is a psychological quantity — hedonic experience, satisfaction, something at least imperfectly reportable — and choices are at best noisy evidence about it, and at worst systematically misleading evidence, given anchoring, adaptation, and preference reversals.

Over the course of the argument she introduced an idea I have not been able to shake off since. She asked me to consider choices made by the same person under two different decision procedures: an instantaneous choice under time pressure, and a choice made after a week of deliberation with the full option list written down. When these diverge — same options, same person, same stakes — which one reveals the preference? My instinct was to say the deliberated one, because that choice is more really hers. But she pointed out that this concedes the whole game: if I can rank the two decision procedures by how well they track her utility, I must be applying a standard of utility that is not itself revealed by either choice. If instead I bite the bullet and say that both are equally her preferences, then her preferences fragment into an indexed family — preference-under-speed, preference-under-reflection, preference-under-whatever-else — and the theory stops representing a person at all.

We never resolved this, and I want to know how much of our argument was substantive and how much was merely verbal. Please draft the joint statement we should have produced at the end of the evening, structured as follows: a numbered section of claims both of us could sign, stated precisely enough that neither would feel the need to add a footnote; a table of our remaining disagreements, with each side's position phrased so that that side would accept the wording; the cruxes — the subquestions such that settling one would move at least one of us — including exactly where the deliberation-indexing problem falls and what each of us would have to concede if it cuts against us; and, for each crux, a concrete case, experiment, or argument both of us would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about the outcome recorded in advance. If you conclude that part of our dispute is only about how the word utility should be used rather than about anything in the world, say which part and why. Please keep it in the register of a document two people who like and respect each other could actually co-sign: no strawmen, and no false convergence either.
