I have taught the epistemology of scientific thought experiments for many years, and every time I return to the Norton–Brown exchange I find it running the same loop. On one side stands John Norton's deflationary view: a thought experiment is, at bottom, just an argument in picturesque dress, with no epistemic powers beyond ordinary argumentation; whatever new belief a thought experiment produces must be underwritten by premises we already accepted, and the celebrated imagery contributes at most to how easily or reliably we execute the inference, not to its justificatory force. On the other side stands James Robert Brown's Platonism: a class of thought experiments — Galileo's reasoning about a heavy and a light stone falling together, or Stevin's chain draped over a triangular prism, which must hang in equilibrium lest it move forever — do not merely restate prior commitments. They overturn a law or reveal a new one on the strength of a single imagined case, with no new empirical data and no awareness of any inference, and Norton's reconstructions, Brown insists, are lifeless epicycles that capture the result while losing the phenomenon. The dialectic between them has become a fixed script: Norton reconstructs the case as an argument from old premises; Brown declares the reconstruction foreign to what the physicist actually experienced; Norton replies that if the experience does not cash out into an argument, its pretensions to produce knowledge should be denied rather than honored. Decades on, neither seems to have conceded anything, and younger contributions increasingly just pick a camp.

I want a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement rather than a verdict on its object. My own hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that the two men are not answering the same question: Norton is answering a justificatory question — what are we entitled to believe, on reflection, given only our antecedent commitments — while Brown is answering an acquisitive one — where do new commitments genuinely come from, in the psychology of discovery — so that each side's criterion of success is invisible to the other, and each can correctly declare victory by its own lights. Is that right? Or is there instead a single substantive issue here, namely whether the imagistic or experiential form of a thought experiment carries evidential weight in its own right — something that a decisive case would settle, for instance a thought experiment whose pictorial presentation is essential to its persuasiveness, such that the inference loses its force completely when restated as a linear argument? Or should we say something harsher: that Brown's Platonic perception of laws is not so much false as ill-posed, and that the debate persists only because the notion of seeing a law is never made precise enough to attack? In your answer, please engage what each side actually claims, tell me what kind of disagreement this is — verbal, substantively factual, or a clash over which cases count as the data — and then say what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis: a conceptual distinction, a formal result about what reconstructions are available, evidence about how people actually process imagined cases, or a well-grounded verdict that the question is hard but perfectly genuine, with reasons why the impasse we observe is the ordinary friction of a hard problem rather than the symptom of a misposed one.
