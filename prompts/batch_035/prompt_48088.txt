Write a satirical op-ed, roughly 600 words, in the voice of the Public itself — the phantom entity Kierkegaard diagnoses in The Present Age (1846). The conceit: after a century and a half of apprenticeship under the party press, the Public has been reborn in the feed and, flushed with success, has decided to write its first newspaper column. Deadpan self-congratulation throughout: the Public explains that it is everything and no one, that it cannot be interviewed or held responsible (there is no one home), that it holds passionate opinions about everything at a comfortable remove, and that its great work — leveling — is nearly complete, since no distinction of excellence, expertise, or character can survive its attention. Let it linger lovingly on its own mechanics: envy as its animating principle, not as petty malice but as the great equalizer that can't bear anyone standing taller than the crowd; the worship of the majority, the idea that a headcount can settle what reflection cannot; opinion as a good without ownership, since to commit publicly and personally would be to become an individual, which the Public cannot afford. The strongest joke should be structural rather than verbal: the Public's op-ed should demonstrate leveling by performing it — every figure it addresses is dissolved into "everyone," every question it raises is answered by noting that everyone is talking about it, and the closest it comes to self-disclosure is admitting that it exists only when everyone is alone. Where it gestures at the press, the update to feeds, shares, and engagement metrics should be unmistakable but never named as a contrast — the Public treats them as mere refinements of what the newspapers already accomplished. Keep the irony legible without winking: the reader should feel the satire landing on themselves as much as on the phantom.

Then append a critical note of 150–250 words, separated from the op-ed, in which you own up to the piece's interpretive debts and weak points. Say plainly which of the op-ed's claims are genuinely Kierkegaard's (the Public as a phantom that is everywhere precisely when everyone is alone, leveling, envy as the negative principle, the press as an abstraction that lets people be spectators) and which are your extrapolation to algorithmic media, since Kierkegaard was writing about Copenhagen's newspapers and could not have had the feed in view. Flag the places where the satire leans on a contested reading — for instance, whether online publics level in his sense or merely amplify, or whether the piece smuggles in Kierkegaard's own aristocratic suspicion of the many under the cover of his analysis. Finally, name the consideration that would make the piece misfire: what argument or evidence about contemporary public discourse, if it were correct, would show that the satire targets the wrong thing, and say where in the op-ed that vulnerability is most acute. If you find yourself unable to be fair to a possible critic of the satire within the space limit, cut a joke rather than cut the honesty.
