I'm drafting a paper for a general philosophy audience on whether it is ever correct to attribute beliefs to large language models, and I'd be grateful for your comments on the argument as sketched below. Please write in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way one would write a long email — and lead with your overall verdict before working through the details.

The draft's core is a distinction I haven't seen made cleanly elsewhere, and I want to know whether it can bear the weight I'm putting on it. The argument runs as follows. Dennett's intentional stance, on its standard reading, licenses belief attribution whenever adopting the stance buys predictive leverage; but this licences attributing beliefs to thermostats and chess engines, so it underdetermines the philosophically interesting question, which is not whether the stance is useful but whether the system is a genuine believer in something like the real-patterns sense. I then propose a further distinction, between systems that are mere carriers of belief-like states and systems that are origination points for them. A language model's apparent beliefs are inherited wholesale from its corpus, in roughly the way a parrot's utterance borrows its content from the sentence repeated; so the states, insofar as they are real, are attributable to the culture that produced the training text rather than to the model. Attribution to the model itself becomes appropriate only when the system can revise a state in a way not traceable to input, through fine-tuning or accumulated interaction. The upshot is that most of what we currently call a model's beliefs are misattributed, though the class of model-owned beliefs is not empty and may grow.

Three things I'd especially like from you. First, does the carrier-versus-origination distinction survive scrutiny, or does it collapse under the obvious objection that most human beliefs are also inherited from testimony and culture, so that the distinction, if it excluded the model, would exclude nearly everyone? Second, is the parrot analogy fair, given that a model must reconstruct the patterns that generate its corpus rather than merely replay particular strings? Third, where would a committed Dennettian push back hardest — I suspect the reply is that origination was never a requirement on real patterns, and that I have quietly helped myself to a stronger, non-stance-based criterion of belief without arguing for it. I'd also welcome your candid view on whether the argument is interesting or merely a relabelling of familiar points about testimony and internalism, and any suggestions for strengthening the weakest section. I have a fuller draft of the middle sections and can send it if it would help.
