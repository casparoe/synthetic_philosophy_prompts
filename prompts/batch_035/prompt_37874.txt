At a recent meeting of my social choice reading group, two members — I will call them Marta and Jonas — argued about what Harsanyi's theorems actually establish, and the argument ran through five exchanges without either of them budging. I was a bystander with no stake in the outcome, and I found both of them persuasive at different points, which is why I am bringing the exchange to you. I would like a move-by-move adjudication rather than a verdict on the general merits of utilitarianism.

The exchange, as faithfully as I can reconstruct it:

(1) Marta opened by claiming that Harsanyi's aggregation theorem gives utilitarianism a mathematical foundation. The setup: each individual has preferences over lotteries of social states that satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms; the social preference ordering over the same lotteries also satisfies them; and the social ordering respects Pareto — whenever every individual prefers one outcome to another, society ranks it higher as well. From these premises, she said, it follows that social welfare must be a weighted sum of individual utilities. That, she insisted, is not the usual philosophical hand-waving; it is a theorem, and its conclusion is as secure as its premises.

(2) Jonas replied that a theorem can only be as strong as its premises, and here the premises deliver the advertised conclusion only in appearance. The theorem shows that the social ordering is representable by some weighted sum of individual utilities — but each individual's utility function is fixed only up to a positive affine transformation chosen separately for each person, and nothing in the axioms pins down the weights or the interpersonal scale. The axioms are therefore consistent with an enormous family of social orderings — any weights, any interpersonal scalings, including wildly inegalitarian ones — and the theorem does nothing to select the utilitarian member of that family, the one with equal weights and a common scale. What does the selecting, if anything does, must be added from outside the mathematics; so the theorem narrows the field at best, and the narrowing is much less than utilitarians advertise.

(3) Marta answered with the impartial observer construction. Suppose a rational chooser must endorse social arrangements without knowing whose life she will lead, holding an equal chance of being any member of society. If, when imagining herself in person i's circumstances, she evaluates those circumstances by person i's own utility function, then expected-utility maximization in that position amounts to maximizing the unweighted sum of everyone's utility. Equal weights, common scale — everything Jonas said was missing is supplied by the veil, and the conclusion follows with the same rigor as before.

(4) Jonas said the veil supplies these things by fiat, not by argument. Why equal probabilities, and why an observer with no independent attitude toward ending up at the bottom? An observer who dislikes doing badly — a natural stance for anyone gambling with lives, including what might turn out to be her own — reaches a prioritarian or maximin-type conclusion from the very same apparatus, so the utilitarian result looks like an artifact of stipulating a risk-neutral, distribution-indifferent observer. He then pressed a fairness objection: consider an indivisible benefit that can go to one of two equally placed people. The deterministic allocation to one of them and an even-chance lottery over who gets it have the same expected sum, so the framework ranks them as exactly equal — it cannot even register that one arrangement gives everyone equal prospects and the other does not. The apparatus is blind to fairness by construction, he said, and then triumphantly proves that fairness does not count.

(5) Marta closed as follows. Jonas was demanding that the theorem do work it never claimed to do. It is a representation theorem: it characterizes which social orderings cohere with Pareto plus the von Neumann–Morgenstern structure, and that is genuine content — it rules out real alternatives, including egalitarian orderings whose indifference curves are not straight lines in utility space. If Jonas's deeper complaint is that von Neumann–Morgenstern utility is the wrong currency — that it tracks attitudes to risk rather than the intensity of well-being, so that summing it is not summing anything morally weighty — then that is a substantive objection to the interpretation of the result, not a defect in the result itself. And as for the observer's risk attitude, Harsanyi's construction has the observer adopt each person's own utility function when imagining that person's lot, so her personal risk aversion need not contaminate the evaluation.

They broke for coffee at that point and never resumed.

Please adjudicate the exchange. For each of the five numbered statements, say whether it is right, partly right, or mistaken, and justify the rating — I care especially about whether Jonas's underdetermination complaint in (2) is as damaging as he thinks, and whether Marta's reply in (5) actually answers his point in (4) about the coin flip or merely concedes it. Then tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing. Finally, give your own view of the underlying question: on its best construction, does either version of Harsanyi's argument support sum-ranking as a doctrine of justice, and if not, what does it establish instead?
