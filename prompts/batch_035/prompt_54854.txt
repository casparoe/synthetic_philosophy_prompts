I am a retired pharmacologist — thirty-five years in drug safety and toxicology, the last decade of my career spent on regulatory advisory committees — and since retiring I have been working my way into philosophy of science. A question I cannot settle, and which the textbooks I have found treat too abstractly for my taste, is the precautionary principle. Rather than argue about its formulation in the abstract, I would like to proceed from my own verdicts on real cases, most of which I lived through professionally, and have you tell me what principle, if any, they add up to.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher of science with expertise in risk regulation and decision-making under uncertainty. Reason carefully and do not flatter me. I would like you to begin with some brainstorming: set out at least three candidate principles before committing to one, and explain why you discarded the ones you discard. No reading lists or citations are wanted; I want the analysis itself.

Here are my verdicts.

1. Thalidomide. I was a junior researcher when this broke. The American reviewer who held up approval in 1960–61, demanding more data while Europe had already licensed it, was right — and this although at that point there was no proof of harm, only a reviewer's suspicion. Withholding pending evidence: correct.
2. Asbestos. I believe industry and regulators were wrong to insist on epidemiological proof before restricting it. By the mid-1960s the case should have been closed on the strength of worker mortality data and animal carcinogenicity alone; waiting for definitive proof cost lives. Acting on suspicion: should have come earlier and harder.
3. DDT. I supported the American ban of 1972 — the eggshell-thinning work, the persistence, the carcinogenicity signals. Right call.
4. CFCs. We restricted them before anyone had observed an ozone hole, on the strength of atmospheric chemistry and a handful of measurements. I think that was right, and I think the same about the Montreal agreement that followed.
5. Saccharin and the Delaney clause. This one embarrasses me. I endorsed the Delaney clause in principle — no food additive shown to cause cancer in animals, full stop — yet I thought the attempted ban on saccharin in 1977 was absurd: the rats were dosed at levels no human could physically consume. I still think the clause was right in principle, and I still think the saccharin episode was silly. Reconcile that if you can.
6. BSE. The feed ban of 1988 was the right call. And I thought ministers reassuring the public that British beef was safe around 1990 were wrong: the species barrier was unknown, the incubation period was long, and the cost of being wrong was catastrophic. Precaution was owed earlier, and more strongly, than it was given.
7. GM crops. I think the de facto European moratorium of the late 1990s was wrong. A testing regime, certainly; a blanket block on already-characterised products, no. By any evidential standard I would accept, that was over-precaution.
8. Neonicotinoids. I think the European partial ban of 2013 was right — and this although the field evidence was contested at the time; it was largely laboratory evidence of harm to pollinators, plus the sheer scale of what pollination services are worth. Suspicion plus stakes was enough.
9. Mobile-phone radiation and brain tumours. I thought funding research was reasonable, but I regarded the precautionary campaigns of the 2000s as overdone: if there is an effect it is too small to matter against the base rates. Precaution inappropriate.

You will see my trouble. For thalidomide, asbestos, BSE, and the neonicotinoids I demand action on suspicion. For GM crops I refuse to act on suspicion, even though the arguments offered — novelty, spread, irreversibility — were structurally similar to those I accepted in the other cases. And for mobile phones I dismiss precaution outright. I would like to know whether there is one principle that delivers all nine verdicts, and if so what it is, stated precisely, clause by clause, each clause tied to the case that motivates it. If there is not, tell me which of my verdicts has to go and why, and be explicit about the choice between a complicated principle that saves every verdict and a simpler one that overturns some. I would also like the principle's predictions for two cases I have not listed: PFAS contamination of drinking water, and antimicrobial nanosilver in consumer textiles. Finally — and here I suspect the philosophy bites — consider honestly whether my pattern tracks anything less flattering than a principled distinction: familiarity, the exoticness of the hazard, or a status quo bias in which old risks are tolerated and new ones must prove themselves. Tell me what my verdicts reveal about me, not only what I reveal about them. There is also one case I cannot give a verdict on at all, because it changes with the day: deliberate stratospheric aerosol injection to cool the climate. If your principle has something to say about why I oscillate there, I would like to hear it.
