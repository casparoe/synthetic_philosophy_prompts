Right, so: I help run a reading group that's been going three years — a mix of historians and a couple of philosophers of science — and it's split clean down the middle on one question, and the arguement has started going in circles, which is a bit embarassing. Half the group (the continuity crowd) think 'the Renaissance' and 'the Scientific Revolution' are filing labels invented after the fact — Burckhardt for the one, and a general twentieth-century habit for the other — and that if you look at what people actually did, taught and read between roughly 1300 and 1700, you get one long continuous tradition full of local changes, with no more of a 'break' around 1500 than around plenty of other dates you could pick. The other half think something genuinely discontinuous happened: that by 1700 the way educated Europeans did natural philosophy, and the cultural standing it had, differed in kind, not just degree, from 1400 — so the label tracks a real joint in history, not a librarian's convenience.

We've decided to stop talking past each other and actually write something down, adversarial-collaboration style. Please draft the joint statement we should be able to produce. Sections, in this order, please:

1. **'What we both accept'** — numbered, roughly eight to ten claims, a sentence or two each, about 350 words total. This should include the boring factual stuff nobody at the table actually disputes: that scholastic Aristotelianism was still being taught in the universities well into the seventeenth century, that Copernicus dressed his case up in appeals to ancient authority, that a lot of what we call Renaissance humanism was about recovering old stuff rather than celebrating newness, and so on. The test of this section is that both halves would genuinely sign every line — nothing smuggled in that one side would balk at.

2. **'Where we still differ'** — a table, six or seven rows, one disputed claim per row with the continuity reading and the rupture reading side by side, each phrased the way that side would want it phrased for itself. No strawmen, and no vacuous rows either (we don't need 'history is complicated' as a row).

3. **'The cruxes'** — three or so sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one side, roughly 150 words each. For each crux, name a concrete case or test both sides would agree bears on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance. One crux should engage the twelfth-century renaissance business (Haskins and all that): the continuity crowd uses it to deflate the Italian Renaissance's uniqueness, so the rupture crowd has to say what, if anything, made the later one different in kind — or concede it's degree all the way down, and then tell us where degree becomes kind. And we'd especially like one crux built on an idea we keep kicking around: take a pile of natural-philosophy and learned writing from roughly 1300–1700, strip the dates off, and have experts sort it into 'before' and 'after'. The rupture crowd predicts the sorting works and the boundary lands somewhere in the sixteenth or seventeenth century, tracking doctrine; the continuity crowd predicts it mostly tracks surface features — Latin versus the vernacular, print versus manuscript, literary fashion — and that scholastic textbook texts end up scattered across the whole period. Is that a fair test as stated? If not, fix it and say what you changed.

4. **'Is any of this just about words?'** — a paragraph or two, around 200 words, on whether part of the dispute is verbal. We keep sliding between 'real' meaning 'carves history at its joints' and 'real' meaning 'a convention that does reliable work' — the Bronze Age is defined by a metal and nobody thinks that makes it fake, so where does that leave us? It might help to treat the whole thing a bit like arguing over whether the Midwest is a real region: everyone uses the word, no two people draw the same boundary, and yet some things — accents, settlement patterns — do cluster. Does that analogy help or hurt? If some of our fight is about the word rather than the world, say which part and why.

Then close with a single sentence, no more than thirty words, that both halves could put their names to, giving an honest account of what these period labels do and don't do.

Keep it all in British English please — learnt, colour, labelled, the lot. And don't go easy on us: if the honest verdict is that one side has to give more ground than it will like, write that in.
