So here's some background: I'm a backend engineer at a mid-size fintech company, and I've gotten into these long lunch arguments with a coworker (Dmitri, who's really into efficient market stuff) about why anyone trades at all. He brought up this no-trade theorem thing — the idea that if everyone's rational and starts from the same prior, and whatever each person knows is basically out in the open, then a willing buyer and seller can't both profit from the trade, because the mere fact that someone wants to trade with you is itself information that should update your price until the trade stops being attractive. I gather there's an older related result by Aumann about how rational folks with common priors can't "agree to disagree," which I assume is the engine under the hood. Anyway, the obvious problem: an absolutely insane amount of securities change hands every single day, and it doesn't look like it's all just rebalancing index funds.

My question: here's a list of candidate explanations for where all that trading volume actually comes from, and I want you to rank them — plus, if you're up for it, give me rough percentages for how much of real-world volume each one plausibly accounts for (they don't need to sum to exactly 100, just be honest about the uncertainty):

(a) genuine liquidity needs — someone sells stock to buy a house, someone else needs cash for an emergency
(b) risk rebalancing — funds drifting away from target allocations, leverage limits, forced deleveraging
(c) heterogeneous priors — traders don't actually share a common prior because they have different background assumptions about how the world works, so they can disagree rationally even with everything out in the open
(d) the common knowledge assumption just fails in practice — nobody ever has the full picture of everyone's information, so the no-trade logic never fully kicks in
(e) plain overconfidence and noise trading — people who think they're smarter than the market
(f) market making and HFT — algorithms quoting both sides, which creates huge gross volume but maybe shouldn't count as "real" disagreement
(g) non-pecuniary motives — hedging tail risks, tax reasons, or honestly just enjoying the gamble

Dmitri is pretty commited to the view that it's like 90% (e), people being overconfident sheep, and he thinks (c) is a cop-out philosophers invented to avoid admitting everyone's irrational. I suspect he's way off, and I suspect the boring stuff like (a), (b) and (f) does a lot more work than he admits, but I honestly don't know how to weigh (c) vs (d) — they both sound like technicalities but I have a feeling the distinction matters. So yeah: rank them, assign your rough fractions, and tell me where you think Dmitri's "it's all dumb money" theory lands on a scale from defensible to delusional. Bonus points if you can explain to me like I'm another engineer why exactly the common knowledge assumption is doing so much of the heavy lifting in these proofs, cause that part still feels kinda wierd to me.
