I spent most of my working life as a marine geologist, and since retiring I've been auditing a moral psychology course at the local university and going to a reading group that grew out of it. The group got stuck for most of last year on what I thought would be a simple question, and now I'm not even sure we're disagreeing about the thing I thought we were disagreeing about, so I want a diagnosis of the deadlock rather than a verdict on who's right.

The question was roughly: if a society could somehow pick, would it be better off running its moral life on guilt or on shame? On one side you've got the people who say guilt is the civilized instrument — it's tied to specific acts, it comes with an internal sense that you owe somebody repair, and it does its work without needing an audience, so it survives anonymity and moral pluralism. Shame, for them, is primitive and dangerous: it attaches to the whole self rather than the deed, it collapses into hiding or rage when it gets intense, and it's hostage to whatever the crowd happens to disapprove of, which is fine when the crowd disapproves of cruelty but not so fine when the crowd disapproves of the wrong things. The other side points out that this picture is mostly built from clinical samples and from people who already live in guilt-heavy cultures, that plenty of work on emotion in other societies (and on families within our own) suggests shame, properly socialized, tracks character rather than episodes, motivates confession and repair as often as avoidance, and that guilt without any sense of being seen degenerates into a private ledger you can quietly audit away. Where it got really stuck was the "can a culture choose" part: half the group thinks that's an empirical question about what you can actually shift with childrearing, institutions, and law, and the other half thinks the very concepts of guilt and shame vary so much across societies that there's no stable thing to be compared, so the question is ill-posed before any experiment could run.

My working hypothesis, for what it's worth, is that this is an empirical causal question wearing a conceptual costume, and that the reason nobody moves is that each side gets to define the independent variable: the pro-guilt people stipulate that "real" shame is the global, self-directed, audience-dependent kind, and the pro-shame people stipulate that "mature" shame is the embodied, virtue-tracking kind, so every study that comes in on the other side can be reclassified as being about something else. But I'm new to this field and may be missing that the disagreement is really about values — about whether morality is fundamentally about repair between persons or about the formation of character — in which case no evidence would move it, only an honest acknowledgment that the two camps want different things from a moral system. So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is my hypothesis about the movable definitions right, or is something deeper doing the work? And if you accept some version of my diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress — an agreed taxonomy of shame-like states, comparative longitudinal work, a decision to split the question into the conceptual part and the policy part, or an argument that we should just give up on the cross-cultural version entirely. I'd also take an honest "this one stays stuck, and here's why," since I've watched a year of meetings produce exactly zero movement and I'd like to know whether that's our fault or the question's.
