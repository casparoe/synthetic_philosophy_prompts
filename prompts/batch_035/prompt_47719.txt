I'd like a thought experiment constructed to order in the philosophy of death, for use in a seminar on whether death is bad for the one who dies. The scenario should be built so that two accounts of death's badness come apart: a comparativist deprivation account, on which death is bad for you when the life you would have had contains more welfare than your actual truncated life, should deliver the verdict that death was bad for the person; a desire-fulfilment account, on which death is bad for you only insofar as it thwarts your desires, should deliver the verdict that it was not. And the desire theorist must not win by default.

Constraints on the scenario:

1. Ordinary life, no science fiction, no demons, no exotic machinery — and the death should be sudden, so that the badness of the dying process is not available to muddle the verdicts.
2. One paragraph.
3. The person must not be desireless, resigned, or serene. The desire theorist must not win by absence of desires. She should have plenty of future-directed desires; the design point must instead be that none of them requires her continued existence for its satisfaction — they are desires about how things go after her death, and they all come off as she wished.
4. The counterfactual continued life must be clearly good, not borderline — but every good it contains should be a good she neither wanted, expected, nor so much as conceived of at the time of her death. Goods that would have arrived as surprises.

The deliverables after the scenario:

(a) Verify the design rather than stipulating it: run each account over the case explicitly, naming the desires the death leaves unthwarted and the goods the counterfactual life contains, and show that each account delivers its assigned verdict.

(b) Identify the feature of the case doing the work. I suspect it is whether the theory's welfare base is restricted to actual, standing desires or reaches to counterfactual goods never wanted — but tell me if it is something else. Then say what happens to the verdicts if that feature is removed: for instance, if she had just one ordinary desire that only her continued existence could satisfy.

(c) Give the desire theorist's best counterattack. I have in mind two moves: counting the desires she would have formed had she lived, or relocating the harm to some time before her death. Assess whether either is ad hoc, and whether either generalizes to cases where it delivers the wrong verdict.

(d) Finally, say whether the case gives any comfort to the historical Epicurean. The no-subject argument — that when death is present the subject is not, and vice versa — is not itself a desire-fulfilment claim, so it is not obvious that a case helping desire-fulfilment helps Epicurus. Or does the case only support a hybrid position, and if so, whose position is it actually supporting?
