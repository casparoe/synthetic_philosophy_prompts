Quick context: I'm a journalist, I mostly cover science and ideas stuff, so I end up at a lot of dinners where people argue about weird thought experiments. Last week was one of those. Two friends of mine -- Dana, who teaches statistics, and Marcus, who does risk at a quant fund -- spent over an hour on this one bet and I genuinely couldn't tell who was winning, so I'm hoping you can referee it.

The bet itself (they made it up over dessert): I flip a coin until it comes up heads. If the first heads is on flip number n, the casino pays you (-1)^n * (2^n / n) dollars. So the payouts alternate -- you win 2/1 dollars... wait, let me just do it the way they did. You LOSE 2/1 if heads comes on the first flip, WIN 2/2 if it comes on the second, LOSE 8/3 if it comes on the third, and so on, with the amounts growing fast enough that the "expected" terms (after multiplying by the probabilities, which halve each flip) come out to something like -1 + 1/2 - 1/3 + 1/4... an alternating series.

Their exchange, as best as I can reconstruct it:

(1) Marcus said the expectation of this bet is a small positive number (the alternating harmonic series converges to something like 0.69), so the bet is worth about 69 cents and it's a boring, barely-worth-it gamble. Dana said that's exactly the mistake: this series converges only conditionally, so the "expectation" isn't really a number at all -- rearrange the order of the terms and you can make the same bet add up to anything you like, or make it diverge. She said the bet doesn't have an expected value, full stop, and calling it "worth 69 cents" is an artifact of the order you happened to write things down in.

(2) Marcus pushed back: the flips happen in time. There's a natural order -- first flip, second flip, third flip -- and summing in that order isn't an arbitrary convention, it's how the gamble is actually played. He said Dana's rearrangement trick requires dealing the outcomes in a scrambled order no one would use, which is like complaining that a book has a different plot if you shuffle the chapters. Dana said that's fine for naming a convention but decision theory can't depend on presentation: a casino that wants to fleece you can structure the payouts so the "natural" temporal order is one of her rearrangements, and a rational agent's valuation of a gamble shouldn't change based on how the schedule of payments is arranged.

(3) Marcus then said the whole thing is a math curio with no bite: any real casino has a finite bankroll, which truncates the series, and truncated series are fine -- everything converges, no paradox, and this is just a reminder that infinite idealizations break. Dana said that if the fix is "the bankroll is finite," then where you cut the series off decides the value, and the sign of the answer depends alot on which tail you ignore; and more importantly she thought the interesting cases are ones like assigning utilities to tiny-probability huge-stakes outcomes, where people really do keep infinitely much structure in the model. Marcus said no, that's a different problem -- tiny probability times huge payoff is about domination by extreme terms, while this puzzle is about the alternating signs cancelling each other out, and conflating the two is how people talk past each other about Pascal's-mugging-style stuff.

(4) Dana's last move: fine, but then what do we say? Her suggestion was that some gambles simply don't have a fair price, the way 0/0 doesn't have a value -- you just decline to price them, and that's not irrationality, it's the theory having a domain limit. Marcus said an agent who refuses to state a price is in worse shape than one with any price at all: you can't buy, can't sell, can't compare, and in his world somebody who won't quote a number eventually gets picked off by somebody who does. Dana rolled her eyes at that and said not every theoretical gap is an exploitable weakness.

They eventually agreed to disagree and, I kid you not, flipped a coin to decide who paid for dessert. Can you go through the exchange move by move and tell me who's right about what? Rate each of the four moves if you can, from clearly right to clearly wrong, and say what (if anything) both of them are missing about the whole setup. Also, if you have thoughts on whether Dana's "just refuse to price it" position can actually be defended, I'd love to hear them, since that seemed like the crux to me.
