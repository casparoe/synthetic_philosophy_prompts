I keep running into the same wall with the "agriculture was the worst mistake" debate and I want a diagnosis of the deadlock rather than another round of it. On one side you've got the affluence crowd: Sahlins's original affluent society line, built on Richard Lee's time studies of the !Kung (something like 15-20 hours a week of actual food-getting, and supposedly limited wants, so wants met = rich by definition), and Diamond's 1987 essay framing agriculture as a catastrophe we never recovered from. On the other side, the people who point out that later time-allocation work (Kaplan and Hill's crew among the Ache, that literature) found foragers working a lot more than Lee's numbers once you count processing and food sharing, and that whatever the farmers lost in height and tooth enamel they eventually won demographically and technologically. And underneath both, the skeletal evidence that gets cited by EVERYONE: shorter stature, enamel hypoplasias, porotic hyperostosis showing up with farming, taken as proof of declining health by one side — while the other side leans on the osteological paradox point (Wood et al., early 90s) that skeletal lesions partly measure who survived long enough to record stress in their bones, so a clean-boned forager population might just be a dead-earlier population, which flips the inference.

So here's my question. This fight is at least fifty years old, both sides have been staring at the same datasets for decades, and as far as I can tell nobody has changed their mind since roughly the Clinton administration. What kind of disagreement is it actually? Some hypotheses I keep chewing on and can't settle between:

(a) it's secretly verbal — the two camps mean different things by both "affluence" (wants-satisfied vs. resources-buffered-against-bad-years) and by "mistake" (worse for the median individual vs. a choice that looks bad from the chooser's perspective, which nobody made, since agriculture spread by outbreeding neighbors rather than by anyone opting in);

(b) it's a comparison-class fight nobody owns up to — if your baseline is the early farmers' contemporaneous forager neighbors, agriculture looks bad; if it's the medieval peasant, farming looks like a step up; if it's a modern office worker with dentists, everything pre-industrial is misery, and people silently switch baselines mid-argument;

(c) it's a hidden moral question doing the work — whether more people living worse lives beats fewer people living better ones is basically the repugnant conclusion wearing a loincloth, and no amount of bone chemistry settles it;

(d) it's Rousseau vs. Hobbes all the way down, i.e., bedrock temperamental priors about what humans are fitted for, which neither side can argue for without circularity.

Or maybe it's genuinely empirical and just hard — the osteological paradox stuff, if taken seriously, might make the health data almost useless for either side, which would be an argument that the impasse is an underdetermination problem rather than a conceptual one.

So: diagnose it. Tell me which of my four guesses is closest, or whether it's some combination or something I haven't listed, and support the diagnosis with what the participants actually say rather than with vibes. Then tell me what would even count as progress given your diagnosis — better paleopathology, a fixed convention for the comparison class, splitting the question into several questions, or just accepting it's a values dispute dressed as an archaeology dispute. I'd also like you to flag how confident you are in each part of the diagnosis, and explicitly single out the point you're least sure about, because I suspect the diagnosis itself is partly hostage to which side of the Rousseau/Hobbes thing you fall on, and I'd rather know where that self-doubt is than have a clean answer.
