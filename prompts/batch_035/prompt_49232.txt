I work in AI safety, and for several years I've sat in a recurring workshop where the same argument about machine ethics replays without anyone moving an inch. The sides are now perfectly entrenched. One camp wants top-down approaches: encode explicit normative theories — a utilitarian decision procedure, a Kantian-style test on maxims, a system of rights as hard constraints — so that the machine's behavior is at least auditable and a bad output can be traced to a specifiable rule. A second camp thinks this is hopeless and wants bottom-up approaches: expose the system to large numbers of ethically loaded cases and let it acquire something like ethical competence inductively, on the grounds that no human moral agent is running an explicit theory either. A third camp — increasingly my own reflex — suspects the entire framing is a category error: machines are not moral agents, cannot coherently be praised or blamed, and what gets called machine ethics is really engineering with high moral stakes, so the honest goal is reliable constraint-following rather than anything that deserves the name of ethics.

At the last meeting a colleague offered a diagnosis she thought would dissolve the dispute: the three camps are answering three different questions under one heading. On her reading, the top-downers are really talking about auditability and legal accountability, the bottom-uppers about generalization to novel cases, and the skeptics about the conditions for being a genuine object of praise, blame, and resentment. Once you disentangle the questions, she claimed, there is no remaining disagreement — just parallel engineering problems plus one separate metaphysical question about agency. I found this clever but too quick. It does not explain why the bottom-uppers bristle so much at the skeptics when, on her reading, they should simply concede that agency was never their claim; nor why the top-downers treat the skeptics' proposed compromise (keep building the systems, but call it constraint-following rather than ethics) as a defeat rather than a truce.

So I would like a genuine diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a merely verbal dispute about the word 'ethics'; a substantive disagreement about what artifacts can in principle do; a disagreement about which question deserves priority; or something else — perhaps a fight about who bears the moral risk if the project succeeds under one description rather than another? One clue I keep returning to: the responsibility-gap worry resurfaces at every meeting. When a learning system does something terrible in a situation nobody designed for, it becomes unclear who is answerable — the operator, the designers, or no one — and the three camps treat this worry in strikingly different ways, which suggests it is doing hidden structural work in the disagreement. Please diagnose the structure of the dispute using what each camp actually says, and tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis: a conceptual distinction, a demonstrated capability or incapacity, a change of question — or an argument that no progress should be expected as long as the word 'ethics' stays in the title.
