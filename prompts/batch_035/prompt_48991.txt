My friend is an economics PhD student, and last night we spent three hours arguing about revealed preference. He takes the strict line — the one he attributes to the Chicago-style tradition — that preference is nothing but a summary term for choice behavior. I tried to counter with weakness of will, and he walked me into the argument below, which I can now neither accept nor refute.

Here is the argument as he laid it out, in numbered steps:

(1) On the revealed-preference view, for a person to prefer X to Y is nothing over and above that person's choosing X when X and Y are both on offer.

(2) Last Tuesday I skipped the gym and scrolled my phone instead. Afterward I said that I had preferred to exercise and had simply given in.

(3) By (1), my after-the-fact avowal cannot be true, because it posits a preference standing apart from the choice itself, and (1) tells us there is nothing for "prefer" to mean apart from choosing.

(4) Moreover, the avowal is not even false; it is meaningless. It attempts to describe a mental state that, by (1), does not exist to be described.

(5) The same holds for every choice made against the chooser's better judgment: relapse, procrastination, impulse purchases, staying in a job one claims to want to leave. In each case, what the person "really" wanted is, by definition, whatever they picked.

(6) So no one ever chooses what they do not prefer.

(7) Practical irrationality, at minimum, consists in choosing what one does not prefer.

(8) Therefore no one is ever practically irrational.

My friend embraces (8) without embarrassment. He says "irrationality" is moralizing dressed up as economics, that regret is just a mood, and that when I say I "really" prefer the gym I am merely predicting how I would choose in some other, cooler, better-rested situation — which is itself just a fact about a different choice set, not a fact about my preferences as such. When I pushed him, he went further: on his view the argument also shows that advice, self-command, and second-order preferences about one's own preferences are all confusions, since each presupposes a preference that isn't exhausted by what gets chosen. That the position can swallow all of this without strain makes me suspect it is unfalsifiable rather than deep, but suspicion is not a diagnosis.

I think (8) is absurd — addiction and akrasia are as real as anything in economics — yet I cannot locate the faulty step. My half-formed thought is that the slide happens somewhere between the definition at (1) and the conclusion at (8): perhaps "prefer" is being used in one sense at (1), as a technical term indexed to a particular menu at a particular moment, and in another sense at (6) and (7), where it ranges over a person's wants as a whole. Or perhaps the trouble is at (4), since even if the avowal cannot report a hidden state, it might still do some other job — for example, committing me to a prediction about how I will choose next Tuesday. But I cannot turn these hunches into a precise objection, and my friend correctly points out that "it's an equivocation, probably" is not an answer.

So I need help with the following:

Tell me which step to reject, and what rejecting it costs. If the argument is valid, tell me which premise is least expensive to give up and say exactly what one pays. If it is invalid, name the fallacy precisely — the two senses of "prefer," the slide from "indexed to a choice situation" to "true of the person as a whole," or whatever it actually is — rather than gesturing at ambiguity in general.

Please structure your reply as follows, with these section titles, in this order:

1. "Verdict" — one paragraph of roughly 100 words naming the step (or premise) you reject and the fallacy or cost involved, with no hedging.
2. "Why the step looked safe" — roughly 200 words explaining what made the faulty move seem unproblematic in the context of the argument.
3. "Pricing the alternatives" — roughly 300 words going through the other candidate rejections — (1) itself, (3) or (4), (6), (7) — and saying what each costs the person who makes it, including what my friend's position loses if he is forced to retreat.
4. "What survives" — roughly 150 words on what the revealed-preference programme can still legitimately claim even after the flaw you diagnose is exposed, so that I am not left thinking the whole framework collapses.

Please write your answer in British English. Argue from the steps themselves; I am not looking for references to the literature or a reading list, just the analysis.
