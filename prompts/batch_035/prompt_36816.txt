For the past few years my game theorist friend and I have been circling the same disagreement about epistemic game theory, and I want a third opinion in the form of a number with its reasoning exposed. Here is the proposition as precisely as I can state it: the assumption of common knowledge of rationality is an indispensable idealization for explaining strategic behavior, in the sense that any analysis replacing the full infinite hierarchy of mutual knowledge with knowledge up to some large but finite depth would get the classical predictions qualitatively wrong in important cases. The main consideration on the "yes" side is the electronic mail game phenomenon: an arbitrarily long chain of confirmations and confirmations of confirmations, with error probabilities tending to zero, still leaves behavior far from what common knowledge would deliver, which suggests the hierarchy's infinite tail is doing real work rather than serving as a convenient closure condition. The main considerations on the "no" side are the epistemic characterization results from the mid-nineties — in the two-player case, mutual knowledge of rationality and payoffs plus a common prior turns out to suffice to sustain equilibrium play, common knowledge being needed only in an unexpected and collateral way — together with the experimental literature suggesting that actual humans reason at one to three levels of iteration and that classical predictions fail for those subjects anyway.

My credence in the proposition sits around 0.55; my friend's is closer to 0.15, on the grounds that the electronic mail game's discontinuity depends delicately on the error probabilities themselves being common knowledge, so the case doesn't show that finite-depth knowledge is generally deficient — just that this particular model was engineered to make it look so — and he would add that a hierarchy truncated at a handful of levels fits the behavioral data better and preserves most of the theory.

Give me your own credence, but what I really want is the decomposition into the sub-questions that produce it: roughly, the probability that the difference between finite-order and infinite-order knowledge is principled rather than an artifact of how the formalism individuates epistemic events; the probability that real, boundedly resourceful agents can ever approximate the infinite hierarchy closely enough that the approximation is explanatorily operative rather than merely mathematically elegant; the probability that the experimental failures of iterated-dominance and backward-induction predictions are best attributed to the absence of common knowledge rather than to competing explanations such as non-common priors, failures of rationality simpliciter, or social preferences; and any further terms you think belong in the derivation. Assign each a rough probability with a line of justification, show how they combine into your top-level figure, and then tell me which term you are least confident in, what argument or evidence would move it most, and whether the divergence between my 0.55 and his 0.15 is most likely located in that term or in a different one.
