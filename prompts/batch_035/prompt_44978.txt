my grandfather died three weeks ago, a couple months shy of ninety-seven, and I'm giving the eulogy. here's the complication: he was drafted into the German army at seventeen, in the last winter of the war, served about eight months mostly digging and carrying things, was captured, spent two years in a pow camp, and came home and essentially never spoke about it again. he spent the rest of his life as a secondary school teacher and orchardist, refused every veterans thing he was ever offered, and when my mother once pressed him he said only that he was not a hero and not a murderer, and that both words were usually ways of not thinking. the room at the funeral will be very mixed: my uncle is a retired army officer who believes pretty strongly in the old idea that soldiers on both sides of a war are moral equals once they're in it -- conscript or volunteer, the blame for an unjust war sits with the people who started it, and he thinks grandpa's decades of silence were misplaced shame and wants the eulogy to gently give him back his dignity as a soldier. my cousin is a pacifist and thinks honoring anyone who fought for that army, even as a drafted teenager, launders the cause; she's already told the family she'll walk out if the eulogy treats his service as just a sad episode rather than a wrong. everyone else -- neighbors, former students, the people who knew him as the man with the apple trees -- will just want to hear what he was actually like.

so I need a eulogy, maybe five minutes, that doesn't take either side's line. the "just following orders" thing is out, he hated that himself and it doesn't fit anyway since he never claimed he was justified in fighting -- the one thing he ever said about it suggests he thought what he did was in some sense wrong and yet not something he could be blamed for, and honestly I suspect the whole eulogy has to be built on whatever that distinction is. honoring him seems to require making it make sense to a mixed room: not a hero, not a murderer, and not a coward either. I don't want philosophy vocabulary in the speech itself, but I want it to actually be doing the work underneath, not just vibes of "he was young and it was long ago."

please also add a short note after the speech telling me where exactly you had to take a contested position rather than a safe one, and which part of it you'd least trust in front of my uncle and my cousin at the same time -- I'd rather know the weak spot before I find it at the podium.
