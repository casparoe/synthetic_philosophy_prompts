I am fourteen months into recovery from alcoholism, and the question of what exactly I believe about responsibility — for the years I was drinking, and for the fact that I am now sober — has recently stopped being academic for me. My spouse and I are attempting to work through what our marriage owes to the past, my sponsor has asked me to write out what I actually think rather than what the program's slogans say, and I have been reading enough philosophy of action to be dangerous. The result is a set of commitments, listed below, that I would like audited for consistency. I cannot tell where the real tensions are, and I would rather find out now than mid-conversation with my spouse.

1. I am a compatibilist, and of a broadly Dennettian cast: the free will worth wanting is the capacity to notice, evaluate, and act on one's own reasons, and whether the underlying machinery is deterministic is simply beside the point.

2. My active addiction substantially compromised that capacity. My responsiveness to reasons bearing on drinking was degraded in a way that my responsiveness to reasons about my job or my finances was not, and this is why the amends I owe for the drinking years are amends rather than full confessions of wrongdoing: I was less blameworthy then than I would be for the same acts now, though not blameless.

3. By contrast, my family history and genetics — the causal story of how I became an addict at all — diminish nothing. Explanation is not exculpation when it comes to how I got here; only what happened while I was in the grip of the thing partially excuses.

4. My recovery is a genuine achievement, and I deserve real credit for it — deservingly, in the way one deserves praise for a hard-won accomplishment, not merely as a pat on the head that happens to keep me going.

5. At the same time, I hold that the whole question of whether someone has the free will worth wanting is partly a question about which practices of holding responsible we have reason to maintain, and that our answer is to be justified pragmatically — by what those practices do for us and for the people subject to them — rather than by the discovery of facts about desert that hold independently of any of us.

6. Yet I also believe that some of my spouse's resentment toward me for the drinking years was fitting: that it latched onto something about me and what I did, and would not have been an error had the practices of blame never existed. I do not think this is just a useful way of talking.

7. Finally, a friend in the program has persuaded me of what she calls the nearby-self test: when I ask whether I could have done otherwise on a given night, the only candidate selves that count are ones with my exact history and dispositions, minimally altered. On the night of my worst relapse, I doubt any nearby self abstains. But on the night I first called my sponsor instead of drinking, I equally doubt any nearby self does anything but pick up the phone — which seems to hand me exoneration and vindication too cheaply on both ends, and I cannot see how to stop the test at just the one verdict I want.

Please go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like they are. For each genuine tension, rank it by severity, say what would have to give, and what that revision would cost me. Then tell me whether there is a single revision that would clean up the most. I am not attached to any particular item on the list; I am attached to knowing where the load-bearing contradictions are before I try to live by it.
