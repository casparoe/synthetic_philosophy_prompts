I've been drafted into running the discussion for my department's "philosophy for mathematicians" reading group -- six grad students in math and CS, sharp people, zero philosophy background, and they've been told we're doing Benacerraf's dilemma about mathematical knowledge (the one that squeezes platonism between having a uniform semantics for math and having any account of how we know it). They will have actually read a summary beforehand, which in this crowd means they'll skim it, decide it's obvious, and show up thinking the problem is trivial because "you just prove things." I get 75 minutes and a whiteboard.

What I want from you is a plan for the session, not a re-explanation of the dilemma for me. Specifically: what has to be in place in their heads before the dilemma can even bite, and in what order -- I suspect they need to feel the semantic half (the part that pushes *toward* platonism, that "there are infinitely many primes" seems to just be true or false independent of us) before the epistemological half can look like anything other than a philosopher inventing a fake problem, but I'm not sure how to get them there without a lecture. What single example should carry the whole thing? I was thinking of comparing knowing a mathematical fact to knowing some humdrum physical fact like "there's a mug on my desk" and then asking what the difference is when the fact is about an abstract object, but maybe that's too flat. There's also a confusion I definitely need handled: some of them have half-heard about the other Benacerraf paper, the one arguing that there's no fact of the matter about which particular set the number 3 *is* (Zermelo vs. von Neumann style), and if I don't get ahead of it, half the session will be spent with people thinking THAT is the dilemma. So tell me where in the sequence to separate those two problems out. Beyond that, map the misconceptions I should expect and where each should be defused -- my guesses are "causal contact is a metaphor, so no real problem," "we know math because our teachers told us / it's in the axioms," and someone smuggling in Gödel and thinking incompleteness is somehow the issue. Finally: what one question could I end on to check whether the dilemma actually landed as a dilemma rather than as a solved or pseudoproblem, and what should I openly decline to get into at their level (the indispensability literature feels like a swamp I should just gesture at, but you tell me).
