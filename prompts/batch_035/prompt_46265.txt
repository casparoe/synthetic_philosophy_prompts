I'm setting the take-home final for an upper-level epistemology seminar (third- and fourth-year undergraduates, two graduate students auditing), and I want one essay question on the lottery paradox and the preface paradox that cannot be passed by reciting the standard labels — Kyburg's threshold account, Makinson's fallibilist preface, the belief/acceptance distinction — and that instead forces students to adjudicate a thesis I defend in the final week of the course, which I call the Symmetry Trap.

The thesis is this: any resolution that treats the two paradoxes as instances of the same phenomenon — a clash between the justification had by each member of a set of beliefs and the justification had by their conjunction — will solve one while misdiagnosing the other. My sketch: in the lottery case the agent has no independent, discriminating reason to accept, of any particular ticket, that it will lose; the acceptances are symmetric and rest entirely on the probabilities. In the preface case the author has first-personal, agentive grounds for each chapter's correctness — she checked each one — plus, separately, a statistical ground, the base rate of errata in books of the kind, for the negation of their conjunction. The preface agent is not merely failing to agglomerate; she has positive justification for a claim inconsistent with what she is justified in accepting, something structurally absent from the lottery. If that is right, then resolutions that deny conjunction closure for justified belief handle the lottery but owe an extra story about the preface, while threshold-tinkering resolutions that keep closure have the mirror problem.

The essay question you write must require students to do four things: restate the Symmetry Trap without smuggling in the assumption it attacks; test it against the strongest symmetric resolution, which I take to be distinguishing rational acceptance from rational belief and denying that acceptance agglomerates; test it against the strongest asymmetric resolution, a dual-threshold view on which base-rate, statistical grounding warrants a different probability threshold than agentive, first-personal grounding; and adjudicate a twist case, which should be stated in the question itself — the delegated-verification monograph. An author publishes a book whose every page records the result of one of ten thousand coin flips she never personally observed; each record rests solely on the report of a transcriber who is accurate 99.99 percent of the time, with errors independent across pages; the author, knowing the base rate of transcription slips, adds a preface asserting that at least one recorded result in the book is wrong. Students must say whether the agentive/statistical distinction the dual-threshold view needs does any work here — every page belief is now grounded only statistically — and, if it does not, whether any residual difference (the author's editorial responsibility for the volume as a whole, or the fact that her preface belief concerns her own book rather than an abstract draw from a known distribution) can carry the asymmetry, or whether the Symmetry Trap collapses into the admission that the two paradoxes are, after all, one.

Please produce the essay question exactly as it would appear on the exam, written for students who have read Kyburg and Makinson but nothing more technical; it should be answerable in about 90 minutes or 1200 words and is worth 25 of the final's 90 marks. Follow it with a model answer of about 700 words and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth (totaling 25), a one-line note on how students typically fumble each point, and a closing line on what separates an A answer from a B answer on this question.
