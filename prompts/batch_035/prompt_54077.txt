You are a decision theorist with a strong background in the philosophy of probability, and you have been asked to referee a dispute between two colleagues. Please think through the matter carefully — beginning with some private brainstorming of the candidate failure points before you commit to a diagnosis — and then write up a rigorous verdict.

The dispute concerns the two-envelope paradox. The setup, stated neutrally: two envelopes, one containing twice as much money as the other, with which envelope gets the larger amount decided by a fair coin. I pick one envelope at random and have not yet opened it.

My colleague argues that I should switch before opening:

(1) Let A be the amount in my envelope. The other envelope contains either 2A or A/2.
(2) The coin was fair, so I am exactly as likely to hold the smaller envelope as the larger: probability 1/2 each.
(3) The expected gain from switching is (1/2)(+A) + (1/2)(−A/2) = A/4 > 0.
(4) A rational agent takes an action with positive expected gain.
(5) So I should switch before opening. And since the same argument applies to whichever envelope I then hold, I should be willing to switch back — indeed to pay for the privilege, repeatedly.

I know the standard reply: the 1/2 in step (3) is illegitimate, because the probability that I hold the smaller envelope given that mine contains a particular amount need not be 1/2, and cannot be 1/2 for every possible amount; once the distribution of amounts is properly taken into account, the unconditional expected gain from switching is zero. My colleague says this reply concedes everything he needs, and he has two follow-ups I cannot answer.

(a) The original argument never conditions on a value — the envelope is unopened. The A in step (1) seems to serve only as a name for whatever is in my envelope, and the 1/2 in step (2) comes from the fair coin, not from any claim about specific amounts. Where, exactly, does the standard reply's "distribution of amounts" enter an argument that never mentions a value? If the reply is that "whatever A turns out to be" implicitly quantifies over values and therefore needs conditional probabilities, then it looks as though the reply is rejecting step (1), or the substitution of A into step (3), and I want to see precisely which step and precisely what equivocation is being alleged.

(b) Worse: my colleague can fill the envelopes so that the conditional version of the argument comes out in his favor. Suppose the smaller amount is 2^k with probability proportional to (2/3)^k, for k = 0, 1, 2, … (the normalizing constant is irrelevant). Then if I open my envelope and find 2^n, the probability that I hold the smaller envelope works out to 2/5 rather than 1/2, and the expected gain from switching works out to 2^n/10 — positive for every amount I could possibly observe. So the standard reply, applied honestly to his envelopes, says: after opening, switch, whatever you see. And if I would switch whatever I saw, then I should switch before opening — looking is free, after all, and no possible observation would change the recommendation. Yet before opening, the situation is manifestly symmetric between the two envelopes, and the expected gain computed the other way — the average gap between the envelopes, multiplied by an independent fair sign — appears to be exactly zero. My colleague concludes that rationality itself recommends a switch that symmetry forbids.

Here is what I want from you. First, diagnose the original argument (1)–(5): name the single step you reject, and explain the equivocation precisely enough that I could teach it — in particular, make clear what A is at each occurrence (a random variable, a fixed constant, or something sliding between the two), and why the 1/2 that is genuinely licensed by the fair coin is not the 1/2 that step (3) requires. Second, deal with the strengthened version. I can see the outlines of an answer involving the fact that his filling distribution gives my envelope's contents infinite expectation, so that "expected gain" has no coherent value before opening and positive conditional gains at every observed value fail to add up to anything at all; I also suspect something St. Petersburg-shaped is lurking. But I want it spelled out: what exactly breaks when one tries to pass from "switching is preferred conditional on every possible observation" to "switching is preferred," what principle licenses that passage in ordinary cases, and what it costs to restrict that principle. Third, price the alternatives as a referee would. My colleague's opponent is prepared to surrender various things — the claim that positive expected gain conditional on each observation settles the matter, the permissibility of unbounded payoffs in decision theory, the dominance reasoning behind "you'd switch whatever you saw, so switch now" — and I want to know what each surrender costs elsewhere in decision theory, not merely in this case. Finally, if you think there is a coherent reading on which my colleague is simply right — that with his envelopes one should switch before opening, symmetry notwithstanding — say so and defend it. Please do not pad your verdict with the literature's greatest hits; I want the working, not the name-dropping.
