Question 4 (20 marks). State the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, making each hypothesis explicit and defining every term used. Explain the precise relationship between the theorem and Arrow's impossibility theorem. Then discuss what the theorem does and does not imply about the rationality of sincere voting in elections with three or more candidates, and describe two standard escape routes from the theorem, explaining in each case why the escape works.

Student's answer: The theorem states that any social choice function whose domain includes all strict preference profiles over at least three alternatives, and which is onto (every alternative is the outcome at some profile) and strategy-proof (no voter can ever obtain a preferred outcome by misreporting, whatever the others report), must be dictatorial: the chosen alternative is always the top choice of one fixed voter. With only two alternatives the theorem fails — majority voting with a fixed tie-breaking rule is strategy-proof and non-dictatorial — which is why the hypothesis of at least three alternatives is needed. The theorem is essentially Arrow's impossibility result in strategic dress: a social choice function is just a social welfare function that reports only its top alternative, and Arrow's independence-of-irrelevant-alternatives condition already rules out manipulation, since a voter who could gain by lying would be changing the social outcome on the basis of information independent of that change. So once Arrow is established, the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem is a corollary, and the two theorems say the same thing. There are two standard escapes. First, restrict the domain: with single-peaked preferences over a one-dimensional spectrum, the median voter rule — select the alternative that is the peak of the voter at the median of the reported peaks — is onto and strategy-proof yet non-dictatorial; Duncan Black proved this in 1948. Second, randomize: random dictatorship, which selects a voter uniformly at random and adopts her reported top alternative, is strategy-proof in expectation, so lotteries dissolve the impossibility. The practical upshot is that in any real election with three or more candidates, every voter has an incentive to misreport her preferences, so sincere voting is never rational and universal strategic voting is inevitable.
