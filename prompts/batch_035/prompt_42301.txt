I'm a backend engineer who somehow got roped into "risk quantification" at my company after we had a bad outage last year, and two things we do have been bugging me because they seem to pull in opposite directions.

On one side, our security team wants a single annual probability for each big risk -- literally one number per risk, like "1 in 500 per year that this database gets breached" -- because those numbers get multiplied by estimated dollar losses in a spreadsheet that ranks all the risks and decides what gets funded. When I said "honestly somewhere between 1 in 100 and 1 in 2000, I can't do better than that" they told me that's useless, pick a number, we all know it's soft.

On the other side, in architecture reviews the same company bans numbers entirely. Confidence has to be reported as a tier: high, medium, low. The stated reasoning is that we don't have enough incidents to estimate real frequencies, so numbers would be fake precision and tiers are the honest option.

So what does each of these practices actually presupose about probability? My naive read is that the single-number side presupposes that a rational agent always has exactly one degree of belief, full stop -- that every question has a true credence even when the evidence is thin, and your job is to dig it out of your gut. And the tier side seems to presuppose almost the opposite: that probability claims need frequency data to be meaningful, and without it there's no fact of the matter, just vague bands. But those two views can't both be right, can they? Or maybe they can, if the practices are secretly answering different questions -- but I can't see what question the tiers would be answering.

Also, and here's the part that actually keeps me up at night: the spreadsheet multiplies probability by loss to produce a ranking, and the ranking drives the budget. If we took the intuition behind the tiers seriously -- that my uncertainty about the probability itself is real and shouldn't just get averaged away -- and let people submit ranges instead of single numbers, then every risk's expected loss becomes a range, the ranges overlap, and the ranking stops being well-defined. Does that mean the "one number per risk" rule isn't just a convention but is load-bearing: the institution literally can't tolerate vague probabilities because budget allocation needs a total ordering? Or is there some principled way to make funding decisions from ranges that I'm missing -- maybe something like being extra cautious exactly where the range is widest?

And is the inconsistency even real, or is single numbers for rare catastrophic stuff plus tiers for everyday stuff actually fine? Part of me suspects it's exactly backwards -- the catastrophic rare events are the ones where we have the least data and the flimsiest single numbers. Any help sorting out what each practice presupposes, whether they're compatible, and what would have to change in the process if we went all-in on ranges would be great. No philosophy background here obviously, so plain language appreciated.
