I'm an undergraduate taking a course on free will and moral responsibility, and we've just finished the unit on Frankfurt cases. In section this week, our TA took the lesson of those cases and pushed it several steps further than anyone in the room was willing to follow, and I have not been able to locate the faulty step since. The argument, as she gave it:

(1) In a Frankfurt case, Jones could not have done otherwise — had he shown any sign of choosing differently, Black would have intervened and produced the same outcome — yet Jones is responsible for what he does on his own. So the absence of alternative possibilities is not what excuses or fails to excuse; what matters is how the action actually came about.

(2) Now take a hostage who signs over her property at gunpoint. The gunman, like Black, guarantees the outcome. She also could not have done otherwise in the sense that matters to (1): had she wavered, she would have been made to sign — except that here no device is needed, because her fear would do Black's work through her own motives.

(3) By (1), the absence of alternatives does not excuse. So (4) she is no less responsible for signing than Jones is for voting.

(5) Nothing in the reasoning so far turns on the gun specifically. It extends to any arrangement that fixes the outcome by working through the agent's own motivations: a confession extracted over months of interrogation, an addict whose craving guarantees relapse, someone raised so completely inside a cult or a controlling family that dissent was never a live possibility.

(6) Therefore, none of these people is ever less than fully responsible for what they do. The TA accepted (6) with some equanimity, and the rest of us could not. It seems to erase the difference between the hostage and the volunteer, the broken confession and the proud one, the person whose world was closed to her before she could speak and the person who chose freely.

I've stared at this long enough to suspect the trouble starts between (1) and (4), because in the Frankfurt case the thing that guarantees the outcome never touches what actually happens — Black stands by, and Jones acts on his own reasons — whereas in the hostage's case the guarantee seems to operate inside her, in the actual path to the signing. But I can't turn that suspicion into a principled objection, and I notice that the hostage does seem to retain something Jones lacks: she could, at cost of her life, refuse, and that outcome is different in every way that matters, not just in its distant causes. So maybe the real failure is in (2) itself.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep (1), since it is the one thing the seminar actually established; but I can't see how to reject (3) or (4) without making the Frankfurt lesson do no work at all, and rejecting (2) looks like it drags the original case down with it. The TA insists the only consistent escape is to say Frankfurt was wrong from the start. Is that right, or does something slip earlier — and if I give up the step you pick, tell me honestly what it commits me to about the addict, the cult member, and the hostage.
