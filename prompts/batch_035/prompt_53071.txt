Acting as a professional philosopher of science with experience designing teaching materials, please think carefully and brainstorm before committing to a structure.

I am a retired biochemist who, since leaving the lab, has been working through philosophy of science and has discovered that the falsificationism I absorbed as a student in the 1970s was one move in a long argument, not the last word. I now run a small adult-education seminar on science and pseudoscience, and I would like a decision tree that sorts participants into a named position on the demarcation problem by how they react to concrete cases, rather than by which labels they happen to like.

Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first choose the branch questions, then locate the actual positions within that space. Every branch should be answerable without knowing where it leads — built from cases such as: Leverrier responding to the anomaly in the orbit of Uranus by postulating an as-yet-unseen planet rather than conceding that Newtonian mechanics had failed; theories whose defenders always seem to have an immunizing patch ready, as in the Marxist and psychoanalytic traditions Popper worried about; and a creationist literature that does make risky predictions about, say, gaps in the fossil record, and has watched them fail. Somewhere the tree must separate falsificationism as a demarcation criterion from falsificationism as a methodological norm for working scientists, and somewhere it must ask whether the unit of appraisal is a single theory at a time or a historical series of theories. The leaves should include naive falsificationism, sophisticated falsificationism, a problem-solving conception of scientific rationality, a family-resemblance or no-criterion view, and the position that demarcation is a pseudo-problem; add further leaves if the logical space demands them. Aim for roughly eight to ten branch questions and around seven leaves. Finally, flag any two leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate, and for each unoccupied region of the space, give a one-sentence diagnosis of why nobody lives there — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
