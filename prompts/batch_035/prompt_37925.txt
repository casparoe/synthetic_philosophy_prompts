Every fall I teach the big intro political philosophy lecture, and every fall some freshman asks why they should vote when their vote has basically no chance of mattering. I've got a pat answer involving civic duty and expressive reasons and all that, and it usually works. But last week a colleague in the economics department — I'll call him Dave — cornered me at happy hour and now the pat answer feels like a dodge. His argument, reconstructed as faithfully as I can:

(1) Your vote is an act of political participation — your small piece of collectively choosing the government — only if it matters to the outcome. (2) For a vote to matter to the outcome, the outcome must counterfactually depend on it: you make or break a tie, or a one-vote margin, something like that. (3) In any large election, the probability that you personally make or break the tie is astronomically small — on the order of one in ten million even in a close race, and effectively zero in a safe state. (4) So with near-certainty your vote doesn't matter to the outcome, and in a safe state it matters not at all. (5) So with near-certainty (in a safe state, with certainty) your voting is not political participation but a private ritual — Dave's phrase is that it's like cheering at a game you're watching on TV, where the players can't hear you and the result would be identical had you stayed silent. (6) And if each of the sixty million votes is like that, then the election as a whole is just sixty million people performing private rituals in parallel, and its outcome is an elaborate, expensive mechanism for generating noise whose statistics happen to correlate with public opinion. (7) Therefore the idea that elections constitute popular self-government is a mistake, and voting is consumption, full stop — nothing wrong with it, but nothing civic about it either.

Dave is perfectly content with (7), and the thing that bugs me is that I can't immediately see which premise is the rotten one. There are two places I keep circling. The move from (4) to (5) — from "doesn't determine the outcome" to "isn't participation" — smells like it's trading on two different senses of mattering, but I can't quite get the equivocation to sit still long enough to name it. And (6) looks like it might be compounding a per-person fact into a fact about the whole, in a way I'd resist if someone did it to me about, say, my one email among fifty thousand to a senator, or my signature on a referendum petition that needed a million signatures — and notice Dave's argument, unchanged, seems to prove that nobody who signed that petition participated in putting it on the ballot either, which I'd have thought was exactly backwards. But is that a genuine reductio, or is he just allowed to bite that bullet? For what it's worth, I want to keep (3) — the probability really is tiny and I refuse to hide behind pretend arithmetic — and I want to keep something in the neighborhood of (6), because I do believe there's a real difference between a people governing itself and a people being polled by Nielsen. So: tell me exactly which step to reject and what rejecting it costs, and don't give me a menu of options with no verdict — commit to a diagnosis and defend it, even if you think Dave ultimately wins more of the argument than I'd like him to. If you think there are two independent things gone wrong, fine, say which one is load-bearing.
