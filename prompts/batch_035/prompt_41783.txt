My roommate and I run a tiny podcast on ethics where every episode is basically us re-fighting the same war: he's a pretty orthodox classical utilitarian (aggregate welfare, pleasure and pain on one scale, creating happy lives is good), and over the past year I've drifted toward suffering-focused ethics — roughly the view that preventing suffering matters more than producing happiness, and maybe that nothing else matters until suffering is dealt with. We keep talking past each other and our listeners have started noticing that we never actually pin down where we disagree versus where we're just using words differently (like whether "utility" even means the same thing for both of us at this point — he's hedonic, I'm leaning closer to a preference-ish account of what makes suffering bad, which maybe quietly changes everything).

Someone suggested we do the adversarial-collaboration thing instead of another episode of us yelling. I want you to draft the joint statement we should be able to both put our names to. Structure it as: (1) a numbered list of claims we can genuinely both sign — and please be careful not to sneak asymmetry into supposedly shared claims, e.g. don't write "suffering counts more per unit" as agreed because he doesn't agree to that; (2) a disagreements section where each residual disagreement is stated in wording the person holding that view would accept — I want to be able to read the classical utilitarian's position aloud to him without him objecting to how it's framed (so no strawman about him wanting to "factory-farm happiness," and don't make my side the literal "blow up the world" caricature unless you also flag that's a bullet some negative utilitarians refuse to bite and say why); (3) the cruxes — I suspect they're something like whether pleasures and pains are commensurable enough to trade off at fixed ratios, whether there's any moral reason to bring into existence a life that's happy rather than absent, whether extreme suffering is lexically prior, and whether the mere-addition / population-level stuff changes the picture, but you tell me if I'm missing one; and (4) for each crux, at least one concrete case or thought experiment we both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about what intuition or verdict we'd report written down in advance — small cases please, stuff a listener could actually answer about themselves, not esoteric sci-fi worlds.

One more thing: include a short section at the end where you say honestly which parts of our dispute, if any, look verbal to you — like maybe we agree on all the acts and only disagree about how to describe the axiology — and which parts are genuinely substantive, such that settling them would actually make one of us change how we'd vote on a charity allocation. That's actually the real motivation for doing this: at the end of the year we each donate a chunk of money and we want to know if the thing we argue about ever cashes out differently at the donation level, or whether it only ever matters in weird population thought experiments nobody can act on anyway.
