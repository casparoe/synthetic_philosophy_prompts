I've been in a reading group on the doing/allowing distinction for about two years now and at some point we became the impasse we were studying, which is kind of a running joke at this point. Everyone knows all the cases — Foot's trolley vs the transplant surgeon, Rachels' bathtub pair where Smith and Jones both want an inheritance and one drowns the cousin while the other flips through a magazine while the cousin drowns on his own, the hospital cases where withdrawing vs withholding treatment supposedly come apart — and nobody has budged an inch. One faction thinks the distinction is morally real and load-bearing, mostly on the strength of the intuition that flicking the switch in trolley cases is fine while carving up the healthy stranger is not. The other faction thinks it's a morally inert remnant, and they lean hard on the bathtub pair: if the only difference between the two scenarios is a small arm movement by Smith, then the killing/letting-die difference can't be the thing carrying the moral weight, whatever else might be.

My working hypothesis, and I'd like you to actually engage with it rather than just nod along: the debate is stuck because the camps are answering different questions under one name. Camp one is doing something like metaphysics of agency — they want to know whether there's a real causal/structural difference between harms that exist because I act and harms that proceed unless I act (the counterfactual test: does the bad outcome depend on me, or do I merely fail to block it?). Camp two is doing normative theory — they concede a difference might exist and ask whether it could carry any deontic weight at all. And underneath both there's a third undertow nobody names out loud: people whose real commitment is that duties to aid are weaker than duties not to harm, which would make the whole fight downstream of a view about the stringency of positive vs negative duties, not about doing/allowing as such.

So here's the ask: diagnose the impasse. Is it verbal, substantive, different-questions-under-one-heading, or bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for? Use what each side actually says — including moves I haven't mentioned, like Quinn's attempt to relocate the whole distinction into whether the agent's will is directed at the victim, or the fact that in the withdrawal/withholding debate in bioethics the sides sort of flip relative to the trolley literature, which feels telling to me but maybe shouldn't. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a new kind of case, a distinction, cross-cultural work on which intuitions are stable, whatever — or argue that this one genuinely shouldn't be expected to move, and why. I don't want a survey of the positions, I know them, I want the structure of the disagreement itself.

If you honestly think none of those fit, use "other" with a short parenthetical. That last line should be a clean one-sentence summary of your whole diagnosis.
