For most of my working life I was a quantitative geneticist: I estimated narrow-sense heritabilities for production traits in dairy cattle and litter size in pigs, built selection indices, and retain an old-fashioned affection for variance-component analysis. Since retiring I have joined a philosophy of science reading group, and we have lately worked through the literature and the public quarrels surrounding the heritability of IQ, height, and the like. There I encountered an argument that I have since reconstructed as carefully as I can, and it troubles me, because every step sounds like something I once taught students, while the conclusion seems to prove far too much — it would condemn as impossible interventions that I know, from my own career and from medical history, to have succeeded. I would be grateful for a careful diagnosis.

The argument runs as follows. (1) Narrow-sense heritability is the fraction of the phenotypic variance of a trait, in a specified population living under its actual distribution of environments, that is attributable to additive genetic differences among that population's members. (2) When a trait's heritability in a population is high — say 0.8 — the environmental differences that actually obtain among its members account for only a small remainder of the trait's variation. (3) A variable that leaves only a small footprint in a trait's variation is, within that population, doing little causal work on the trait. (4) A population-wide intervention — a nutrition program, a school reform, a screening scheme — is simply a deliberate rearrangement of environments: it delivers to everyone environments of the same kinds, over roughly the same range, as those already enjoyed by some members of the population; it cannot furnish an environment of a sort that no member has ever had. (5) So such an intervention can touch only the small environmental residual: it may trim the edges of the variation, but it can neither substantially reorder individuals nor substantially raise or lower the population's level of the trait. (6) And if within each population the members' differences are owed mainly to their genes, then differences between populations — my herd and my neighbor's, this generation and the last, one country and another — in any such trait must in the main be genetic as well.

Here is what I cannot swallow. Run on phenylketonuria in the years before newborn screening, when the resulting intellectual disability tracked genotype essentially perfectly and the heritability of the condition was as close to one as makes no difference, the argument concludes that the low-phenylalanine diet could not have worked; yet it emptied the pediatric metabolic clinics. Run on my cattle, it concludes that the ration and housing programs we imposed on entire herds, for production traits whose heritabilities we had ourselves estimated, could not have lifted mean yields; yet they did, generation after generation. Run on human height, with its heritabilities of 0.8 in modern populations, it makes the many centimetres gained by European populations over the twentieth century a mystery rather than a fact. My instinct is that the trouble lies somewhere in (4) or (5), but each time I try to deny (4) I find myself asserting that a school reform creates an environment no child in the population has ever had, which sounds like precisely what a heritability skeptic would say to escape an inconvenient number; and each time I deny (5) I seem to concede that a small variance share can conceal a large effect, which leaves me wondering what my published estimates were estimates of. So: which step do you reject, what does rejecting it cost, and can the rejection be made in a way that leaves narrow-sense heritability as useful for my old breeding work as it always was? If several steps are guilty, say which is guiltiest, and price them against one another.
