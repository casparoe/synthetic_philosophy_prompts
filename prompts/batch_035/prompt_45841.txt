Does this pattern have a name? I keep noticing it and it feels like the kind of thing somebody must have carved out already. Setup: some action is available to any one of several people, and once anyone takes it, it's done — you can't unpublish a blog post. Everyone in the group privately thinks doing it is a mistake, but each person also figures that holding back personally changes nothing, since all that matters is whether SOMEBODY does it. And the ugly part: the more poeple who have the power, the more likely the thing gets done, because it only takes one person whos judgment is off that day. Security researchers who all agree to sit on an exploit until one of them publishes; a moderation team where any admin can ban a user, and everyone assumes someone else must have had a reason; one person naming an abuser and thereby forcing the story out for everyone else. It looks like the mirror image of the bystander effect — more people around makes the bad action more likely, not less. I know the volunteer's dilemma from game theory, but that's about people failing to step up for a good cause, whereas here stepping up IS the problem. Feels adjacent to free-riding too but not quite the same. So: is there an established term for this, and if so does it cover the epistemic bit (each actor has an equally good claim to knowing better, so nobody can just defer to the others) or only the structural bit (one actor suffices)? If there's no established term, coin one and define it properly, and tell me where the edges are — is a firing squad a case of this, or does shared responsibility dissolve it? And do the obvious fixes people propose (require k-of-n agreement, or appoint a designated decider) actually fix the epistemic problem, or do they just relocate it?
