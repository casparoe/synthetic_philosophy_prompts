I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, so forgive me if I mangle the terminology below. Two of my coworkers — Daniel and Maya — had a long, surprisingly heated argument at lunch about where to direct their charitable giving, and they eventually asked me to referee. I couldn't. I'm hoping you can adjudicate it properly, move by move.

Some background: both of them donate a fixed percentage of their salaries and try to pick causes in a roughly reasoned, spreadsheet-friendly way. Daniel has lately been pushing insect welfare research; Maya thinks this is insane. Here is the exchange as it unfolded, reconstructed as faithfully as I can.

(1) Daniel opened with a back-of-envelope estimate. Something like 10^18 insects exist at any given moment, and a plausible case can be made that their lives are, on balance, bad — brutal predation, parasitoid wasps that consume a still-living host from the inside, and enormous mortality in the earliest life stages. Even if you assign only a small probability, say 5%, that insects are sentient enough for their suffering to matter morally, and even if you grant each individual insect only a tiny fraction of the moral weight of a factory-farmed chicken, the multiplication gives you a total that dwarfs the roughly 70 billion land vertebrates in industrial farming. The arithmetic, he said, is not close: expected-value reasoning points at insects, and our donation dollars should follow.

(2) Maya replied that the arithmetic is not the problem — the inputs are. Every number in his estimate has error bars spanning multiple orders of magnitude, and at work we would never ship a decision on the output of a model like that. Multiplying a speculative probability by a speculative moral weight by an astronomical head count, she said, is a machine for converting imagination into apparent obligation: turn the knobs and it will output any conclusion you want, including the opposite one. Where estimates are that ungrounded, she refuses to act on expected value at all, and she thinks that refusal is rational rather than timid.

(3) Daniel said she was describing a different situation than the one he's in. He is not multiplying by an arbitrary probability pulled from nowhere — there is actual evidence: insects have nociceptors, their behavior changes when given analgesics or substances that act on opioid receptors, and they trade off feeding against avoiding noxious stimuli. A small, evidence-based probability is like the success odds of an early-stage startup: genuinely uncertain, not fictional, and a small positive expected value is still positive. And he accused her of a general danger: refusing to count small probabilities of enormous stakes is exactly how people end up ignoring catastrophic risks until it's too late.

(4) Maya answered on two fronts. First, on the evidence: she wasn't claiming the probability is zero, she was claiming the precision is illusory — and crucially, unlike an A/B test, this calculation has no feedback loop. Nothing will ever tell Daniel whether his 5% was calibrated, so the number can never be corrected by reality, which to her makes it not really an estimate at all. Second, and more fundamentally, she said the multiplication presupposes that welfare adds up by simple summation across individuals. If what matters morally cannot be totalled across a trillion barely-conscious lives the way you total CPU-hours on a bill, then the arithmetic never gets started no matter how good the evidence is.

(5) Daniel's counter: nearly every moral view aggregates somehow — even one that says each individual matters equally and separately will give insects overwhelming weight once the count is astronomical — so he doesn't think he needs the full additive utilitarian package. And folding her worries about moral uncertainty into the expected value just adds another small-probability term, which lands you back at step one. Besides, he said, her feedback-loop requirement would forbid spending money on any one-shot, irreversible risk — which we obviously shouldn't forbid.

(6) Maya closed by saying his position proves too much. If tiny-probability-times-astronomical-count is allowed to carry the argument, nothing in it stops at insects — the same structure licenses agonizing over bacteria, or about vast numbers of hypothetical minds, and a decision procedure that can't say where it stops is broken, not our discomfort with it. She would rather fund research into insect cognition precisely because we don't know enough to compute the expected value — reduce the uncertainty first, decide later.

The strange ending: they both walked away saying the right move is to fund research on insect cognition — Daniel because he thinks expected value already identifies it as the best marginal use of a dollar, Maya because she thinks the expected value is currently uncomputable and the research is how you fix that. I genuinely can't tell whether that convergence is real agreement or two people accidentally pointing at the same door for opposite reasons.
