i teach a second-year political philosophy seminar — 16 students, one 90-minute session — and next week is luck egalitarianism: the view that inequalities are unjust when they trace to luck but fine when they trace to choices. i don't want to lecture the distinction at them. i want them to actually run a miniature welfare state for an hour and hit the hard cases from the inside, before any names get attached to anything. design the session for me.

some things i want built in:

- use a token currency (raffle tickets toward extra credit or something) so the stakes are real-ish without actual money. split the class: roughly a third are administrators of the scheme, the rest are citizens. the administrators hold the treasury and decide who gets topped up.
- phase it so it starts clean and gets murky on purpose. first phase: pure luck — coin flips, card draws, some citizens just end up poor and everyone agrees the scheme should fix it. then a phase with genuine choices under known odds (option luck). then the murk: a phase where a citizen's "choice" is driven by a risk-preference card that was dealt to them randomly, and a phase where someone's needs are randomly assigned — like, they're dealt a card saying they need twice the tokens as anyone else to function at baseline. you work out the exact mechanics of what's dealt, what's chosen, and what's hidden from whom.
- somewhere in there, before the risky choices happen, citizens should be able to buy insurance from the treasury. i want to see whether the group discovers that move does real work.
- the administrators should only observe outcomes, not the underlying draws. so when a citizen ends up destitute, the administrators have to decide whether to compensate without knowing if it was chosen — and if they want to investigate, investigations cost the treasury tokens. i want them to feel how expensive it is to actually track responsibility.
- the endgame: at least one citizen who knowingly gambled everything and lost is broke, and the administrators must either enforce the principle (no bailout, that was your choice) or break it. i want the harshness of the view felt as a live decision they have to make, not a slide they read.
- the debrief maps each phase onto the philosophical pieces — the luck/choice line, option luck, responsibility-tracking costs, the harshness worry — and tells me what will predictably go wrong the first time i run it. my guess is the administrators will just go soft and equalize everything by round two, and i want to know if that's actually a failure or if its the lesson.
- be honest about what no classroom game can reproduce here. i'm guessing the interpersonal comparison stuff and the fact that real responsibility comes in degrees rather than card draws, but tell me what the activity structurally can't do and what i have to supply myself in the debrief.
- no names or literature until the debrief. i want at least one student to say something like "wait, but was that even really a choice" before i tell them philosophers have been fighting about that sentence for forty years.
