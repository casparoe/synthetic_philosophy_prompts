My reading group has spent several evenings on the frame problem, and we have come away with an argument that seems to prove far too much, though none of us can locate the break. Here it is, cleaned up.

Picture a robot with a large store of beliefs about an open world — open in the sense that arbitrarily many further facts bear on any one of them. It performs an action, say pulling a trolley out of a room. Almost all of its beliefs survive the action unchanged; a few do not (the trolley is no longer in the room; the fuse it was carrying is no longer burning there). The question is how the robot distinguishes the two classes.

(1) Appropriate action requires beliefs that remain accurate as the world changes; acting on stale beliefs will eventually be fatal.
(2) For any action, only some beliefs are affected; the rest persist unchanged.
(3) A system that identified the affected beliefs by examining them one at a time would face an unbounded task, since beliefs about an open world are indefinitely numerous, and real-time action rules that out.
(4) So a real-time system must be able to mark, in advance of any examination, which beliefs an action can touch — call this marking its relevance structure.
(5) A relevance structure either comes from examination, which (3) forbids, or from what the beliefs and the action mean.
(6) Meaning alone cannot sort relevance: from the content of a belief that the fuse is burning, nothing follows about the trolley's position, the room's temperature, or what is happening two rooms away, unless one already possesses the connecting generalisations — and those are as indefinitely numerous as the beliefs they connect.
(7) So every finite system either re-examines unboundedly many beliefs per action, or else acts while some of its beliefs are stale in ways it cannot from within itself detect; in neither case can a finite system act appropriately in real time in an open world.
(8) But bees, rats and we plainly do act appropriately in real time in open worlds.

To me, (1), (2), (3) and (8) look unassailable, and (6) looks like a mere truism about content. Two of us suspect the trouble lies in (5), because it reads like an exhaustive dilemma and yet neither horn seems to leave room for what actually happens when a rat bolts down a hole; but neither of us can say what the third option would be. I also notice the argument would seem to entail that we never notice our own beliefs going stale, which cannot be right, since plainly we do.

Please tell me which step to give up. Then answer as a numbered list of exactly five points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion. The five points should between them name the faulty step (or, if you think the argument is valid, the least costly premise to abandon), explain why that step looked fine, and say what rejecting it costs. Please write in British English.
