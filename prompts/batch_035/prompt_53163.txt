A friend of mine who works in evolutionary biology — and who periodically complains that evolutionary psychology is a genre of just-so storytelling — has a settled record of verdicts about which traits count as adaptations and which are mere byproducts. So far she has ruled as follows: the dark form of the peppered moth in industrial England, an adaptation; fever, an adaptation; our craving for sugar and fat, an adaptation; the panda's thumb, the wrist bone repurposed for stripping bamboo, also an adaptation, even though she readily concedes it is badly engineered; the redness of blood, a byproduct, since the chemistry of hemoglobin does all the work; male nipples, a byproduct of a shared developmental program; the ability to read, a byproduct, because writing is far too recent for selection to have built a dedicated capacity for it; and, though she allows it is a close call, she refuses to count the enjoyment of music as an adaptation.

Our next argument is already scheduled. On the table: emotional tears; adult lactose tolerance in populations with a long history of dairying, which arose only a few thousand years ago; our enjoyment of the burn of chili peppers, a compound plants evolved precisely in order to repel mammals; and goosebumps, the vestige of fur-raising.

If she is to stay consistent with herself, what should I expect her to say about each of the four? More than the verdicts themselves, I would like help reconstructing the rule she is implicitly applying, the alternative rules that fit her record just as well, and which of the four cases would most sharply divide those rival rules — and, if none of the four turns out to be decisive, what further case would be.
