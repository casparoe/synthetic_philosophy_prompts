Writing my thesis on state apologies and I've coded our parliament's full record, but there's a new case coming up and I genuinely can't tell what principle they're running. The record: they apologized (with compensation) for the wartime internment of an ethnic minority — legal at the time, carried out by the army, survivors still alive. They apologized for the forced adoptions of children born to unmarried mothers in the 60s — legal and officially endorsed back then, done through state agencies and church-run homes the state funded, with mothers and kids alive and organized. They refused to apologize for the 1840s colonial famine response — historians still argue whether the relief policy was negligent by the standards of the day, and there are no living victims. They apologized for secret chemical tests on soldiers — illegal at the time and covered up, some survivors alive. They refused to apologize for a colonial-era massacre — carried out by a settler militia the government had armed and later put up statues to, not by regular troops. And they apologized for unilaterally breaking an 1870s treaty with an indigenous nation — no living victims, but a straight breach of a treaty that was law when it was made. In the famine debate the PM of the day kept insisting the state would never apologize for a policy failure, only for things the state had actively done to people, though I can't tell if that's a clue or after-the-fact rationalization.

The new case: for about fifteen years the national water authority sat on its own test results showing lead contamination in a mining town's supply. Kids got sick, some adults died, survivors are alive and organized, and the authority was arguably within the legal duties of the day, since disclosure rules back then were vague. What's the verdict if they want to stay consistent with themselves? I can get both a living-victims-or-broken-undertaking rule and a direct-act-not-omission-or-proxy rule to fit all six earlier decisions, and this case is exactly where they split — so tell me which one they're more plausibly running, the closest precedent for each answer, and what each choice commits them to going forward, because theres a request to apologize for conscripting colonial subjects into the army a century ago already queued up behind this one.
