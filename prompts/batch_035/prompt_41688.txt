I serve on the release review board of a mid-sized AI lab, and I want a decision procedure we can actually run in a single two-hour meeting. Some background so the procedure fits our situation: candidate releases come to the board roughly twice a quarter — a new base model, an API-only release, an open-weights release, or a decision about whether to allow a third party's derivative of something we already released. We have two weeks per decision, a standing membership of six (two engineers, two policy staff, one security lead, one outside member), and escalation to the CEO only when we deadlock. Our recurring argument is always the same unstructured fight: the openness side says releasing weights lets the world inspect, harden, and build on the model, and that keeping it closed concentrates power in whoever can afford training compute; the caution side says an open-weights release is irreversible, hands capability to anyone regardless of intent, and can't be recalled the way an API can be throttled or withdrawn. Both sides are gesturing at real considerations — dual use, security through transparency, differential progress, the freerider problem, irreversibility — but we never convert them into questions we can actually answer in a meeting.

Please write the procedure. Specifically:

1. An ordered list of questions the board works through, one at a time, with for each: (a) what would count as a sufficient answer, given that our evidence is usually capability evals, threat-modeling exercises, and comparisons to models already public — not perfect information; (b) which consideration from the underlying debate the question is operationalizing, so members can see why it's on the list; (c) an explicit stop condition that yields "do not release open weights" (or "API only," if that's the intermediate verdict your procedure needs).

2. Marked points where the question is not an empirical one but a values question — where no eval could settle it and the procedure must hand off to an explicit vote, with the vote recorded as a vote rather than dressed up as a finding.

3. Rules for the three structural situations the procedure must handle beyond a simple new-model decision: a fine-tune of a model we already released (including ones we didn't authorize), the case where the weights have already leaked or a comparable model is openly available from elsewhere, and the case where a capability sits just under one of our internal thresholds, where the caution side says "just under means under, that's why we set thresholds" and the openness side says that reading makes the threshold do all the work.

4. Worked applications to three cases: (a) a 7-billion-parameter model fine-tuned for coding, whose capability evals come in around the median of models already openly available; (b) a frontier multimodal model whose biology-related evals landed just below our internal release threshold, with the evaluation team noting the margin is within the evals' noise; (c) an unauthorized third-party fine-tune, with our safety training visibly stripped out, of a model we open-weighted last year. For each, walk the procedure through to a verdict and note where the board would have had to vote.

5. Honesty requirements: identify at least one point where the procedure yields a verdict I won't like — I sit on the openness side of our internal argument — and say so plainly; and state what the procedure cannot do at all, including the kinds of cases where it will misfire or merely launder the majority's priors into an official-looking flowchart.

I want the result usable by people who have never read anything in the open-release debate, so keep whatever distinctions actually matter for the decisions and drop the ones that only matter in seminars — but tell me which distinctions you dropped, in case one of them turns out to be doing hidden work.
