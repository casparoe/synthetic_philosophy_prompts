I'm a novelist, four books in, and I've been trying to figure out when I should trust my own read on my work versus when I should step back and go by the statistics — the outside view, I think people call it. My gut verdicts, honestly: on my debut I was convinced I'd written something brilliant, and the base rate for debut novelists who feel that way should have humbled me but didn't, and the book was mediocre. On my second novel I was sure it was a mess and almost shelved it, and it's the best thing I've done, so there the inside view was worthless even though it was negative. Halfway through my third I switched from third person to first on a pure hunch, even though everyone knows late pov changes sink a draft, and it worked, and I'd do it again. But when a friend in my workshop asks if his book is any good, I give him the outside-view answer — his last two books sold badly, his agent is cooling on him — and that feels right to me, yet when my agent does the same thing to me ("literary books about grief don't sell right now") it feels like he's not even reading the book, and I resent it. Meanwhile if every beta reader tells me a scene doesn't work and I love the scene, I cave — I trust that aggregated outside view over my inside one, no resentment at all. And there's one place I flatly refuse the outside view: I write long, weird, genre-bending books, and the base rates there are brutal, and I've decided I just don't care. Is there any principle that gets all these verdicts, or something close, and does it survive my current situation: fifth book, inside view says it's the real thing, outside view says this kind of book almost always fails commercially but occasionally becomes a cult classic? If a principle can't save every verdict, tell me which ones have to go and why, and please answer in American English.
