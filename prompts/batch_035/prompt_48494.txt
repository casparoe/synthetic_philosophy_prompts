In the parliamentary democracies that retain them — Britain, Scandinavia, Spain, Japan — constitutional monarchies exhibit an arrangement whose surface logic is strange: the hereditary head of state stands at the formal apex of the constitution, holding powers that on paper remain vast (appointing and dismissing ministers, assenting to legislation, dissolving Parliament), yet each of those powers is neutralized so completely by convention that no British monarch has withheld assent since Queen Anne did in 1708, that the speech opening each parliamentary session is written entirely by the government of the day, and that in Japan the postwar constitution goes furthest of all, defining the emperor as a symbol of the state with no powers of government while locating sovereignty in the people. Sweden presents the limiting case: its 1974 constitutional reform stripped the monarchy of even the formal political functions, which passed to the Speaker of the elected chamber, who now nominates the prime minister. So the shared pattern is to strip a hereditary office of every actual exercise of power and then keep the office — ceremonially central, constitutionally inert, hereditary in succession, and in most of these countries consistently more popular than the elected politicians beneath it.

What theory of democratic legitimacy, if any, does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view, and in doing so address four questions.

First, does the arrangement presuppose a sharp distinction between the symbolic or constitutive functions of a state and its operative ones, such that only the operative functions need democratic authorization? Or is that a distinction the practice fits with but does not strictly require — could a pure electoral-authorization view, under which every office exercising public authority must trace to the ballot, also license the arrangement, on the ground that an office which never exercises power needs no mandate?

Second, the load-bearing mechanism is convention: unwritten, legally unenforceable norms. Does the practice therefore presuppose a view of constitutional normativity on which norms can constrain power without any sanction behind them? Is that presupposition stable, given that dormant reserve powers do occasionally surface — as when an unelected governor-general dismissed an elected Australian government in 1975 — and does the one exercise of a dormant power falsify the implicit theory, or instead reveal what that theory really was?

Third, a familiar defense holds that the monarch's exclusion from politics is precisely what prevents the elected government from being identified with the state itself: a politically neutral apex is supposed to make it possible to oppose the government of the day without opposing the state. Does the practice presuppose that political neutrality is a coherent and attainable status for a hereditary official, and can that presupposition survive the observation that the neutrality is itself a convention that could fail rather than a self-guaranteeing property of the office?

Fourth, say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined: which rival theories of legitimacy would each license it as it stands, and what, specifically, would have to change in the institutions if the implicit view were replaced by a strict electoral-authorization theory on the one hand, or a purely consequentialist one on the other?
