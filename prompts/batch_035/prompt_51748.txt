So, bit of background on me: i'm a longtime rationalist blog/fourm reader, been in that world since the old LessWrong days, internalized all the "probability is in the mind" stuff, and now I've gone and volunteered to present to my department's philosophy of science reading group, which was maybe a mistake.

The thesis I'm defending, roughly: once you take the Principal Principle seriously as a *deference* norm, the "objective" in "objective chance" stops doing any work. My pitch is that what PP actually enforces is "match your credences to the best predictor you could in principle construct" — the idealized forecaster who has seen all the macro-level history and does the best systematizing of it available. The chance of the coin landing heads is 0.5 not because some propensity is sitting inside the coin, but because the class of maximally-calibrated predictors converges on 0.5 given everything admissible, and PP just tells you to defer to that. So chance is the consensus of the best calibrated predictor you could build, and that's the whole story — the extra metaphysics (chances as sui generis quantities in the world) is idle machinery that never gets traction on anything. I'm NOT saying probabilities are all in the mind in the crude sense — I'm saying the mind-independence we care about is reference-class-shared expert deference, not mind-independent quantities. That's the line I'm trying to walk and I suspect it's where I'll get torn apart.

The reading group is very much not my home crowd. About ten people: two hardline Humeans who know Lewis's chance stuff backwards (they'll definitely bring up the bug — the thing where conditioning on the full Humean mosaic plus PP seems to hand you inconsistent advice), one powers/nomic-necessitation guy who thinks chances are real dispositions out there in things, a couple of decision theory people who only ever ask about betting quotients, and the rest are grad students who'll stay quiet unless blood is in the water. None of them read the blogosphere; if I open with "calibrated superforecaster" they'll hear "he got this from the internet" and tune out. And honestly my argument is thin: it's basically (1) PP says match credences to chance given admissible info, (2) the only non-magical candidates for what "chance" could denote are best-system statistics or idealized-predictor credences, (3) once you spell out the deference norm fully, the extra metaphysics drops out as idle. I can already feel step 3 being were they'll come after me.

For each one: how damaging is it really, what should I concede up front, the two-minute reply, and where the reply is soft. Order them by how much damage they'd do if I flub them.
