I'm an undergraduate in a political theory seminar on Hannah Arendt, and our department has invited a senior scholar who has spent her career defending the banality-of-evil reading of Eichmann in Jerusalem against the revisionist scholarship that followed the opening of the Argentine material. I've been asked to moderate the student Q&A, and my professor wants the questions to have real teeth — not a walk through the book's reputation, but questions that press where the literature actually presses.

Some of the things I'd want covered, so you can shape questions around them: Arendt's own insistence that the banality she attributed to Eichmann was a factual observation about him as a person — his inability to think from another's standpoint — and not a thesis that the deeds themselves were banal; her remark that in Kantian terms Eichmann's conduct was a case of thoughtlessness rather than stupidity; her account of Eichmann's grotesque invocation of the categorical imperative, which she read as a sign he could not tell thinking from reciting; the shift from radical evil as she characterized it in The Origins of Totalitarianism to banal evil in 1963, and whether the two are compatible; and her legally awkward position that the court was right to hang Eichmann even though she thought the trial's framing, with its focus on Jewish suffering and its audience, mostly served purposes other than justice.

The hardest material for our guest: while hiding in Argentina in the late 1950s, Eichmann sat for a series of recorded conversations with a group of former Nazi journalists, the transcripts of which became the core of Bettina Stangneth's Eichmann Before Jerusalem. On that record he comes across as an ideological antisemite, proud of his role in the deportations, conversant with the details of the extermination program, and planning his own memoir as a contribution to the cause. Our guest, to my knowledge, argues that none of this refutes Arendt — that the Argentina Eichmann was performing for a Nazi audience as the Jerusalem Eichmann was performing for an Israeli one, and that a man can be both an ideologue and incapable of genuine thought.

Please draft eight to ten questions for the session. I want at least one question each on: the thoughtlessness-versus-ideology problem raised by the Argentine transcripts, and what it would take for the new evidence to count against Arendt rather than merely against her factual assumptions about one man; the Kant material and what it shows about Arendt's concept of thinking; and the tension between her contempt for the trial's theater and her endorsement of its verdict. For each question, include the follow-up you would ask if she gives the answer a defender of her position is most likely to give — for instance, if she meets the Argentina evidence with the claim that Eichmann was merely performing again, I want the follow-up that asks how, absent access to his head, we could ever tell the performance from the man, and whether a thesis that survives every possible counterevidence that way has been protected rather than defended. Avoid biographical questions about Arendt or the guest, and avoid anything that turns on settling scholarly details we can't check in the room.
