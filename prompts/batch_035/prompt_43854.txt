My husband died about seven months ago and, long story short, I ended up going down a philosophy of time rabbit hole instead of sleeping. I know, classic grief behavior. Anyway I got kind of obsessed with the block universe idea — eternalism, where past and future events all exist tenselessly and "now" isn't special, nothing really passes away because nothing was ever "going away" in the first place. At first this felt weirdly comforting, like he's not gone, he's just... elsewhere in the block. Then it started feeling horrifying, like every moment of his dying is permanently stamped into reality and always will be "there." I can't tell which one it is and honestly both readings keep flipping on me.

So here's my ask: write me a short story that actually dramatizes this, not an essay with characters pasted on. Some constraints, because everything I've found that touches this stuff turns into a physics lecture in a trench coat:

- No character who is a physicist or philosopher who explains things to the protagonist. The idea has to get into the story through the plot or through something the character does or notices, not through dialogue where someone helpfully defines eternalism.
- I want the story to turn on the specific thing I keep circling: whether tenseless existence should change how we feel about loss at all. Like, if the metaphysics is true, does grief have a rational object or not? The story should put a character in a position where they have to act on one answer or the other, not just muse about it.
- Somewhere in there I'd like the character to run into the growing block view (past exists, future doesn't) as a rival possibility, and the story should make that alternative feel like it matters — like a real fork, not just a footnote.
- Please resist the two cheap endings: the "and she finally felt peace" ending and the "and she realized it was all cold and meaningless" ending. I'm fine with sad, I'm fine with ambiguous, I just don't want a greeting card or a nihilism bumper sticker.

Length-wise, whatever feels right for the story, maybe 800-1500 words? I'm not going to count. And dont worry about getting the physics perfectly right, it's more the existential angle I care about. If it helps, the character doesn't have to be a widow — honestly maybe it's better if it isn't, so it's not just my life with the serial numbers filed off. Could be a demolition worker, a preservationist, an amnesiac, a hospice nurse, whoever makes the concept actually do narrative work.
