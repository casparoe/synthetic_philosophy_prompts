I've been doing competitive forecasting for a few years now — a couple of IARPA-style tournaments, then one of the commercial platforms — and I did annoyingly well, which is exactly my problem. Next month I'm giving a talk at our annual forecasting meetup, and the audience is maybe sixty serious tournament forecasters, several of them ex-Good Judgment Project types who are proud of their track records, a couple of people who literally trade on their forecasts, and one philosopher of science who has never once let an Aumann agreement argument go to waste. My thesis is one this crowd is going to hate: that the edge good forecasters have over simple reference-class base rates is real but small, and that it's mostly calibration discipline and knowing when not to update, not superior inside-view reasoning — so teams and platforms should be pushing people toward deferring and aggregating (visible crowd medians, celebrating the aggregate instead of the individual leaderboard hero) rather than rewarding heroic inside-view synthesis. Basically a modesty position: the outside view and the crowd over your own clever chain of reasoning, and the institutional design should bake that in.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet for this. I want the objections this specific crowd will actually raise, not the ones I'd get from a philosophy department — phrased the way a superforecaster would actually phrase them, ordered by how much damage they'd do to my talk, each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft. I can already predict some of whats coming: the guy who'll say the tournament data itself shows meritocratic aggregation beats equal-weight crowds, so deference is leaving accuracy on the table; the one who'll say modesty is self-defeating because somebody has to generate the signal the crowd aggregates, and if everyone defers the whole thing collapses; and the philosopher will push that if I'm right about modesty, my confidence in my own argument should shrink too. What I want is the full field beyond those, including the objection Im hoping nobody asks — and for that one specifically, tell me whether my prepared answer ("I defer when I can identify who's more likely to be right, and I couldn't find anyone more right about this than me, which is itself the phenomenon I'm describing") actually holds up or is just a cute line that falls apart the moment someone pushes on it.
