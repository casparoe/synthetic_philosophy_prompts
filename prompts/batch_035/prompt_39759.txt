In a class debate about whether soldiers who fight for a country waging an unjust war do anything wrong, a classmate of mine closed her turn by declaring that all's fair in love and war, and sat down as though the matter were settled — her point, I take it, being that the soldiers on the unjust side are no more to be blamed than those defending against them. I have a rebuttal due in the next session, and I suspect the proverb is quietly doing several different jobs at once, only some of which are available to her. Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence — your verdict on whether any defensible reading of the proverb actually supports what she used it to say — and then give the reasoning. Lay out the distinct precise claims the proverb could be expressing, ordered from the near-trivial to the most radical: that both love and war license ordinary deception; that in war the rules of ordinary interpersonal morality are suspended rather than merely altered; that whatever is permissible for one side is permissible for the other; that combatants on both sides are moral equals regardless of the justice of their cause; that no one outside a war is positioned to judge those inside it; and any further reading you think genuinely lurks in the sentence. For each reading, say whether it is true, whether it is substantive or a platitude, and above all whether it would deliver my classmate her conclusion — since some readings excuse everyone equally, some excuse no one, and some excuse only the side that did nothing wrong, and only the first kind helps her. Flag any reading that is true but too weak to end a debate, and any that would end the debate but is false. Please give me this as a list I can bring to class, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
