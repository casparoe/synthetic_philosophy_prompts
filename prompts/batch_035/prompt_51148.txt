My legal-philosophy reading group, six of us, spent last term on the 1958 Hart–Fuller exchange in the Harvard Law Review, and this week two members got into an argument the rest of us could not settle. The trigger was the grudge informer case, which both knew from Hart's essay: in 1944, a woman who wanted to be rid of her husband reported to the authorities the derogatory remarks he had made about Hitler while home on leave from the army; he was sentenced to death by a military tribunal, though after serving time in prison he was sent to the front instead of being executed. After the war, the wife, along with the judge who had sentenced him, was prosecuted for the unlawful deprivation of liberty under § 289 of the German Criminal Code of 1871, and in 1949 the court of last resort in criminal matters acquitted the judge but convicted the wife, on the ground that she had, of her own free choice, made use of a Nazi statute contrary "to the sound conscience and sense of justice of all decent human beings."

Here is how the argument went, as nearly as I can reproduce it:

(1) Anouk began on the court's side. The ruling made sense, she said, because the wife's only defense was that the denunciation statute was valid law in 1944, and the court was right to refuse that defense. A statute of that kind is not law at any time; treating it as law defames the concept, and the wife deserved punishment under the ordinary code, which is just what she got.

(2) Dev answered that the ruling was disguised retroactivity. Either the statute was valid law in 1944 — in which case the wife did nothing illegal then, and convicting her under the 1871 code means punishing an act that was no crime when done — or it was not law, in which case the court needed a natural-law test to say so. His own view, following Hart: the honest choice is between two evils, letting her go unpunished or punishing her under frankly retrospective law, and the second is the lesser one — but it should be called what it is.

(3) Anouk said his honest cure was worse than the disease: punishing under a law that did not exist at the time violates prospectivity, one of the very values he claims to be protecting, so his candor merely relabels the same violation. And she added a wider point: the Nazi system had forfeited legality as a system — secret decrees, retroactive orders, statutes contradicted by what officials actually did — so the ruling was a statement about that system's claim to be law at all, not a denial that the 1944 statute had clear wording.

(4) Dev pressed her on the level at which she was operating: by Fuller's own tests — publicity, generality, prospectivity, clarity — the 1944 denunciation statute was formally unobjectionable; it was published, prospective, and about as clear as criminal statutes get. If the inner morality of law is to do the work she wants, it either collapses into efficacy, in which case the Nazi system worked and that counts in her favor too, or it quietly becomes substantive natural law, in which case her real authority is Radbruch's formula about intolerable injustice rather than Fuller's proceduralism — and her two defenses come apart.

(5) Anouk replied that the acquittal of the judge shows the court was neither vengeful nor drawing an ex post line wherever it liked: it distinguished the judge, whose position she granted was harder, from the wife, who had a free choice and used the machinery of terror for private ends. Refusing to call the statute law was the court's way of saying that fidelity to law had been withdrawn from that system, and that the wife could not invoke a legality she had helped to corrupt.

(6) Dev closed with the point he thought decisive: once the systemic test does the work, the question of whether it was law just becomes the question of whether the regime was good enough to count as a legal system — a moral question in legal dress. The court answered it with the conscience formula while presenting itself as applying the law; Dev's position only names what the court actually did. He would rather the legislature pass an openly retroactive statute and take responsibility for it than have a court pretend the 1944 statute never existed.

They asked me to referee, and I honestly could not. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each numbered move, say whether it is right, wrong, or right about less than it claims, with your reasons. Then identify anything of substance that both of them missed. End your reply with a single line of the form Verdict: followed by one sentence stating who, if either, comes out more right overall, and the single strongest reason.
