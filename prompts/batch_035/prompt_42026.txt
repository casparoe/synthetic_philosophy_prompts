I'm teaching a seminar section on punishment this term and have been preparing the Camus unit — his 1957 essay on the guillotine, the one that opens with his father's collapse after witnessing an execution and ends by proposing hard labor for life in place of the death penalty. Rereading it, I tried to reconstruct what I take to be the essay's most forceful line of argument as an explicit chain of premises, and I've ended up with something that looks valid and yet appears to prove far more than Camus can have wanted. I'd like help locating the failure, because I honestly cannot tell whether the problem lies in Camus or in my reconstruction.

Here is the chain. (1) Our criminal law treats premeditation as an aggravating circumstance: the killing planned in cold blood is more culpable than the killing committed in rage or panic, even where the outcome is identical. (2) An execution is, by the essay's famous characterization, "the most premeditated of murders": it is scheduled months or years in advance at trial, fixed on a date, announced to the condemned man, who is then held at the state's mercy through the whole course of his appeals. (3) What makes premeditation aggravating is the deliberateness with which the agent persists in killing despite every opportunity to desist, so the more deliberation and the more opportunities to stop, the greater the culpability. (4) Official capacity cannot transform a wrong into a right: no arrangement among people can license them, acting in their collective person, to do what would be a wrong for each of them taken singly — this, I take it, is the premise Camus most needs, since it is what blocks the standard defense that the executioner kills lawfully rather than murderously. (5) So the execution is not merely a wrong but, by the standards of (1) and (3), the most aggravated deliberate killing a society performs — worse, in point of premeditation, than the vast majority of the crimes it answers. (6) A killing that is worse than the killings our law ranks among the most serious murders, and that is performed deliberately by many people acting in concert — judge, jurors, executioner, the head of state who declines to pardon — is itself murder, and aggravated murder at that. (7) Therefore every participant in an execution, and everyone who by vote authorizes the code that requires it, is guilty of aggravated murder, and a consistent legal order would have to say so.

The trouble is what (7) implies. It makes accomplices of millions of ordinary citizens in retentionist democracies, most of whom never deliberate about the matter at all; it makes Camus's abolitionist journalism committed to addressing judges as the principals in a crime; and it sits awkwardly with the fact that his own remedy — a ten-year trial of hard labor for life — treats the officials who would administer that regime as reformers rather than as members of a criminal conspiracy. Something has gone wrong, but each premise looks defensible to me in isolation. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? For what it is worth, I would rather keep (4) than any other premise, so if (4) has to go I would want to know exactly what the abolitionist case loses when it does — and if instead the flaw is a slippage between steps, tell me what shifts and why the shift is invisible on the surface.
