Back in 2016 I covered the AlphaGo–Lee Sedol match for my magazine — well, "covered" from my laptop in London, which felt appropriately modern. The moment everyone remembers is move 37 in game 2: the shoulder hit on the fifth line that the pros in the commentary booth first called a mistake until they realized it was winning the game, and the DeepMind people said afterwards that by their own model's reckoning a human pro would find that move maybe one time in ten thousand. The less-quoted mirror image is move 78 in game 4, Lee Sedol's wedge in the center — the move the press dubbed God's move — which found the blind spot, and after which AlphaGo, by all accounts, did not seem to register that it was now losing for another dozen moves or so. I've finally sat down to write the retrospective essay I've been putting off for way to long, and I keep coming back to Dennett's three stances as the frame — but I don't trust my grip on it, and the draft keeps sliding into vibes.

Here's the obvious line, as I understand it: at the physical stance, move 37 is just a specific forward pass through a specific stack of weights, something we could in principle trace multiplication by multiplication; at the design stance, AlphaGo is a system built to win Go games, so expect moves that are good at winning Go games; at the intentional stance, AlphaGo "believed" it was ahead and "wanted" a particular kind of trade, and — the crucial part — this stance apparently worked as prediction, better than most of us managed with the other two. Move 78 looks like the stress test: what looked from the outside like serene confidence was a value network misreading, and for a stretch AlphaGo kept "playing to win" a position it had already lost. So what I'd like is a careful, disciplined analysis of the match through the lens of the stances, not a vibes piece: what each stance said at move 37, at move 78, and in the sad stretch afterwards, and where the intentional stance genuinely pays for itself versus where it's just compression of the design stance with anthropomorphic flavoring sprinkled on top.

Two more things, because they're what the essay actually has to settle. My editor's pushback is that since DeepMind can in principle open the box and hand you the exact computation, any "beliefs" we ascribe to AlphaGo are shorthand for commentators, a broadcast convenience — and I want to know whether Dennett's real patterns stuff genuinely answers that (the claim that a pattern is real when tracking it lets you compress and predict, whether or not you can also derive it from the microlevel), or whether it just restates the question in a way that sounds deeper than it is. And then the asymmetry that bugs me: during the match Lee Sedol could take the design stance toward AlphaGo — study it, probe for blind spots, which is essentially what move 78 was — while AlphaGo, as far as anyone could tell, never updated its model of its opponent at all mid-match. Does that tell us something about the limits of the intentional stance, or about who the stance is actually for — us spectators, trying to narrate — rather than for anyone actually playing? Feel free to tell me if some part of my framing is off, I'd rather fix it now than after the fact.
