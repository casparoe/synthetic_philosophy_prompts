I run a small sea-kayak outfitting company on a stretch of coast where the weather turns fast, and every evening from roughly April through October I have to make a go/no-go call for the next day's tours: launch as scheduled, delay to the afternoon, move to a sheltered route, or cancel outright and refund. A wrong call in either direction costs me — a cancellation on a day that turns out calm means lost revenue and annoyed customers, while launching into an unexpected squall means genuine safety risk. I have no academic background, but I've spent a lot of evenings reading whatever popular material I could find about chaos and prediction, and a few ideas have stuck with me: that even a perfectly deterministic system can be impossible to forecast beyond some horizon, that small measurement errors can double and redouble until they swallow the forecast entirely, that some situations are far more forgiving than others, and that when the different runs of a forecast model disagree wildly, that disagreement is itself information. I've also noticed that the same coastal conditions are not equally predictable — a stable high-pressure week is easy to call, while the transition periods when a front is approaching are where I keep getting burned.

I'd like you to turn all of this into a decision procedure that I — and eventually a part-time guide with no weather background at all — can actually run in about ten minutes on any given evening, using only the free forecast products I can see on my phone: the single "official" forecast, the map of model runs drawn as diverging lines, the recent track record of forecasts for my specific launch spot, and my own morning observation of what the sky and wind are actually doing. The procedure should tell me, in order: what to check first, second, third; what counts as a green light, a yellow light, or a red light at each step; when the spread of the model runs means I should distrust the headline forecast and plan for the worst plausible outcome instead of the most likely one; and how to use the fact that some forecast variables (temperature, say) are much more reliable than others (wind gusts, sudden fog) to weight what I read. Somewhere in it I want a step for deciding how far out my own local knowledge extends — at what point my ten years of watching this particular bay stop counting as data and start being a hunch I should discount.

I also keep getting into an argument with my brother, who took some philosophy in college and says that because the atmosphere presumably obeys fixed physical laws, the weather tomorrow is already settled one way or the other, so a forecast is simply either right or wrong, and all this talk of probability is a dodge. I don't think that's right — it seems to me that the forecast being uncertain and the weather being determined are somehow both true at once — but I can't articulate why without hand-waving. Please fold into the procedure a short plain-language explanation I could give him of why knowing that a system is deterministic tells you almost nothing about whether you can predict it, and where the honest limits of that explanation lie.

After laying out the procedure, run it through three worked evenings: (1) a stable summer high-pressure day where the tours obviously go; (2) an approaching-front day where the headline forecast says light winds all day but the model runs split sharply between calm and gusting well past my safety threshold; and (3) a day where my own experience of the bay says the forecast is wrong, and I want the procedure to overrule me. In at least one of these, the procedure should land on an answer I won't like. Finally, be explicit about the places where the procedure runs out and the call comes back to me as a judgment — I'd rather know where the math stops than pretend the checklist decides everything.
