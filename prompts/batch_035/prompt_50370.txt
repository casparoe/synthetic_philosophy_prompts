Trying to sort out the reference class problem by going on gut verdicts, and I'd like to know whether there's a principle behind them or whether I'm just being inconsistent.

For estimating how much longer I'll live, "men my age who don't smoke" seems like the right class — not all men my age, but also not "men my age, non-smokers, non-drinkers, with my exact cholesterol," which feels like gerrymandering. For whether my startup will fold within five years, I want the base rate for startups generally, and swapping in "startups with smart founders" feels like motivated reasoning, even though it's the finer class. For whether to take a statin, I want the class matched to me on cholesterol, blood pressure, and family history — as fine-grained as the data supports. For whether my marriage will last, though, the published divorce rate feels like it tells me nothing at all, even though the startup base rate felt informative — that pair is the one I can't reconcile. And there's a case that seems clearly wrong: a friend estimated his odds of getting rich off the class of people who, like him, were sure they'd get rich. I can't articulate what distinguishes that from the statin matching, but its got to be distinguishable somehow.

Is there a principle that saves all or most of these? If not, which verdicts should go? And what does the surviving principle say about the case I keep flip-flopping on — my odds of eventually getting tenure, where conditioning on "people who finished a PhD in philosophy" ignores a lot, conditioning on "assistant professors currently on the tenure track" bakes in an earlier win, and I can't tell whether the right move is to pick one, blend them, or refuse the question.
