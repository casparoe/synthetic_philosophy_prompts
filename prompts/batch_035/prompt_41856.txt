I spent the better part of my career in vision science — first single-unit recordings in primate V1, later psychophysical and imaging work on perceptual learning in human observers — and since retiring I have taken up philosophy of perception with something between enthusiasm and exasperation. My reading group has recently turned to what philosophers call cognitive penetration: the thesis that our beliefs, expectations, and desires can shape the content of perceptual experience itself, rather than merely how we attend to it or what we judge on its basis. My scientific instincts are hostile to the thesis, but I have found the dialectic slipperier than I anticipated, and I would like help constructing a properly argued case rather than a reflexive defense of modularity.

Here is the assignment I would like you to carry out. Please argue for the position that the classic cases of perceptual expertise — the radiologist who sees the tumor that is invisible to the medical student, the chicken-sexer who sees the sex of the chick, the mycologist who sees the Amanita where a novice sees only a white cap — do not, on reflection, constitute cognitive penetration, and that treating them as such trivializes the thesis in an epistemologically damaging way. In developing the argument, I have three strands in mind, though you should feel free to reorganize or supplement them. First, a taxonomy distinguishing diachronic from synchronic effects: expertise reshapes the perceptual system over months and years, plausibly through the same low-level plasticity that drives ordinary laboratory perceptual learning, whereas whatever is philosophically interesting about penetration concerns whether occurrent beliefs can, here and now, alter what an experience presents. Second, a suspicion about attention and post-perceptual processing: much of what passes for "seeing" in expertise cases may be very fast recognition applied to unpenetrated perceptual output, so the lay description overreaches. Third, the epistemological stake: if slow, content-driven learning counted as penetration, then essentially all adult perception would come out theory-laden, which would flatten a distinction worth preserving and concede more to the skeptic about observation than the phenomenon warrants.

Please make the argument as strong as you honestly can, and anticipate the most obvious objection — namely, that the diachronic/synchronic line is ad hoc, and that a top-down, content-driven effect operating over weeks is a top-down effect all the same. If you think the argument ultimately fails at some joint, identify where; but do the best faithful job on its behalf first, so that I know whether my instincts are earning their keep.
