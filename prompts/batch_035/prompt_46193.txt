I chair the centennial planning committee of a small former mill town, and the anniversary has turned into a fight about our own past. The event we plan to commemorate is a 1927 labor dispute that ended when the town's largest mill burned to the ground. The town's collective memory, kept alive by descendants of the mill-owning family and by a plaque erected in 1952, holds that striking workers set the fire, and that the militia's arrival was a rescue. The county archive, recently digitized by volunteers, tells a different story: coroner's inquest records, insurance investigation files, and two newspaper accounts from towns twenty miles away suggest the fire began in a storage shed the company had been using to house strikebreakers, and that guards fired on picketers earlier the same evening. Half the committee wants the centennial to follow the archive; the other half argues that the plaque's version is who we are, that the town's identity and its fragile reconciliation between old mill families and old union families rests on it, and that "correcting" it now would reopen a wound the community deliberately closed. I need to run the committee's next meeting like an adversarial collaboration rather than a shouting match.

Please draft the joint statement that a committed memory-keeper (who holds that a community's shared memory is entitled to standing in its own right, not merely as a faulty draft of history) and a committed archival historian (who holds that the record, once established beyond reasonable doubt, is the only legitimate basis for public commemoration) could both sign after a good-faith collaboration. I want the following, in this order:

1. A numbered section of claims both parties accept — about what the archive does and does not show, about what the plaque's version means to the community, about the limits of both memory and records, and about the town's obligations to the dead on all sides.
2. A table of their residual disagreements, each phrased in wording its own side would accept — including, at minimum, whether collective memory has epistemic weight at all, whether public commemoration is a truth-apt practice or a civic-ritual one, and who the "we" that commemorates is supposed to be.
3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party. I suspect one crux is whether the 1952 plaque counts as evidence about 1927 or only as evidence about 1952, and another is whether a community can be said to have a right to its own self-understanding — but you should identify the real cruxes, which may differ from my guesses.
4. For each crux, a concrete case, procedure, or piece of reasoning both would agree bears on it, with each party's prediction about the outcome written down in advance. (For example, if you think a structured oral-history project with living descendants would settle something, say what each side expects it to find and why.)
5. A short concluding section on whether any part of their dispute is verbal rather than substantive — for instance, whether they are using "true" in different senses when they say the plaque's story is or is not true — and, if so, which part and why.

The statement should be usable as a working document for my committee: specific to our fire, our plaque, and our archive, not a generic essay on memory versus history. Please do not invent citations, archives, or scholarship; where a general point would normally be supported by reference to historians of memory or to real commemorative controversies, make the point directly and on its merits instead.
