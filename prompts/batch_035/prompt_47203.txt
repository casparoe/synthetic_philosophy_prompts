As a novelist, I have long assumed that judgments of literary taste answer to something beyond private pleasure — that when a trusted reader tells me a chapter drags, she has caught something real about the prose rather than merely reporting her mood. A critic friend has now put to me an argument that dissolves this, and each of its steps seems defensible on its own. (1) Beauty is not a quality residing in objects themselves; it exists only in the mind that contemplates them, as a sentiment of pleasure. (2) A judgment that expresses nothing but the speaker's own sentiment cannot be incorrect, any more than a wince or an outburst of laughter can be; sincerity supplies all the correctness there is. (3) My editor sincerely judges my ending overwrought, and I sincerely judge it the finest passage I have written, so each of our judgments is correct. (4) If both judgments are correct, then there is no further fact about the ending that either of us has got wrong, and nothing left to adjudicate between us. (5) Therefore there are no better or worse judges of literary merit; submitting manuscripts to discerning readers rather than to the first person in the street is mere ceremony, and the search for a standard of taste is empty. I cannot accept (5) — I know some readers are better judges than others as surely as I know anything — yet I cannot locate where the argument bends. Which step should I give up, why did that step look innocuous, and what must I concede in rejecting it? In particular, must I retreat all the way to treating beauty as a real quality in things themselves, which I am loath to do, or is there some intermediate position the argument has quietly ruled out without earning?
