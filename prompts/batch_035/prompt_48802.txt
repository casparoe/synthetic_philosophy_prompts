In Norway and Sweden, anyone's tax return is public record -- you can look up what your neighbor, your coworker, or your boss earned last year and what they paid in tax. Norway even let anyone search all of this anonymously online for a while, and newspapers ran searchable databases of the richest people in your area; then they changed it so you have to log in and the person you look up gets notified (journalists are exempt). And here's the puzzle: these are the same countries that are privacy maximalists about basically everything else -- health records, personal data, all of it. So what does public tax data actually presuppose? Some view where wage transparency serves equal dignity (nobody gets to bluff about status, weaker parties get leverage in salary negotiations, the tax system stays honest because everyone can check on everyone)? Or is it better read as presupposing high trust rather than producing it -- the practice only being tolerable in a society where people already trust each other not to misuse the info -- and if so, isn't there a circularity problem there? Reconstruct the implicit view of privacy and equality the practice needs, tell me whether it actually rules out treating privacy as a basic right or can live alongside it, and then say what would have to change before you could export this to a lower-trust country without it turning into a shaming machine or a tool for scammers. I'd also like your take on whether that exportability question generalizes to the whole "can the Nordic model be exported" debate or whether it's a special case.
