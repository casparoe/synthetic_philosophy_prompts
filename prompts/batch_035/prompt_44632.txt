I'm a novelist, about two thirds through a book set in a fictional northern European kingdom — the kind where the royal family takes the bus and the king does his own grocery shopping — and my narrator is the queen herself: someone who opens parliament reading a speech written by ministers whose policies she privately loathes, gives assent to laws she argued against at the weekly audience, and is constitutionally forbidden from saying a word about any of it in public. To write her inner life properly I need to understand what the institution actually is underneath, and I keep snagging on what looks like a straight-up contradiction. These systems — the British, the Scandinavian ones, the Japanese — all insist that all authority flows from the people; sovreignty rests with the people, and the postwar Japanese constitution even went so far as to redefine the emperor as a symbol of the nation while vesting sovereignty in the people. And yet every formal act of government is still performed in the name of a person who got the job by birth, can never be fired or voted out, and holds sweeping powers on paper that everyone agrees must never, ever be used. The British monarch hasn't refused assent to a bill in roughly three centuries, and the shared understanding is that actually using the power would trigger a constitutional crisis — but nobody deletes the power from the books. Meanwhile the very same democracies have been dismantling every other hereditary element, like the hereditary peers in the House of Lords, precisely on the ground that birth is no title to political power. So we abolish hereditary legislators but keep a hereditary head of state, and the one is treated as a scandal while the other is treated as the glue of the whole constitution.

So here's what I want from you: reconstruct the implicit theory of political legitimacy that this arrangement presupposes. Is it something like Bagehot's old distinction between the dignified and the efficient parts of a constitution — the monarch as the embodiment of the state's continuity, kept ceremonially distinct from the government of the day, so that consent-based authority and symbolic continuity get assigned to different actors? Or does the practice quietly presuppose something more cynical and maybe more honest: that consent was always mostly theater, and the monarchy simply keeps the theater visible instead of laundering it through elections? Or is the whole thing underdetermined — could the exact same practice sit on top of several rival theories of legitimacy, and if so, tell me which parts of the practice each theory would have to strain to explain, and which parts fit them all equally well, so I can't infer the theory from the practice. And lastly: what would actually have to change if you swapped in a strictly literal reading of popular sovereignty and had the people elect the head of state directly? Could the nonpartisan, above-politics function survive an election, or does that function depend on the accident of birth precisely because nobody chose her? I need all this for the queen's monologue, so the more precisely you can pin down what the crown is *for*, the better — and please be honest if the answer is that the institution doesn't presuppose any one theory at all, because that ambiguity might be the most interesting thing about her.
