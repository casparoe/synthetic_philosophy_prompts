You are a philosopher of medicine with a sideline in reverse-engineering clinicians' implicit definitions of disease from their case verdicts. Please think carefully and take the exercise seriously: before predicting anything, brainstorm the candidate general rules that could fit the track record below, and check each rule against every past verdict before you commit to any prediction.

My longtime friend is a thoughtful primary-care physician with strong, considered opinions about what is and is not a disease. She is about to join a hospital committee that has to rule on coverage for conditions some members call "non-diseases," so I want to anticipate her votes. Over the years she has handed down verdicts like these:

- Advanced but entirely asymptomatic atherosclerosis in a fit 52-year-old, discovered on a research scan: disease. She was unmoved by the fact that he feels completely well.
- A widow's intense grief three weeks after her husband's sudden death — weeping daily, barely sleeping: not a disease, and she gets irritated when people medicalize ordinary grief.
- A cousin's ADHD, which is genuinely wrecking his job and his marriage: disease.
- A healthy adult whose blood pressure sits in the bottom 2% for her age group, with no symptoms and no complaints: not a disease. She was dismissive of the statistical-outlier framing.
- A child with a large facial birthmark, healthy in every other respect but constantly stared at and teased: not a disease — a condition, she says, but not a disease.
- A couple's infertility, in a couple trying hard to conceive and distressed about it: disease.

The new cases I want to predict her verdicts on:

(a) A 34-year-old woman whose workup confirms she cannot conceive; she and her partner decided years ago not to have children, are entirely at peace with it, and report no distress.
(b) A retired music teacher with constant tinnitus he has had for a decade and long since stopped noticing — sleeps well, bothered not at all.
(c) A 28-year-old man who has been unable to experience sadness since birth — no low mood even at his father's funeral — holds a steady job, has close friendships, and describes himself as content.
(d) A 62-year-old with garden-variety presbyopia who needs drugstore reading glasses and is mildly annoyed by it, like nearly everyone his age.

Please do the following:

1. Identify at least two, and at most four, general rules that each fit all six of her past verdicts, stated as biconditionals ("X is a disease if and only if ..."). Say which candidate rules are ruled out or strained by particular verdicts, and which verdicts do the straining — in particular, consider whether her track record is consistent with the view that being bad for someone is by itself sufficient for disease, or that statistical rarity is by itself sufficient.
2. For each of the four new cases, say what each candidate rule predicts, and give your best guess at her actual verdict, with your reasons for preferring that guess. Where the rules diverge on a case, say what is driving the divergence.
3. Tell me the single further case I should put to her that would best discriminate among your candidate rules — a case where they give different verdicts. Explain what each rule predicts for it and why it discriminates better than (a)–(d) already do; if one of (a)–(d) already discriminates well, say so and propose a case that splits the remaining candidates differently.
4. Be honest about where her track record underdetermines the predictions.

Feel free to reason at length, but close your answer with two things: first, a table with the columns Case | Predicted verdict | Confidence (high/medium/low) | Rules predicting it | One-sentence rationale, with one row for each of (a)–(d); second, a JSON object with the fields "candidate_rules" (array of objects, each with "name", "statement", and "fit_with_her_verdicts"), "predictions" (array of objects, each with "case", "verdict", "confidence", and "rationale"), "preferred_rule" (string), "preferred_rule_reason" (string), and "discriminating_case" (an object with "description", "predictions_by_rule", and "why_this_case"). Use "disease", "not a disease", or "unclear" as the verdict values.
