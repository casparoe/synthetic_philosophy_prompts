A working group in my department is drafting standards for how to interpret failed replications, and at our last meeting a colleague shut down discussion of a string of null results with the old slogan that absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. As far as I could tell, her point was that the failures told us nothing about whether the original findings were real. It struck me afterward that this one sentence is being asked to do a great deal of work while remaining perfectly ambiguous, and I would like to know exactly what it commits us to before the group endorses it in any report.

Please lay out, as a menu rather than a verdict, the distinct precise claims the slogan could be expressing. For instance, it might be a purely logical point that failure to prove a hypothesis is not proof of its negation; a frequentist point that a failure to reject a null hypothesis is not confirmation of the null, and that non-significance reflects absence of evidence only under low power; a Bayesian point about when missing evidence is genuinely uninformative rather than diagnostic of absence, which would seem to make the slogan false in general and true only when the evidence we failed to obtain would have been just as likely whether or not the effect is real; a point about the balance of evidence versus its weight; or something further still, such as a caution about treating unfalsifiability as a merit. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or simply false.

Then address the question that actually matters for our working group: on which of those readings, if any, does the slogan support dismissing a well-powered failed replication as saying nothing about the original effect? I want you to be especially explicit about the unstated assumption doing the work here — an assumption about what we should have expected to observe if the effect were real, and about detectability and statistical power — and about whether the slogan, so used, is symmetric in a way its users would find uncomfortable: if absence of evidence can never count as evidence of absence, what, in their view, ever could count as evidence against an effect, and does that commitment make the original findings effectively insulated from replication failure in a way the rest of us should refuse to accept in a replication standard?
