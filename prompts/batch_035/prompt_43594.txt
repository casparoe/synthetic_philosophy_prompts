Background: I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for general readers, and I've sold my editor on a piece taking a position she thinks is a hard sell: that shame is actually the better moral emotion for a culture like ours. The angle I have in mind isn't the feel-good version — it's that guilt is built for a world of discrete acts by identifiable agents, and more and more of our wrongdoing is diffuse complicity instead: supply chains, algorithms, the situations where everyone did a little and nobody did a lot. In those, guilt can't get a grip, because there's no one clean act to pin on you, but shame can attach to what you're tangled up in. The piece also wants to claim a culture can choose its dominant moral emotion rather than just inheriting it, and that part cant be hand-waved.

Please make the case as a numbered list of exactly five points, each a sentence or two, no introduction and no conclusion. Treat the five as the load-bearing moves of the argument: one should hit the standard knock on shame head-on — that it makes people hide while guilt makes them repair — and show why it doesn't sink the case; another should carry the "can a culture choose" claim (my hunch is cultures already tune their emotion mix through what they punish in kids, what gossip circulates, what gets dragged into public), and it has to argue the choice is real, not just assert it. Keep the whole thing under about 200 words.

American English, please, and keep jargon out — my readers wont know what a "moral emotion" is, so define anything technical in passing. One more thing: this is for a fact-checkable publication, so no appeals to studies or numbers you'd have to go look up; the argument should do the work on its own. Thanks — sorry for the brain dump, I'm on deadline.
