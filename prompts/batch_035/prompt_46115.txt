I'm a retired chemist (thirty-odd years in a polymer lab) who got into philosophy a few years back, mostly through a reading group at the community center. Last week we had an argument I couldn't referee and it's still bugging me. Maybe you can go through it for me.

The topic was a thought experiment usually called Pascal's mugging, though I'll give the setup as we recieved it, in case the name means nothing to you: a stranger stops you on the street and demands your wallet. If you refuse, he says he'll use magic powers -- and he won't demonstrate them, that's the whole point -- to torture you for an absurdly huge number of days, and each time you hesitate he raises the number. The question is whether expected utility says you should pay.

Dave, a retired engineer and our resident utilitarian, went first: the math is undeniable. Whatever probability you assign to the stranger actually having those powers, it can't be literally zero, and any nonzero probability times a big enough number beats the value of your wallet. If you use expected value anywhere, you have to use it here.

Maria, who taught high school math, said that proves too much: by that logic you're muggable by anyone who can name a bigger number, forever. So either you pay every crank in town, or you need a principled place to stop, and she wanted to hear where that place is.

Dave said fine, here's the place: credence in a claim that weird and that specific should shrink faster than the payoff grows -- something about simpler hypotheses taking most of the probability mass, so a demand that gets more outlandish the longer you bargain should lose credibility faster than the number climbs. He admitted he couldn't prove the decay always outruns the payoff but said it's the reasonable default.

Maria said that's an assumption installed by hand exactly where you needed it, and worse: if your probability for such claims is really a gut feeling about "simplicity," then expected value isn't doing any work at all -- you've already decided not to pay and dressed the decision up after the fact. She also noted it isn't just street cranks: the same shape of argument shows up when a fundraiser tells you your donation affects astronomically many future lives, and "the number is big" can't be the whole story there either.

Dave's last move was bounded utility: if there's a ceiling on how much you can value anything, no payoff, however many digits, can outrun a small enough probability, and the problem dissolves. Maria said that looked like motivated reasoning to her -- rewriting your whole value system across the board (charity, career, everything) just to escape one uncomfortable street corner, the tail wagging the dog.

It seemed rediculous to me at first but the thing is wierdly hard to kill. Then they both turned to me -- apparently I'm the one who's supposed to know when a quantity is well-defined enough to multiply -- and I honestly couldn't say. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: at each step, who's right and why. And then tell me whether there's any position at the end of this that's actually stable, one you'd defend yourself, rather than each of them having just picked a different bush to hide behind.
