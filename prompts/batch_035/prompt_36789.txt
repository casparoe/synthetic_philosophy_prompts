Two of my grad students got into it at our workshop coffee break over negative utilitarianism, and they asked me to referee, which I'm cheerfully delegating to you. The exchange went roughly like this:

(1) Dana said classical negative utilitarianism is refuted on arrival: if suffering is the only thing with moral weight, then a world containing nothing but bliss and no suffering is no better than an empty rock floating in space, which is insane, so the view is dead.

(2) Marco replied that Dana is attacking a theory of value nobody serious needs to hold. Suffering-focused ethics can be a theory of priorities — minimize suffering first, care about happiness after — and on that reading the bliss-world conclusion doesn't follow at all, since happiness still counts for something, just less. He claimed this is closer to the spirit of the original negative utilitarians anyway, whom he said were reacting to political utopianism, not doing axiology.

(3) Dana said that's not negative utilitarianism, that's just utilitarianism with the dial turned way up on suffering — prioritarianism, basically — and that if you weight suffering infinitely heavily you get the benevolent world-exploder problem back: any trade of all the happiness in the world for the elimination of some suffering is a bargain, so painless universal extinction is permitted or even required.

(4) Marco said no, the threshold view handles that: only *severe* suffering dominates, ordinary suffering trades off against happiness normally, so the world-exploder only shows up for a world full of torture, which is a bullet lots of people are at least tempted to bite anyway. Dana shot back that the moment you add a threshold you've written a hybrid theory with the word "negative" stamped on the label, and the interesting question is whether anything survives the label coming off.

(5) Marco's last word was that something does survive: the asymmetry people feel about creating lives. Nobody thinks there's a duty to create a happy child, but most people think there's a duty not to create a miserable one, and that asymmetry is a real datum that suffering-focused ethics explains better than symmetric utilitarianism does. Dana grumbled that whether that's a datum or a bias is exactly what's in dispute, and then their coffee got cold.

So: adjudicate this move by move — who's right at each step, not just overall. Where I'd especially like you to be careful is in separating what's actually settled among people who work on this from what's genuinely contested. E.g., whether the procreation asymmetry is a datum or itself a philosophical position, whether "threshold" views count as negative utilitarianism or as something else, and honestly whether the historical claim in (2) about what the original negative utilitarians were up to holds up — I don't want contested readings presented as consensus, and if something is a judgment call, flag it as one. Also tell me if there's a move both of them are missing. For what it's worth, I don't think Dana gets (2) entirely right, but I'm not telling you more than that.
