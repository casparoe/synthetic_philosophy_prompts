Two friends of mine from my philosophy of mind reading group had it out over split-brain patients last week and since I was the only one not fighting, they've decided I'm the referee, so here I am. Quick context so we're on the same page: commissurotomy, cutting the corpus callosum to treat severe epilepsy, done back in the 60s and studied experimentally by Sperry and Gazzaniga's group. The famous result: flash something to just the left visual field (which routes to the right hemisphere) and the patient says they saw nothing, but their left hand can pick the object out; flash it to the right visual field and they name it with no trouble.

Here's the exchange as best as I can reconstruct it.

Dan's position, roughly:
(1) After the surgery you've got two systems, each with its own perception, its own knowledge, its own motor control. One of them literally cannot report what the other knows. That's not one mind with a reporting glitch — that's two minds, and the scalpel made them.
(2) Saying the patient "saw nothing" is just the left hemisphere describing a channel it never had, sure, fine. But the hemispheres also act like separate agents — intermanual conflict, one hand unbuttoning while the other buttons, the left hand doing things the patient disavows or claims not to know about. You don't get into conflicts with yourself like that.
(3) The kicker is cross-cuing: the right hemisphere can't talk, so it signals — taps, points, small gestures — and the left hemisphere learns to read those signals and answer for it. Maya thinks cross-cuing shows unity. Dan thinks having to communicate with yourself via hand signals is about the strongest evidence of two-ness you could ask for.

Maya's position, roughly:
(1) The experiments show divided access, not divided experience — one stream, two information channels, one of which can't reach the report system. Unity of mind was never a matter of every part knowing what every other part is up to; normal people can't report most of what their visual system computes either.
(2) In daily life the patient is one person — one narrative, one set of plans, feels completely unified. If there were genuinely two minds you'd expect two viewpoints fighting over the body all the time, and mostly that doesn't happen; the "two minds" only show up when a clever experimenter isolates the channels. At minimum the metaphysics shouldn't be settled by the lab setup while ignoring the other 23 hours of the day.
(3) The intermanual conflict stuff is real but rare and, as she understands it, mostly temporary post-surgery — and there's a difference between a hand misbehaving and a whole second agent with beliefs and a point of view.

Then they got stuck, and here's the thing I couldn't stop noticing while they yelled: they seemed to mean two different things by "one mind." Dan cares about whether the agent splits — whether there can be two centers of intention fighting over one body. Maya cares about whether the experiential stream splits — whether there's one point of view or two. On my reading they might both be right on their own question? But I might just be trying to make peace rather than adjudicating.
