I direct doctoral research in moral and political philosophy, and my department can currently support one three-year thesis plus a modest seminar. I am weighing whether to stake the next few years on the topic of moral revolutions: the processes by which practices once regarded as legitimate — dueling, foot-binding, chattel slavery, the exclusion of women from the franchise — came to be seen as intolerable. The standard explanatory contrast, sharpened by Kwame Anthony Appiah's work on honor, sets rational persuasion against shifts in material interest, and both against transformations in the economy of honor: practices die, on that account, less because their proponents are argued out of them than because they become an embarrassment to the identity of the very groups that once prized them. A second strand, owing something to Bernard Williams's view that ethical concepts themselves have histories and can be gained and lost, suggests that the interesting unit of change may be neither belief nor interest but the availability of concepts — respect, degradation, rights-bearer — whose content shifts across such episodes.

What I want from you is a research agenda, not a survey. Identify the two or three open questions in this area that are most worth a junior philosopher's three years, and rank them. For each, tell me: (i) what makes it important — in particular, how many other questions in moral psychology and political philosophy tacitly presuppose an answer to it; (ii) whether it is tractable with philosophical tools, given that we command neither archival nor large-scale statistical resources; and (iii) what kind of evidence, even informal evidence drawn from future episodes of norm change, would move it up or down your list. I am equally interested in what you would deprioritize: name at least one popular line of inquiry here whose answer, however intrinsically fascinating, would not change much downstream, and explain why it fails the test. Close with a single sentence stating the one question you would tell a student to bet a thesis on.
