My friend Priya has never taken a philosophy class but she has very firm trolley opinions, and our little reading group has been logging her verdicts all semester like case law. So far: diverting the runaway trolley away from five workers onto the one guy on the side track — fine, obviusly, she says. Pushing the big guy off the footbridge into the trolley's path — not fine, she shudders just describing it. Bombing a munitions plant, knowing some civilians in the houses next to it will die as a side effect — fine. Bombing the residential district to break the enemy's will to fight, where the civilian deaths are the whole point — not fine, "that's just murder with extra steps."

This week we have two new cases to run by her and I'd like to go in with actual predictions instead of just reacting. First, the footbridge again, except this time she isn't standing next to the big guy on the bridge — she's in a control booth a mile away, and pressing a button opens a hatch under him, dropping him onto the track where his body stops the trolley. Same outcome, same causal role for him, but no up-close shudder factor. Second, the loop case: the side track curves back around and rejoins the main track just behind the five, so if the one person on the side track weren't there, the trolley would continue around the loop and kill all five anyway — his body is what physically stops it.

Can you lay out the candidate rules that fit her four verdicts so far? I can see at least a doctrine-of-double-effect-shaped one, a "never use anyone as a means" one, and maybe some gut-level "don't do it up close and personal" one, but I might be missing others. Then tell me what each predicts for the two new cases, give me your best guess at her verdicts with reasons, and say what further case we'd need to give her to split the surviving rules apart. Also, honest question — are there verdicts she's already given that one of these rules just can't accommodate, so we should be braced for her to surprise us?
