I'm a retired physicist -- thirty-odd years in condensed matter, retired about four years back -- and at some point I got roped into a little philosophy of mind reading group at the local university. Mixed bag: some faculty, a few grad students, and two of us retirees who think we're smarter than we are. This term it's philosophy of mind and math, and in two weeks it's my turn to present. I've gone full Penrose, basically. My thesis: Gödel's first incompleteness theorem shows that human mathematical understanding can't be the product of any algorithm. Roughly the old Lucas line, but with Penrose's version -- for any formal system F that's supposed to model us, we can see the truth of F's Gödel sentence, and F can't prove it, so no F captures us. I actually believe this, embarrassingly. What sold me wasn't the formal argument but those moments in my own research career where you just *see* that a proof strategy won't work before you can say why -- that always felt non-mechanical to me in a way nothing else in forty years of doing science did. I know that's not an argument, but it's why I care.

The room will not be gentle. There's a logician in the group who works on computability theory and considers the whole Lucas-Penrose business an embarassment to the field. There's a grad student doing AI alignment work who thinks the mind is probably just a big learned function and rolls her eyes at anything that smells like human exceptionalism. Two older phenomenology types who will attack from the *opposite* direction -- they'll hate the mathematizing of conciousness even more than they'd hate a mechanist story. And then regular epistemology faculty, agnostic but sharp, who will notice any sleight of hand.

Prepare me a prep sheet. Give me the objections I'll actually get from this specific room, in roughly the order they'll land, phrased the way that logician would phrase them -- I want to recognize them in real time, not realize what was asked on the drive home. Each one gets a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is honestly weak. Include the one I'm dreading, something like: if we can't verify our own consistency, what exactly is the "seeing" that we do when we look at the Gödel sentence? I've never had a good answer to that and I'd like one, or at least a graceful way to not have one. Also flag anywhere the honest move is to concede the point but argue the ship stays afloat -- I'd rather visibly know what I'm giving up than get caught guarding a dead position.

One more thing: keep the physics out of it. I don't want to defend quantum effects in microtubules, I know that fight is a losing one, so I want the argument to stand on the Gödel material alone if it can. And if it can't -- if the honest verdict is that the whole thing collapses without extra premises nobody should grant -- tell me that too, but tell me before I'm standing up there.
