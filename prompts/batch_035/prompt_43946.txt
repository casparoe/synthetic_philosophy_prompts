A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether individual acts matter when each is one drop in an ocean of near-identical acts, and I ended the argument with a list of my own verdicts that he insists no principle could capture. I want to know whether he's right. Here are my verdicts, such as they are. Eating a chicken burrito when a veggie option was just as convenient is wrong, and my friend's argument that my one burrito changes nothing about how many chickens are raised and slaughtered doesn't move me at all. Skipping a national election because it's a hassle is a small civic failing but not a moral wrong — even though, if the polls are right that the race is close, my single vote plausibly has a larger expected effect on how things go than my single burrito does. Taking one long-haul holiday flight strikes me as permissible, or at least nowhere near as bad as the burrito, even though that one flight emits more than a year of my meals. Dropping one wrapper into an overflowing bin on a beach where everyone litters is fine — the place is already a mess, and my wrapper adds nothing anyone could measure — yet that same "adds nothing, the harm is already done" reasoning is exactly what I refuse to accept for the burrito, since the chicken was dead before I ever reached the counter. And the case I genuinely cannot settle: buying the store's last carton of caged-hen eggs, where if I leave them the next shopper will probably take them within the hour, so my refusal may change nothing at all — though I half suspect that's an excuse I'd talk a friend out of.

Is there a principle, or a short family of principles, that captures all of this? Please reply as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the simplest principle that captures as many of my verdicts as possible; (2) the one verdict that principle cannot save, and why; (3) the amendment or second principle that would save it, and what it costs in simplicity or plausibility; (4) what the resulting account says about the egg-carton case, including which side of my wavering it puts me on; and (5) your honest bottom line on which of my verdicts I should simply give up rather than contort the principle any further.
