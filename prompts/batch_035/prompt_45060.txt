I'm a novelist, and for years the writers I know have leaned on a certain analogy: getting to know a fictional character, we say, is just like getting to know a person. You start with a sketch, and then at some point the character begins to push back — you try to make her take the job in Lisbon and it feels wrong, she wouldn't, and you find you can predict what she'd do in situations you never planned for. My workshop friends use this to say our characters have a life of their own, that there's a fact of the matter about what she'd do which exists independantly of me, and that forcing her off her path is a kind of vandalism. I've even started telling people my characters are "real" in the way Dennett meant when he talked about real patterns — real as patterns, not as concrete objects — because honestly the compression feels the same: a handful of traits lets me predict her behaviour better than I could by working through every sentence of prose.

But it struck me the other day that Dennett also had the three stances — physical, design, and intentional — and I can't work out how to line them up for a character. The physical stance would presumably be the words on the page (or in my head?), the design stance is me the author with my plan for the plot, and the intentional stance is the one where I treat her as an agent who believes and wants things. Except here's what nags me: I'm on both sides of it. I designed her, and I'm also the one taking the intentional stance toward her, and there's no world out there for her to have been tracking — her beliefs were never about anything, unless you count being about my plot. So I'd like a proper audit of the analogy my workshop uses. Lay out what maps onto what: the person-knowledge side against the character side, and then the three stances applied to the character case. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and which of the disanalogies actually matter for what we use the claim for — that she wouldn't take the job, that there's a fact of the matter about her. Does my conclusion survive: is she a real pattern in anything like Dennett's sense, or is the predictability just me being consistent with my own earlier drafts, a trick that would vanish the moment I wasn't the only reader? If the analogy was carrying the whole conclusion, say so plainly.

One request about format: please write it as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — just flowing paragraphs, the way you'd write a considered email to a friend. Whatever length the question needs is fine.
