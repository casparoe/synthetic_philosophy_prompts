Two colleagues of mine, Maya and Theo, won a two-person category at a company hackathon. The prize is €9,000 in cash plus one conference package — flights, hotel, and registration for a conference in Lisbon — which must be used by one of the two winners and cannot be sold or transferred. They contributed equally, and both are comfortable enough that need plays no role. The sponsor requires a single signed joint allocation by Friday; if they can't agree, the entire prize lapses and returns to the competition fund. Neither wants to haggle; both want a principled answer, and they spent an evening with a cooperative game theory textbook before each sending me their case. Maya would genuinely pay about €4,000 for the conference experience; Theo would pay about €1,000 (he dislikes flying, and the conference is outside his area). They asked me to referee, and I'm out of my depth. Here is the exchange, reconstructed from their emails:

(1) Theo: "We're equal contributors, so symmetry applies, and we should take the efficient arrangement: you use the package, since it's worth €4,000 to you and €1,000 to me, which puts €13,000 of value on the table. The principled division is the Nash bargaining solution: of all feasible agreements, pick the one that maximizes the product of our gains over the no-deal point, which here is zero for both of us since the prize lapses. That's an even split of the €13,000 — €6,500 each. You take the package plus €2,500 cash; I take €6,500 cash. And it's not arbitrary: it's the unique rule that is efficient, treats equals equally, is invariant to how we scale utilities, and is independent of irrelevant alternatives."

(2) Maya: "Your split ignores how much each of us could possibly get from this prize. Your maximum is €10,000 — the package plus all the cash, even though you'd enjoy the package less. Mine is €13,000. The Kalai–Smorodinsky solution equalizes our fractions of those maxima: each of us ends up with 13/23 of our best case. That's about €7,350 for me — the package plus €3,350 in cash — and €5,650 for you. It's the unique rule that is efficient, symmetric, scale-invariant, and monotonic: when one side's possibilities expand while everything else is fixed, that side's share cannot fall."

(3) Theo: "Your rule counts fantasy. Your 'maximum' is a world in which I take the package and every euro — an outcome neither of us would ever choose. Notice what the rule does in practice: the less I value the conference, the more cash you take. If I happened to love Lisbon as much as you do, your own rule would hand me roughly €850 more and you €850 less, even though every contract we could actually sign would be unchanged. Fairness shouldn't be hostage to my travel tastes. And there's double counting: you get the enjoyment of the trip, plus extra cash for being the sort of person who enjoys trips."

(4) Maya: "Your rule has the mirror-image blindness. Because the prize happens to contain an indivisible part I value more, your rule hands you €6,500 of the €9,000 cash — 72% of it — between equal partners. And Nash isn't as stable as you pretend: it violates monotonicity outright. There are cases where one side's possibilities expand, everything else stays fixed, and that side still ends up worse off, because Nash anchors to whatever compromise maximizes the product rather than to what each of us could get."

(5) Theo: "Then we're trading axioms — stability against responsiveness — and I'd take stability. Besides, look at what each natural principle gives me: even splitting the surplus the package creates (it adds €3,000 of value by going to you rather than me; divide that evenly) leaves me with €6,000. Only your proportional rule dips me below that, to €5,650 — and solely because the prize is structured around something I happen to value less."

(6) Maya: "Every principle is natural to whoever it favors. The real question is which property to insist on when they conflict: I say the division should respond to how much each of us could get from the prize; you say it should be immune to unchosen possibilities. That's the whole disagreement, and about €850 riding on it."

They've half-seriously proposed flipping a coin between the two numbers but would rather settle on the right principle first.

You are a specialist in cooperative game theory and fair division, asked to referee this dispute. Think carefully, and begin by re-deriving the numbers yourself: verify Theo's Nash calculation and Maya's Kalai–Smorodinsky calculation, stating plainly if either is wrong. Then adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each numbered turn, say who is right about what, flag any mischaracterization of the other's rule or of the underlying theory, and identify the strongest point on each side. Close by saying what, if anything, both of them are missing, and give an overall verdict, including the allocation, if either, that you would endorse. Your entire response must be at most 600 words.
