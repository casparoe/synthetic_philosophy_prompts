I'm taking a philosophy of mind course where we recently covered functionalism and multiple realizability, and it's made me look differently at something I learned last year in an abnormal psychology course. My instructor there emphasized that the DSM defines disorders entirely in terms of patterns of cognition, emotion, and behavior — depressed mood, loss of interest, sleep disturbance, and so on — and that the manual deliberately declines to commit to any particular cause of those patterns, whether neurochemical, psychological, or social. At the same time, the field of psychiatry describes itself to funders and to the public as a branch of medicine and increasingly as clinical neuroscience: depression is called a brain disorder, research money flows overwhelmingly toward neurobiology, and treatments are often pitched as correcting something in the brain. So we have a discipline that individuates disorders functionally at the level of diagnosis while talking about them biologically at the level of explanation and treatment, and it's not obvious these two halves are pulling in the same direction.

My question is what this practice actually presupposes about mental disorders as kinds. Does diagnosing by symptom profile — where two patients who share no underlying pathology could receive the same diagnosis — commit psychiatry to something like functionalism, that is, to the view that a disorder is a higher-level functional state multiply realizable in different substrates and mechanisms? Could a type-identity theorist, who thinks each mental kind just is some physical kind, coherently accept the DSM's categories as they stand, or would they have to treat today's diagnoses as placeholders for a future taxonomy that carves disorders by their neural realizers, the way epilepsy is ultimately defined by characteristic brain activity rather than by the patient's report of staring spells? And where is the practice genuinely underdetermined rather than committed — for instance, maybe symptom-based diagnosis presupposes only that symptoms are a reliable epistemic guide, not any metaphysics of disorder at all?

I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory of mental kinds that psychiatric diagnostic practice rests on, say which rival theories of mind it quietly rules out or merely sits uneasily beside, and identify whether the tension between functional diagnosis and biological explanation is a real inconsistency or just a division of labor. It would also help if you could say what would actually have to change in clinical practice if psychiatry decided to individuate disorders by their physical realizers rather than by their functional profiles — who would gain or lose a diagnosis, so to speak — since that thought experiment is where I get most confused about what the current system is really assuming.
