I got in over my head in my philosophy of physics seminar and now my own argument is haunting me. We did the hole argument, and after class I tried extending it to something way outside spacetime, and the extension seems to prove there are no electrons. Here's the chain:

(1) Manifold substantivalism: the point-individuals of the spacetime manifold are real, existing independently of whatever fields sit on them.
(2) Take any solution of Einstein's equations, pick some small region (the "hole"), and smoothly drag everything inside it around, so each point comes to carry the field values that used to belong to a neighboring point, with everything outside the hole left alone.
(3) The shuffled version is also a solution, and every qualitative fact is the same: same observables, same measurement outcomes, same entire history of anything anyone could notice.
(4) If (1) is true, the two solutions describe genuinely different possible worlds, because different point-individuals got different careers.
(5) So GR plus substantivalism is wildly indeterministic -- nothing in the laws or the past fixes which of these worlds we're in.
(6) Now my extension: run the same move on two electrons. Take the actual world and swap their entire histories, electron a gets b's trajectory and spin story, b gets a's. Every qualitative fact is unchanged.
(7) So by the same reasoning, either the world is indeterministic about electrons (nothing determines which electron is which), or electrons aren't real individuals at all.
(8) Physicists look at (6)-(7) and shrug that permutations of identical particles just aren't different states. My classmate says thats exactly what the substantivalist should say about (2), so the hole argument is the permutation argument in a fancy dress, and anyone who laughs at "electrons aren't individuals" but takes the hole argument seriously is being inconsistent.

I can't tell whether the flaw is in (4), or in the analogy between sliding points around and swapping particles (points can be permuted continuously, maybe thats a difference that matters?), or whether (8) is actually right and I have to pick between biting the electron bullet and giving up the hole argument's force. Which premise should I give up, why did it look fine, and what do I pay for rejecting it? I'd honestly rather not conclude electrons aren't individuals, but I also don't want to just declare the problem away like the physicists do.
