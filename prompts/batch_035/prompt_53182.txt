I'm taking an intermediate ethics course and we just covered Bernard Williams, so I went to a friend who did this course a couple years back and asked him for the gist. This is roughly what he told me, and I want to know how much of it is fair:

Williams's big anti-utilitarian argument is the integrity objection. Utilitarianism treats you as just a channel for maximizing overall welfare — you're as responsible for what you fail to prevent as for what you actually do — so if the optimum requires abandoning your own life projects, you have to abandon them, and Williams thought that's wrong because it destroys your integrity, so he basically refuted consequentialism. He had a second argument about impartiality in personal relationships. If a man can save one of two drowning people, and one of them turns out to be his wife, Williams said the reason of her being his wife is partial, so it's not enough on its own — the man needs to supplement it with an impartial consideration, like that she's the closer one or that rescuing her does more good overall. If he skips that impartial check and just acts on the wife reason, that's what Williams meant by having one thought too many, and it shows consequentialism is self-effacing, because it erodes the spontaneous affection that personal relationships are supposed to be made of.

Some of this lines up with the lecture, but a couple of parts felt off — the lecturer seemed to say the extra thought was the impartial one, not the wife one, which is like the opposite of what my friend said. How much of my friend's version should I trust?
