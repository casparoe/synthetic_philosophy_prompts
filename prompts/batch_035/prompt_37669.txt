I'm taking a philosophy of psychiatry course this semester and we just did a unit on Szasz, and honestly his argument hit me harder than I expected. The way he tells it, calling depression or schizophrenia illnesses is a category mistake: real diseases are bodily pathologies, and what psychiatry treats is really what he calls problems in living — unhappiness, deviance, interpersonal conflict — dressed up in medical vocabulary so we can justify intervention. I've basically been converted, and I've been insufferable about it too (my cousin is on SSRIs and I nearly ruined Thanksgiving). But my professor said something that stuck: the interesting question about Szasz is what evidence could ever have counted against him, since he already conceded that brain diseases exist and just kept insisting that no specific mental disorder had ever been shown to be one.

So here's what I want: treat me as a committed Szaszian and map out the position's exposure. What observations, arguments, or findings would actually count against it? I can guess at some candidates — general paresis turning out to be neurosyphilis, reliable biomarkers for schizophrenia, the heritability stuff, maybe the way we now classify Alzheimer's or epilepsy, which were once in the same bucket as madness. But for each of these I want to know whether it's fatal or merely awkward, and which ones Szasz himself would just shrug off by saying fine, that one is a brain disease and was never what I meant anyway. Relatedly: has the position been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so is that a defect in the view, or is requiring bodily pathology just a substantive definition of disease that rules things in and out like any other definition? And there's a middle position we covered, Wakefield's harmful dysfunction analysis, which tries to say a disorder is a failure of a natural function that's also harmful by social standards — does that count as a partial victory for Szasz, a refutation, or a change of subject?

Be direct about where I'm most likely getting fooled here. Also, whatever verdict you land on, tell me what would change your mind — which argument or observation about the concept of disease would push you to a different conclusion, and where in your own reasoning you think the whole answer is most likely to go wrong. My professor keeps hammering on this self-criticism thing and I want to actually pull it off in my term paper, so I'd rather see it done honestly than see a tidy wrap-up.
