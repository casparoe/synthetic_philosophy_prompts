I've been reading Kripke for a reading group and I walked myself into an argument I can't get out of. The whole rigidity machinery was supposed to explain how "Hesperus is Phosphorus" comes out necessary a posteriori. But look what happens when you run it on an ordinary biography fact:

(1) 'Aristotle' is a rigid designator: in every possible world where the man exists, it picks out that very same man.
(2) In fact, Aristotle taught Alexander.
(3) If a subject-predicate sentence is true, and its subject term is rigid, then the sentence is true in every world where the subject exists. In no world does 'Aristotle' pick out some other guy — some non-teacher — and it's true of the man it does pick out that he taught Alexander.
(4) So necessarily, Aristotle taught Alexander.
(5) Nothing here turns on which name or which fact: every historical, biographical, whatever-you-like truth about any named individual comes out necessary.
(6) That can't be right — Kripke of all people insists Aristotle could have done something else with his life.

I tried patching it by saying (3) only goes through when the predicate side is rigid too. 'The teacher of Alexander' isn't rigid, so no necessity. But 'water' and 'H2O' are both rigid, and that's exactly how "water is H2O" gets to be necessary a posteriori. So maybe my patch just IS the doctrine? It still feels like I'm saying "identities between rigid designators are necessary, other predications aren't" and can't give a reason beyond wanting that conclusion.
