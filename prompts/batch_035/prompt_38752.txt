My department has this writing-partship program where two people who've been arguing past each other for a year co-author one document instead of trading papers, and this year it's me and a colleague on consent. I'm on team "yes means yes": if someone competent, informed *in the moment*, and unpressured says yes, that's valid consent — deception and manipulation make the act wrong, or add separate wrongs, but they don't reach back and unmake the consent itself. She's on team "conditions": consent only does its transformative work when it's given under decent decision conditions, so deceit and wrongful pressure don't just stack wrongs on top, they void the yes. Draft the joint statement we could both actually sign: a numbered agreed-claims section (we do agree on some things — that consent is normatively transformative when valid, that coercion and deception are paradigms of wronging, that mere regret doesn't retroactively invalidate), then the residual disagreements stated in wording each of us would accept for our own side, then the cruxes. I suspect one crux is the but-for test — consent is flawed when, and only when, the person would have refused but for the flaw — and whether it survives cases where the deception "insulates" the choice (she'd have accepted the fallback option she'd have preferred anyway) or where the lie concerns something she had no right to be told. Another is offers vs. threats: is the baseline for coercion what you were entitled to, or what you would factually have had absent the offer? A third is whether competence is a fixed threshold or scales with the stakes — a mild impairment that leaves someone fine for everyday choices but shaky about a genuinely risky one. For each crux, name a concrete case or test both of us would agree bears on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance. End with a section flagging which parts of our dispute are just verbal and which are real. No mushy "both sides have a point" filler — if we genuinely can't agree on a formulation, say so.
