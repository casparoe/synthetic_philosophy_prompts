I teach a historiography elective for second-year students — sixteen of them, one ninety-minute seminar — and next week I want to run a structured activity that makes them feel hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy in their own explanatory habits, before either term is put on the board. My working plan is built around a dossier of sources describing an obscure episode that I will manufacture myself: a composite, invented-but-plausible case, something like a boundary commission dispute between two minor powers in the first decade of the twentieth century, documented through fabricated diplomatic dispatches, press excerpts, and a fragment of a memoir — invented precisely so that no student can smuggle in outside knowledge, and so that I control what the record does and does not settle. Each student would receive the identical dossier, but half the room gets a cover sheet stating that the dispute ended in an armed skirmish, while the other half gets one stating it was resolved by arbitration. Each half then independently writes a short paragraph explaining why the outcome unfolded as it did, and separately rates, on a scale, how foreseeable that outcome was given the sources alone.

Design the full activity for me: the sequencing and timing across the ninety minutes, how to construct the dossier so that both endings can be plausibly read out of the same materials (what the sources should underdetermine, and what they should seem to settle), what students produce at each stage, and how to stage the reveal — ideally the two halves should discover each other's incompatible "inevitable" explanations at the same moment they discover that the outcome on their cover sheet was simply assigned by me. Then a debrief that cleanly separates the two ideas I want them to leave with: the contamination of their foreseeability ratings, which is the hindsight bias, versus the fluency and internal coherence of the explanatory paragraphs they wrote, which is the narrative fallacy — and that connects the exercise back to the historian's actual situation of reasoning before the outcome, or arguing against the grain of a record that already seems to point one way.

I'd also like an honest operational note. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I already suspect some students will smell a setup the moment the two cover sheets differ, and that my manufactured sources will read as either too tidy or too obviously balanced — and, more importantly, tell me which feature of genuine historical judgment this activity cannot reproduce no matter how well I run it, so that I know what I have to supply myself in the debrief rather than pretend the exercise demonstrated it.
