Analyze the defeat of the corpuscular theory of light by the wave theory in early nineteenth-century France as a case study in Lakatos's methodology of research programmes. The standard narrative, which you may take as given, goes roughly as follows: by 1800 the emission theory — light as a stream of particles obeying Newtonian dynamics — had been the dominant programme for a century, with Newton himself explaining the fringes at the edge of shadows by adorning the corpuscles with "fits" of easy reflection and transmission and by appeals to forces exerted by obstacle edges on passing rays. Augustin Fresnel then recast diffraction as wave interference, and in the 1818 French Academy prize competition, Siméon Poisson — an emissionist on the judging panel — derived what he took to be a reductio of Fresnel's theory: that a bright spot should appear at the very center of the shadow of a circular disc. François Arago performed the experiment and found the spot; Fresnel took the prize, and the emission programme slid into irrelevance.

I want a disciplined mapping, not a victory lap. Identify the hard core, the protective belt, and the positive and negative heuristics of each programme as they stood around 1818, and show precisely why the wave programme's period counts as progressive and the emissionists' patching counts as degenerating by Lakatos's lights — attending to which of their auxiliaries were ad hoc in his technical sense (generating no independently testable content) rather than merely unsuccessful. Then press where the framework strains. First, the spot was derived by an opponent as a purported absurdity, not by a wave theorist hunting for confirmations; does Lakatos's criterion of a novel fact — that it not be used in constructing the hypothesis — care who did the deriving, and should a theory be credited for surviving a hostile test any more than a friendly one? Second, Arago needed the wave theory to tell him where to look and what would count as success; say whether this theory-ladenness damages Lakatos's claim that the episode shows an objective, rational choice between programmes rather than a Kuhnian conversion. Third, and hardest: light turned out, a century later, to have corpuscular aspects that no 1820 wave theorist anticipated; explain whether Lakatos can accommodate the partial resurrection of the defeated programme — as a new programme with a different hard core, as a fusion, or not at all — and what this does to his narrative of one programme "winning." Conclude by saying whether the episode is better evidence for Lakatos's rational reconstruction than for Popper's falsificationism or Kuhn's paradigms, or whether it exposes a gap that only a patched Lakatos — with auxiliaries of his own — can close.
