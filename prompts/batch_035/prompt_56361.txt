I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a Sunday broadsheet, and I've been given space for a series of three long essays — roughly four thousand words each — on what rich countries owe poor ones. The philosophical literature here is vast, and I cannot summarise all of it; I have to choose. From your point of view, which unresolved questions in that debate genuinely matter, in the sense that answering them would change what a serious newspaper tells its readers about aid, debt, trade and migration — and which are academic footballs that look momentous but would change nothing downstream?

To show you the grain I'm after, here is the sort of thing I mean by a question. Is the core duty one of justice — structured, enforceable, owed to particular people because of how the global order treats them — or one of beneficence, open-ended and owed by anyone who can help? Both camps lobby me for coverage, and I suspect the difference does a great deal of quiet work: for instance, in whether conditional lending by creditors reads as paternalism or as simply the creditor's own business, or in whether a rising power's infrastructure programme counts as largesse or as a claim being settled. Is that suspicion right, and is that question upstream of the others? What about the corruption objection — the worry that channelling money through badly governed states wrongs their citizens rather than helping them? Does it defeat the case for state-to-state transfers, or merely relocate it to claims about trade rules and border regimes? And where should historical wrongs sit: do they generate claims of their own, or do they only make the forward-looking case more vivid? Finally, when the policy in question shapes who will exist — population programmes, health systems that alter demographic futures — does the fact that the beneficiaries owe their existence to the policy genuinely weaken what we owe them, or is that a technicality that should never make it into print?

I don't want a survey of positions. I want a defended ordering: which two or three of these, or of questions you think I've missed, deserve the essay slots, and by what criteria — importance to readers, how settled the surrounding empirical facts are, neglect by other outlets — together with the trade-offs between those criteria you would accept. Tell me also what would make you reshuffle the list: a particular kind of empirical finding, a political change, something else. Please keep the whole answer under seven hundred words; compression is part of what I'm testing, as I may commission shorter pieces from you later. And no reading lists, no citations — if you must allude to a position, characterise it in a phrase instead.
