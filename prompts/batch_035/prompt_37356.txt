I am a novelist currently drafting a book set largely in England in the 1950s, told through a close third-person narrator inclined to free indirect style, and I have been going back and forth over whether that narrator may reach for the generic 'he' of the period or whether I must keep such locutions out of the prose even at the cost of anachronism. At my writing group, another member — far better read than I in this area — offered a summary of Douglas Hofstadter's essay 'A Person Paper on Purity in Language', which I have not myself read, and I would like to know how far it can be trusted. According to her, it is a linguistics paper Hofstadter published in an academic journal under his own name, in which he imagines an English whose pronouns sort people by height rather than sex, with the tall form doubling as the 'generic' pronoun for persons of unspecified size. She says Hofstadter's demonstration is that anyone who uses the generic 'he' is by that very fact a sexist, since the essay establishes that such usage demonstrably shapes hearers' default assumptions about who counts as a standard human being, and that he ends by prescribing a fully rebuilt vocabulary of occupational and personal terms as the only acceptable remedy — hence, she concludes, even my period narrator must be denied the idiom of his own time, because each use of generic 'he' implants the relevant default in my readers. Something about this feels off to me — both the claim about individual speakers and the jump from the essay to instructions for my narrator — but I am in no position to say. Please go through her summary part by part: what is accurate, what is a subtle distortion of what the essay actually attempts, what its satirical apparatus is really doing, and whether her conclusion for my novel follows from whatever survives the corrections. Please answer in British English.
