I'm a robotics engineer. My daily vocabulary is state estimation: belief states as posteriors over world states, Kalman filters, POMDPs, observability, sensor fusion, calibration, fault detection. I have no philosophy training beyond half-finishing a popular book. For people like me, "belief" is a technical term — the robot's belief state is just its current posterior, and it can output that distribution on request. From where I sit, Moore's paradox looks like it should dissolve immediately. Consider the two canonical sentences: "It is raining, but I don't believe that it is raining" and "It's raining, but I believe that it is not raining." In my terms these say: true state = rain, while my posterior puts low mass on rain (or high mass on its negation). That is an estimator being wrong about the world — ordinary estimation error, or perhaps miscalibration — and there is nothing paradoxical about a third party reporting it: "it's raining, but the robot doesn't believe that it is" is a perfectly coherent pair of claims, one about the world and one about the estimator, each independently checkable. Yet the philosophers insist the first-person, present-tense versions are somehow absurd despite being consistent and possibly true.

Restate the actual Moorean debate for me in my vocabulary, as honestly as you can: the omissive and commissive forms, the point that the sentences are consistent rather than contradictory, the fact that the past-tense and second-person counterparts are fine, and the main lines of explanation on offer (the pragmatics of assertion, Hintikka-style treatments in terms of what belief-ascriptions do, and the Wittgensteinian thought that avowing a belief functions more like expressing a state than reporting one). Then give me the residue report: which parts map cleanly onto estimation and meta-estimation, which parts you had to distort to fit, and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all.

Here is the part I actually care about, and I want a definite position, not a survey. My systems do have self-models in a weak sense: fault-detection and calibration modules that monitor the main estimator and estimate whether it is wrong, so I can build an architecture in which a meta-estimator watches an estimator. Say whether such a system could ever be guilty of the Moorean absurdity in the full sense — not merely output the sentence, which is trivial, but be incoherent in the way a person is when they assert it — or whether that incoherence lives somewhere no arrangement of estimators and meta-estimators reaches. And tell me plainly whether my hunch — that the paradox is an artifact of conflating the observation channel with the estimator, and that nothing survives the translation except a fact about how humans talk — is right, or whether it is the predictable blind spot of a vocabulary that has reports all the way down and nothing like an avowal. If that is the defect, commit to a view on whether it is my vocabulary's defect or a sign that the paradox is less deep than Wittgenstein took it to be.
