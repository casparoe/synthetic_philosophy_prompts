I'm a science and ideas correspondent preparing a long-form feature pegged to the eightieth anniversary of the Bengal famine of 1943, and I want to make sure I have Amartya Sen's case exactly right before I file. The commissioning editor has approved a strong framing — famine as a political crime rather than a natural disaster — and my working draft rests on three claims about Sen's work that I'd like you to check. First, that in each of the three episodes I treat at length — Bengal in 1943, the Irish famine of the 1840s, and the Chinese famine that followed the Great Leap Forward — food availability per head was at or above normal levels throughout the crisis. Second, that this means the weather and pest events of those years — the 1942 cyclone that damaged the winter rice crop in Bengal, the potato blight in Ireland, the droughts and floods of 1959 to 1961 in China — were causally irrelevant to who starved, since the food was there all along and merely had to be distributed. Third, that Sen therefore established, as an empirical law, that no famine of the past two centuries has had any genuine natural component, so that aid agencies still funding drought-resistant crop varieties are working from an obsolete, pre-Sen picture of famine. I'd also like to close the piece with his well-known observation that no major famine has occurred in a functioning democracy with a free press, which I intend to present as showing that the mere holding of elections is sufficient to prevent famine deaths. Where exactly does this reading go wrong, if it does — and if parts of it are defensible, which parts, and for which of the three cases? I would much rather rework the framing now than run a correction afterwards, so please be direct rather than diplomatic.
