Two people in my metaphysics reading group spent our whole session arguing about Max Black's two-sphere universe and I couldn't tell who was getting the better of it. Quick setup in case you need it: the thought experiment imagines a world containing nothing but two qualitatively identical spheres, in otherwise empty space, positioned perfectly symmetrically (say, a meter apart). Every qualitative fact about one sphere holds of the other. The argument went like this:

(1) Dana said the example flatly refutes the identity of indiscernibles: you've got two distinct objects sharing all their qualitative properties, so the principle, in its interesting form, is just false.

(2) Farid said not so fast -- each sphere has a property the other lacks: the property of being *this very sphere*. One of them has the property of being identical to sphere a, the other doesn't. Pure thisness, no qualitative facts needed.

(3) Dana called that cheating: if you let haecceitistic properties into the property pool, the principle becomes trivially true and stops saying anything. Everyone agrees distinct objects differ in their thisnesses. The whole interest of the principle is about qualitative properties, and on that reading Black's world is a counterexample, full stop.

(4) Farid answered that then Dana's preferred reading can't even be evaluated in that world. By Dana's own admission, no qualitative description picks out one sphere rather than the other -- Dana can't say which sphere is which, can't even gesture at one of them. So how is Dana so confident there are two?

(5) Dana said counting doesn't require labeling. The two-sphere world is qualitatively different from a one-sphere world -- twice the matter, a gap in the middle, a center of mass sitting in empty space between them. The qualitative facts settle that there are two spheres; they just don't settle which is which, and they don't need to. That's exactly what makes it a counterexample.

(6) Farid pushed back with a different suggestion: maybe in that world the "spheres" aren't two objects at all, but two *features* of one big symmetric object -- like the two ends of a perfectly symmetric rod, or the north and south facing of a sphere. Nobody thinks the principle of identity of indiscernibles applies to ends of rods. If the world's total qualitative character can be described without two distinct individuals, maybe the honest description is of one symmetric arrangement, and Dana is smuggling in the number two by force of habit.

(7) Dana's last word: rotate the whole two-sphere universe 180 degrees around the midpoint. In an ordinary world, a rotation like that moves objects around and things change. Here nothing changes at all -- yet the rotation, if you insist there are two spheres, has swapped their positions. Two things that can literally trade places with zero difference made are the paradigm of distinct-but-indiscernible, and Farid's "it's really one thing" move just refuses to say what the thought experiment puts in front of us. Farid never got to reply because we ran out of time.

They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't. Please go through the exchange move by move (1 through 7), rating each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a short justification, then tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing about the two-sphere setup. And I want an actual verdict at the end -- does Black's world refute the identity of indiscernibles or not -- not a "well it depends how you formulate it" hedge. Take the side you think is correct and defend it. If you think the whole thought experiment is somehow confused or question-begging, say that and explain which of the two you think would be more at home with that conclusion.
