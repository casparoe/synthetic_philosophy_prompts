I help run a small seminar on deterrence policy, and one participant has a comparison he deploys in nearly every session on nuclear brinkmanship. Two climbers are roped together on a steep slope: neither can credibly threaten to let go on purpose, since that would be announcing a willingness to die. But one can induce concessions from the other simply by moving down the slope toward the drop — placing both of them in increasing jeopardy — because the fall, if it comes, will be involuntary and fatal to both. The risk of the fall does the coercive work; no one has to be believed to be irrational. He attributes the comparison to Schelling's discussions of coercive diplomacy, and he leans on it to defend two conclusions at once: first, that brinkmanship is a rational, even prudent, instrument of policy rather than a reckless one; and second, that the credible form of nuclear threat is what he calls the threat that leaves something to chance — a deliberate, incremental accumulation of shared risk, rather than any threat that would have to be carried out as a chosen act.

Please audit the analogy. Lay out what maps onto what: the climbers onto the rival states, the rope onto mutual vulnerability, the slope onto the escalation ladder, the involuntary fall onto war arrived at by accident, momentum, or misjudgment rather than decision. Then say where the mapping breaks. I am especially interested in the disanalogies that do real work for his conclusions, as against the ones that are idle for this purpose. For instance: in the climber case the risk is a fixed feature of the terrain, while in the nuclear case the something to chance is human judgment under stress — false alarms, misread signals, leaders convinced the other side has already decided — which the analogy quietly converts into an objective hazard, as though a slope could be careless. Consider also whether the rope is a fair stand-in for mutual assured destruction, given that the climbers never chose to be tied together. Distinguish, for each of his two conclusions, what it needs from what the analogy actually delivers, and then tell me whether the conclusions survive once the disanalogies are on the table: does the comparison genuinely support prudential brinkmanship, or was it carrying that verdict the whole time? If a better analogy is available — one that keeps what the climber picture gets right while importing fewer foreign features — propose it, and say what it would predict the climber picture gets wrong.

Finally, hold yourself to a standard I can use. At the end, identify the step in your audit you are least confident in, and state what argument or consideration would move you to the opposite verdict on whether the conclusions survive. If your audit would come out differently under a plausible rival reading of what brinkmanship amounts to — say, coercive bargaining aimed at extracting concessions, versus a self-protective policy of raising the costs of attack — say so, and show where that fork changes the result.
