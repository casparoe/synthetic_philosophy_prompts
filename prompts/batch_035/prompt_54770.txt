A popular philosophy book I've been reading (an overview of theories of well-being, written for a general audience) summarizes a position it calls "tranquilism" as follows: "Tranquilism is the view that the only thing with intrinsic value is the absence of suffering — or, in its more refined form, the absence of craving. Pleasure, on this view, has no value whatsoever: a life of quiet contentment is no better than a life of ecstatic joy, and someone savoring a wonderful meal has nothing of value that a person in a dreamless sleep lacks. This follows directly from the asymmetry between suffering and happiness: since the removal of a headache is a real improvement while the addition of pleasure is not, the tranquilist is committed to saying that if a button existed which would painlessly extinguish all life, pressing it would be obligatory, since a world without suffering must be ranked above any world containing suffering, no matter how happy that world is. The position is essentially the Buddha's diagnosis of the human condition translated into utilitarian bookkeeping: craving is the disease, and the cure is the annihilation of want, which is why the tranquilist must regard any deliberate pursuit of pleasure as a form of self-harm. It also follows that tranquilists should oppose having children, since a child's future happiness counts for nothing in the decision while its future suffering counts against it." The book uses this as a stepping-stone to its own preferred theory, so it has some incentive to make tranquilism look bad, but I can't tell whether the badness is in the position or in the summary. For what it's worth, my instinct is that the absurdist conclusion about the button is where something goes wrong, but I'm honestly not sure whether the author smuggled it in or whether the tranquilist really is stuck with it.

Could you go through this passage carefully and tell me which claims a thoughtful defender of tranquilism would accept, which are misrepresentations, and which conclusions drawn at the end actually follow from the accurate parts? In particular I'd like to know: is the claim that pleasure has no value at all the same as the view's actual claim about intrinsic value, or is a distinction being collapsed there? Is the world-destroying button presented fairly, or does it conflate what the tranquilist values with what the tranquilist must do — and are there versions of the view (say, ones that treat the elimination of suffering as lexically prior rather than as the only value, or ones that note that extinction itself involves suffering) that escape it? And is the author right that the position simply is Buddhist doctrine in modern dress, and that it forbids the pursuit of pleasure outright? Please be specific about what a defender would grant, where they would object, and whether the final practical conclusions about self-harm and childbearing survive once the errors are fixed.
