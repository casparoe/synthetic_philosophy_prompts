I host a history-of-ideas podcast, and for a special episode I have been granted, in the manner of these things, an hour with Niccolò Machiavelli: it is 1513, he has recently been stripped of his Florentine chancery post, is confined to his farm near Sant'Andrea, and is at work on the little book on principalities. The topic is fixed in advance — fortune and virtù — and he has been told so. Draft for me ten to twelve interview questions. I am not after restatements of the text: every question should exploit a genuine tension in his position, and every question should come with a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give, so that I can press him however he responds.

At least one question should be devoted to each of the following:

1. He assigns fortune the government of about half of human affairs and leaves the other half to us. Why exactly half? Is the figure a serious estimate, a gesture of even-handedness, or a device for keeping failure half-excusable? And how, on his own terms, could anyone ever check it?

2. The chapter on fortune first makes her a capricious goddess and then a river in flood, which can be held back with dikes and embankments. If fortune is a dammable river, in what sense is she still fortune? Put it to him whether the second image quietly concedes that she only governs men who lack virtù — in which case the chapter has abolished its own opening claim — or whether he means something subtler by the change of image.

3. Agathocles of Sicily: he credits him with real virtù in acquiring power, yet refuses to describe the killing of one's fellow citizens as virtù at all. Ask him to separate, once and for all, virtù as technical statecraft from virtù as any excellence of character; and ask whether a quality that must never be fully exercised — a ruler needs the capacity for cruelty together with the judgement not to use it — can be a stable trait of a person rather than a description of lucky timing.

4. The same chapter remarks that fortune favours the young and the impetuous over the cautious, in a notorious passage about fortune being a woman that has embarrassed his readers for centuries. Ask him plainly: does that remark do any argumentative work, or is it decoration? Would his doctrine survive if the passage were deleted — and if not, what does that say about a theory of statecraft that leans on a conceit about women?

5. In the Discourses, fortune seems to be tamed structurally — by institutions and orders rather than by the acute judgement of one man — in a way that a prince, however gifted, cannot manage. Ask him whether the chapter on fortune is really a covert chapter about the constitutional inferiority of principalities: whether a state whose fortunes hang on a single man's virtù is not already, by his own analysis, a lost cause that fortune has merely not yet noticed.

Beyond these five, I would like two or three further questions that press on personal ground without being invasive: whether the disasters of his own generation — the collapse of the republic he served, and his own fall from office — function in his writing as evidence about fortune or as grievance dressed up as evidence; whether virtù can be taught at all, given that he writes as though instructing an audience that by hypothesis lacks it; and, building on his sour observation that fortune raises a man by training habits into him that ruin him once the times change, whether virtù includes the almost inhuman steadiness of being able to abandon one's own habits on cue.

Please phrase the questions so that they could plausibly be put to a proud, witty, newly humbled man in his mid-forties — without flattering him and without provoking him into theatre. And please write everything, questions, follow-ups and any framing, in British English.
