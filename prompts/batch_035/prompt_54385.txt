I'm taking an intermediate game theory course this semester, and I've noticed something in two different situations that I suspect is the same phenomenon, though I don't have a name for it and can't find one in our lecture notes.

The first situation happened last term. A seminar meeting was cancelled, but the lecturer didn't announce it anywhere; instead, he emailed one student, who then messaged the rest of us one by one. By evening, every member of the class knew the meeting was off, and most of us probably knew that most of the others knew — some of us had even heard it from two different people. Yet four students still showed up at the seminar room at the usual time, and their reason was the same each time: they couldn't rule out that some classmate hadn't received the message and would be sitting alone in an empty room, waiting. Compare this with the following term, when a cancellation was posted on the official course page. Then everyone treats the matter as settled and stays home. The propositional content is identical in the two cases — in both, everyone learns that the meeting is cancelled — and in the relay case the news may actually have reached each person through multiple independent channels. Nevertheless the two cases feel completely different in terms of how safe it is to act on the information.

The second situation came up in our tutorial, in a discussion of coordinated action. We were asked to imagine a workplace where employees would all join a protest if they were confident enough others would, and none would go if they expected to be alone or nearly alone. Our tutor pointed out that an organizer who speaks to people individually, however many conversations she has, seems to accomplish much less than one public event — a rally, an announcement in front of the whole group — even if, after all the private conversations, everyone has in fact been told and is in fact willing. Again the same facts are distributed across the group, and again the public version produces action where the private version doesn't.

So my question is this. Is there an established concept in game theory or epistemic logic for the difference between information that everyone in a group possesses and information that is, so to speak, out in the open? And does the theory say anything about how many levels of "I know that she knows that he knows" are actually required, or explain why no finite number of such levels ever seems to suffice — why the chain has to be, in some sense, endless before anyone can rely on it? I'm not looking for a bare label. I'd like to understand what exactly the difference consists in — whether it is a difference in degree or in kind — what the nearest existing concepts are and where they fail to capture it, and how theorists model agents who plainly cannot run an infinite chain of reasoning but behave, at least sometimes, as though the whole chain were given to them. If the phenomenon has no established name of its own, I'd be grateful if you could say so plainly and propose one, with a definition and some boundary cases that distinguish it from neighboring ideas.
