For an upcoming departmental workshop I've been asked to circulate a short statement of my considered view on why promises bind, so that my colleagues can then attack it. Before I send it, I want an audit of whether the package I've arrived at is internally tenable. Here is the position as I actually hold it, in the form of eight commitments:

1. Promissory obligation is directed: when I break a promise, the primary wrong I commit is a wrong to you, the promisee — not a wrong that merely happens to bear on you.
2. What I do in promising is hand you a measure of authority over whether I perform. You, and only you, thereby acquire the standing both to demand performance and to release me from it. This transfer of authority, and not the expectations my promise predictably raises, is what generates the obligation.
3. Promises exacted by coercion do not bind at all: a promise made at gunpoint creates no obligation whatsoever.
4. Promises made against a background of structural injustice do bind, at least defeasibly — an undocumented worker's promise to show up for a shift, made to an employer who exploits her precarity, still generates a genuine obligation, weakened though it may be.
5. A promise to a dying friend to tend her grave continues to bind after her death, and not merely weakly: it binds just as firmly as it did while she lived, for as long as she would have had a claim on me.
6. Waiver is decisive and total: if the promisee explicitly releases me, the obligation is extinguished, full stop, with no remainder.
7. Promising does not presuppose any pre-existing social practice: two castaways stranded together on an island, with no shared conventions and no society behind them, could nonetheless make genuine promises to one another.
8. Breaching a promise also involves an impersonal wrong — a small wrong done to everyone who depends on the institution of promising — and this is part of what makes breach wrong, not a mere side effect.

I'd like you to go through this set and tell me whether it hangs together. Specifically:

- Identify which pairs or larger subsets of these commitments are in genuine tension, and which only appear to be. For the apparent ones, say exactly what distinction dissolves the tension. For the genuine ones, explain what each commitment's own motivation requires such that honoring one undercuts another — I don't want a list of possible complaints, I want the real conflicts.
- Rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally serious, and for each, say what the least costly repair would be: which commitment to give up or weaken, how much of the rest of the set that revision disturbs, and whether the resulting position would still be one a reasonable person could hold rather than a patchwork of ad hoc exceptions.
- Conclude by telling me which single revision would clean up the most overall, and be honest if you think the right answer is that no one-line diagnosis captures where the pressure is greatest.

A few constraints on what I'm after. Don't give me a tour of the general theories of promising — I know the taxonomy, and I'm not asking you to sort my commitments into pre-labeled boxes. Judge the package on its own terms, and pay particular attention to the combinations, not the items in isolation. If you conclude that some of my tension is merely verbal — that two of these commitments use a word like "obligation" or "bind" in different senses and so only seem to collide — say so explicitly and defend that verdict, because I will suspect special pleading otherwise. Finally, don't soften the verdict: if the set is untenable, I would rather learn it now than at the workshop.
