I'm an undergraduate taking a seminar called The Epistemology of Markets, and we've just finished the unit on no-trade results — Aumann's agreeing-to-disagree theorem and the Milgrom–Stokey extension showing that agents who share a prior and commonly know one another's posteriors can never benefit from betting against each other. As I understand the argument, if two rational agents with a common prior hold different beliefs, their disagreement must trace back to asymmetric information, and once the willingness to trade makes that information common knowledge, it gets absorbed into their posteriors until the trade is no longer mutually acceptable. This has left me with a conclusion my professor says I've overreached on, though she wouldn't tell me where: if trading volume in prediction markets is enormous, then either the traders are irrational or they're violating some assumption of the theorem, and in either case the market price can't possibly be read as an aggregate of anyone's genuine credences — it must be an artifact of noise, exploitation, or outright error, and treating the price as a probability would be a category mistake. On my reading, then, the entire case for prediction markets as epistemic instruments collapses: at best they are machines for harvesting money from biased traders, and whatever number they display is a measure of bias rather than of belief. My question is whether there is any principled way, consistent with the no-trade results, to interpret a prediction-market price as an informationally meaningful aggregate — for instance, by relaxing the common prior or the common-knowledge conditions in ways that are individually rational — or whether the skeptical conclusion really does follow. Please walk me through where the argument breaks down if it does, explaining exactly which assumption the volume of real-world trading is best attributed to; and as you go, flag how confident you are in each step of the diagnosis, and single out the step you are least sure of.
