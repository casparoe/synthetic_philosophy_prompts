hey, longtime lurker here (since the Overcoming Bias days, back when the comment threads were mostly about AI risk and everyone had a different username). I mostly argue in comment sections, but today I want something written for me.

The thing I can't get out of my head lately is Dennett's late-period obsession: nature built competence before comprehension, and it did it twice. Darwin's inversion — the stuff that looks designed doesn't need a designer, the competence just gets churned out by a blind selection process. Then Turing's — a machine can do the work of thinking without there being anyone in there doing any thinking. Comprehension shows up late in both stories and takes credit for the whole operation, like a manager who strolls into the factory a billion years after the production line started and starts signing things. He basically wrote an entire book (From Bacteria to Bach and Back) about how we're still tempted to flip this back around and put comprehension in charge, even though every worked example we have goes the other way.

So I want a satire of the flipped-around picture. Concretely: write me a mock school prospectus, roughly 600-700 words, for a school that has taken the inversions literally as its operating model. Give it a name — something like the Bottom-Up Academy — and a motto over the gate, ideally a bad pun on selection (bad in the way real school mottos are bad, not winking at the reader). The curriculum is pure selection and mutation: children drill, the worksheets that produce correct answers get copied and slightly altered, nobody is ever taught *why* anything works, and a comprehension department exists on paper but only convenes after graduation, when its function is to retroactively claim the results. The satire's target is the top-down assumption itself — the idea that competence flows from comprehension — which means the school has to be shown *winning* by the logic of the inversions, not failing at ordinary schooling. The league tabels look exactly as good as they would if someone in there had understood the material, and the prospectus should be smug about this in a way that never quite says what you expect it to say.

The jokes need to come from getting the ideas right, not from mocking a strawman: the difference between a reason and a cause, why the comprehension department is never actually needed for the school to function (and what it's *for*, if anything), the visiting inspector who demands to see the understanding and gets shown the exam results, the question of whether anyone at the school — including the headmistress — could pass a test on the reasons rather than the answers. Two hard constraints: don't let it tip into awww the kids secretly understood all along, because that's the top-down picture sneaking back in through the back door, which is precisely the thing being satirised; and keep it deadpan throughout — the prospectus should never editorialise or wink, the reader should feel the vertigo of not being able to point at the page where the school is wrong. If the piece can leave genuinely unresolved whether the headmistress comprehends anything, that's the ending I want.

Oh, and please write the whole thing (prospectus, and your reply generally) in British English — realise, colour, maths, that lot. I've spent too long on American-hosted forums and I want my spelling back.
