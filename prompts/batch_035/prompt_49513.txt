For some months I've been collecting my own verdicts on when foreseen side-effects count as intentional, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, captures them. A chairman who keeps a profitable program knowing it will harm the environment, caring nothing about the environment: I say he harms it intentionally. The mirror-image chairman who keeps the program knowing it will help the environment, equally indifferent: I say he does not help it intentionally. A manager whose project incidentally restores a wetland, and who is delighted about it — she highlights it in her performance review, though she'd never have spent a dollar of budget to achieve it: here I catch myself saying she restored the wetland intentionally, which I can't square with the second verdict. A driver composing a text who kills a cyclist: plainly blameworthy, but I resist calling the death intentional, even though he knowingly flouted a norm just as the first chairman did. And a demolition crew whose controlled collapse unavoidably cracks the neighbors' windows: not intentional, and no temptation to say otherwise. My tentative principle is that we call a foreseen side-effect intentional when the agent is indifferent to it, but that plainly fails on the delighted manager (not indifferent, a benefit, intentional) and on the texting driver (not indifferent to the risk, a harm, not intentional). Is there a single principle that fits all five verdicts — perhaps norm-violation for harms and endorsement for benefits — or must some verdict be abandoned? Please state the principle precisely, say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate if any, and tell me what it predicts for a case I keep going back and forth on: an executive who foresees the environmental harm, proceeds anyway, but is privately anguished about it and pays for offsets out of his own pocket.
