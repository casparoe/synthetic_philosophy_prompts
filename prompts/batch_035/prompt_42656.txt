My decision theory reading group has now spent three meetings on sunk costs and we're going in circles, which is strange becuase this is supposed to be the one bias that follows straight from the axioms. One faction holds the textbook line: the past is the past, only marginal costs and benefits going forward matter, and a rational agent just never lets a sunk cost tip a decision. The other faction keeps producing cases where honoring sunk costs looks right, or at least not obviously dumb — the person who finishes the terrible multi-season show because five seasons are already in, the company that sees a doomed project through partly because abandoning it signals that its commitments mean nothing, the wine collector in Thaler's mental accounting papers who won't sell his bottles for less than he paid even though he wouldn't buy them at that price today. Their line is that sunk costs carry information — about your own past judgment, about your reliability — and can work as commitment devices, so the textbook view is only true under assumptions of perfect self-knowledge and costless commitment that basically never hold outside a whiteboard.

And here's the thing: nobody moves an inch. The textbook people say every one of those cases smuggles in a forward-looking benefit (signaling, reputation, self-discipline), so it isn't the sunk cost doing the work at all — it's a new payoff you'd count even if the cost weren't sunk. The revisionists say that reply proves too much, because if any effect of a sunk cost can be redescribed as forward-looking, then "sunk costs are irrelevant" stops being a substantive principle and becomes a bookkeeping convention about what you're allowed to call a payoff.

So what I want is really diagnostic, not a verdict on whether to honor sunk costs. Is this a substantive disagreement about the structure of rational preferences, a verbal one about what it means for a sunk cost to matter "as such," or two groups answering different questions — one about ideal agents under the axioms, one about boundedly self-aware humans in social settings — under a single heading? My own guess, offered only as a guess, is that the textbook view is doing conceptual work (defining what a rational preference structure looks like at all) while the revisionist view is doing empirical work about which heuristics perform well in the mess, and the fight only exists because both sides present their answer as the answer to "should you honor sunk costs." But I could be wrong, so argue it out rather than nodding along. Tell me what each side is actually committed to once you pin them down, whether there's a case or a formal setup that could adjudicate between them — a scenario where the redescribe-it-as-a-payoff move fails, or a model where honoring sunk costs is provably optimal and you can see exactly which assumptions are doing the work — and what would count as progress here: a distinction, an experiment, a change of question. Or make the case that no progress should be expected and explain why.

Two requests on how you write it. First, keep clear what's settled versus what's genuinely contested among people who actually work on this — the experimental fact that people honor sunk costs seems pretty robust, but its interpretation is not, so don't hand me one interpretation dressed up as the consensus. Second, half my group are economists and the other half are psychologists who've read the escalation-of-commitment literature and have opinions about it, so if your diagnosis is "they're operating with different implicit models," spell the models out — don't just gesture at them.
