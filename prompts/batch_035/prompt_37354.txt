I'm in Year 13 and working on an Extended Project Qualification about what dispositional properties really are. It started when my chemistry teacher remarked that calling salt "soluble" seems to be saying something about the salt itself, not about whether anyone will ever stir it into water, and I wanted to know what sort of property could be like that. Reading around the subject, I found that philosophers usually try to analyse dispositions by means of conditionals — roughly, something is fragile if it would break if struck — and that this analysis faces two famous kinds of counterexample: finks, where something would intervene to remove the disposition before it could manifest, and masks, where a standing obstacle blocks the manifestation without removing the disposition. The mask idea is meant to capture the intuition that a glass packed in bubble wrap is still fragile; it just never gets the chance to show it.

My project partner has now produced an argument which she says proves too much, and I can't find where it goes wrong. (1) Fragility is an intrinsic property: whether a glass is fragile depends on its own structure and not on what surrounds it. (2) A glass packed in bubble wrap is still fragile — the wrap masks the disposition, blocking the breaking without taking the fragility away, so a disposition survives being masked. (3) The same holds if we coat the glass in a thin, tough, transparent skin: the skin is a mask just like the bubble wrap, and the glass inside remains fragile. (4) We may repeat this as often as we like: each time we add a further layer, that layer is a mask, and by the same reasoning the object inside is unchanged in its fragility; there is no stage at which the rule changes, and at no finite stage is the coat anything more than a mask around a fragile glass. (5) A solid glass brick is simply the limit of this process — layer upon layer of the same stuff, with no glass "inside" any more than a mille-feuille has one particular sheet of pastry "inside". (6) So the whole brick is fragile: if every layer is a mask, and masks never destroy fragility, then the brick, which is nothing but a fragile core plus masks, is itself fragile.

She draws the consequences out further: entomb a wine glass in the pier of a Victorian railway bridge and the whole bridge becomes fragile; seal a grain of salt somewhere inside a steel girder and the girder becomes soluble. On this pattern almost every object has almost every disposition, and disposition ascriptions become trivial — which I cannot accept. Which premise or step should I give up, and why did it look so harmless when stated? What does rejecting it cost? If I am forced to choose, I would sooner abandon (1), the claim that fragility is intrinsic, than accept her conclusion, but I suspect that abandoning (1) has consequences of its own that I have not yet seen, and I would like to know what they are before I write the next section of my project.
