I teach a first-year course in philosophy of language, and my section has fourteen students and runs for seventy-five minutes. Next week we reach Wittgenstein's later remarks on words that apply without a common essence — the family-resemblance discussion in the Philosophical Investigations, the fibre-and-thread metaphor, and the exchange with the interlocutor who complains that he is letting himself off the part of the investigation that once gave him the most headache. I do not want to lecture this material; I want a staged activity in which the students run into the phenomena themselves, with the debrief doing the mapping back to the text only afterwards. Please design that activity.

Build it in two movements rather than around a single task.

The first movement should target the word "chair" — deliberately not "game" or "sport", which every textbook trots out and which the students will half-remember from school. Participants should be induced to write a definition of "chair", test it against a set of borderline items I will present (a beanbag, a saddle, a tree stump that people in fact sit on, a throne, a chaise longue, an office chair that has lost a leg, an item that seems to have grown out of the floor, one that behaves in ways chairs never do), revise the definition, and fail repeatedly. The strangest items are meant to echo the thought experiment in the Investigations around §§79–80, where Wittgenstein asks whether our use of the word "chair" is everywhere bounded by rules when confronted with a chair that misbehaves. Then there must be a twist: after every definition has failed, the group is asked to classify a final set of genuinely new items, and the votes should be recorded publicly so that the students can see the datum I want them to feel — that they continue to agree with one another about what counts as a chair while disagreeing completely about what makes something one. Agreement without shared criteria, not as a claim they are told but as something that happened to them.

The second movement should be shorter and structured around the "Moses" case from §79: a list of facts about a named figure (that he led his people out of Egypt, that he bore the name "Moses", that he did certain things certain texts ascribe to him, and so on) stripped away one at a time, with students voting at each stage on whether the name still applies and being asked to justify their votes — which should reveal that different students were holding different determining features in place all along. I would like the activity to make it visible that the indeterminacy here is of a different shape from the borderline-item indeterminacy of the first movement, even though the text treats them as one conversation.

For the whole activity, please provide: the rules, the materials (I have a whiteboard, a projector, and no budget), a minute-by-minute timeline, the mechanism for recording votes and revisions so the trail of failures and convergences is visible at the debrief, and the debrief itself, which should map specific moments in the activity onto specific parts of the view — the family-resemblance network, the fibre-and-thread metaphor, the point that a word's application need not be everywhere bounded by rules, and the remark later in the Investigations (around §§241–242) that what lies at the bottom of the language-game is agreement in judgements, agreement in form of life, rather than agreement in opinions.

Three constraints on the debrief. First, my students did the sorites paradox last term, and they will predictably conflate family resemblance with mere vagueness — the debrief must prise these apart rather than blur them together, and I would like you to tell me precisely at which moment in the debrief that distinction is best drawn. Second, some students will conclude that if there is no common essence, then anything goes and any competent speaker may call anything a chair; the activity should set that misconception up so it can be knocked down with the same material Wittgenstein uses against his interlocutor — the tennis ball hit arbitrarily high is still not hit out of the game. Third, tell me honestly which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and I must supply in the debrief: I suspect a classroom group converging over seventy-five minutes is not the same thing as the inherited background of a form of life, and the felt normativity of our judgements — that we are answerable to something in them, not merely describing what we happen to do — may be exactly what a one-off activity cannot stage.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — the ways students will try to game the definitions, the groups that will refuse to take the odd items seriously, the moments where the vote mechanic will misfire — and how to handle each without simply asserting the answer. Please write the whole plan in British English.
