My cousin lent me a philosophy of science book she finished, and there's a section that's had me thinking all week but I also suspect it's kind of sneaky. It summarizes structural realism roughly like this: science can only ever tell us about the relations between things, not the things themselves, and John Worrall proved this is the only honest response to the fact that successful theories keep getting overturned. His example is light: Fresnel thought light was a wave in the ether, Maxwell kept the wave math but dropped the ether, and since the equations survived while the ether didn't, the lesson is that only the mathematical structure of our theories is real. From there the book gets more aggressive: since everything is ultimately just structure, electrons don't exist as objects at all, only as nodes in a relational web, and quantum mechanics has experimentally confirmed this because identical particles have no individual identity (you can't tag them). It closes by saying that since two structures with the same form are the same structure, any two possible worlds containing the same number of objects are literally the same world, so physics has quietly dissolved all the old metaphysical questions about what matter is made of.

I'm in no position to check any of this, but it feels like a lot of mileage from one example about light, and something about the last step smells off even to me. Can you go through it and tell me what a structural realist would actually sign off on, where the book is quietly swapping in a stronger claim than the argument supports, and whether the stuff about electrons and identical worlds actually follows from anything Worrall or the quantum mechanics part establishes? I'd especially like to know whether the book is running together two different versions of the view — I have a vague memory that there's a distinction between saying we can only *know* structure and saying structure is all there *is*, but I can't reconstruct it well enough to say if that's what's happening here.
