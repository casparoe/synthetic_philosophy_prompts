I'm a journalist who mostly covers science and ideas, and as a side gig I sit on the three-person jury for a small prize for popular science books. The written guidelines say a book makes the shortlist iff it's scientifically sound and a genuinely good read for a lay audience — both, not either. Our procedure every cycle: each juror scores each entry on soundness and on readability, and also gives an overall yes/no on shortlisting it, and then the jury as a body issues one ruling per book. We keep scorecards, so I can reconstruct exactly how everyone voted last cycle. The other two jurors are R., a veteran reviewer who has chaired this jury forever, and D., a young editor with strong opinions.

Last cycle went like this. (1) A big cosmology book: all three of us found it sound and engaging, all three voted to shortlist, and we shortlisted it. (2) A microbiome book that was fun but full of howlers — I caught one of the errors myself, so nobody called it sound; R. and I thought it was engaging, D. didn't; nobody voted to shortlist; we didn't. (3) A beautifully written epigenetics book that oversold its claims: I found it sound and engaging and voted to shortlist; R. and D. both liked the prose but not the soundness, so they voted no; we didn't shortlist it. (4) A technically impeccable stellar physics book that was a slog: all three of us called it sound; R. and I didn't find it engaging but D. did, so D. voted to shortlist and we two voted no; we didn't shortlist.

This cycle we have a book on the neuroscience of sleep that's got us deadlocked, and it's the first time I can see two different ways of counting our own record giving different answers. The votes: I find it sound and engaging, so I voted to shortlist; R. thinks chapter 6 bends the evidence but agrees its a great read, so she voted no; D. checked the claims and they're fine, but she found the middle third dense, so she also voted no. So a majority of us says the book is sound, a majority says it's engaging, and a majority says don't shortlist it. I say both criteria pass by majority, so in it goes; R. and D. say the jury's overall judgement is clearly no. Before friday's meeting I want to know: given our track record, what's the ruling that's consistent with how we've actually decided, and which of the four earlier books is the closest analog? What rules genuinely fit our record — I can see the two obvious ways of counting, and I suspect there are quieter ones, like "R. decides" or "we only shortlist when nobody objects," and I'd love to know if any of the quieter ones is already ruled out by one of the four. If the record truly underdetermines it, tell me what each choice would commit us to going forward. Also: what kind of entry would best tell the competing rules apart, if we ever get one? And separately — I have a nagging feeling there's a name for this whole mess somewhere in the philosophy literature, possibly from an ideas piece I read years ago, but I can't place it. Be straight with me throughout: if I've misrecorded our votes somewhere, or if the question can't be answered the way I've posed it, say that plainly instead of picking an answer. And if the track record really doesn't favour any rule, an honest "coin flip, here's why" beats a confident call.
