My friend and I had one of those arguments that went way too long last night, about Good's theorem -- the value-of-information thing, where a Bayesian agent facing a decision can never lose in expectation by learning the truth of some signal for free before choosing. He took the super strong reading: it's *always* fine to peek, cost or no cost, whoever's watching, whatever the setup, because "more knowledge can't hurt you." I kept trying to tell him that "free" and "you're the only agent" are each doing real load-bearing work: pay a fee for the signal and obviously the expected value can go negative, but even with a literally free signal, if the person on the other side of your decision can observe *that you chose to look* (or infer what you looked at), the info can burn you. He kept saying nobody would ever actually refuse free info in a real decision, and at some point I realized I want to demonstrate this with actual humans rather than a whiteboard, so:

I teach an intermediate decision theory seminar, 18 students, one 75-minute session. Design an in-class game that makes them feel both halves of this. Stage one should be the theorem working: a simple choice under uncertainty where some students get a free signal before deciding and strictly beat the ones who don't, so the "obviously just look" intuition gets built up. Then I want the theorem to break, with the setup kept as identical as possible except one thing moves each time: a stage where the signal is the same but now costs a small fee (so the students can actually compute that it's negative expected value even though it's still genuinely informative), and a stage where the signal is again free but there's a counterpart -- some kind of negotiation or sealed-bid thing -- who can condition their behavior on whether you acquired it, so the acquisition itself leaks information about your side of the deal.

Constraints: payoffs in course tokens or points, no real money, everything printable, no software, and the decision matrices small enough that the math is transparent rather than intimidating. Crucially the students should run into the failure themselves before anyone says Good's name -- nothing gets named until the debrief. The strategic stage should feel like actual bargaining, not a contrived grading rule that just stipulates "info is bad here" -- the damage has to fall out of the payoffs and the other person's incentives, because otherwise my friend will (correctly) accuse me of rigging it.

Then a debrief that maps each stage onto a specific assumption of the theorem: what exactly "costless" buys you, what exactly "single agent" buys you, and which assumption each breakdown stage violates. Also be honest about what predictably goes wrong the first time I run this (my guess is students won't believe the info is really free, or will overcorrect and start refusing genuinely useful free signals in the early stage just to be contrarian), and tell me which feature of the theorem no classroom activity can reproduce and I'll just have to supply verbally. My friend is sitting in on the session and he is insufferable when he thinks he's won an argument, so if the design has any hole where info looks bad only because I cheated, he will find it.
