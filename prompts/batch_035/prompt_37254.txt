So here's my situation. I'm presenting to our department curriculum committee next month, and I need to be ready for the question period, because this crowd is going to come at me.

The proposal: in the intro course, students can use AI for whatever they want on the essays — brainstorming, drafting, the whole thing — but they have to append what I'm calling a "vouching section": for every substantive claim they keep, they defend it in their own words without the tool, and they explicitly mark any claim they could not defend. The grade weights the honesty of the marking, not how many they got right.

The thesis I'll be defending: the erosion of understanding doesn't happen when the machine writes the sentence. It happens when the student's only relationship to a claim is trust in a source that has no stake in being right — what I've started calling "testimony without a testifier" in my notes. The model doesn't remember what it told you, won't defend it tomorrow, and will contradict itself without embarrassment if you ask twice. That's different from depending on a book or a living expert, and I think it's the difference that matters. I admit I'm partly trading on intuition there, and I know it.

The room: about a dozen colleagues. The chair, who is basically Thamus in the Phaedrus and will have his writing-destroys-memory bit ready within five minutes. A junior hire in formal epistemology who thinks resistance is futile and will say I'm just rediscovering Hardwig's point about epistemic dependence — we trust experts we can't check all the time, why is the machine different — and, separately, that "erosion of understanding" is empty talk until I give her an operationalization she can work with. A philosopher of mind guy who will ask whether an LLM's output even counts as testimony, given that there's no speaker — which is annoying because it's my own point turned back on me. A virtue epistemologist who'll say the real problem is declining curiosity and no course policy fixes a character trait. And two grad students sitting in who use these tools daily and think the vouching section is busywork designed to make them feel scholarly.

What I want from you is a prep sheet. The objections in roughly the order I'll actually get them, phrased the way these specific people will phrase them (especially the chair — he loves a classical allusion, give him his due). For each one: a reply I can deliver in under a minute, what I should concede up front, and where the reply is soft. Flag the two or three objections that could genuinely sink the proposal if they land, as opposed to the ones that just sting. And tell me the question I'm hoping nobody asks, because someone will ask it.

One more thing: dont give me the standard "AI makes students lazy" op-ed objections. The committee has heard all of those and so have I, and frankly I could write them myself in my sleep, which is exactly the kind of output I'm trying to get my students to stop producing. What I need is the in-the-weeds material: what exactly distinguishes depending on an AI from depending on a book, whether "understanding" is even the right thing to be protecting, and whether my vouching section measures understanding or just measures what students can perform on demand. If you think the vouching section itself is the weak point, say so — I'd rather hear it from you than from the formal epistemologist in front of everyone.
