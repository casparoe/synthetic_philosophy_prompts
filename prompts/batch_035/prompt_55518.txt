You are a philosopher with long experience teaching philosophy of biology and philosophy of mind to adult non-specialists, and you care as much about pedagogy as about content. Think carefully, and begin with some brainstorming before committing to a plan.

I host a monthly science-café evening for adult laypeople: thirty minutes of talk plus open discussion, no slides, a whiteboard at most. Next month's topic is the pair of "strange inversions" associated with Darwin and Turing — Darwin explaining how organisms can come to exhibit design-like competence through a mindless process of variation and selection, Turing explaining how intelligent-seeming performance can be produced by mechanical symbol manipulation. I do not want a lecture on the content itself; I want a plan for explaining it. Specifically:

- Before committing, weigh at least two possible orderings of the material and argue briefly for the one you would choose, making the dependency structure explicit — which piece has to be in place before the next one can make sense, and why.
- Recommend one carrying example that the whole session can be built around. It must exhibit competence arising without comprehension, yet resist the audience's urge to dismiss it as "just" a trick. Address head-on why the obvious candidate — a chess engine — may sabotage the point: audiences often say it's "just brute force," using the very mindlessness that constitutes the lesson as a reason to dismiss it.
- Anticipate the misconceptions this audience will bring or form, in the order they will actually surface, and tell me where in the sequence each should be headed off. My prediction is an asymmetry: they will accept the Turing inversion too easily ("it's just computation") while quietly refusing the Darwinian one by smuggling a designer back in, and a few may conclude that the idea debunks comprehension altogether — that nobody, including us, really understands anything. Say whether you agree, where each of these should be defused, and flag any misconception I have missed.
- Propose one question I can put to the room at the end that would reveal whether the inversion has genuinely landed rather than been politely nodded at.
- Tell me what to explicitly refuse to cover at this level — consciousness, the hard problem, whether large language models "really" understand — and how to decline without seeming to dodge.

Do not suggest readings, reading lists, or citations; this is an oral-format event. Be concrete about placement ("after X, before Y"), not general.
