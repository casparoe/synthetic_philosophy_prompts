I've spent fifteen years in data governance deciding, professionally, what records an organisation keeps and what it deletes as dead weight, and I've just moved sideways into a county records service — so the theory in our staff-development module about archives and power has abruptly stopped being academic. It has also become personal: a close family friend, born in Jamaica and brought to Britain in the late 1960s, is now assembling what the Windrush compensation scheme requires of him, which amounts to documentary proof of a childhood, from records whose survival people like me determine. For the module's final paper I want to analyze the Windrush scandal as a case study of that theory, and I want it done as a disciplined mapping, not an editorial.

The theory, as we've been taught it, runs roughly like this: archives are not neutral stores of memory but instruments of whoever administers them; records are created for the administrator's purposes, not the recorded's; and archival silence is produced rather than accidental. The scandal's relevant facts, all common knowledge but worth fixing: Commonwealth citizens arrived between 1948 and 1971 and, under the 1971 Immigration Act, acquired settled status — yet were never issued any document confirming it. The Home Office destroyed the landing cards, the single set of records establishing dates of arrival, in 2010 as part of routine disposal, while retaining whatever its enforcement apparatus still needed. From 2012 the 'hostile environment' framework required people to prove their status in order to work, rent, and access healthcare; those who could not, through no fault of their own, were detained, and some were deported. The scandal broke in 2018, through press reporting rather than through any archival mechanism.

Please analyze the episode through the theory, running in both directions. Map it properly: who held the power to create, keep, destroy, and demand records; whose administrative purposes each surviving record served; what counted as evidence and why an absence of state record was read as an absence of the person. Then be honest about the fit. I have come to suspect the theory's key unit is wrong — it treats 'the archive' as a monolithic institution, but Windrush turned on something finer-grained: which particular file, for which administrative purpose, stayed operationally 'live' and which was allowed to become housekeeping. Call this differential retention, if it helps to have a name for it. I'd like you to say whether that refinement rescues the theory or merely relabels its problem, where the theory goes silent (I keep noticing it has nothing to say about how the scandal was actually exposed, or about how the compensation scheme now manufactures a new layer of records that will one day be someone else's evidence), and whether the episode is a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the theory never anticipated.

Structure your response in five sections, in this order: 'The theory, restated in operational terms' (roughly 150 words), 'The mapping' (around 400), 'What the theory explains' (around 300), 'Where the theory goes silent' (around 300), and 'The episode as a test of the theory' (around 250). Please do not cite or recommend literature; argue from the theory as I've stated it and the facts as I've supplied them.
