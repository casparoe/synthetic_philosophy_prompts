I'm a PhD student in cognitive science, working on language acquisition, and I've spent enough time in seminars and reading groups to notice that the poverty-of-the-stimulus debate has been running for over half a century without either side conceding anything. I'd like a diagnosis of the impasse rather than a verdict on who's right, and I'll describe the positions as I understand them so the diagnosis has something to bite on.

The shape of the dispute, as I see it: the generativist side holds that children arrive at grammatical knowledge that the primary linguistic data leave underdetermined. The stock example is structure dependence in question formation — the child learning to form questions by inverting the auxiliary of the main clause rather than, say, the linearly first auxiliary, as in asking about the boy who is crying, where the wrong generalization would invert the auxiliary inside the relative clause. The child is said never to entertain the linear rule, never to be corrected for it, and never to receive data that would rule it out; hence some structure-sensitive bias must be antecedently present. The empiricist side replies along two lines. First, a methodological complaint: the argument has rarely been stated with the rigor its conclusion demands — nobody has specified the child's actual input distribution, the hypothesis space over alternatives, and shown that no learner consistent with the data reaches the attested generalization, so the claim is an intuition dressed as a theorem. Second, a positive program: distributional statistics in child-directed speech carry more signal than nativists credit — infants track transitional probabilities, and corpus work has found cues correlated with the right structural generalizations — so the stimulus is not as poor as advertised. The nativist rejoinder is that a learner able to exploit such cues is itself already doing sophisticated work, which pushes the contribution back inside the learner, where the nativist wanted it all along. And lately each camp has absorbed the same new evidence in opposite directions: language models acquire surprisingly structure-sensitive behavior from pure prediction, which the empiricists read as learnability without syntax-specific innate endowment and the generativists discount by pointing out that the training corpus dwarfs a childhood of input and contains nothing childlike about its sampling.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis only: the disagreement is not empirical at all, but about where to draw a line that is in fact conventional — the line between the stimulus and the learner. Any experimental demonstration that infants exploit some regularity can be redescribed by the nativist as an innate mechanism detecting it and by the empiricist as the regularity being part of the input, and I cannot see what fact of the world decides between the descriptions. If that's right, the debate is verbal in the pejorative sense, and the apparent factual content — how much the child brings to the task — has no determinate answer until someone fixes the boundary, which looks like a stipulation rather than a discovery.

But I'm not confident, and I'd like the diagnosis done properly. Is the impasse really a boundary-drawing dispute of that kind, or is there a hidden formal question doing the work — for example, whether the attested input is in principle sufficient to identify the attested grammars, in the sense studied in formal learning theory, where certain language classes are known to be unidentifiable from positive data alone? If so, is the obstacle that no one has done the corpus work, or that the result, even if done, would be too idealized to settle anything? Alternatively, are the two camps answering different questions under one name — the generativists addressing logical learnability and the empiricists addressing the developmental psychology of how children actually parse and store what they hear — so that both could be right and the appearance of conflict is an artifact of shared vocabulary? I'd also like to know what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress: a jointly accepted formalization of the input and hypothesis space, a particular kind of corpus demonstration, a conceptual distinction that forces one side to reclassify its claims, or a decision to split the question in two. And if your honest view is that no progress should be expected — that this is a stable equilibrium sustained by something like divergent starting intuitions about what an explanation of acquisition must look like — say that, and say what keeps it stable. Please engage with the positions as I've described them rather than substituting a cleaner version of the debate, and if you think I've misdescribed any of the moves, flag it, since a diagnosis of a misdescribed dispute is worthless.
