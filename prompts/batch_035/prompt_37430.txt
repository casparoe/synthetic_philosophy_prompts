My cousin works in ML and gave me his take on the frame problem over dinner: "It's basically the old robotics puzzle of how a system knows which facts change when it does something. If a robot picks up a block, it has to work out that the block's location changed but its colour didn't, and in the sixties this needed zillions of axioms spelling out everything that stays the same, which obviously didn't scale. Dennett had that example of the robot pulling a cart out of a room that has to keep checking stuff like whether pulling the cart changes the cart's colour, until it just sits there forever. The technical version got sorted in the eighties with default assumptions about persistence, and honestly the rest was mostly Fodor making it sound mystical. With big neural nets now it's just a compute question." It sounded plausible at the time but the "basically solved" bit felt a bit quick to me. How much of this would someone who actually works on the frame problem accept, what's a subtle conflation or two that's doing sneaky work, and does his "just compute now" conclusion follow from the parts of his summary that are actually true? Also, if the technical and the philosophical versions of the problem have come apart over the years, which one is he even talking about, and does it matter for whether large language models touch it?
