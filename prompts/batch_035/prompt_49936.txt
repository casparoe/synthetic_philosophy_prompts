I'm on the board of a mid-sized family foundation that funds both immediate-need stuff (food banks, disaster relief) and long-horizon work (pandemic preparedness, nuclear risk reduction, basically benefits that mostly land decades from now). We're redoing our grantmaking rubric and have to pick a discount rate for comparing grants whose benefits arrive at differnt times, and we're completely deadlocked. One faction wants pure time preference set to zero — their line is that caring less about someone just because their benefits arrive later is as arbitrary as caring less about someone because they live farther away, and that the only legitimate reasons to discount are things like risk and changing wealth levels, not time itself. The other faction thinks that's naive: there's a real chance the foundation doesn't exist in 50 years (foundations die, heirs raid the endowment, they get absorbed by bigger funders), and pretending otherwise means systematically overfunding the far future. Plus, if we really refuse to discount at all while markets return anything like they historically have, the rational move on paper is to never spend anything and just compund forever, which is obviously absurd.

There's also a compromise floating around that I half-suspect is worse than either extreme: keep pure time preference at zero, but discount for the annual probability that the foundation (or whatever institution would carry a grant forward) fails, maybe with a rate that declines over time. Honestly I find all three options kind of persuasive, which is why we've gone in circles.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before our October board meeting. It should recommend one concrete option — an actual number or rule, not a menu of options — with the reasoning behind it, and it has to take the losing side's strongest point seriously rather than steamrolling it. Lead with the recommendation in the very first sentence, then give the reasoning, no preamble and no restating this background.
