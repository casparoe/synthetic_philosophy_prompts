I'm a software engineer with zero formal philosophy training, but I read a lot of ethics stuff for fun, and there's one debate I keep coming back to that seems totally and permanently stuck: the paradox of deontology. My understanding is roughly: deontologists say there are constraints, like "don't murder an innocent person," that you can't violate even to prevent *more* violations of the very same constraint. So if I can kill one person to stop a bad actor from killing five, the strict deontologist says I shouldn't — even though the outcome is now five murders instead of one, which looks worse by the deontologist's own lights. It's supposed to be self-defeating, like a firewall that refuses to block one packet because blocking is censorship while letting a million through. Deontologists have a bunch of replies: it matters whether YOU do the deed versus merely allowing others to do it, there's something like moral pollution in getting your own hands dirty, agent-relative reasons just don't aggregate the way consequentialists want them to. And this exact exchange has been repeating, as far as I can tell, for 40+ years with nobody switching teams.

I've also seen the supposed compromise called threshold deontology, where constraints hold until the stakes get astronomically big, and everyone who discusses it seems to find it ad hoc — which might itself be a clue about what's really going on. My working theory is that the two sides aren't actually disagreeing about one question: consequentialists are answering "which world-state should exist" while deontologists are answering "what may I do," and the "paradox" only bites when you demand a single framework answer both at once. But I might be completely wrong about that. So here's what I want from you: a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about what counts as a moral reason, a hidden factual claim about how agency works, or bedrock intuitions (about doing vs. allowing, or hands-on involvement) that neither side can argue for without circularity? Support the diagnosis with what each side actually claims, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress — a new kind of thought experiment, a formal treatment of agent-relative reasons, some distinction nobody has drawn yet — or whether the impasse is a sign the question itself is ill-posed. Please write your answer in American English.
