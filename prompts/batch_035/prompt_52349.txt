ok so I'm trying to get a better feel for how different ways of aggregating across moral theories can come apart in practice, and I want you to build me a thought experiment to spec. This is for building intuition about when intertheoretic value comparisons actually do real work, so the case needs to be honest about what's driving the result — not rigged.

Background so we dont need to look anything up: say you have credences in a few moral theories but no single one dominates, and you have to pick one option. Two ways to decide:

1. Variance voting (roughly the move recommended in the Bykvist/Ord/MacAskill *Moral Uncertainty* book): each theory assigns a choiceworthiness number to each option. Rescale each theory's numbers so they have mean 0 and standard deviation 1 across the option set (subtract the mean, divide by the sd). Then take your credence-weighted average of the rescaled numbers, option by option, and pick the highest.

2. Borda count: each theory just ranks the options. An option gets points equal to the number of options ranked below it (split the points for ties). Take the credence-weighted average of the points, highest wins.

What I want: a realistic scenario, at least three options, where the two methods pick *different* options — and the divergence has to come from the structural difference between the methods (Borda throws away all cardinal info, variance voting keeps it), NOT from weird credences like 0.5% belief in something insane, and not from a theory no reflective person would entertain. The theories should be ones a normal thoughtful person genuinely splits credence between (some form of consequentialism, a deontological view with side constraints, maybe contractualism — your pick). The setting should be ordinary life: a career choice, a donation decision, how to handle something at work. No sci-fi, no trolleys. And the cardinal gaps have to be plausible as stated: if a theory thinks option A is a hair worse than B but both are far better than C, that near-tie at the top should be the sort of thing you'd actually beleive, not an arbitrary decimal I could jiggle to flip the answer.

Deliverables:

(a) the scenario, plus explicit numbers — a choiceworthiness table for each theory along with the credences, so I can check the math;

(b) the variance voting calculation and the Borda calculation both worked through, showing the two verdicts;

(c) an honest note on which feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to the verdicts if you delete that feature (e.g. drop the theory whose variance is dominated by a different axis, or shrink the gap between its top two options);

(d) how someone who swears by Borda — lets say because they think cardinal moral magnitudes within a theory are meaningless, you can't just say "A is 3.2 units of choiceworthiness better than B" — would push back on the case, and whether that pushback survives the fact that variance voting never compares magnitudes ACROSS theories, only within each one.

And if it turns out the two verdicts can only be made to differ with contrived numbers, tell me that instead and explain what breaks — I'd rather get the honest answer than a rigged case.
