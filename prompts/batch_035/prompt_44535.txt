I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and every quarter we host an evening salon for roughly thirty subscribers — intelligent, well-read adults with no mathematics beyond school, ninety minutes plus a break. For the next one I want to build the session around a puzzle I first met years ago in Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach: his tiny formal system for making strings out of the letters M, I, and U. The setup, so you don't have to dig it up: you start from the string MI, and at each step you may apply exactly one of four rules — (1) a string ending in I may have a U appended; (2) from Mx you may form Mxx, doubling everything after the M; (3) any occurrence of III may be replaced by U; (4) any occurrence of UU may be deleted. The question posed is whether you can ever produce the string MU. What I find riveting is less the answer — which, I'm told, turns on an invariant that no amount of rule-following will ever get you past — than what seeing the answer requires: what Hofstadter calls jumping out of the system, the realization that no amount of cleverness inside the rules can settle the question, and that stepping outside to reason about the rules is not cheating but a different and more powerful kind of thinking. I gather he even coined an acronym for it, JOOTSing. That three-way distinction — following rules, breaking rules, and reasoning about rules — is exactly what I want thirty non-specialists to feel in their hands rather than hear lectured about, because it's the move underlying Gödel's theorems and, if you believe Hofstadter, quite a lot else besides.

Design the evening for me. I'd like a warm-up that teaches the rules quickly and painlessly; a main phase in which teams grind away at derivations with some light competitive structure — a scoreboard, named theorems, whatever makes staying inside the system feel genuinely productive and absorbing, so that the pull of the inside is real before anyone is tempted to leave it; and then, crucially, an arranged situation in which participants supply the outside move themselves rather than being told it. I've toyed with planting a rules tribunal or handing one table the sealed answer, but I'm wary of anything that makes stepping outside feel like rule-breaking, since conflating the two is precisely the confusion the evening must not leave behind. Afterwards, a debrief that maps what happened onto the distinction and shows where else it does work — chess strategy versus chess rules, mathematics at its Gödelian limits, Hofstadter's own Zen-tinged suggestion that the right response to some questions is mu, to unask them rather than answer them — in terms my readers can carry home to their own jobs, most of which involve systems of one kind or another.

Please give me a timeline, a materials list, the facilitation script where it matters, and an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how I should handle it. I'd also like you to be frank about which feature of the genuine article no ninety-minute game can reproduce — the self-referential twist in Gödel's actual proof, say, or the fact that in real life the moment of jumping out is never scheduled — so I know what I'll have to gesture at across the bar after we pack up the scoreboards.
