My department's upper-level seminar on rational choice is wrapping up and I'm writing the final, and I want your help with one question. Last week I had a huge argument with my friend Marco (he's in the business school) about the Ellsberg stuff. He kept insisting ambiguity aversion is just risk aversion wearing a costume, and I kept saying no, the whole point is that the Ellsberg choices can't be reproduced by ANY assignment of probabilities to states, risk-averse or otherwise — that's what makes it a separate phenomenon. But by the end of the argument I realized I couldn't cleanly say what the *rational* verdict on the choices is supposed to be, and that's exactly the thing my students can't do either. Every year they recite the three-color urn, say "people prefer known odds, this violates the sure-thing principle, therefore ambiguity aversion," and recieve their marks, without ever engaging whether the violation is a problem for the chooser or for the principle.

So: write me one essay question on ambiguity aversion and the Ellsberg paradox that cannot be answered by reciting the standard setup. I want it to force them to do at least three things. (i) Explain why the Ellsberg preferences are inconsistent with any single probability assignment over states — and I want the question to lean on the two-urn version (one urn with exactly 50 red and 50 black, one with 100 red/black in unknown proportions), where people prefer the known urn for bets on red AND for bets on black, since that variant is nice for showing the preference is for knowledge itself and not for better odds. (ii) Grapple with the argument that these choices are irrational because a clever counterparty could exploit them — the line where you end up accepting a bet on the ambiguous event that you'd reject under either resolution of the ambiguity — and say whether that argument actually works or whether it just assumes the very thing in dispute, namely that a rational agent must have precise probabilities. (iii) Say whether modeling the behavior with maxmin expected utility (act as if nature gets to pick the worst prior in some set) actually explains the pattern or merely renames it. If they can also say something about whether "rational" here should even mean "consistent with one probability function," treat it as bonus material, but don't require it — I can't grade essays on material I didn't ask for.

Please give me the question as I'd print it on the exam paper (90 minutes to answer it), then a model answer of about 600 words, then a marking scheme worth 20 points total built around five must-hit points, with the marks each point is worth and a one-line note on how students typically botch each one. And make the A/B boundary explicit: name the specific moves an A answer contains that a B answer will lack. From past grading, the B answers usually stop at describing the paradox beautifully and never take a side on whether the exploit argument succeeds — I want the scheme to catch exactly that, so the difference between an A and a B is visible to a grader who's tired at 11pm, not something they have to intuit.
