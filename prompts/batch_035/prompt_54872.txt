I teach a graduate seminar that traverses rationalist metaphysics, and we end the term with Leibniz's ethics, which students tend to find either the crown of his system or its most dubious appendage. The interpretive controversy I want to put before them, and that I would like you to adjudicate in essay form, is this: does Leibniz's doctrine that God creates the best of all possible worlds, and creates it under a merely moral rather than metaphysical necessity, provide the grounding his moral philosophy needs, or does it quietly undermine that philosophy?

The dispute turns on a cluster of commitments Leibniz repeats across his works on justice and on theodicy, which I will state as faithfully as I can rather than quote. First, the will of God is determined by the good in a manner that inclines without necessitating: motives tilt the will toward the best, but the choice remains free in a sense that contrasts with geometric compulsion. Second, his recurring definition of justice as the charity of the wise casts justice as a virtue internal to a wise agent's character, flowing from love and understanding, rather than as a rule imposed on an unwilling subject. Third, happiness for rational creatures is not a terminus but a perpetual progress toward greater perfection, so that a rational creature never attains a final state; its good lies in unending advance.

On one reading, these commitments supply exactly what a moral theory needs. If value is objective and the best possible world is a determinate object of knowledge, then obligation can be anchored in what a wise and loving agent would will, and moral necessity marks off responsible, motivated action from blind mechanism, preserving both freedom and normativity in one stroke. The wise-agent account of justice then gives obligations a ground in the character of an ideal agent rather than in mere command, and the endless-progress view makes striving, not satiation, the human good.

On the skeptical reading, the same commitments corrode the ethics they were meant to support. If this world is the best possible, then every evil act in it, including every sin, is contained in the best overall plan; it becomes unclear in what sense the sinner could have done otherwise, since the alternative was, by hypothesis, a worse world that God would not choose and so could not have been actual. The distinction between moral and metaphysical necessity starts to look like a verbal device: if moral necessity finally issues in the actual, and the actual is the best, the agent's deliberation seems to run on rails. And if motives merely incline without necessitating, the skeptic presses, either the inclination is decisive in fact, in which case talk of an open choice is a courtesy, or it is not, in which case God's selection of the best looks arbitrary and the wise-agent account of justice inherits the arbitrariness.

Write an essay that weighs both readings seriously and reaches a verdict; do not end on a non-committal note or split the difference with a plague-on-both-houses conclusion. If you think the grounding reading survives, say precisely where the skeptical argument goes wrong, and which premise it relies on that you would reject. If you think the skeptical reading carries the day, say what this costs Leibniz's ethics as ethics — whether it leaves him with something like a description of the wise life but no genuine obligations, or something weaker still. You are welcome to argue that the controversy rests on a false presupposition, but if so, you must identify the presupposition and show that abandoning it changes the debate rather than dissolving it under a new name.

Two further requests. First, throughout the essay, flag explicitly how confident you are in each substantive claim you make about Leibniz's views, distinguishing what is textually central and secure from what is a matter of contested interpretation, and close by single out the single point about which you are least sure and explaining why. Second, do not pad the essay with bibliography, citations, or references to particular editions; argue from the stated commitments, not from apparatus. The students have the texts; what I want to test is whether the reasoning holds.
