I sit on the software certification panel of a national medical-device regulator. We're currently reviewing a submission for an implantable insulin pump whose control software has been formally verified: the behavioral requirements were written as a mathematical specification, and a proof assistant machine-checked a proof that the implementation meets that specification. The vendor's kernel is tiny and was itself independently verified, and the vendor also paid for a full requirements review by clinicians, so the usual worries about a buggy checker or a mistranslated spec are mostly handled. The dispute before the panel is about wording. The submission claims the pump's control software is "provably safe," and one of our members — a logician by training — has circulated an objection that has split us down the middle. She argues that the machine-checking is only as trustworthy as the axioms of set theory underlying the whole formal apparatus; that Gödel's second incompleteness theorem shows no consistent formal system can establish its own consistency; and that there is a nonzero, perhaps permanently unknowable, chance that the axioms themselves are inconsistent, in which case every formally verified proof collapses together with every informal one. In her view, "provably safe" overclaims, and we should certify the device only on condition that the vendor drop the phrase. Another member, an old-school engineer, thinks this is metaphysical hand-wringing: if elementary mathematics turns out to be inconsistent, he says, we'll have bigger problems than this pump, and in the meantime the formal verification is better evidence than anything else the panel has ever seen — so why hold it to a standard of certainty no evidence anywhere meets.

Draft the short memo I'll circulate ahead of our meeting next month. It should give the panel a concrete, actionable recommendation — approve as submitted, approve with conditions on the specific claims the vendor may make, or something else you can defend — and it should do the philosophy properly rather than papering over it. In particular: what the second incompleteness theorem actually establishes and what it doesn't; whether the bare possibility that the axioms of mathematics are inconsistent is the kind of risk a safety regulator is in the business of weighing, or whether it sits outside the risk calculus entirely; and what, if anything, is really being asserted when we say software is "proven safe" as opposed to "has met the strongest standard of correctness we know how to produce." Please take the logician's objection seriously rather than dismissing it; if the engineer's reply is ultimately the right one, the memo should explain exactly why it's right, in terms the logician would accept, and not just by appeal to practical convenience. It would also help if the memo drew the line between what the panel strictly needs to decide about this device and what is really a broader epistemic question about how much formal verification should reshape our confidence levels across the whole portfolio.
