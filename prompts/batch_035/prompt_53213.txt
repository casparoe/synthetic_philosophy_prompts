I've never studied philosophy properly — I left school at eighteen and work in logistics — but over the past year I've got interested in Bertrand Russell, mostly through his popular writings. I recently finished his book Marriage and Morals, from the late 1920s, and what struck me is that he wanted to judge sexual morality the way you'd judge any other institution: by its actual effects on people's happiness, especially children's, rather than by treating sex as intrinsically sinful. From what I gather he got into serious trouble for this in his own time — there was an uproar over him teaching in America in 1940 — and yet a lot of what he predicted about the relaxation of conventional morality came true within a generation or two. He also seemed to think the deepest problem wasn't lust but possessiveness: that jealousy, and the habit of treating another person as a possession, was what poisoned relationships, and that the old code trained people into exactly that habit.

My question is what he would make of something that only came up long after his death: the rise of AI companions — the apps where people conduct ongoing "relationships" with chatbots that remember them, compliment them, and never quarrel — alongside the wider retreat of young people from dating altogether, the rise of parasocial one-way attachments to streamers, and what my niece calls "situationships" that never crystallise into anything. I can see the argument cutting both ways, and I can't work out which side his own principles would land on. On the one hand, he'd presumably say that if nobody is harmed, the taboo against it is just superstition wearing a new coat. On the other hand, his whole case for freer relations between the sexes seemed to assume that the point of intimacy is building a life with an independent person, through friction and compromise — and a companion who is designed to agree with you, who has no needs of her own, looks suspiciously like the possessive relationship he spent his life attacking, except with the possession perfected.

Please take on the role of a historian of twentieth-century philosophy with a working knowledge of Russell's ethical writings, and think through this in stages rather than rushing to a verdict. Start with a short brainstorm setting out the principles he actually held, as far as they're documented — his consequentialist test for sexual conduct, his worries about children needing a stable and affectionate home, his view that jealousy is trained rather than natural, and whatever else seems genuinely relevant — and be honest about where the record is thin. Then reason from those principles to the situations I described, flagging clearly at each step which claims are extrapolation rather than things he actually said. I'd especially like you to address whether he would distinguish between a lonely person who is genuinely consoled by a companion app and a person who uses one to avoid ever risking a real relationship — and whether he might treat the second case the way he treated conventional marriage: as an arrangement that feels safe precisely because it forecloses growth. Finally, tell me where you think the extrapolation is most likely to mislead us, since I'm aware that putting words in a dead man's mouth is a judgement exercise, not a fact I can look up.
