I'm helping edit the annual spoof issue of a graduate philosophy journal, and this year's theme is "moral epistemology gone wrong." I'd like a satirical news article in the style of The Onion: a headline plus roughly 400–500 words reporting that a local man has been frozen at an online charity's donation page for three years because his expected-value calculation keeps spawning further expected-value calculations. The underlying target is the problem of consequentialist cluelessness — the worry that our actions have indefinitely far-reaching and intertwined effects, so that even the sign of the aggregate consequences of donating (or of anything else) may be beyond our knowledge.

Crucially, the humor should come from getting the philosophy right, so that someone who works on this literature laughs rather than winces. In particular: the distinction between simple cluelessness (many unknown effects that roughly cancel, so uncertainty doesn't change what to do) and complex cluelessness (systematic reasons to think effects point in specific directions we can't determine, so that even expected value is undefined rather than just hard to compute); the way each first-order estimate drags in second-order uncertainty about the reliability of the first-order estimate, and so on without a natural stopping point; the gap between what's objectively right, given the actual consequences, and what's subjectively right, given what he could reasonably believe; the fact that closing the laptop is itself an action with ripple effects, so exit is not an option; and, for flavor, moral uncertainty stacking on top of the empirical kind. He should not be portrayed as stupid or as bad at math — the joke is that the recursion is doing exactly what a conscientious expected-value reasoner would do, not that he misunderstands it. Somewhere include a quote from a "concerned" neighbor, a deontologist who has finished his tax returns and walked his dog every day of the three years without incident.

Please keep the satire fair: it should not reduce cluelessness to "the future is hard to predict" or imply the man just needs better software, and it should end in a way that leaves the reader genuinely unsure whether his paralysis is a failure of consequentialist reasoning or its most honest expression — perhaps he finally lets a coin flip pick the charity and immediately opens a new tab to compute the counterfactual value of the coin flip. Deadpan Onion house style throughout: short declarative sentences, matter-of-fact attribution of absurd quotes to invented officials, and a closing line that lands the reductio without editorializing.
