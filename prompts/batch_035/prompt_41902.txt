So my roommate is deep into the longtermist stuff, and his whole position is basically: tiny chance we make it, astronomical number of future people, so expected value says we should pour everything into making sure civilization makes it, even at big present-day cost. Whenever I try to push back I end up reaching for the simulation argument, and lately I've been putting it to him as an analogy and I want to know if it actually holds up.

My move goes like this: if Bostrom-style reasoning is right and we're probably living in an ancestor simulation, then the "far future" he wants to protect isn't real in the relevant sense. It's just whatever the simulators decide to render before they lose interest. A simulated civilization doing AI safety research and stockpiling resources for the year 500,000 AD is like an actor who refuses to break character after the play ends, or like an NPC hoarding potions for a retirement the game engine will never simulate. It's all props. So his expected value math is about loot in a game nobody's going to keep playing, and the fanaticism about it looks a bit insane.

He has two comebacks and I don't know how good they are. First, he says the move proves too much: if being simulated makes future people worth less, it makes *present* people worth less too, since we're equally simulated, and I apparently just committed myself to nihilism about everyone. Second, he says I've got the argument backwards: the simulation argument's whole setup is that mature civilizations run tons of ancestor sims, so if we're simulated that's *evidence* somebody made it to maturity, which is kind of his side's dream scenario, not a refutation of it.

So audit the analogy for me. What exactly maps onto what — who's the player, what's the save file, where's the rendering — and which parts are doing real work vs decoration. Where does it break? I suspect it smuggles in an assumption that what happens inside a simulation doesn't matter morally, and that that's precisely the thing that needed arguing rather than assuming. Then tell me: once the disanalogies are on the table, does my anti-fanaticism conclusion survive at all, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? And if the analogy is junk, is there a better one that gets at whatever's genuinely concerning about betting everything on simulated far futures, or does the simulation argument just not touch the fanaticism question either way? Be honest, I've been using this line at dinner parties and I'd rather find out now.
