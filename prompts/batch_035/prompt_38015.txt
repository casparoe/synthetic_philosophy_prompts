I've spent years in the rationalist blogosphere, so the moral vocabulary I actually reach for is decision-theoretic: agents with preference orderings, utility functions to be aggregated, revealed preferences, explicit tradeoffs — the general project of making ethics into a well-specified optimization problem, with impartiality as the demand that the optimizer not privilege its own index. Lately I've been trying to work through the care ethics literature: Gilligan's argument that Kohlberg's stage sequence treats one moral voice as the whole of maturity and scores the other voice as a developmental failure; Noddings' analysis of caring in terms of engrossment and motivational displacement, where the one-caring receives the other and the cared-for completes the relation by responding; the broader care-ethical complaint that contractarian and ideal-observer frameworks presuppose fully individuated, mutually independent agents bargaining or legislating from nowhere. As I read, I keep reaching for my usual tools and suspecting they're quietly begging the question against these authors before any argument starts.

So here is the task. Restate care ethics in my vocabulary, as faithfully as the translation allows, piece by piece: partiality toward particular others as agent-relative weights in a utility function; the caring relation as a two-agent interaction with a specified payoff and information structure; the critique of impartiality as a claim about which policy an agent ought to run, compared against which alternatives; the different-voice thesis as an empirical hypothesis about the distribution of moral heuristics across agents, testable in principle. Where my vocabulary has no direct expression, say so rather than fudging — I would rather have an honest hole than a smuggled assumption dressed as a translation.

Then give me the residue report, which is what I actually care about. Sort the care-ethical claims into three bins: translates cleanly, translates only with distortion, no expression at all. And engage a specific suspicion of mine: it seems to me that the deepest care-ethical objection is that preference elicitation is the wrong instrument — that by the time a person's needs surface as preferences in a utility function, the carer's attentiveness has already done the moral work, and the decision-theoretic framework takes that work as given, free of charge. If that is right, my vocabulary is not neutral ground, and translating care ethics into it is like measuring a theory of measurement error with a ruler that has the error. But it may run the other way: perhaps the residue consists of gestures at a real phenomenon — constitutive relationality, or motive that is neither rule-following nor outcome-optimizing — that could in principle be made precise and simply never has been. Do not lay out both readings and leave the choice to me. Commit to one: tell me which bin the residue belongs in and why, flag explicitly every point where your translation does violence, and state plainly whether the honest conclusion is that my vocabulary is missing something, that care ethics is less clear than it looks, or both.
