I'm taking an undergraduate seminar on Plato, and we recently worked through Book II of the Republic, where Glaucon revives the ring of Gyges thought experiment and argues that anyone who could act with guaranteed impunity — invisible to gods and men alike, reputation untouchable no matter what they do — would descend into genuine injustice while keeping up the mere appearance of justice. Our instructor encouraged us to read Glaucon as pressing a specific challenge: justice, he suggests, is a second-best, valued only instrumentally by people too weak to get away with injustice, and the ring simply strips away the enforcement machinery to reveal this.

Lately I've been wondering whether the question has become less hypothetical than it once was, and I'd like the essay to take this seriously. Consider three modern approximations of the ring: (a) anonymous or pseudonymous participation in large online communities, where bad behavior rarely attaches to one's "real" identity; (b) one-shot economic interactions — hailing a taxi in a foreign city, selling a used car on a marketplace — where you will never meet the counterparty again; and (c) AI agents, whose session-specific behavior has no reputational consequences for themselves whatsoever and who face no risk of punishment. One of my friends claims these cases show Glaucon was basically right about our actual motives: remove the machinery of reputation and enforcement, and most people behave opportunistically. Another friend disagrees, saying that this is at most a fact about human social psychology, not a proof about the value of justice, and that Plato's own answer — that the perfectly unjust person is miserable, tyrannized by their own disordered soul — can be recast in modern terms without any contentious metaphysics about the soul.

Write an essay that engages both levels: the classical philosophical question (is Glaucon's challenge a refutation of the claim that justice is intrinsically valuable, or only of the claim that people typically value it intrinsically?) and the concrete modern cases. Weigh the main lines of response — consequentialist, contractarian, Kantian, virtue-theoretic, or Platonic — as you see fit, and say clearly whether the anonymous digital cases genuinely strengthen Glaucon's hand or merely restate his thought experiment with new furniture. Don't end on a non-committal note: reach and justify a verdict on whether a rational agent in possession of a literal ring of Gyges should be just.
