For most of my career I was an experimental physicist — accelerator beamlines, timing electronics, the daily discipline of checking which quantities in an equation are invariant and which merely frame-dependent — and since retiring I have been reading the metaphysics of persistence with the stubborn habits of that training. Endurantism and perdurantism strike me as the two live options, but the stock examples (the statue and the clay, the ship, Descartes' and Locke's socks) do nothing to separate them, because they are all posed in a single frame of reference. I have worked up a case of my own that does not have this defect, and I would like you to dramatize the dispute over it.

Write a dialogue between an endurantist and a perdurantist, both first-rate and both arguing in good faith, in which they must adjudicate the following scenario. A metal cylinder drifts through a laboratory at four-fifths the speed of light. At fixed positions in the lab stand two devices: as the cylinder passes, the first swaps the atoms of its leading third for fresh atoms, and the second does the same to its trailing third, the two replacement events being spacelike separated. In the lab frame the replacements are simultaneous — there is a moment at which the cylinder is two-thirds new matter and then a moment at which it is wholly new. In the cylinder's own rest frame the two replacements happen at different times — for an interval the cylinder is part old and part new, and there is no moment at which it is wholly new. Have the two of them tell me whether the cylinder survives, whether its survival or the manner of it can consistently come out differently in the two frames, and what exactly is being counted when we say the same cylinder persists across the events. The perdurantist should press the point that the four-dimensional object is invariant and only the slicing into stages varies with the frame; the endurantist should reply that frame-relative persistence facts may be no worse than frame-relative lengths or simultaneity itself, and should press back on whether momentary stages deserve ontological status or are merely an artifact of choosing a foliation. A third character, a retired physicist broadly like me, should intervene at least once to keep the physics honest and to demand precision when either disputant gestures at "the" moment of replacement. Require genuine back-and-forth — each must engage the other's strongest point, not the nearest convenient one, and each should concede what ought to be conceded — and forbid them from retreating into the stock examples. At the end, each should name the specific consideration that would move them to the other side, and the physicist should say which of them he came away favoring, with his reasons. I care less about who wins than about whether the frame-relativity of composition-over-time is a real asymmetry between the two views or a burden they can share; please make the dialogue honest about that.
