I've been a regular on the rationalist blogs and forums since the late-2000s Sequences days, and I've followed the long argument about Newcomblike problems through timeless decision theory and its successor, functional decision theory (FDT), which Eliezer Yudkowsky and Nate Soares developed at MIRI. The core idea, as I understand it, is that when choosing you shouldn't ask which physical act of mine causally produces the best outcome (the causal decision theorist's question) or which act of mine would be the best news to learn I took (the evidential decision theorist's question), but rather which output of this very decision procedure produces the best outcome, since other agents and predictors may be computing the same procedure. FDT famously prescribes one-boxing in Newcomb's problem, cooperating with a perfect psychological twin in the prisoner's dilemma, and paying Parfit's hitchhiker after being dropped off. What I want is an argument for a specific position: that this framework, as standardly presented, gets the pair of cases known as counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail wrong in opposite directions — that the correct verdicts are paying in the mugging and not paying in the blackmail — and that this reversal exposes a real flaw in FDT's treatment of what its advocates call subjunctive dependence.

Since I'm going to lean on the setups, let me state them as I'll use them. In counterfactual mugging, Omega, a predictor with a perfect track record, flipped a fair coin yesterday. If it came up heads, Omega predicted how you would respond today if asked, and if that prediction was that you would hand over $100, it left you an envelope containing $10,000; if the prediction was that you wouldn't pay, no envelope. If the coin came up tails, no envelope was left and Omega shows up today, explains all of this, and asks you for $100 anyway. You receive the request; there is no envelope; so you know the coin came up tails and you know that whether you pay now has no effect on the past. The purely causal evaluation says: you lose $100 and gain nothing. But if you're the sort of agent whose decision procedure pays in the tails branch, then in the heads branch Omega's prediction of you said you'd pay, and your counterpart walked away with $10,000. Averaged over the two branches, the policy of paying is worth roughly $4,950 per coin flip against $0 for the policy of not paying, so an FDT-style agent pays. In XOR blackmail, you hear an unverifiable rumor that your house has a termite infestation that would cost $1,000,000 to fix. Omega, the same perfect predictor, prepared a letter stating that it knows whether you have termites and that it sent you this letter if and only if exactly one of the following is true: the rumor is false and, upon receiving the letter, you will pay Omega $1,000; or the rumor is true and you will not pay upon receiving it. Omega then predicted your response and sent the letter exactly when one of those two conditions obtained, so the letter's content is guaranteed true. You receive the letter. Here the causal reasoning is clear: if you pay, you lose $1,000 and, by the letter's own truth condition, your house has no termites and you've bought nothing; if you don't pay, you lose nothing and, by the same condition, you either have no termites and no cost, or termites that cost $1,000,000 to fix regardless of what you do now. Either way your current action is causally inert with respect to the infestation. The FDT recommendation, as the framework is usually applied, is not to pay: an agent whose decision procedure refuses such letters never receives them in the first place when the rumor is false, so refusing functions like a precommitment that spares you $1,000, and the refusal is supposed to be licensed by the same reasoning that yields one-boxing.

So here is the position I want you to argue, in a polished and fairly formal treatment: the two cases should come out the opposite way around from the FDT folklore. In counterfactual mugging, the agent should not pay — or at least the standard argument for paying is much weaker than its proponents suggest, because it quietly converts an ex ante policy comparison into a verdict about a single decision while giving the agent no way to distinguish the branch where the policy's value is actually realized from the branch where it is sunk; precommitment has value only before the coin is flipped, and by the time you're asked, you possess full information about which branch you're in, so that by FDT's own lights the subjunctive dependence between your current procedure and Omega's past model is doing all the work while the branch-specific evidence is doing none, which is an unstable basis for the prescription. In XOR blackmail, by contrast, the agent should pay — or at least the standard argument for refusal fails, because the argument that refusal protects you from receiving the letter collapses once you notice that receiving the letter is correlated with the rumor's being true rather than false only via Omega's prediction of your response, and a policy of paying is what makes the letter's arrival informative that your house is termite-free. Spend some care on what exactly breaks the symmetry between the cases: is it that in the mugging the predictor's model and your decision are linked through a shared procedure in both branches, while in the blackmail the dependence is conditional on facts (the termites) whose value is fixed independently of anything you or Omega compute? Or is it something about the direction of the informational asymmetry? Please be candid about the strongest FDT rejoinders — the precommitment-on-the-fly framing and the insistence that rational agents are evaluated by the lifetime performance of their decision procedures — and either rebut them or concede honestly where the FDT verdicts remain defensible. I care less about which side ultimately wins than about seeing the disagreement stated with the precision these discussions usually lack.
