I want you to build me a thought experiment where the "one thought too many" intuition runs in reverse, and I want you to make it work.

Quick recap of the background so we're on the same page: Williams's famous example is the guy who can save one of two drowning people, one of whom is his wife, and he saves her — but the wrinkle is that Williams thinks the man does fine acting straight from his attachment, whereas the man who first pauses to reflect that it's permissible (or maximally good, or whatever) to prefer his wife has, as the saying goes, one thought too many. Deliberating there seems to somehow corrupt or at least cheapen the act. Fine. Everyone has heard this a hundred times, and the usual lesson people draw is: acting directly from love/loyalty is good, inserting agent-neutral deliberation in the moment is bad.

Now flip it. Construct a realistic scenario — everyday life, a workplace, a family thing, a hospital, nothing sci-fi — where the agent who acts *directly* from the attachment, no deliberation, zero hesitation, is the one we fault, and where we actually *want* the agent to have the extra thought before acting. Constraints, because these are where cheap versions fail:

1. The attachment itself must be normal and healthy. It can't be a creepy or toxic relationship, and the flip can't come from the relationship being secretly bad. If the only way you can make the direct agent look bad is by making the love itself suspect, you've cheated.
2. The extra thought must be genuinely moral, not prudence or fear of getting caught. Something like the agent registering that her attachment gives her no *right* to an advantage here, or that it would tilt the field unfairly. Self-interested calculation doesn't count.
3. Don't stipulate the verdict. The scenario has to make us feel the flip, not announce it.

Give me the scenario in one paragraph, then the analysis. Specifically: what exactly is the thought we wanted her to have, and why does having that thought *in the moment* seem right here when it seemed so wrong for the drowning-wife guy? What changed?

Then here's the part I actually care about, so don't skip it. Williams (or a Williamsian) could reply to my flipped case as follows: any thought we genuinely *demand* from the agent was, by that very fact, not one thought too many — so his principle never really gets tested, it just labels thoughts we like "needed" and thoughts we don't like "too many," and it comes out looking right either way. That looks suspiciously unfalsifiable. Is the reply any good? Or is there a non-empty version of Williams's complaint that survives my case — maybe the real issue isn't that deliberation happens at all, but that agent-neutral reasoning is allowed to *displace* the attachment as the operative reason, and in my case it merely regulates the attachment from outside? Try to say precisely what the difference is between a thought that operates on the attachment and a thought that replaces it, and whether my case puts the extra thought on the right side of that line.
