I got into an argument with my brother-in-law about whether my cat is smart, and he backed me into a corner with something I can't find my way out of. It goes like this. (1) To act intelligently, you have to pick the option whose consequences you'd prefer. (2) The consequences of any action go on without end — me flipping the light switch changes the room, the electricity meter, the odds the bulb burns out a bit sooner, what the cat stares at, and so on forever. (3) You can't prefer one set of consequences to another if you don't know what they are. (4) So intelligent action requires knowing infinitely many consequences. (5) Nothing finite can know infinitely many things. (6) So no finite thing — my cat, me, anything — ever acts intelligently. Which is obviously wrong, but each premise looked fine to me when I went through them one at a time.

Then he said the fix has to be something like "the agent only tracks what's relevent," and claims that just pushes the problem back a step: how does the agent know what's relevant without checking everything, and checking everything is exactly the infinity we were trying to avoid? A friend of ours who did computer science in the nineties muttered that this is the frame problem in a different coat and that it's been bothering people since the late sixties. I tried looking it up and got lost almost immediately.

So, two things. Which step should I reject, and what am I actually giving up if I do? And is he right that "relevance" just moves the problem around, or is there some way the relevant bits get picked out without the agent having to do the picking itself? If it comes to a choice I'd rather keep (1) than anything else, but I want to know what that costs me.

Please write the answer in British English, and go easy on the jargon — I've never studied philosophy or computer science.
