Each spring I write for a magazine on science and ideas, and each spring I get dragooned into opening the public-debate night at our city's festival of ideas, in front of an audience that mixes curious members of the public, a large contingent of law students, and a scattering of actual legal philosophers. This year's motion is that the state has no business enforcing morality as such, and the two debaters are a retired appellate judge who is locally famous for having once upheld a morals-based ordinance on the grounds that a community's settled moral convictions are a legitimate basis for criminal law, full stop, and a younger philosopher who has built her career on the harm-principle side of the old Hart–Devlin dispute. The judge is charming and quotable; the philosopher is precise and dry; the audience adores them both.

The difficulty is in the format. The festival's rules require that the opening address be genuinely even-handed — I am there to give the audience the conceptual map, not to score points before the debate begins — and the organizers have twice rejected drafts of mine for covert tilt. But I've come to think the task is philosophically harder than it looks, and that's what I'd like your help with. Even the motion's own phrasing, with its morality as such, seems to presuppose a Hartian distinction between enforcing morality for its own sake and enforcing it as a means to preventing harm; describe the other side's position instead as protecting the shared moral fabric on which a society's cohesion depends — the way a language survives only because people keep speaking it — and the tilt runs the other way. So the speech has to do two things at once: give the lay audience a clean, jargon-free account of what legal moralism is and what the disagreement is really about, and simultaneously signal to the sophisticated minority in the room that the speaker knows neutrality of framing is itself a philosophical achievement, without turning the whole thing into a seminar.

There is also a live controversy I want woven in, carefully: our city council is currently weighing a proposal to criminalize the purchase (not the sale) of sexual services, and what makes it awkward for the debate framing is that the proposal's own supporters are split — some defend it purely on harm-reduction grounds, others openly on the ground that commercial sex is a wrong the community need not tolerate, and a few seem not to have noticed there's a difference. That case, where the moralistic and harm-based rationales travel inside a single ordinance, would let the speech show the audience why the abstract question matters without telling them which answer is right. Please draft roughly ten minutes of remarks — call it twelve hundred words — with at least one laugh, no technical term left unglossed, and a closing line that hands the stage to the two debaters without so much as a raised eyebrow in either direction.
