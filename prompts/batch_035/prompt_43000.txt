I teach a comparative philosophy elective, and next week we're doing Zhuangzi on death. I want to give my students a probability question rather than the usual essay prompt, and I want to try my own answer first, so sanity-check me: what's your credence that Zhuangzi (or the Zhuangzi — I know the book is a pile of different hands, which is part of what I want you to weigh) actually believed that death is not bad for the one who dies, as opposed to merely performing equanimity, consoling himself, or baiting conventional mourners like Huizi?

I want the decomposition, not just a bare number. The terms I'd suggest, though feel free to add or drop some: (a) that the famous scenes — drumming on a tub after his wife dies, the dream conversation with the skull, telling a dying friend that whatever made us a body gets to decide what we become next — are offered as assertions with truth-conditions at all, rather than therapeutic theater; (b) that the equanimity depends on the underlying metaphysics (death as the dispersal of a temporary gathering of qi, life and death as phases of one process) rather than being detachable from it; (c) that the skeptical move — nobody has come back to report, so hating death is like a runaway who doesn't know the way home — is meant as an argument that could stand on its own even if the metaphysics is junk; and (d) that the inner chapters and the death-obsessed outer ones (the skull bit is outer chapter 18, I believe) represent one perspective closely enough that it even makes sense to attribute a single view to "Zhuangzi" here.

Give each term a rough probability, one line of justification apiece, then combine them into an overall credence and show me the arithmetic. Then tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to — e.g., whether discovering that the skull episode is a later, more crassly hedonistic addition should drag your number down, or whether it barely matters.

Also, flag where your reasoning is most likely to fail: whatever you say gets repeated to a room of teenagers who will take it as settled fact, so I need the soft spots marked. What would actually change your mind — a philological finding, a better reading of one scene, or what? And don't hedge into mush; commit to the numbers. Keep it under 450 words, it's going in a handout.
