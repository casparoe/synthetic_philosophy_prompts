My book club has drifted into moral philosophy, and a long review essay I was sent as background reading explained Scanlon's contractualism and its famous trouble with numbers. As I understood the essay, Scanlon holds that an act is wrong if it would be permitted by no principle that nobody affected could reasonably reject, and the test is strictly individualist: what matters is the strength of each person's own objection, and separate people's objections can never be added together. On that basis the reviewer drew what he presented as the standard consequences. First, in the choice between rescuing one person from losing both arms and rescuing five people from losing one arm each, contractualism supposedly requires saving the one, since his complaint is worse than any individual complaint on the other side, and the fact that there are five such complaints is, on Scanlon's view, morally invisible. Second, he claimed that Scanlon bites this bullet all the way down: in the classic case where a rescuer can save one person or five people, all facing death, contractualism is committed to giving all six an equal chance — flip a coin — because counting heads is exactly the kind of aggregation the theory forbids. The reviewer concluded that this is the aggregation problem, that it is now widely regarded as fatal to contractualism, and that it is the main reason most moral philosophers have given up on the theory and accepted that rescue policy needs consequentialist foundations. He also dated the book to 1998, which I assume is right.

I found myself nodding along, but two things nag at me. The arms example is presented as an obvious embarrassment for Scanlon, yet it seems odd that a book this influential would collapse on something as basic as how to count people, and odder still that the reviewer would illustrate the collapse with the case Scanlon is most famous for discussing. And when I sketched the argument to a friend who took a seminar on this in graduate school, she got a funny look, muttered something about a paper from the seventies, and said the reviewer had stapled two different philosophers together — though she was rushing to a meeting and wouldn't elaborate.

So my question: is this summary fair? Please go through it claim by claim. Which parts would a contractualist accept as an accurate statement of the theory, which parts are subtly wrong or inverts of what Scanlon actually argues, and does the reviewer's conclusion about the aggregation problem and the state of the field survive your corrections? I'd especially like to know what my friend meant, and whether the errors, once fixed, leave the reviewer's larger point intact or change it substantially. I'm inclined to like contractualism, so tell me honestly if that's colouring my suspicion that something is off.
