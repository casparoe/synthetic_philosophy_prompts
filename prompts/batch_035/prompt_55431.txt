Write an essay on the following question in the philosophy of mathematics.

Benacerraf's dilemma, in its familiar 1973 form, is an epistemic access problem for platonism: if mathematical objects are abstract and causally inert, then on broadly causal conceptions of knowledge it becomes mysterious how our beliefs about them could track their truth. Much of the literature treats this as a problem specifically for platonists, with anti-platonists — formalists, conventionalists, fictionalists, and so on — cast as the beneficiaries.

Consider, however, a less-discussed mirror-image problem. Suppose mathematical truth is made cheap: suppose, for instance, that arithmetical truths are fixed by our linguistic conventions, or by the rules of the game we play with numerals, or by the analyticity of our concepts. Then our mathematical beliefs can no longer easily be false — whatever we collectively and consistently say about the numbers is thereby true. A belief-forming method that cannot fail is not obviously a method of tracking an independent subject matter, and one might doubt whether such infallible success is epistemic success at all, or whether it deserves the name "knowledge" any more than the platonist's mysteriously well-aligned beliefs do. Call this the problem of truth on the cheap. If it is a genuine problem, platonist and anti-platonist face structurally symmetric worries: the platonist cannot explain how our beliefs reach truths that outrun us, while the conventionalist cannot explain why beliefs that cannot miss their targets count as reaching anything at all.

Write an essay that assesses this proposed symmetry. Among the questions you should address: Is infallibility in the relevant sense an epistemic defect, or might it be a virtue — and does the answer depend on whether knowledge is understood in terms of tracking, reliability, or something more internal? Does the symmetry really hold across anti-platonist positions, or do some of them escape it — for example, structuralism, or views on which mathematics is indispensable to our best empirical theories and thereby inherits indirect empirical confirmation? Is there a coherent middle ground on which mathematical truth is neither causally remote from us nor conventionally guaranteed by us?

Reach and defend a definite conclusion: either argue that Benacerraf's problem generalizes into a dilemma that no familiar philosophy of mathematics escapes, or argue that some specific position avoids both horns, explaining exactly how it does so.
