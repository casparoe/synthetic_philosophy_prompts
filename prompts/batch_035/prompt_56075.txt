So I'm sketching out a paper on pain asymbolia and the badness of pain, and I'd love a second pair of eyes on the argument before I commit to writing the whole thing. Quick setup: pain asymbolics are patients who report that they feel pain but say they don't mind it — it doesn't feel unpleasant or threatening to them, and they don't recoil from it. The usual story is a lesion in the insular cortex somewhere. The standard use of these cases is as a natural experiment that separates the sensation of pain from pain's badness: the sensory component is intact, the "this is awful" component is missing.

My twist is that I want to insist this cuts both ways, and I don't think the literature is always honest about that. On the one hand, it seems to count against a crude hedonism: if the raw feel can be present while the badness is absent, maybe what's intrinsically bad isn't the sensation itself but something like the felt aversiveness, the "get this out of me" component, which asymbolics just lack. On the other hand, a hedonist has an easy-looking reply: the painfulness of pain just IS that aversive component, so asymbolics don't feel pain in the full sense at all — they feel something merely pain-like. And its at this point that I try my main move: I claim that reply is a definitional stop, not an argument, and that the right lesson is that pain is a cluster concept — intensity, location, quality, motivational pull, badness are strands that normally travel together but can in principle come apart, and the asymbolic case shows they can come apart in fact. Then my proposed payoff: the badness of pain is real but relational — it's the sensation playing a certain role in a threat-monitoring system — not an intrinsic feature of the quale itself.

Here's what I'd like comments on, roughly in order:

1. Is my "cuts both ways" framing fair to the hedonist, or am I building a strawman? Are there hedonist replies to the asymbolia literature that I'm just ignoring that would do better than the definitional stop?
2. Does the cluster concept move actually earn the relational conclusion, or am I sliding from "the strands can come apart" to "badness is relational" without an argument in between? I have a sneaking suspicion there's a gap there.
3. Am I leaning on empirical claims about asymbolia (the insular story, how clean the dissociation really is) that I should hedge a lot more than I currently do in the sketch?
4. Brutal honesty welcome: is the payoff interesting enough to hang a paper on, or is it kind of an "ok, and?" result? I've stared at this too long to tell anymore.

Also totally fine to poke at the setup itself — e.g., if you think the cases don't support even the weaker "strands come apart" claim, say so. Better I find out now than from a referee.
