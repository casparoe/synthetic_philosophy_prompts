hey, so I'm a novelist and I've gotten myself into something. my latest book is about two sisters in a shrinking prairie town, and one of them covers up a hit-and-run for the other. I wrote it mostly on instinct, but every review calls it "a novel about shame," and at festivals people keep asking me whether shame is just guilt with an audience, or whether one of them is the "better" emotion, and I've been nodding along like I know. I clearly don't, at least not in a way I can say out loud without rambling.

now a philosophy department at a university that shall remain nameless (they liked the book, God bless them) has invited me to run one session of their second-year moral psychology seminar. fifty minutes, maybe fifteen students, no philosophy background to speak of beyond whatever they picked up in intro. the professor told me the theme of the week is guilt versus shame — which is the better moral emotion, and whether a whole culture can actually choose between them — and said "do whatever you like, it'll be good for them to hear from someone who thinks in characters."

fine. but I think in characters, not in lesson plans, and I refuse to show up and just read passages at them for fifty minutes like every other novelist they've probably inflicted on them. so what I need from you is a plan for the session, not an explanation of the topic — I need the explanation planned, if that makes sense.

specifically: what has to come first and what depends on what. I assume the whole "shame is about being bad, guilt is about doing bad" thing has to land before anything else, but I don't actually know if that framing is even the right first move, or whether it's a cliché that will cause problems later. then there's the question of which example should carry the whole session — I'd love to use my own two sisters, one of whom collapses into "I am a monster" and one of whom quietly works to undo what she did, but tell me honestly if a cleaner example would do better, and what the example needs to have and not have either way.

also: what misconceptions should I expect from a room of twenty-year-olds? my guesses are (a) that shame is just what you feel when other people are watching and guilt is private, (b) that guilt is the modern civilized emotion and shame belongs to smaller, older, more traditional societies, and (c) that the obvious answer is "shame bad, guilt good" and the session is just confirming that. tell me where in the sequence I should head each of those off, and any others I'm missing. the "can a culture choose" part worries me most — I can feel the room sliding into "so we should all just raise our kids on guilt then," and I want to know what has to be in place before that question is even askable, so it doesn't collapse into a self-help conclusion.

one more thing: is there a single question I could put to them at the end — some little scenario or judgment call — that would show me whether they actually got the distinction and its complications, rather than just that I made them feel something? I'm very good at the second thing and I need the first thing. and please tell me what I should openly refuse to get into at this level: I can already tell there's deep water here (anthropology of whole societies, whether shame can ever be healthy, stuff about honor) and I'd rather say "that's beyond what we can do today" than fake my way through it. the session is in three weeks and I'd like to stop dreading it.
