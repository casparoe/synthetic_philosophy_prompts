I've been turning something over and can't quite get it straight, so I want to know what's going on underneath it.

There's a consistent pattern in how ordinary people (and politicians, and tbh me too) talk about different ways of making money. A plumber, a baker, a nurse "earns" their money in the fullest sense — nobody hesitates. A shopkeeper who buys wholesale, marks it up, and sells retail is basically fine too. But a currency speculator, a house flipper, someone running pure arbitrage, a landlord who just collects rent — these get spoken about as if the money is somehow less deserved: they don't really make anything, they just move money around, or let money make money. And it's not about amounts — a modest-living day trader gets alot of side-eye while a rich surgeon mostly doesn't. Nor is it simply "middleman = bad", because the shopkeeper gets a pass while the landlord collecting rent doesn't (or does he? feels like it varies by era and by how hard he's squeezing people).

So my question: what theory of money and exchange does this pattern of judgment presuppose? My hunch is it needs some view of what money is FOR — money as a means for getting what you need, with something going wrong when an activity makes the pile itself the end. I half remember the ancient Greeks drawing a line like this — some old idea that one kind of wealth-getting was natural because it served the household, and another kind, aimed at unlimited money-making, was somehow against the nature of the thing, especially when it involved making money breed more money. Does the modern pattern actually presuppose that whole theory, or only a fragment of it — maybe just the weaker thought that real contribution means producing goods or services, and trading is zero-sum shuffling? And where exactly are the edges: in the shopkeeper-vs-landlord case, what's doing the work — being a middleman, extracting payment without producing anything, or how passive the income is? Reconstruct the implicit theory for me, say which rival theories would license the exact same judgments (so the pattern doesn't pin down just one), and tell me where the pattern is just inconsistent and can't be saved by any single theory. Bonus question if you have thoughts: does modern tax treatment of work income vs. capital gains track this theory at all, or is it just unrelated politics?
