Writing an essay for a module on the history of animal ethics, and I want to try something a bit unusual: read Anna Sewell's Black Beauty (1877) against Kant's doctrine of indirect duties to animals. As I understand him, Kant holds that we owe animals nothing directly — they're not persons, so they can't wrong us and we can't, strictly speaking, wrong them — but cruelty to animals is still wrong because it damages the disposition we need for our duties to humanity: the person who is hard on a horse becomes hard on people too, and he runs vivisectionists through the same argument in the Lectures on Ethics. So the animal's moral standing is entirely derivative; its suffering counts only as practice for ours.

Now, Black Beauty is almost a designed experiment for the psychological half of that claim. The novel is obsessed with how the treatment of horses and the character of men track each other: the drunken groom who rides Beauty into ruin, the London cab trade grinding down Jerry Barker and the horses in the same mill, Ginger brutalised into meanness and then written off by everyone as a born biter. But the causal arrow seems to run the opposite way in the book, atleast thats my reading — humans make the horses what they are, not the other way around, which is not the transfer Kant needs. And theres a further wrinkle: in Sewell's world kindness to animals is economically punished (Jerry's decency costs him), which the Kantian story says nothing about.

Then answer me straight: does Sewell end up vindicating Kant's position, or does the novel quietly assume exactly what Kant denies — that the horse's suffering matters in itself, whoever's character it builds or damages? And is there anything the novel gives the debate that the Kantian literature itself doesnt — for instance the economic structure of cruelty, which arguments about dispositions never touch?
