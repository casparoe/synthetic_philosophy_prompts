In a seminar on institutional reform, a colleague defended a strong conservatism about inherited arrangements by way of an architectural metaphor. Chesterton's fence, he said, is really a load-bearing wall: no one can tell from the outside which parts of the social structure are structural, the ceiling above us — civil order — is held up by conventions whose function nobody remembers, and the persistence of an old institution is itself evidence that it is bearing weight somewhere. Removing any of it without an engineer's survey risks collapse; therefore the burden of proof lies squarely on the reformer, and preservation is the default.

I'd like a serious audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on conservatism itself. What corresponds to what — the wall, the load, the invisible connection between wall and ceiling, the engineer, the blueprint, the collapse — and how far does each mapping actually hold? Where I want you to press hardest is this: buildings are designed, so their structural members have knowable, intended functions, their loads are static, and their failure is a single catastrophic event; institutions, by contrast, have no designer, change while standing, can fail slowly and adaptively, and can be partially weight-bearing in one region and decorative in another. Please separate the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion my colleague wants from the ones that don't — some differences between buildings and societies may be irrelevant to how caution about demolition should be priced.

Then take up two complications. First, the standard counterexample: some fences were built for reasons we now know were bad. A wall built to keep enslaved people from leaving bore real weight in the plantation economy — if the survey comes back and certifies the wall as load-bearing, does Chesterton's own principle (understand the use before removing it) tell us to keep it, or does the analogy quietly import an assumption that whatever is load-bearing is therefore worth preserving? Second, the function problem: persistence can result from drift rather than selection, and evolutionary biologists distinguish genuine adaptations from byproducts of other traits; a fence can remain standing simply because no one ever bothered to tear it down. If the persistence-as-evidence-of-function inference fails for a whole class of institutions, what does that do to the burden-of-proof claim?

Finally, tell me whether the engineer-who-surveys-first reading of the fence supports the strong default my colleague wants or only something weaker — a duty of investigation rather than a duty of preservation — and whether there is a better analogy available that carries the same caution without the imported baggage.
