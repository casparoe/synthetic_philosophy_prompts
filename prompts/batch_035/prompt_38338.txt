My roommate and her sister got into a really long argument at dinner about whether dreams are experiences at all, and eventually they asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't. Can you go through their exchange and tell me who's right at each step?

Some background: the sister (Maya) had been reading some old philosophy paper claiming dreams aren't experiences. The idea, as I understood her version of it, is that "I dreamt I was flying" reports an impression you have upon waking — the way the dream feels long and storylike is just how that waking impression presents itself. There's no independent way to check what, if anything, was going on in your head while you slept, so the standard picture of a running show you watch all night is unsupported, and the "experience during sleep" talk is just confused.

Jon (my roommate) thought this was obviously wrong: you're conscious during dreams — you remember whole narratives with events happening one after another, you can be terrified mid-dream, etc. Also, everyone has had the alarm-clock thing, where the dream seems to build the alarm sound into its own plot — snowball fight that ends with an avalanche right as the alarm goes off. That feels like evidence of real-time experience.

Maya said the alarm case actually cuts the other way: the alarm only starts a second before you wake, yet the dream seems to have a whole plot leading up to it. If the dream had really been running in real time, it couldn't have known to build toward the alarm. Much more likely, she said, the whole "narrative" gets composed in the instant of waking, out of whatever's happening right then (the alarm sound) plus whatever's in your head — and the feeling that the dream ran for minutes is itself part of what gets composed. So the seeming length of the dream proves nothing.

Jon conceded that some dreams probably get their ending written at the moment of waking, but said that doesn't show the entire dream is written then.

Maya said fine, but how could you ever tell the difference between a dream that really ran in real time and one that was fully composed at wakeup? Every scrap of evidence about dream content comes from the report you give after you're awake. So the real-time picture is unfalsifiable, and she definately wins by default.

Here Jon brought up something I'd never heard of: lucid dream signaling. Apparently experimenters figured out back in the late 70s that some people can realize they're dreaming while still in the dream, and can then move their eyes in prearranged patterns (look left-left-right, say) to signal the lab that they're lucid. The eye movements show up on the recording during REM, in real time, before the dreamer wakes up. So there is a way to peek at a dream while it's happening — or at least at some dreams.

Maya was unimpressed, for two reasons. First, lucid dreams are a special case — maybe lucid dreamers are partly awake, their brains more aroused than in ordinary sleep, so it doesn't follow that ordinary non-lucid dreams are experiences. Second, even granting the signaling, it shows at most that those particular dreams are experiences; the ordinary ones could still be composed at wake, and for those her unfalsifiability point still stands.

Jon's reply: if Maya now admits some dreams are real-time experiences, then "dreams aren't experiences" as she originally stated it is just false. What's left is a conjecture about some subset of dreams that she can't verify either — so the unfalsifiability sword cuts against her too.

They ended with Maya saying Jon had at most shown lucid dreams are experiences, not dreams in general, and Jon saying she'd moved the goalposts. Please adjudicate step by step: who's right about what, where (if anywhere) does each of them go wrong, and is there anything both are missing? I'm especially curious whether Maya's "composed at the moment of waking" idea and her "no independent check" idea are actually the same idea, or whether one could be true and the other false.
