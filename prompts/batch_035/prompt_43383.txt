I am teaching an introductory philosophy of mind course, and we are about to reach the knowledge argument. Rather than relying on the papers alone, I plan to screen Gary Ross's Pleasantville (1998) and let it carry some of the argumentative weight, but I want the reading worked out in advance so that I know whether the film genuinely clarifies Jackson or quietly substitutes a different thesis for his.

The obvious mapping first: the town is Mary's black-and-white room scaled up to a civilization. Nothing unplanned can happen there — fire cannot be started, the fire department has only ever rescued cats from trees, every road loops back into town, the library's books are blank until David and Jennifer supply their contents. Then experience arrives from outside, and color arrives with it. So far, so Jacksonian.

The disanalogies are where I need help. In the film, color is never triggered by mere perceptual exposure; it arrives with the acquisition of capacities and concerns — sexual passion, a love of literature, existential unease at the repetitiveness of one's own routine, courage in defense of another, and, in the mayor's case, rage and shame. That reads less like gaining a new piece of knowledge-that and more like what the ability hypothesis of Lewis and Nemirow describes: knowing-what-it-is-like as a cluster of abilities, recognitional and otherwise. And the film goes further than the debate ever does: the epistemic gain is immediately registered as a social identity, since the residents who have gained color are segregated, banned from public venues, and eventually put on trial. Jackson never claimed that Mary acquires moral standing when she leaves the room. Is the film thereby running a third thesis the literature lacks — that phenomenal knowledge functions socially as a credential — and is that an insight or a confusion?

Accordingly, I would like a reading that provides: a scene-by-scene account of which moments best dramatize each position (Betty Parker's bathtub scene and the first fire; Bill Johnson's crisis; David remaining black and white even after the romance he understands best; Big Bob's colorization through anger, which is surely a problem for any reading on which color tracks virtue); a verdict on whether the film as a whole vindicates the pre-recantation Jackson, the ability theorists, or neither; and an honest statement of where the film resists the reading — above all the worry that it conflates lacking experience of certain properties with lacking phenomenal consciousness altogether, which is precisely not Mary's situation, since Mary is a master of the physical facts while the townspeople cannot yet conceive of the questions. I would also like your view on whether the film dramatizes something the papers cannot: students who cannot feel the intuition that Mary learns something seem to grasp it instantly once they have watched the town change, and I want to know whether that pedagogical effect depends on the film being philosophically sloppy.
