I run a third-year seminar on animal communication and signalling theory — 16 students, mostly philosophy and biology mixed, and we get a 90-minute slot. We've just finished the Zahavi/Grafen saga around the handicap principle, and the students can recite the formal result fine — costly signals stay honest because faking them isn't worth it — but I don't think they feel the moving parts, specially the bit almost everyone gets wrong: that it's not costliness as such that keeps a signal honest, it's the cost falling harder on the low-quality signallers. An expensive signal that burdens everyone equally just burns resources and tells you nothing. I want an in-class game where they discover that distinction the hard way, before I name it.

Design the activity for me. Roughly what I have in mind: some students are senders with a hidden quality (dealt by card, maybe high or low), who choose whether to pay a cost to signal; the others are receivers who have to decide whether to trust, with payoffs arranged so that mimicking is tempting. I'd like it to run in phases — a first phase where signalling is cheap and the whole thing should collapse into everyone signalling and nobody believing anyone, then a second phase where the cost structure is tweaked so honesty re-emerges — so they can see exactly which lever did the work. Use points or tokens rather than real money. Give me the rules, rough timings for each phase, materials (I've got a whiteboard and can print stuff, no laptops in the room), and a debrief that maps what happened onto the formal machinery — separating vs pooling, the single-crossing intuition, why people thought Zahavi was hopeless until Grafen's models. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I'm guessing receivers just turn cynical and stay cynical, or the senders collude — and be honest about which feature of the real theory the game can't reproduce and I'll simply have to assert in the debrief; I assume something like stability against mutant strategies or the evolutionary timescale.

Act as a game designer with a background in evolutionary biology and economics: sketch a couple of candidate payoff schemes first, sanity-check them for degenerate strategies, and give me the one that actually works rather than your first idea. Think carefully about the numbers before committing — a bad payoff matrix kills these games. Please write in British English, and keep the whole thing under 800 words — tight, I know, so prioritise rules and debrief over fluff.
