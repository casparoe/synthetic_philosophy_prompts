For about fifteen years my closest friend and I have played a running game: she delivers verdicts on invented cases about who is badly off and who is owed what, and I try to reverse-engineer the principle she is applying. She is a retired political philosopher and, on questions of equality, the most consistent person I have ever met; my notes on her rulings fill a small notebook. She is currently on a walking holiday with no signal, and before she returns I have promised to produce, sealed, my prediction of her verdict on a case that has come up in our circle, so I want to get the reasoning right rather than hazard a guess. Her verdicts so far, in brief: a well-paid colleague born with a mobility impairment, who insists she is perfectly content, is nonetheless badly off, and the state is right to fund her accessible housing at public expense; a friend who cultivated a taste for Burgundy on a teacher's salary has no claim to wine money; a woman in a village where girls have never been permitted schooling, who reports wanting nothing she does not have, is badly off and wronged, contentment notwithstanding; a gambler who freely took a known even-money shot at ruin and lost is owed the ordinary safety net and nothing more; and a naturally cheerful person, content with modest things, is not in the relevant sense better off than her grumpy twin on the same budget. The new case is an old classmate of ours with lifelong, treatment-resistant anhedonia: he is healthy, solvent, professionally successful, and free to do nearly anything he might want to do; the condition is constitutional, dating from adolescence, before any choice of his could have produced it; and, having made his peace with it, he judges on reflection that his life is going well. Is he badly off? I have no record of her verdict on anything quite like this.

My difficulty is that at least three principles seem to fit her track record equally well: something in the neighbourhood of equality of welfare, with an authenticity condition to handle adapted preferences and a responsibility condition to handle the gambler; something like equal real freedom to achieve the doings and beings one has reason to value; and something like resource equality, compensating brute bad luck but not the costs of choices. My notebook tells me these come apart on the anhedonia case, but I cannot tell which rule she is actually running. Please help me. First, state the candidate rules precisely enough to generate predictions, including any serious candidate I have missed; second, say what each rule predicts about the anhedonia case and why; third, commit — give your single best guess at her verdict, with your reasons for preferring that rule over its rivals given her track record, rather than laying out the options and leaving the choice to me; and fourth, describe one further case, in the spirit of the ones above, on which the two strongest surviving rules would give opposite verdicts, so that I know what to put to her over dinner when she is back.

Please write in British English, and structure your reply under these headings, in this order: Candidate rules (roughly 250 words); What each predicts (roughly 200 words); My best guess, and why (roughly 300 words); The case that would settle it (roughly 150 words).
