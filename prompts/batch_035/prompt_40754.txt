I'm taking an evening course in decision theory, and at the pub afterwards two of my fellow students, Mira and Tom, spent over an hour arguing about Kavka's toxin puzzle and got nowhere. In the version we were given: at midnight tonight, a trustworthy billionaire will pay you one million dollars if, at that moment, you intend to drink a vile toxin tomorrow afternoon; the toxin will make you extremely ill for a day but leave no lasting harm; payment depends only on your intention at midnight, not on whether you actually drink. Mira went first: she said the rational course is obviously to take the deal and form the intention, because rationality is about maximising expected utility, and the intention nets you a million at no required cost — a rational person will simply decline to drink once the money is banked, so she called it 'a free million'. Tom replied that this trades on a false picture of intention: you cannot genuinely intend to do something when you already know, in complete detail, that at the moment of action you will have decisive reason not to do it — whatever Mira has at midnight is at best a fleeting feeling she mistakes for resolve, and the billionaire, who can apparently read intentions perfectly well, will notice the difference.

Mira came back with what she took to be a decisive analogy: people constantly form intentions they know they are likely to abandon — to start the diet on Monday, to quit smoking after the holiday, to stop checking email at breakfast — and we count these as genuine intentions even though everyone, including the intender, predicts failure; so Tom's claim that foreseeing abandonment undermines intention is simply false. Tom said the analogy misses a crucial asymmetry: the dieter intends something there is, and will remain, ongoing reason to do, and merely foresees that temptation will outweigh that reason; in the toxin case, the drinker can see from the armchair that drinking will be all cost and no benefit by her own lights, so the intention is not fragile but incoherent — and, he added, Mira's first move concedes this, since 'a rational person simply won't drink' just is the admission that the intention cannot survive rational scrutiny.

They both kept appealing to me as the group's supposed expert on rational choice, and I honestly could not say who was winning. Please adjudicate. Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: the first four should assess each of the four moves above in turn — Mira's free-million argument, Tom's no-genuine-intention reply, Mira's dieting analogy, and Tom's asymmetry response — and the fifth should give your overall verdict, including anything you think both of them are missing about what the puzzle actually shows.
