I write long-form pieces for a science and ideas magazine, and every few years the doomsday argument resurfaces in my orbit: the Carter–Leslie reasoning that, by treating our birth rank among all humans as a random sample, we should update toward fewer future humans existing; Gott's version derived from the Copernican principle and time intervals; and the family of rejoinders, most prominently the self-indication assumption, according to which the fact that I exist at all is evidence that favors hypotheses on which there are more observers. I've interviewed people on several sides of this, and my honest observation is that the debate has been essentially static for decades: everyone can recite everyone else's argument, new papers mostly recombine the same moves, and smart, technically literate people who agree on all the ordinary probability theory nonetheless end up with opposite conclusions about whether the argument is a profound constraint on humanity's future or a subtle parlor trick. Before I commit to a feature-length piece on it, I want a structural diagnosis rather than yet another tour of the positions.

My own tentative hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that the disagreement is not really about probability at all but about what kind of thing self-locating evidence is: perhaps one side is treating the indexical fact of my being this particular observer as ordinary evidence to be conditioned on, while the other regards the very existence of a sampling process over observers as illegitimate absent a physical mechanism selecting me. If that's right, the dispute may be partly verbal, trading on an unclarity about what an updating agent's total evidence includes. But I can also see a rival diagnosis under which the sides are answering different questions — one about the rational credences of a specific agent with a specific birth rank, another about what the persistence of disagreement tells us about whether anthropic reasoning has determinate content at all — and I genuinely cannot tell these apart from the outside. There is also the reference class problem, which some treat as a fatal flaw and others as a solvable refinement, and I'd like to know whether the stalledness lives there instead.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, really — verbal, substantive-but-unformalized, or a case of two research traditions answering different questions under one heading? Diagnose it using what the main parties actually claim, and be explicit about what evidence from the diagnosis you'd expect to observe. Then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress at this point: a formal result, a decisive thought experiment like a simulated-world analogue, convergence on a way of handling self-indication, or a principled argument that the question is ill-posed and the debate will simply never terminate. Lead with your bottom-line diagnosis in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question. I care more about the structure of the impasse than about which side is right, though if your diagnosis commits you to a verdict, say so.
