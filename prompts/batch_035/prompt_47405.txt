I'm a novelist, and I'm stuck on a scene. The book: a ghost spends the last half hour before noon pressing a light switch on an old desk lamp — first press at 11:30, each next press at half the remaining interval, so all the presses fit before noon. Thomson's lamp, obviously, but I'm the kind of writer who can't write a character I don't understand, so I sat down and wrote out what I actually believe about supertasks, and now I need somebody to check whether my notes hang together, because a philosopher friend read them and just cackled. Here's where I've landed:

(1) Zeno is fully solved by the standard math. Achilles really does complete infinitely many sub-intervals in finite time, and that's not a paradox, that's just what motion IS. So completed infinite tasks aren't merely possible-in-principle, they're happening every time anything moves.
(2) The lamp, though: the ghost finishes all the presses, noon arrives, and I want to write that the lamp's state at noon is genuinely indeterminate — not unknown, not flickering, but unsettled in the world itself. Unlike a coin under a cup, where there's a fact and we just don't have it.
(3) But the book also leans hard on nature being gapless: any closed physical system, at any instant, is in some fully specific total state. The universe doesn't do "undecided."
(4) I accept the standard verdict on the balls-and-urn supertask (drop in balls 1–10, take out ball 1; drop in 11–20, take out ball 2; ever faster, all before noon): the urn is empty at noon. Every individual ball has a removal time, so none survive.
(5) And yet I want (5) [sorry, the urn thing] to be vindicated as suspicious: the ball count grows without bound at every single stage, nothing is removed "at the end," and if you keep going the urn gets ever fuller. The empty verdict feels like it falls out of bookkeeping rather than out of the world.
(6) About the ghost: I think a task counts as completed exactly when every instance of it is done, so the ghost completes the pressing even though there's no final press. But I also want to say nothing counts as "the moment the task got done" except noon itself, and I can't tell if that's deep or just dumb.

(And please ignore engineering objections like "no machine could press that fast" — he's a ghost, the presses happen, that's settled within the fiction.)
