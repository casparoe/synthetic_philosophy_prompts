I'm a science and ideas journalist, and for a few years now I've been loosely following the debate over the extended mind thesis -- the idea, roughly, that cognitive processes can run partly outside your skull, so Otto's notebook full of memories functions the way Inga's biological memory does, and the notebook literally counts as part of Otto's mind. Andy Clark and David Chalmers put this forward back in 1998, and for a while it felt like one of those rare things in philosophy that actually bled out into other fields -- robotics people took it up, education people, the embodied cognition crowd, even some tech writers who wanted to say their smartphone was part of their mind.

But here's my journalist's observation: the actual philosophical core of this debate seems totally frozen. Clark and Chalmers' side keeps refining the parity idea (if an external process works the way an internal one does, why not count it as cognitive?). The critics keep saying the same thing back: that something being causally coupled to your thinking doesn't make it a constituent of your thinking, it just makes it a handy tool -- the coupling-versus-constitution complaint, associated with critics like Fred Adams and Ken Aizawa. Wash, rinse, repeat for twenty-five years. Nobody seems to have changed their mind since roughly 2010. Every so often someone declares the debate resolved, usually in favor of whatever they believed going in, and then it just keeps going.

So my question is a diagnosis, not a verdict. What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it verbal -- two sides meaning different things by "mind" or "cognitive process," so they can both be right about Otto and still talk past each other? Is it a hidden empirical question, say about whether Otto's notebook really does function the way memory functions, that cognitive science could in principle settle? Is it one of those boundary-drawing disputes where the facts are all agreed and the fight is about where to draw a line that has no fact of the matter behind it -- more like arguing about whether Pluto is a planet than arguing about whether Pluto exists? Or is there a real substantive issue about explanation and causal constitution hiding in there that just happens to be hard?

My own hunch, for what it's worth, is that the two sides are actually answering different questions: one side is doing something like a metaphysics of mind (what are the boundaries of the thing?), and the other is doing something more like a methodology for cognitive science (which units should we study?), and the word "extended" glues these together into what looks like a single disagreement. But I'd want you to pressure-test that hunch rather than just agree with it -- maybe the functionalist assumptions the whole thing runs on really do force a substantive answer either way.

Whatever diagnosis you land on, tell me what would actually count as progress given that diagnosis. A new kind of evidence? A conceptual distinction that splits the question in two? A demonstration that one side's position collapses into the other's once you state it carefully? Or is this the kind of dispute that will just be with us forever, and if so, is that a defect in the debate or just what philosophy looks like at the edges?
