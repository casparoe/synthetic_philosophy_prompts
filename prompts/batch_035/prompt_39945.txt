The exam question was:

"Malthus argued in 1798 that population, when unchecked, grows geometrically while food supply grows at best arithmetically, so population will press against subsistence and be held there by positive checks (famine, disease) or preventive ones. Two centuries later, world population is vastly higher than in his day and global mass famine has not arrived. (a) Explain why this fact does not by itself show his central claim was false, distinguishing what was actually predicted (a conditional) from an unconditional point-prediction. (b) Under what conditions, if any, is it legitimate to 'rescue' a failed-looking long-run prediction by restricting it to conditions that did not obtain, and when does such a rescue become ad hoc?"

And here's the student's answer, typed out as written:

"Whether Malthus was wrong depends on what exactly he predicted. His model was a conditional one: if checks on population are absent, population grows geometrically and presses on subsistence. What did not happen is the antecedent — the industrial and agricultural revolutions, including the Green Revolution, massively raised the food supply growth rate, so the ceiling kept moving up out of the way. Since the conditional was never tested, because the antecedent never obtained, the model was never falsified, only left idle. In that sense Malthus was right but early: if we ever return to conditions where food supply cannot be raised, the model applies again. This is also why Malthus never needed to revise the theory — he kept the same geometric/arithmetic model through all six editions, and since the model was conditional it needed no repair.

As for rescuing predictions: a rescue is legitimate when the restriction is independently motivated, meaning there is some independent reason to think the added condition matters. It is ad hoc when the only justification for the added condition is saving the prediction from failure. The Green Revolution counts as a legitimate rescue because Borlaug's work was motivated by agronomy, not by any desire to rescue Malthus. By Popper's standards, an auxiliary hypothesis that is independently motivated is never ad hoc, so the Malthusian rescue passes."

A couple of things I half-noticed but don't trust myself on: I'm fairly sure the bit about the editions is wrong — from my reading Malthus revised substantially between the 1798 essay and the much bigger 1803 edition, including adding moral restraint as a check, which makes "never needed to revise" look pretty bad — but I don't know this literature well enough to be sure how it bears on the grading. The last paragraph also smells off to me, though I can't put my finger on whether the student is misreading Popper, or whether the Borlaug point is doing real work or is just decoration.
