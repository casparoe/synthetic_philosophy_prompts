A guy in my phil of math reading group has been on my case about this argument for weeks (he says it's basically the guts of the first half of what everybody packages as Benacerraf's dilemma, from an old paper of his). It runs:

(1) If numbers are objects, then each of them has a determinate identity — for any set s, either 3 = s or 3 ≠ s.
(2) Zermelo built the naturals so that 3 = {{{∅}}}, von Neumann so that 3 = {0, 1, 2}; both constructions do everything arithmetic could ask of them, even though they disagree about things like whether 3 ∈ 17.
(3) If 3 were really one of these two sets rather than the other, some fact would have to make it so.
(4) But any such fact would have to show up either in arithmetic itself or in set theory, and by (2) it shows up in neither.
(5) So numbers aren't sets — and since every attempt to tell us what numbers are, as objects, hands us some set or other to identify them with, numbers aren't objects at all.

So the conclusion is supposed to be that numbers are positions in a pattern rather than things. What bugs me is that (5) seems to proove too much. My flatmate says that by the same logic his fifty euro note isn't an object, since it could have been any of a million indistinguishable slips of paper, and honestly I cant immediately say why he's wrong — though I also can't shake the feeling he's changing the subject. What I really don't want to give up is (1), which looks like just excluded middle plus the idea that identity facts are there to be found.

Go through it as exactly five numbered points, one per step, a sentence or two each: verdict on that step, and if you're rejecting it, what rejecting it costs you. No intro, no wrap up, just the five points.
