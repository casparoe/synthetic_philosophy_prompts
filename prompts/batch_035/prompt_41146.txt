I teach a course on the metaphysics of technology and I've been sitting on a short essay draft about virtual objects that I want to workshop at our department reading group next month before I try to send it anywhere. I've read it so many times at this point that I honestly can't tell anymore whether the central argument works or whether it just sounds like it works. The occasion for the essay: a twenty-year-old MMO I'll call "Emberfall" announced it was shutting down its last server, and among the players who were devastated was a guild that had spent something like six years building an enormous cathedral in-game, a real landmark of that world. The studio offered them an offline archive file of their build as a consolation. That gesture is doing a lot of work in my head, and the essay is basically my attempt to figure out why.

Here's the draft. I argue that the players' grief was grief for the destruction of a real object, not confusion, and that the interesting question is what kind of object it was. I contrast the cathedral with a fictional character like Sherlock Holmes: on the common view, fictional objects are abstract artifacts that depend on their authors and works, which is why you can't destroy Holmes by burning every copy of the stories — the dependence is on there having been a work, not on the work continuing to be physically instantiated. The cathedral, I claim, is different in exactly one respect: its existence depends on ongoing physical instantiation. It isn't stored on the server the way a novel is stored on a shelf; the server is more like a performance that never stops, so when it stops, the thing is gone — the studio didn't destroy copies of the cathedral, it destroyed the cathedral. Then I take the archive file seriously as the obvious objection: if the build can be re-instantiated on a new server, maybe nothing was destroyed after all, just moved, like taking a painting from a burning gallery. My reply is that the archive is a recipe, not a dish — a score without a performance — and that whether the re-instantiated cathedral would be the same cathedral is a genuine persistence question, not something we get to stipulate away, so the archive softens the loss without showing there was no loss. The last paragraph gestures at virtual harm more broadly: if I'm right, some virtual losses are losses of real things, and the perennial "it's just pixels" dismissal gets the metaphysics backwards even when it gets the proportionality roughly right.

What I'd most like from you: (1) does the recipe/dish move actually carry the weight I'm putting on it, or is it a relabeling of the problem rather than a solution — I worry that once I admit the recipe exists and is copyable, a stubborn opponent says the "real object" talk was doing nothing; (2) does the comparison with fictional characters clarify or just import a pile of contested assumptions about fiction that I then inherit; (3) my persistence point leans on a loose analogy to personal identity puzzles that I never spell out, and I suspect a referee will want it spelled out — tell me whether it can be made precise cheaply or whether I should cut it. Also flag anything thats just wrong, overclaimed, or stylistically embarrassing — I wrote the middle section on a plane and its probably worse than I think.
