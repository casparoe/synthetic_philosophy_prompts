I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm at work on a piece about ranked-choice voting and the broader electoral reform debate. When I mentioned the assignment to a colleague on our data desk, he waved the whole subject off. Here, as best I can reconstruct it, is what he said: there's a result called the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, from a pair of 1970s papers, that he thinks settles the matter. In his telling, it proves that every voting system is manipulable — that voters can always gain by lying about their preferences, so there is no mechanism that aggregates preferences honestly. He said it's essentially Arrow's theorem in a different costume, and that it's also the deep reason gerrymandering works and why primaries keep feeling rigged. He concluded that ranked choice, approval, or anything else makes no difference, that every system is equally gameable, and that the sensible response is to stop chasing fairness and just pick whichever method is easiest to administer.

I don't want to put anything like this in print until I understand what the theorem actually says, and my colleague is confident enough that I can't simply dismiss him. Could you audit his summary for me? Go claim by claim and tell me which parts a social choice theorist would accept, which would make them wince, and which are just wrong. A few specific worries: I've since gathered that the theorem needs at least three alternatives on the ballot and that it only rules out manipulability for systems that are not simply dictatorships — my colleague mentioned neither condition, and I'd like to know whether omitting them changes anything. I'd also like to know whether the theorem's technical notion of manipulation — a voter misrepresenting preferences — has anything to do with gerrymandering, which sounds to me like an institutional rather than a voter-level problem, and whether his identification of the result with Arrow's theorem is fair. Finally, and most importantly for my article: does his deflationary conclusion — that all systems are equally gameable and the choice among them doesn't matter — actually follow from the accurate parts of his summary, or does the theorem leave room for real differences among voting systems in how often and how badly manipulation bites?
