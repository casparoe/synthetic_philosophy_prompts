My friend Elena and I have argued for years about what individuals owe in collective harms, and I've started writing up her views as a case study for a practical ethics seminar I help teach. Her track record so far: she votes in every national election and is openly contemptuous of friends who don't, even though she cheerfully concedes that any single ballot in our district is almost certainly causally idle. She went vegetarian after concluding animal agriculture is a moral catastrophe, and when her brother told her one person's groceries won't change production by even a single animal, she said the point was irrelevant and hasn't wavered since. She buys her phones and laptops from a manufacturer with well-documented labor abuses in its supply chain, and says she'd feel no guilt even if the abuses were twice as bad, because her abstention would accomplish literally nothing. She flies to two or three work conferences a year and pays for carbon offsets every time, calling it the least she can do — though she has never once suggested giving up the flights. And when someone told her that her recurring donation to a large anti-malaria fund is a drop in the ocean, she replied that drops count.

Now her cousin has announced a destination wedding on another continent, and I want to predict her verdict before she announces it, as a test of whether I actually understand her position. The flight would emit several tonnes of CO2, a large fraction of her annual footprint under any responsible budget, and she can buy an offset for about a sixth of the ticket price. The rehearsal dinner is a plated beef dinner for the full guest list: the caterer has locked in the count, so her portion will be produced and then eaten or discarded whether or not she touches it, and nobody will notice either way. So there are two verdicts to predict — the flight (does she fly, and if so does she buy the offset, or does she decline the invitation?) and the dinner (does she eat the beef or quietly decline it?).

I'm less interested in the answers than in the rule she seems to be running. As far as I can tell, several principles fit her record equally well: a pivotal-chance view on which an individual's act matters only when it might be decisive; a smooth expected-marginal-harm view on which tiny contributions generate proportionally tiny reasons; and a clean-hands view on which one refuses participation in serious harms regardless of efficacy. Note that some of these fit only given assumptions about how she weighs the underlying harms — animals seem to register with her differently than the factory workers do, and I don't know whether that reflects severity or something else. Please tell me which rules genuinely fit the record, what each predicts for the wedding, your best guess with your reasons, and, if two rules survive, the single further case outside the wedding that would best discriminate between them. End with a line in the form \answer{[flight]: fly-with-offset | fly-without-offset | stay-home; [dinner]: eat | refuse} and nothing after it.
