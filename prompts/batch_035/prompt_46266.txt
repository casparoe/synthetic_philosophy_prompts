so I keep going around in circles on the heritability of IQ thing and what I actually want is a diagnosis, not another recap. The setup: twin studies, adoption studies, the whole toolbox have put out high and fairly stable heritability estimates for IQ in wealthy western countries, call it 50-80%. and yet the same argument never dies -- people slide from "highly heritable" to "hard to change", or to "group differences are probably genetic", or to "compensatory education is a waste of time" -- and the other side fires back with the standard counters. The seedling thought experiment (Lewontin's I think): same batch of genotype, split across a poor-soil pot and a rich-soil pot, heritability within each pot can be perfect while the entire difference between pots is environmental. The Dutch shooting up in height over the last century while height stayed highly heritable. The whole within-group/between-group distinction. To me the counters look decisive, and yet the fight hasn't moved in fifty years -- the Bell Curve fight, the Mismeasure of Man fight, and every online argument since, it's the same moves every time.

my working theory, offered as just a theory: the disagreement isn't really about genetics at all. It's partly verbal -- one side hears "heritable" as a variance ratio in a specific population, the other side hears something like "biologically fixed" or "in the blood" -- and partly a values/burden-of-proof standoff about which side has to prove what, all dressed up as an empirical dispute. But maybe that's too generous to one side. Maybe there's a genuine residual empirical question hiding in there, like whether within-population heritability gives you even weak evidence about between-population differences under some set of assumptions, or whether GxE (gene-environment interaction) is serious enough that the whole ratio is unstable.

so: what kind of disagreement is this actually? verbal, values, hidden empirical question, different questions under one heading, or just a burden-of-proof standoff where neither side can lose? make the case from what the sides actually say, not from a survey of the literature. and then given your diagnosis, tell me what would even count as progress -- a better term, a specific kind of empirical result, everyone just conceding its about values, or nothing, and why.

one more thing: as you go, please keep a running separation between what is actually settled among people who know the area -- the variance-ratio definition itself, the point that within-group heritability by itself licenses no between-group inference -- and what is genuinely contested, like the equal-environments assumption in twin designs or how big GxE is. I keep running into people presenting contested stuff as the consensus position and its making the whole thing harder to think about, so I want to know where the firm floor is and where the arguing is still live.
