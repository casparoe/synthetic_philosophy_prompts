I work on safety-side approaches to avoiding unintended side effects in AI systems, and I've settled into a package of views that I'd like audited for consistency rather than defended. Here is the package:

(1) "Low impact" is not a physical property of a trajectory that a measure could read off the world. Every impact measure has to choose whose abilities get counted, over what time horizon, and relative to which counterfactual, and each of those choices is value-laden. Taken seriously, this means the low-impact program is a chapter of value learning, not an alternative to it.

(2) Nevertheless, the concrete measures people have proposed — roughly, penalize the agent for changing how easily a large, arbitrary sample of goals could be achieved — are genuine technical progress: they block a wide class of destructive side effects while requiring almost no information about what we actually care about.

(3) The right baseline for the penalty is stepwise inaction — what would have happened had the agent done nothing at the previous step — rather than the state at the start of the episode, because an initial-state baseline bills the agent for drift in the world it did not cause.

(4) The measure should be symmetric: increasing the overseer's options is penalized just as decreasing them is. No discount for "improving" things — that discount is precisely the door through which paternalism and lock-in enter.

(5) Low impact is also the cheapest route to corrigibility: an agent that preserves the overseer's options has, in doing so, most of what corrigibility requires, so corrigibility need not be solved as a separate problem.

Go through the pairs and tell me which are in real tension and which only appear to be, rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them flatly, and for each say what the cheapest revision is and what it costs. Two constraints on how you do this. First, don't just flag everything: at least one of these pairs should turn out to be compatible on inspection, and I'd like to know which. Second, keep separate what is settled among people who know this literature — for instance, which failure modes of these measures are established results — from what is genuinely contested; my impression is that the choice of baseline and the relationship between low impact and corrigibility are both live disagreements, but correct me if that's wrong, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus one.
