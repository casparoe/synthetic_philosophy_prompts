My friend and I spent half of last night arguing about the problem of old evidence in Bayesian confirmation and now I can't tell if we were disagreeing about anything real. Background so we're on the same page: Mercury's perihelion advance was known for decades before general relativity explained it, and it's treated as a huge confirmation of GR. But standard Bayesian confirmation runs through your conditional probabilities, and if you already assigned probability 1 to the perihelion data (you'd learned it long before GR existed), conditioning on it can't move you. So Bayesianism seems to can't say what everyone agrees is true — that the old evidence confirmed the new theory.

Here's where it got heated. My friend's position is that the whole debate is a fake problem generated by an artifact of the formalism: Bayesians idealize agents as having a fixed hypothesis space, so when a genuinely new theory shows up, there's no well-defined place in the machinery where that event gets absorbed, and old evidence is just the visible symptom of that. On her view, the various patches people proposed — reinterpreting things so that what you condition on is learning that the theory entails the evidence, rather than the evidence itself, or the move of imagining you didn't know it yet — are all ways of smuggling the theory-discovery event in through the back door, and once you're honest about that, the "problem" dissolves into the general problem of how to model conceptual change, which is not specifically Bayesian and not solved by anyone.

I pushed back that this makes the problem too big and too vague. The patch where you condition on discovering the entailment looked fine to me: you learn "GR entails this precession value," that had a middling prior, and you update on it like anything else. She said that patch is circular unless you already have probabilities over the entailment claims of theories you haven't thought of yet, which is just the fixed-hypothesis-space assumption wearing a disguise, and also that it makes confirmation depend on the agent's logical limitations in a way that felt weirdly ad hoc. We ended up talking past each other about whether "the same problem in fancier clothes" and "a genuine solution" are even distinguishable here.

So I want a diagnosis of this stalled literature, not a survey of it. Is the disagreement between the patchers and the dissolvers substantive, or is it verbal — do they mean different things by "the problem is solved"? Is there a hidden question doing the work, maybe about whether confirmation is a relation between a theory and evidence or a process an agent undergoes? And what, concretely, would count as progress — a new formal toy model, a distinction, or nothing, because the real target (modeling theory invention) is out of everyone's reach?
