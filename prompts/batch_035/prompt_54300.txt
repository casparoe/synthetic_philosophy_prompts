Retired physicist here -- 35 years in condensed matter, now that I've hung it up I've fallen down the philosophy of history rabbit hole and I can't get out. Before I embarrass myself at the local discussion group, I want a professional philosopher (or a damn good historian of ideas) to audit whether my package of views actually hangs together. Think carefully before answering -- maybe lay out the logical geography first -- and then go claim by claim. Here's what I currently believe:

(1) History is in principle a science like any other. Planets and populations obey laws; I see no reason human societies should be exempt, and I want genuine, testable generalizations about them.

(2) Polybius' cycle of constitutions -- monarchy sliding into tyranny, aristocracy into oligarchy, democracy into ochlocracy, and around again -- strikes me as a real observation about how power decays, not just ancient armchair speculation.

(3) Ibn Khaldun's asabiyyah story feels basically right too: rough groups with tight solidarity conquer, settle into luxury, lose cohesion over something like four generations, and get replaced by hungrier outsiders. When I read it I thought, this man was doing sociology before sociology existed.

(4) Spengler is garbage. Civilizations as giant organisms with a genetically fixed lifespan and a destiny is mysticism dressed up as biology, and as someone who did actual biology-adjacent science for a living, it makes my skin crawl.

(5) And yet I find myself liking Toynbee's challenge-and-response, even though everybody tells me Toynbee is just Spengler with footnotes and worse prose.

(6) Human nature is fixed. Evolution doesn't turn over on political timescales, so the raw material -- tribalism, status hunger, nepotism -- was the same in Sumer as it is in Ohio.

(7) Despite that, moral progress is real and cumulative. The abolition of slavery, the spread of rights, the retreat of torture -- these are improvements, not just fashion changes, and I'd defend that against any relativist.

(8) Contingency is huge. One assassination in Sarajevo redirects a century; history is sensitively dependent on initial conditions in a way that would make my old dynamical systems colleagues nod.

So: which of these are actually in tension, which only look like it, and where? My physicist instinct says (8) sits fine with (1)-(3) because chaotic systems can still have attractors -- cycles and sensitivity to initial conditions aren't mutually exclusive -- but maybe I'm fooling myself. And I genuinely can't tell if holding (6) and (7) together is a problem, or if (4) and (5) cancel each other out. Rank the tensions by severity rather than giving me a flat list of "hmm, interesting tension"s, and for each one tell me what would have to give and what it would cost me. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I'm not attached to any particular one of these -- I just want to know where I'm being incoherent before someone else figures it out first. cheers.
