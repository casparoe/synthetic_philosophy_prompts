Question (out of 20): A critic argues that negative utilitarianism implies that if a painless method of destroying all life existed, we would be obligated to use it, since this would guarantee the absence of all future suffering. Explain why this conclusion follows from strict negative utilitarianism as stated, describe at least one way contemporary suffering-focused ethicists respond, and assess whether the response preserves what made the view attractive in the first place.

Student's answer: Negative utilitarianism (NU) holds that we should minimize suffering rather than maximize happiness. Bentham was actually the first negative utilitarian, since his felicific calculus treated pain as weighing more than pleasure, so NU is really just classical utilitarianism read carefully. The critic's argument goes through because for NU, happiness has no intrinsic value, only instrumental value at best. Therefore a world with zero suffering and zero happiness is better than any world containing suffering, and since a painless destruction of all life guarantees zero suffering forever, NU makes it obligatory. This is the main reason Bentham's version was eventually abandoned.

Most suffering-focused ethicists today avoid this by adopting lexical thresholds, where suffering above a certain intensity counts infinitely more than milder suffering and than happiness. Once you accept a threshold, the critic's argument fails, and it also fails for every other form of utilitarianism including classical, so the world-destruction problem is really just a problem for the strict version and doesn't tell against aggregative ethics generally. Note too that NU is basically the same as the widely accepted asymmetry between harm and benefit — we all agree creating a child who will suffer is bad but declining to create a happy child is not wrong — so anyone accepting the asymmetry is already committed to NU and its implications.

The response does preserve the view's appeal, because what made NU attractive was that it respected the intuition that relieving agony matters more than adding amusements, and the threshold view keeps that priority. So the critic scores a point against a strawman, but the core insight survives intact.
