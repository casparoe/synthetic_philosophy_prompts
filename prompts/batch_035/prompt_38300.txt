You're a professional decision theorist who also spends a lot of time actually running classroom experiments -- so you know both the maths and the messy reality of getting undergrads to bid on lotteries with chocolate coins. I need your help designing a session.

I teach a second-year undergrad module called "Rational Choice" (yes, very original name). There are 18 students, and I have one 75-minute seminar slot in a room with movable desks. Next week we hit the St. Petersburg paradox -- you know, the coin-flip game where the payoff doubles every time heads fails to show, so expected value is infinite, and yet no sane person pays even £50 to play. The textbook treatment is: here's the paradox, here's Bernoulli's diminishing marginal utility fix, here's the "casino has finite bankroll" fix, the end. Students nod along, memorise it, feel nothing.

I want an activity where they *feel* the problem instead of just recieving it. My idea -- improve on it, push back on it, do what you like with it -- is some combination of:

1. An actual playable version of the game with a capped bankroll. Say the house can pay out at most £200 (in fake currency, redeemable for sweets at the end of term, nothing with real money). So the "paradox" is truncated and the expected value is now finite but still sizeable. Students should have to decide, individually and silently first, the maximum they'd pay to play. Then we run an auction for the right to play -- I'm thinking a sealed-bid second-price auction so they have some incentive to bid their true valuation, but you tell me if that's right or if it just confuses philosophy students.

2. A syndicate round. Everyone who wants in pays the same amount, the lottery gets played once (or a few times?), and the payout is split. I suspect something interesting happens when it's not your own stake -- I've noticed students get much braver with the department's money than with theirs -- and I want the debrief to ask whether that's just noise or whether it tells us something about whether the unwillingness to pay is really about the utility curve of money or about something else.

3. Something I'm calling the charity variant but I'm not sure it works: same game, but the winnings go to a cause the students picked at the start of term. Does the paradox get weaker, stronger, or just weirder? If you think this round is a waste of 10 minutes and muddies everything, say so and cut it -- but if you can make it land, it might be the most interesting bit.

Please think carefully before you write, and maybe brainstorm a couple of variants before committing to the final design. What I need in the end:

- Full rules and materials list (I have a coin, a whiteboard, a printer, and a bag of chocolate coins -- that's basically it)
- A minute-by-minute timeline that actually fits 75 minutes, including setup between rounds
- The debrief: map specific moments in the activity onto expected value, Bernoulli's log-utility fix, bankroll truncation, and the one-shot vs repeated-play distinction. I particularly want them to see that capping the payout "solves" the paradox by making it about a different game -- and then to argue about whether the original game was ever a game anyone could be offered.
- An honest section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (my guess: someone bids a silly amount to be funny, and the auction collapses) and how to handle it
- And be frank about which part of the paradox no classroom activity can reproduce and I'll just have to supply by talking at them for five minutes at the end.

One more thing: the module handbook insists we're "developing students' analytical writing," so where you mention any terms of art, define them in a sentence the students could copy into their notes. Also please write everything in British English -- "behaviour", "colourised", the lot -- since I'm UK-based and I'll be pasting your text straight into my slides.

If it helps you calibrate: last year I tried running a Prisoner's Dilemma with them and it was a disaster, half the class cooperated out of politeness, so assume they are nice, literate, and slightly too willing to please the person running the room. Design around that.
