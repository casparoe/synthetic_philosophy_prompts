A fight broke out in my reading group last week and I need someone to sort it out. The topic was indirect normativity — the idea that instead of figuring out directly what's good or what rules to live by, you specify a procedure: take our current values/preferences, run them through some idealized process (imagine ourselves fully informed, free of bias, thinking long and carefully, maybe having read everything worth reading and having grown up a bit), and then go with whatever *that* version of us would endorse. Sounds nice in principle. Then it got ugly.

It was between Dana, one of my grad students, and Marcus, a colleague who does decision theory. Dana opened by saying the whole thing is circular. The idealization has to be pointed somewhere, she said — more informed, less biased, more reflective, fine, but *reflective in the direction of what*? Something has to decide which counts as an improvement, and the only raw material available is our current, unidealized values. So the procedure doesn't generate values, it just launders the ones we already have, and anything "new" it outputs got in there because we put it there.

Marcus said that's just wrong, and that it confuses circularity with ordinary growth. When somebody goes to college, or has kids, or spends a decade in a trade, their values change as a result of experience and reflection, and we don't say the change is fake because they had the values that got changed. Deliberation under good conditions is a cause of value change, not a transcription of prior content. If you deny that, you're committed to saying nobody ever genuinely comes to care about anything they didn't already care about, which is obviously false as a description of actual people.

Dana conceded that actual people change, but said he'd missed the target. In the real cases Marcus described, there's a continuing person who is *subject to* the change — she notices it happening, resists it or assents to it, and it happens slowly, against a background of her other projects, so it stays recognizably hers. But the idealized agent of indirect normitivity isn't like that. It's a constructed endpoint, and there's no fact of the matter about how far the reflection is supposed to run. Two hundred years of it? The endpoint could differ from us about almost everything, and at that point, Dana said, you're not consulting an idealized version of *us*, you're consulting a stranger and calling it us. The normative force evaporates: why should I care what that thing wants?

Marcus replied that the "stranger" line proves too much. Actual people already undergo value changes we'd call transformative — religious converts, someone who survives a war, the classic examples — and afterward we do not say a different person came home. If continuity of identity can survive that in real life, he didn't see why it can't survive idealized reflection in principle. Personality is not a fixed inventory of preferences; it's a pattern, and the pattern can persist through deep shifts in what it wants.

Dana's last word was that this just re-describes the problem. The war-vet changes are bounded by a life — they happen to a person embedded in the world, over time, with the person along for the ride. Idealization is unbounded and occurs nowhere: there is no process, no duration, no agent undergoing it, so the "how much reflection, and of what kind" question gets answered by *somebody*, and that somebody is us, now, with our current values, and the circle is right back where it started. Idealization without a specified endpoint isn't a value-generating procedure, it's a blank check that whoever fills it out gets to write morality in their own hand and call it ours.

They both appealed to me at the end and I honestly did not want to referee — Marcus is up for tenure. So: go through their exchange move by move, tell me who's right at each step (not just who wins overall), and if there's something both of them are missing that would dissolve part of the dispute, I'd like to hear that too.
