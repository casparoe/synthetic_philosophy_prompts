I'm on the collections committee of a mid-sized regional museum, and we have a case that's turned into a running arguement about what our own records even mean. Our centerpiece is a seventeenth-century terracotta Madonna, catalogued as "circle of a Roman Baroque sculptor" — so no big name attached, but it's the thing visitors come for. A fire last year badly damaged it: conservators salvaged maybe a third of the original fired clay, mostly the torso and some drapery, while the head, hands, and base are gone or unusable. The proposal on the table is a full reconstruction — the salvaged fragments consolidated onto a new armature, the missing parts rebuilt in new clay and resin, tinted to match, so that visually it will read as one continous statue. The director wants it back on display as "the Madonna, conserved"; the registrar argues that what comes back from the studio is at best a new object that the old one is embedded in, and wants a new accession number; and the insurers will only pay the full claim if we call it a restoration of the same object rather than a loss followed by a replacement, which has obviously concentrated minds.

The committee is split along a line I've since realized is basically the statue-and-the-clay puzzle from philosophy: is the artwork the physical object, so that its identity depends on how much original material survives and how it's connected? Or is it something more like a design that's only contingently embodied in matter, so material continuity matters much less than we instinctively feel? Some of us think that if the surviving fragments are spatially continuous with the newly modeled parts, it's one statue and the same artwork, just heavily restored — the way a river stays one river. Others think the artwork died with the fire, and what we'd be putting back on the pedestal is a replica with relics built in, which the public would be misled by unless the label is brutally honest.

Please draft the decision memo I'll circulate before the committee votes. It needs a concrete bottom line — same accession number or a new one, exactly how the wall label should read, and what we can defensibly say to the insurers — plus the reasoning behind it. And I want it to genuinely engage the strongest point of whichever side it rules against, not steamroll it: either way, the losing side has a real point about what a museum is promising people when it puts "17th century" on a label, and the memo should show why that point doesn't win rather than pretending no one made it.
