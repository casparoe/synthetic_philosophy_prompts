My decision theory reading group has spent the last three weeks on the two-envelope paradox (one envelope has twice as much as the other, you pick one at random, and someone tells you you should switch) and I've come out of it with a pile of commitments that I cant fully reconcile, so I want an audit rather than another lecture. Here's roughly where I've landed. (1) For one-shot choices under uncertainty you maximize expected utility — no risk aversion, money roughly linear at these stakes, and it doesn't matter that you'll never face the same choice again. (2) The conditional calculation is sound: if, given that my envelope contains x, the other is equally likely to be 2x or x/2, then switching gains x/4 in expectation, and in that situation I should switch. (3) But I'm equally confident in the symmetry side: nothing distinguishes the envelopes, and the switch-back reductio shows switching can't be strictly better than keeping. (4) Last week I also convinced myself of something that still feels like a free lunch: open your envelope and switch iff the amount you see is below some cutoff c. That rule never does worse than keeping, and strictly does better whenever there's any chance the pair straddles c — e.g. amounts of $10 and $20 with c = $15, where you switch on seeing $10 and keep on seeing $20. I've checked it on a bunch of examples and cant find the flaw. (5) Separately, I think the St. Petersburg lottery really does have infinite expected value, I'd pay any finite price to play it, and I take "well, utilities must be bounded" to be an ad hoc rescue rather than a lesson of the paradox. (6) And I have a strong methodological feeling that the two-envelope paradox must admit a distribution-free resolution — whether to switch can't depend on facts about how the host filled the envelopes that I have no access to.

What I want from you: go through the pairs, tell me which are in genuine tension and which only look like it, and rank them by severity rather than flagging everything equally. I have a suspicion the St. Petersburg thing is connected to the rest — someone in the group muttered that if you allow unbounded payoffs, both envelopes can end up with infinite expectation and the whole framework quietly goes silent, or maybe it was that my (1) goes silent — but I couldn't reconstruct the argument afterward and I don't want to pretend I did. I honestly can't tell whether the thing that has to give is my confidence in symmetry, the conditional calculation, my prior-independence requirement in (6), or my willingness to chase infinite expectations. Also, if (4) really is right, does that mean my friend's slogan "no strategy can beat symmetry" is just false, or does symmetry only govern what you should do before you look inside the envelope? For each genuine tension, tell me the cheapest fix and what that fix costs elsewhere in the set, and then say which single revision would clean up the most.
