I have recently been appointed to the clinical ethics and resource-allocation committee of our regional health authority, and we are facing a recurring shortage of a high-cost therapy, which has forced me to write a position memo on how to weigh patient benefits against one another. Before I draft it, I want a consistency audit of my commitments, because I have come to suspect that the philosophical positions I have long held in the abstract do not obviously fit together now that I have to apply them. Here is my position set:

(1) I am convinced by Harsanyi's aggregation theorem: if each individual's preferences are coherent in the von Neumann–Morgenstern sense, if the social evaluator's preferences over social states are also coherent in that sense, and if a Pareto condition holds, then social welfare must be expressible as a weighted sum of individual utilities. I regard this as close to a proof that policy evaluation must proceed by additive aggregation.

(2) I believe the veil of ignorance is the right device for thinking about fairness, and I am specifically drawn to Harsanyi's version of it, in which behind the veil you take an equal probability of being any member of society.

(3) At the same time, I am a committed Rawlsian about the basic structure of institutions: I hold that they must be arranged so as to maximally benefit the worst-off representative person, and I also take this to follow from reasoning behind the veil.

(4) I believe interpersonal comparisons of utility are not merely difficult in practice but conceptually confused — that there is simply no fact of the matter whether your headache is worse than mine — and I regard this as a strength of my overall outlook rather than an embarrassment to be hidden.

(5) On the committee, I have argued for giving extra weight to the claims of the worst-off patients in allocation decisions — not as a political compromise, but as a requirement of fairness that I take to follow from my other views.

(6) I reject expected utility theory as a correct account of individual rationality: Allais-type preferences strike me as rational, and I do not think a normative theory of social aggregation should be built on a false psychology of the individual.

Please tell me whether this package hangs together. For each genuine tension, say precisely which commitments generate it, how severe it is, and what the cheapest revision would be and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the set. Please also identify any pair that only looks inconsistent but dissolves on closer inspection — I do not want to abandon something I can keep. Rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally serious, and conclude by telling me which single revision would clean up the most. I am especially interested in whether the problem is in my meta-level commitments about comparability and rationality (4) and (6), or in the object-level verdicts (3) and (5), because the memo I write for the committee will have to operationalize whichever of those survives the audit. Please note that the memo concerns a fixed population of current patients, so you can set population ethics aside.
