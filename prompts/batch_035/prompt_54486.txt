I'm taking a philosophy of language course and we just covered the necessary a posteriori. My tutor keeps making the same move at the whiteboard: she draws a little tag being pinned on a thing at its christening, then a chain of arrows showing the tag getting handed down speaker to speaker, like a baton in a relay. The point is that nothing in the passing depends on any description being true -- you can pick up the name "Aristotle" from someone who got it from someone else, all of them wrong about nearly everything, and the tag still rides the chain onto the same man. And then, because that picture works so nicely for names, she says natural kind terms obviously work the same way: "water" got pinned to the stuff in the local rivers at some original dubbing, handed down ever since, and the chemists later discovered what the tagged stuff really is -- which is supposed to be why "water is H2O" came out necessary even though it took experiments to learn it.

I mostly bought this at the seminar, but I'm not sure the baton picture isn't carrying more weight than the argument. For one thing, the chain story is a story about the actual world, but rigidity is about counterfactuals -- her drawn arrows never have to say what happens to the tag in worlds where Aristotle died in infancy, so I can't see how the picture delivers the modal part at all; it just draws the ordinary-world part. Also I vaguely remember hearing that real reference chains drift: something about "Madagascar" starting out as the name of a bit of the African mainland and getting reassigned to the island by a mistake somewhere down the chain, which sounds like a label quietly coming unstuck and being pinned somewhere else -- exactly the thing the baton picture says can't happen.

Can you audit the analogy for me? Please give me exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) what maps onto what -- the christening, the chain of hands, the tag, rigid designation, and the original water sample; (2) the disanalogy that does the most damage to the uses my tutor puts the picture to; (3) what the picture quietly imports that does some of her work for free; (4) whether the necessary a posteriori conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the picture was carrying it the whole time; and (5) whether there's a patched version of the analogy, or a better one, that a defender of the causal picture should switch to.
