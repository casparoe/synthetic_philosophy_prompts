For several years my closest friend and I have played a running game of counterfactuals about her own origins, and her answers have turned out to be consistent enough that I suspect one could reconstruct the rule she is applying. Her verdicts so far:

1. If a different sperm had fertilized the very egg from which she in fact developed — same month, same parents, everything else untouched — the resulting child would not have been her. The same holds for the actual sperm and a different egg.
2. If the actual zygote had been frozen for two years, carried by a surrogate, and raised by another family on another continent, the resulting person would still have been her. Nothing about gestation, upbringing, or psychology seems to move her verdicts.
3. If the actual zygote had split and both identical twins had been born and lived full lives, neither twin would have been her; on her telling, she simply would never have existed.
4. If the zygote had split in the same way, but one of the two embryos had quietly failed to implant — no one ever knowing there had been two — the surviving child would have been her.
5. A child cloned decades later from one of her cells would not have been her.

Three new cases come up next week, and I would like to walk in with a prediction. (a) A week after the actual conception, her embryo fused with a second, independent embryo — different sperm, different egg — and the two developed into a single, apparently normal person whose body contains cells descended from both zygotes, as sometimes happens with chimeras. Is that person her? (b) At the four-cell stage, the embryo split in two, and a day later the two halves fused back into one embryo, which then developed normally. Is the resulting person her? (c) The zygote split, both twins were born and lived separate lives, but one twin died at thirty in an accident while the survivor lived to old age. Is the survivor her?

Please predict her verdict on each of (a) through (c), and reconstruct the rule she seems to be running. As far as I can tell, at least two distinct principles fit her record on cases 1 through 5 equally well — each consistent with all five of her answers — yet they come apart on at least some of the new cases. Identify the plausible candidate rules, say what each predicts for each of the three new cases, give your best guess at her actual verdicts along with your reasons for preferring one rule over the others, and tell me what further case, if I added it to next week's game, would most cleanly discriminate between the candidate rules.
