I am a PhD student in moral philosophy, and this term I am teaching an upper-level undergraduate seminar on contractualism and its critics, built around Scanlon's paper "Contractualism and Utilitarianism" and the relevant chapters of What We Owe to Each Other. I have been asked to contribute one of the three essay questions for the ninety-minute final, and I want mine to be on the aggregation problem. My difficulty is that the standard material here invites recitation: the students have memorized the transmitter room, the slogan that complaints are compared one complainant at a time, and the line that numbers do not create complaints. So I have built a case of my own, and I would like the question to be built around it rather than around the textbook examples.

Here is the case, which I call the warden. A county must keep its flood-siren network tested each year. It can either conscript a single resident, the warden, to spend one working month at the monitoring post, about a hundred and seventy hours: tedious and isolating, but tolerable, and stipulated to involve no risk to health or safety; or it can distribute the chore, with every resident spending ten seconds a year confirming a siren test. The case comes in three configurations. In the village, there are four thousand residents, so the distributed option costs about eleven person-hours in all, and every theory agrees the county may distribute. In the metropolis, there are three million residents, still at ten seconds each, so the distributed option costs roughly eight thousand person-hours, nearly fifty times the warden's annual burden; yet no individual complaint is any larger than it was in the village, since ten seconds is ten seconds however many people are standing behind you. In the hamlet, there are five hundred residents, each of whom would owe five minutes a year rather than ten seconds, for a total of about forty person-hours.

I want the question to force students to do the following, in whatever order the question's logic suggests: state precisely what Scanlon's individualist restriction requires, which is that complaints be compared one complainant at a time with no summing across persons, though harms to the same person may be summed within her complaint; determine, for each configuration, what contractualism permits and what a utilitarianism of total burdens would require, and identify exactly where the two theories' verdicts come apart; say whether the invariance of the contractualist verdict across a three-hundredfold growth in total burden is a feature, on the ground that arithmetic does not create complaints, or a bug, on the ground that the crowd is being exploited by its own thinness; and evaluate at least two possible repairs, for instance allowing complaints to carry weights that sum across persons, or making the reasonableness of a rejection sensitive to how many people could lodge it. The model answer should say what each repair costs, in particular whether the first collapses contractualism into a utilitarianism of complaints and whether the second merely relocates the aggregation from the level of complaints to the level of the theory of reasons.

Please deliver three things in order: the question as it will appear on the exam; a model answer of about 750 words that a strong student could realistically produce in ninety minutes; and a marking scheme worth 25 marks that names the specific moves separating an A answer from a B answer, and that says, for each thing that costs marks, the mistake it penalizes. The question should presuppose only the seminar's core reading, should not ask for citations, page references, or secondary literature, and should not be answerable by reciting the transmitter room, though the model answer may contrast my case with it. One formatting requirement, which I ask you to observe throughout: continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, so that the whole reads the way one writes a letter to a colleague; the marking scheme in particular should carry its mark allocations in ordinary sentences.
