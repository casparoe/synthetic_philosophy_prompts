I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training — what I know about Dennett comes from blog posts and one conference talk — and I've drafted the notes for a short essay I'd like comments on before writing it up properly.

The essay's working thesis is that Dennett's idea of a "real pattern" can be made precise in engineering terms as a compression claim. I'll state it the way I would at work: an intentional-stance attribution (the model wants higher click-through; the load balancer believes that node is dead) picks out a real pattern just in case attributing that belief or desire lets you predict the system's behavior with less information than you would need to predict it from its internal mechanism — and that savings is measurable in practice: fewer pages of documentation, fewer lines of simulation code, less telemetry you have to retain. The draft's central observation is that this matters specifically for systems with learned components. In classic software the design stance suffices, because the artifact is essentially its own documentation: you can read the code and see why it does what it does. With a trained model there is no design to read in that sense, so we fall through to the intentional stance — and my claim is that the fall-through is legitimate exactly when it buys compression. My main example is the postmortems my team writes: a one-line gloss like "the recommender was optimizing for freshness and that's why it buried the evergreen items" predicts dozens of concrete behaviors that a gradient-level description would take pages to cover.

I close the notes with a worry I can't resolve on my own: by my own criterion, almost any system under stable optimization pressure acquires intentional states on the cheap. A spam filter ends up with "beliefs" about what we look for; a thermostat is one line away from "wanting" a target temperature. I suspect that at this point "real pattern" has become so inclusive that it no longer does any discriminating work between genuine minds and everything else — and I can't tell whether that is a fatal flaw in my compression gloss, a fatal flaw in the real-patterns idea itself, or just the correct and surprising conclusion.

Please comment on two things specifically. First, accuracy: is the compression gloss faithful to what Dennett means by a real pattern, or have I reinvented something he explicitly rejects? If he draws the line somewhere other than compressibility, I'd like to know where, stated in plain terms. Second, the cheapness worry: is it a genuine problem for the view as I've framed it, or is there an obvious answer I'm missing? Please keep your comments under 500 words, and prioritize the two or three points that most affect whether the essay is worth finishing rather than covering everything.
