My department's ethics reading group has spent the better part of a year on expressivism and the Frege–Geach problem, and we have hit a wall I'd like to understand better. As we now all appreciate, the original challenge is that in a valid argument like: lying is wrong; if lying is wrong, getting your little brother to lie is wrong; therefore getting your little brother to lie is wrong — the premise's occurrence in the conditional is unasserted, so an expressivist who says the sentence expresses disapproval of lying seems to have nothing to say about the antecedent occurrence, and no way to explain why the argument is valid rather than equivocal. But everyone in the room, including the skeptics, concedes that the sophisticated responses meet this challenge in its original form: Blackburn's machinery of higher-order attitudes toward combinations of attitudes, Gibbard's account in terms of plans and hyperplans, Schroeder's story about the single attitude of being for ruling out lying. You can, if you work at it, get every intuitively valid normative inference to come out valid, and every invalid one invalid.

Here is where we stall. The anti-expressivists in the group say the compositional machinery is a shell game: it was reverse-engineered from exactly the inference patterns it was supposed to explain, so "explaining" modus ponens by stipulating an attitude toward attitude-combinations that happens to preserve it is not explanation at all — it's bookkeeping with the same surface grammar the realist uses. The expressivists answer that every compositional semantics is fitted to usage somehow, and that demanding a deeper justification of the expressivist's clauses than we demand of the realist's just assumes realism about the contents being assigned. Meanwhile each new solution breeds a new objection — the negation problem, the worry that a solution general enough to handle negation generates too many attitude-types to keep the theory noncognitivist, the complaint that hybrid theories quietly concede everything to the cognitivist — and the dialectic rotates through this cycle without either side giving ground. Notably, the two camps do not disagree about any actual inference: they agree that the little-brother argument is valid, that it's not equivocal, that the negation of a moral claim is expressible. The fight is entirely about whether the expressivist account of these agreements deserves to be called a semantics.

My working hypothesis, which I'd like you to test rather than flatter, is that this is not a substantive disagreement about compositionality at all but a disguised disagreement about what an adequate semantic theory is for — describing the inferential role of sentences versus explaining or grounding that role in something else — and that beneath that lies a genuinely substantive but different dispute, about whether the belief/desire taxonomy carves normative judgment at its joints, which no amount of compositional ingenuity can touch. Is that diagnosis right? Are the two sides in fact answering different questions under one heading, or is there a real formal or metaphysical question on which they divide? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: an independently motivated test case that any semantics must handle, a criterion for when a compositional clause is ad hoc rather than merely tailored, or the recognition that the debate is a proxy war over naturalism about the normative, and should be conducted on that terrain instead? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a tour of the positions, and an honest verdict on whether anything could move this debate or whether the impasse is structural.
