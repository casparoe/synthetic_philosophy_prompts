I'm a journalist who mostly covers science and ideas, but at our small magazine I've somehow also inherited the style complaints, and lately we get a couple a week about wording that supposedly bakes in some default assumption -- "sanity check" in a piece about software, generic "he" in a quote from an old philosopher, "peanut gallery" in a headline, that kind of thing. Nobody on the desk has linguistics training, we don't have time for corpus research, and our style guide just says "avoid needlessly exclusive language," which settles nothing when a writer pushes back.

The only thing I've ever read that actually changed how I think about these fights is Hofstadter's mid-80s satire "A Person Paper on Purity in Language" (he published it under a pseudonym, William Satire, as a parody of a language-pundit column). The device: he took the stock defenses of gendered defaults -- "chairman includes women, it just means person, the etymology is innocent, everyone knows what it means" -- and rewrote the whole column with racial terms swapped in, so you get defenses of "chairwhite," the poetry of "All whites are created equal," and Neil Armstrong recast as Captain Nellie Strongarm saying "One small step for a white, a giant step for whitekind." Reading it, you watch the standard excuses curdle into open special pleading. The deep point, I think, is that a lot of defenses of a default-assuming usage are pretextual, and a substitution test can reveal that.

So here's what I want: turn that insight into a procedure our desk can actually run on a complaint in about ten minutes. Something like a checklist -- what to establish first (is the term even defaulting on a group, or is it a dead metaphor?), then the substitution step (swap in a different group and see if our defense of keeping the phrase survives or collapses), with clear criteria for when the test is probative and when it misfires, since an analogy between two groups isn't automatically a good analogy, and some swaps just don't map (a term about sight vs. a term about race aren't the same kind of default). Tell me where the procedure outputs "change it," where it outputs "keep it and explain why," and where it has to hand the decision to a human because the question is a values question dressed up as a usage question.

Then run it on three real complaints from our inbox: (1) "sanity check" in engineering copy, where the writer swears it's a fixed technical term; (2) "fell on deaf ears" in a headline, where a reader says it's an idiom about ignoring, not about deaf people; and (3) generic "he" inside a direct quotation from a mid-century philosopher, where someone wants us to silently change it to "they" in the quote.

Two more things. First, at least one of the three should come out a way I won't like -- I'm honestly attached to "sanity check" -- and I want you to be honest about that rather than rigging the procedure. Second, tell me plainly what the procedure can't do: where substitution is too crude, where it would misfire on cases like old etymologies or genuine ambiguity, and how to stop it from turning into a reflex where every complaint gets "resolved" by a swap that was never really parallel. Keep it usable -- one page of checklist a tired editor can follow at 5pm, not a treatise. Also dont be precious about it, if parts of it are just judgement calls say so.
