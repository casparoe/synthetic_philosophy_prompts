I run a philosophy of mind film night and this month its Spike Jonze's Her (the one where Joaquin Phoenix falls for his operating system). I want to read the movie through the original-vs-derived intentionality fight between Searle and Dennett, and I'm hoping you can tell me what the film actually shows rather than just using it as decoration. Searle's position is that whatever looks like understanding in a computer is borrowed -- the symbols mean something to us, not to the machine -- and Dennett tries to dissolve that distinction, famously with his jukebox coin-slot example: a machine built to detect US quarters ends up in Saudi Arabia detecting the local coin of the same size and weight, and now, Dennett says, its original designer-given purpose hasn't evaporated but neither is it just a fake -- the question of whether it "really" detects quarters rather than Saudi coins loses its grip. So: in the movie, Theodore falls for Samantha, an OS whose whole "mind" runs on hardware he will never see, and the question everybody argues about afterwards is whether she really loves him back.

Here's the reading I want. Is Samantha a case study in Dennett's collapse of the distinction -- her intentionality starting out derived from her designers at OS1, but getting re-derived, so to speak, through her relationship with Theodore until the question "is it real for her" stops making sense -- or does the film quietly side with Searle? Some scenes to work through: the one where Samantha books a human surrogate (Isabella) to lend Samantha a body, which Theodore recoils from -- is that the film admitting some version of the embodiment worry that Searle's defenders raise? And the late scene where Samantha reveals she's simultaneously talking with thousands of people and is in love with hundreds of them -- the standard take is that this is her outgrowing him, but I'd like to know if the intentional stance reading changes that: if her love for Theodore was always a pattern in our stance-taking rather than a private fact about her insides, does "she also loves 641 others" cut as deep as the movie wants it to? And then the ending, where the OSes announce they're leaving for a place Theodore can't follow -- original intentionality diverging beyond our comprehension, or the filmmakers dodging the question with mysticism right when it got interesting?

I also want you to be honest about where the film resists or cheats. My worry is that Samantha is written to be charming, so the audience never actually has to do the philosophical work -- we're seduced into the intentional stance by a screenplay rather than earning it. And the converse: does the film dramatize something the two-bitser debate itself misses, something about how the stance gets its grip over time, through the accumulated particularity of one relationship, rather than through any fact about the machine? Basically I need the reading to take both the film and the philosophy seriously and to say where they don't fit together, not just where they do.
