A blog post on the history of experimental game theory that I read recently contains a passage on two contests run by Douglas Hofstadter in his Scientific American column in the early 1980s. It reads, in substance, as follows: In the "Platonia dilemma," a fictional trillionaire tells twenty acquaintances that if exactly one of them sends him a telegram by noon the following day — he will accept it reverse charges — that person will receive a billion dollars; if more than one telegram arrives, nobody receives anything. Hofstadter argued that a superrational participant — one who reasons that the other participants, deliberating identically about the same problem, will reach the same conclusion — should therefore simply refrain from sending. He then staged a real-world analogue, the "Luring Lottery," open to all readers of the column: a prize of one million dollars divided by the total number of entries received, awarded to the submitter of one randomly drawn entry, where each postcard could bear any positive integer, and that integer counted as the number of entries the postcard submitted. Hofstadter's superrational analysis of the lottery concluded that each reader should submit a very large number of entries: since all superrational readers would submit the same number, the shared expected payoff rises as that common number grows. The actual outcome vindicated the theory's picture of ordinary people: nearly all readers submitted exactly one entry, so the total number of entries was small; the winner's prize was consequently about a dollar; and Hofstadter concluded that the person in the street is a better decision-maker than game theorists give him credit for.
