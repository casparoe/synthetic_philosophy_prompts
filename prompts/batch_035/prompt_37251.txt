I teach an introductory philosophy of language course, and we've just finished the unit on conditionals: the indicative/subjunctive contrast (compare "If George didn't jump, he'll regret it" with "If George had never been born, he'd have never been missed"), Lewis and Stalnaker on counterfactuals and closest possible worlds, and the old cotenability problem of how to hold fixed just the right background facts while letting the antecedent's consequences ripple outward. For our last meeting before the winter break I want to screen Frank Capra's It's a Wonderful Life (1946) and run the discussion as a case study, and I'd like you to write the reading I'll teach from: a careful philosophical-critical essay that takes both the film and the conditionals literature seriously.

Here are the threads I'd like you to pull on, though please don't treat this list as exhaustive or mandatory -- brainstorm first, and feel free to push back on my framings. Clarence's demonstration has always struck me as philosophically strange: the antecedent is blatantly counterfactual (George knows perfectly well that he was born), and yet the film presents the no-George world as something George walks around in and observes, and he seems to update on it as though it were evidence -- can a counterfactual be verified by observation, or does the film quietly convert it into an indicative ("here is what is now the case in Bedford Falls") as a dramatic trick, and does that trick teach students something true about conditionals or something false? Then there's the closest-world question: is Pottersville really the nearest world where George never existed, or has Clarence cherry-picked a world selected for rhetorical effect? Consider the cotenability puzzles in the details: Harry's grave says he drowned as a child, but in the nearest world with no George, would Harry even have been sledding on the pond that day? Mary works at the library, alone and bitter -- is that the closest outcome, or a convenient one? And the lovely trans-world oddity of Zuzu's petals, which travel in George's pocket from the actual world into the counterfactual one and back: what would Lewis say about an object that survives a change of world? Also worth thinking about: the film never confirms any of the counterfactuals -- the ending returns us to the actual world, and the no-George world evaporates unverified -- and whether the conditional Clarence most needs George to grasp ("if you had never existed, the town and the people you love would have been worse off") is the kind of conditional closest-world semantics was built to handle, or the kind that depends on picking a world for its point rather than its proximity.

Please take the role of a philosopher of language who is also a patient, fair film critic. Be faithful in both directions: don't reduce the film to an illustration of Lewis, and don't bend the semantics to fit the plot; where the film resists the reading, say so plainly. Since we'll be working from clips and memory rather than the script, work from the major scenes as commonly known and paraphrase dialogue rather than quoting it. I'd like the essay to reach a verdict: does the film vindicate the closest-world approach by dramatizing how counterfactuals earn their truth, does it expose its limits by showing that the counterfactuals we care about when evaluating a life are selected for salience rather than similarity, or is something else going on? And close by telling me what you think the film adds to the indicative/counterfactual debate that the papers don't -- I want my students to leave the screening feeling they've watched a philosophy of language argument, not just a Christmas movie.
