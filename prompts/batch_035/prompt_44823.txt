Hi, so I'm a novelist, and the book I'm drafting has a pivotal scene where a shrinking mill town votes on whether to keep funding their little volunteer fire department, and the whole third act turns on people quietly deciding it's not worth showing up to the town meeting. I've been reading up on the paradox of voting and I get it intellectually (the famous calculus where you multiply the benefit of your preferred outcome by the minuscule chance you're the pivotal voter, subtract the cost of participating, and get a number that says stay home) but I can't write the scene until I've felt the thing myself, and I don't trust myself to fake it.

So here's my ask: my writing group is coming over next month — 10 people, we meet at my place for an evening, they're used to me making them do weird exercises (last time I had them write the same scene three times as an unreliable narrator). Design a game, maybe 90 minutes total, where they actually face the choice of whether to vote, over several rounds, with costs and payoffs that feel real but involve no actual money — I'm thinking things like who gets first pick of the snacks, who's exempt from hosting duties for the next three months, who has to read their pages last during critique. Arrange it so the chance of any one person's ballot mattering is genuinely tiny (split the group, stagger things, whatever works — you figure out the mechanics) but the stakes of the group outcome are felt. I want at least a couple of them to experience that flicker where they're holding the pen and doing the arithmetic and something other than arithmetic starts tugging at them — and I want others to cheerfully defect and go refill their wine while the rest vote, and to feel nothing.

Then give me a debrief that maps what actually happened onto the pieces of the classic decision-theoretic setup (the pivotality probability, the cost, and that extra term Riker and Ordeshook bolted on for duty or expression or whatever it is) without it turning into me lecturing tired writers at 10pm. And be honest with me about which parts of real turnout a living room with friends and snack-based stakes just cannot reproduce, plus what will predictably go sideways the first time I run it — I have one guy who will absolutely try to game whatever rules you write, so tell me how to handle him. Keep in mind these are sharp people but none of them are political scientists, so no jargon the can't pick up from the debrief itself. Also if there's a way to work in a round where they can vote strategically rather than sincerely I'd love that, since the fire-department scene has a character who votes for an option she doesn't want, but dont let that clutter the main thing.
