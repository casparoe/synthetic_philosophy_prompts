I'm a second-year undergrad taking a political philosophy module, and we've just had a seminar on Mill's On Liberty that mostly got eaten up by arguments about whether hate speech counts as harm, so I went back to the text afterwards and found chapter 4 much stranger than the seminar made it sound. There's this passage near the beginning that I keep rereading and I honestly can't work out what Mill is doing:

"Though society is not founded on a contract, and though no good purpose is answered by inventing a contract in order to deduce social obligations from it, every one who receives the protection of society owes a return for the benefit, and the fact of living in society renders it indispensable that each should be bound to observe a certain line of conduct towards the rest. This conduct consists first, in not injuring the interests of one another; or rather certain interests, which, either by express legal provision or by tacit understanding, ought to be considered as rights; and secondly, in each person's bearing his share (to be fixed on some equitable principle) of the labours and sacrifices incurred for defending the society or its members from injury and molestation. These conditions society is justified in enforcing at all costs to those who endeavour to withhold fulfilment. Nor is this all that society may do. The acts of an individual may be hurtful to others, or wanting in due consideration for their welfare, without going the length of violating any of their constituted rights. The offender may then be justly punished by opinion, though not by law."

A few things are bugging me. First, the opening: he flatly denies society is founded on a contract, one assumes deliberately sticking it to Hobbes and Locke, but then immediately says everyone who receives protection "owes a return for the benefit" — which to me sounds suspiciously like tacit consent with the serial numbers filed off. Am I missing a difference, or is this a genuine tension? Also that "enforcing at all costs" phrase seems really strong coming from the guy whose whole book is about limiting what society can do to people. Second, there's the little "or rather" in the middle where the first duty quietly shrinks from not injuring anyone's interests into not violating certain interests-that-count-as-rights — that redefinition seems to do a LOT of work for his whole framework, since if you define rights narrowly you gut the harm principle and if you define them widely it swallows liberty, no? And third, the last bit about being "punished by opinion, though not by law" — in chapter 1 he goes on about the tyranny of the prevailing opinion and feeling being more insidious than political tyranny, so how can informal social sanction be simultaneously the disease and the cure? If you could walk me through what's going on in the passage, especially how the "or rather" move fits with the famous bit later in the chapter where Mill lets you warn a drunk man not to cross an unsafe bridge, I'd be really grateful. My tutor keeps saying the harm principle is deceptively simple and I'm starting to see what she means, in the sense that it seems to be doing about five jobs at once.
