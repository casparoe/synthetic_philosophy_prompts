I've been reading about the debate over whether machines could ever genuinely have beliefs and desires, or merely mimic them, and a seminar handout I was given summarizes the disagreement between John Searle and Daniel Dennett roughly as follows: 'Searle holds that intentionality -- the aboutness of mental states -- comes in two kinds. Words and pictures have only derived intentionality: they are about things only because we, their users, interpret them that way. Brains, by contrast, have original (or intrinsic) intentionality: they are genuinely about things in themselves, without any external interpreter. This is why a computer cannot really believe anything: symbols in a program are about things only in the derived sense, so no software will ever have real beliefs. Dennett replied with his two-bitser story. A vending machine accepts quarters and rejects slugs; it is, in effect, designed to discriminate between genuine and counterfeit intentional states. But imagine the machine transported to Panama, where the balboa is a coin the same size and weight as the U.S. quarter. There the machine will happily dispense candy for balboas, which it was supposed to treat as counterfeit. This shows that intentionality is relative to a practice of interpretation, and hence that there is no further fact of the matter about original intentionality: since even our own "original" states can be reinterpreted this way, Searle's distinction collapses, and Searle's view is thereby refuted.'
