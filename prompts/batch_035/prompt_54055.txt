I work on entity resolution for a large knowledge graph, and a recurring design decision has made me realize I spend my days re-fighting a fifty-year-old battle in philosophy of language. When my system discovers that two descriptions in text — "the morning star" and "the evening star" — denote the same object, it faces a choice: merge them into a single node and inherit the consequences (anything said of either is now said of both, and the identity statement itself becomes a non-event), or keep the two guises separate and maintain a separate layer of asserted identities on top. My colleagues argue about this in a way that maps eerily onto the Frege–Mill dispute: the mergers say co-reference just is sameness of semantic content, and whatever else distinguishes the two expressions is a matter of history or cognitive habit; the separationists reply that if the identity of two co-referring expressions were contentless, nobody could learn anything from being told that the morning star is the evening star — and yet Babylonian astronomers plainly did learn something. Half a century of seminars seems not to have moved either side. What I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict: is this a substantive disagreement about the semantics of identity statements, or a verbal one about the word "meaning"? Or are the two camps answering different questions — one about the compositional contribution of a name to truth-conditions, the other about what a cognitive or information system must distinguish in order to store, update, and transmit knowledge — that have been run together under one heading? I half suspect the latter, and my pipeline is my evidence: it needs both the merged node and the guises, because they do different jobs, and it needs an account of how they interface. If my diagnosis is right, what would count as progress — a principled division of labor between the two levels, a formal treatment of the interface? If it's wrong, say why, and tell me what kind of result, empirical or formal, could actually settle the underlying disagreement.
