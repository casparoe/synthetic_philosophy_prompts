I'm in an animal ethics course this semester and we just finished a unit on insect welfare, which honestly broke my brain a little. We have a short essay due soon and I want to get a head start on drafting it. The topic I chose is the scale argument, and I'd like you to write the essay.

Quick setup so we're on the same page: the argument is that even if the probability that any given insect is sentient is tiny, there are something like 10^18 of them around at any moment (plus trillions more being farmed as feed, e.g. black soldier fly larvae), so the expected total of insect suffering could plausibly dwarf all the suffering in farmed vertebrates combined. If you take expected value seriously, maybe the most important thing we could do for animals is reduce insect suffering, even while being pretty unsure insects feel anything at all. That's the claim in question.

The essay should weigh the main arguments on both sides and then actually land somewhere — no fence-sitting at the end, though a conditional verdict like "the argument goes through if you're an expectational utilitarian, and that's a reason to give up expectational utilitarianism" is totally allowed.

A few things I want the essay to engage with specifically:

1. My professor floated what she called the "evidential discount view" — I'm not sure whether it's a real position in the literature or her own coinage, so treat it as a proposal. The idea: when you're uncertain whether a being is sentient at all, you don't just multiply its welfare by your credence in sentience and carry on; you instead discount beings below some evidential threshold to zero for decision-making purposes, so that speculative trillions don't swamp concrete suffering you're confident about. Is this a principled stopping point, or is it an ad hoc patch that just refuses to bite the bullet wherever the numbers get uncomfortable? And is the threshold idea even coherent — what would make a threshold non-arbitrary?

2. The obvious comparison is Pascal's mugging (tiny probability, astronomically large payoff). Is the insect case different in a way that matters — e.g. because the probabilities here aren't astronomically small, or because we have an actual evidence base about insect nervous systems — or does the mugger analogy show the whole expected-value move is broken in the same way for both?

3. Worth touching on at some point: even granting sentience, we can't really individuate the interests involved. Is the welfare-bearing unit the individual ant, or does a colony-level organization change what we're even counting when we multiply?

Assume the reader has done intro ethics (utilitarianism, expected value, the basics of moral uncertainty) but hasn't read anything specific about insects, so don't lean on jargon without a sentence of explanation.
