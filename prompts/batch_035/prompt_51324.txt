You are a philosopher specializing in rationality and practical reasoning, comfortable with formal decision theory but skeptical of its overextension into ordinary life. I need you to adjudicate an argument I witnessed and could not referee.

My brother Daniel is in year seven of a PhD in history. His dissertation has stalled, he describes his days with something between dread and boredom, and last month he received an offer to leave for a job he actually wants. At a family dinner, his partner Sam and my cousin Lena argued about whether he should take it.

(1) Sam said the case is a textbook sunk cost: the seven years are spent either way, so only the remaining costs and benefits can count, and by Daniel's own account the remaining work is worth less to him than the job would be. Continuing so that the years won't have been wasted makes nothing un-wasted.

(2) Lena granted that that rationale is confused, but said Sam's principle proves too much. We keep promises and see through long engagements even when the next stretch looks bad, partly because being known as someone who completes things is an asset — Daniel's committee, his referees, and Daniel himself have all staked something on his being a finisher. The past is not sealed off from reasons; it is where obligations and reputations live.

(3) Sam answered that there was no promise — enrolling is not an undertaking to finish — and that the committee's investment is simply the fallacy again, seen from the other side: you cannot rescue one sunk-cost error by committing a reciprocal one.

(4) Lena replied that the deeper issue is epistemic. Only the future matters is fine as accounting, but the past is where all the evidence about Daniel lives: if seven miserable years predict two more miserable years, that is a reason to quit, and by the same token a happy seven years would have counted in favor of continuing. So past states bear on the decision as evidence; the real question is whether past costs can bear on it as costs, and Sam has asserted that they cannot rather than shown it.

(5) Sam responded that once evidence is doing the work, nothing honors sunk costs anymore — the case for quitting only got stronger — and that the reputation point cuts both ways: a grimly finished dissertation reads as finishing only until someone opens it.

(6) Lena's last word was that this shows the fallacy label cannot be applied from inside. A person who chose, in advance, the rule of seeing things through when the finish is achievable and the start-up losses are large cannot tell, midstream, whether he is executing that rule or rationalizing a loss — and a principle that labels every continuation a fallacy is not a diagnosis but a stipulation.

They then turned to me, and I honestly could not say who had won what. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the six numbered steps, say whether it is right, partly right, or mistaken, and why. Then tell me who is more right overall, and identify what, if anything, both of them are missing. Before you answer, take a moment to brainstorm where the argument could legitimately turn — in particular, keep separate the three roles the past is being asked to play here, as cost, as evidence, and as source of obligations — and do not let the word fallacy do any work the argument has not earned.
