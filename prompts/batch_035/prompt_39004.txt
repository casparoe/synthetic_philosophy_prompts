I'm trying to work through Darwin's chapter on the moral sense in The Descent of Man and honestly it's doing my head in a little. He opens by saying the moral sense or conscience is "by far the most important" difference between us and other animals, quotes Kant going on about duty and asking "whence thy original?", and then basically announces he's going to answer that exact question with natural history. His core claim:

"any animal whatever, endowed with well-marked social instincts, the parental and filial affections being here included, would inevitably acquire a moral sense or conscience, as soon as its intellectual powers had become as well, or nearly as well developed, as in man."

And the mechanism, in the second step of his argument:

"Secondly, as soon as the mental faculties had become highly developed, images of all past actions and motives would be incessantly passing through the brain of each individual: and that feeling of dissatisfaction, or even misery, which invariably results, as we shall hereafter see, from any unsatisfied instinct, would arise, as often as it was perceived that the enduring and always present social instinct had yielded to some other instinct, at the time stronger, but neither enduring in its nature, nor leaving behind it a very vivid impression."

So conscience = the social instinct sticking around and nagging after some transient instinct (hunger, fear, lust) got satisfied and faded. Fine, I think I get that part. Then the bit I keep going back and forth on, the hive-bees:

"If, for instance, to take an extreme case, men were reared under precisely the same conditions as hive-bees, there can hardly be a doubt that our unmarried females would, like the worker-bees, think it a sacred duty to kill their brothers, and mothers would strive to kill their fertile daughters; and no one would think of interfering."

And immediately after that:

"In this case an inward monitor would tell the animal that it would have been better to have followed the one impulse rather than the other. The one course ought to have been followed, and the other ought not; the one would have been right and the other wrong; but to these terms I shall recur."

Here's what I can't get straight. Is the "ought" in that last sentence supposed to be read from the inside — this is how it would feel to the bee — or is Darwin himself asserting that the killing really ought to happen? He says he'll come back to these terms, and when he does (I don't have the book in front of me right now, so paraphrasing from memory) he seems to say that the imperious word ought basically just amounts to being conscious of a persistent instinct, innate or partly acquired, that serves as a guide even when it's disobeyed. If that's his considered view, then "X is wrong" seems to cash out as "X went against the more persistant instinct" — but that can't be all of it, because he also talks about the moral sense being extended by reason and sympathy until it covers the whole tribe, then the nation, then all sentient beings. If ought is literally just the lingering ache of a social instinct that lost a fight at the moment of action, what exactly is doing the pushing when conscience "expands"? The instinct evolved for the small band; it shouldn't care about strangers at all. Is the expansion still the instinct talking, or has something else (habit, reasoning, public opinion) quietly taken over the work while Darwin keeps calling it the moral sense?

So my basic question: is Darwin explaining why creatures like us feel the pull of ought, or is he explaining ought away? And if it's the second, doesn't his own evaluative language throughout the chapter ("by far the most important," "the most noble of all the attributes of man") become a problem for him — he sounds like he's ranking moralities, not just describing them? I'm fine with the answer being "he's just not careful here" btw, but I'd want to know what the more charitable readings cost.
