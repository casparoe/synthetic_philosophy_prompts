I chair the nosology committee that will decide, for the next revision of our national psychiatric classification manual, whether to admit a proposed diagnosis provisionally titled Maladaptive Absorption Disorder. The proposal, submitted by a group of clinicians and self-advocates, describes a syndrome of many hours per day spent in elaborate, immersive daydreaming — often with pacing or repetitive movement as an accompaniment — accompanied by distress at lost time and impaired functioning at school or work. The evidence base is thin but not empty: a handful of small clinic samples, several hundred self-identified cases recruited through online communities, and some neuroimaging work whose quality is contested. What has unsettled the committee is the history of the category: it was to a significant extent brought into being by patients themselves, who found each other on forums, named the condition, developed symptom checklists, and lobbied for research before any working group had studied them. Two of my colleagues argue this is disqualifying — they invoke Ian Hacking's ideas about looping effects and "making up people": once the manual blesses the category, clinicians will look for it, adolescents searching for an explanation for ordinary imaginative absorption will find a checklist that tells them they are disordered, and the classified population will change in virtue of being classified, leaving us diagnosing a moving target we helped set in motion. The proposers reply, first, that this is true of many categories we already carry, from social anxiety to ADHD, and we did not therefore delete them; second, that refusing the label leaves real sufferers without access to care, research funding, and self-understanding.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate to the committee before our final vote. It must recommend one of three outcomes — full inclusion, exclusion, or inclusion with conditions (e.g., in a section for conditions requiring further study, with a mandated revision cycle) — and give the reasoning in terms the losing side could actually engage with, not just a recital of the evidence hierarchy. I want you to take the looping-effect objection seriously as an epistemological point about what kind of object a diagnostic category is, not merely as a worry about overdiagnosis, and to say plainly whether it yields a reason against admitting this category that it does not yield against our existing ones — or whether, if the objection really does generalize, that shows the objection is doing no distinctive work here. Address the practical stakes too: what happens to the self-identified community if we exclude them, and whether a "further study" designation can be squared with the fact that the community's own definitions have already shaped the samples any study would draw on. Please commit to a recommendation and defend it; do not close by handing the trade-offs back to the committee to weigh.
