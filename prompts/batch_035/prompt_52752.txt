My reading group has spent most of a year on the expanding circle idea — the Singer thing where moral progress is the circle of moral concern getting wider, slaves then women then (maybe) animals — and the argument is completely stuck. I don't want a verdict on who's right; I want a diagnosis of why it's stuck and what kind of disagreement it actually is.

The two camps, concretely. The expansionists say the pattern is real and the mechanism is something like rational consistency: once you grant that suffering matters wherever it occurs, you can't give a principled reason for excluding the next group, so the exclusion eventually erodes. The skeptics push back with history: moral revolutions seem to ride on honor and status contests, not consistency arguments (the Appiah moral-revolutions line), and the circle metaphor hides that "widening" in one place often traveled with narrowing somewhere else — abolition coexisting with hardening racial categories in the colonies, that kind of thing. They also complain the metaphor smuggles in a picture of history as monotonic when actual history is full of contractions, including some spectacular ones in the most self-described "enlightened" century.

Here's where it stalls: everyone agrees the circle is a metaphor. Everyone agrees some exclusions got dismantled. And then nobody moves an inch, for months. My working hypothesis — presented as a hypothesis, tell me if it holds up — is that the two sides are answering two seperate questions without noticing: the expansionists are doing normative forecasting ("here's what consistency will require next") and reading history as evidence about the direction, while the skeptics are doing causal history ("here's what actually moved things") and reading the normative stuff as a promissory note nobody ever cashed. Is that right? Or is there a real empirical dispute hiding in there — say, over whether consistency arguments did actual causal work in the historical campaigns, versus post-hoc rationalizations of shifts that honor and status dynamics had already produced? That one seems testable in principle (you'd look at what campaigners said to each other behind the scenes versus what they said in public), but I genuinely can't tell whether both camps would accept that as the crux, and I'd like your view.

And then there's the thing nobody in the group has an answer to. Someone floated the idea that moral concern might work like a fixed budget — the circle can get wider while the concern per entity inside it gets thinner, so "expansion" and "progress" quietly come apart. Maybe what looks like widening concern for animals is partly just reclassification (pets moved into the family column) rather than more concern going around in total. If that's even half right, does it dissolve the whole debate, or does it just relocate it to a fight about the bookkeeping — how you'd even measure "amount of concern" across a population? I have no idea and neither does anyone else in the room.

So, given all that: what kind of disagreement is this, really? Verbal, empirical, standards-of-evidence, bedrock intuitions about history that nobody can argue for? And depending on your diagnosis, be honest with me about whether anything could count as progress — a sharper distinction, a better metaphor, some agreed way of scoring expansions against contractions — or whether this is one of those debates with no fact of the matter and we should just drop it and pick a new book.

One caveat: we're philosophers, not historians, so if part of your diagnosis turns on a substantive historical claim, flag it as a claim rather than reciting it as settled fact.
