I've been reading about George Berkeley's economics stuff, not the idealism -- his book The Querist from the 1730s where he argues that money isn't really about the silver in a coin, it's basically a ticket or counter that society agrees to accept, and that Ireland was poor not because it lacked precious metal but because the tickets weren't circulating in the right hands. He even floated a national bank for Ireland to issue paper money and get industry going. What I want to know is: if you dropped Berkeley into 2025 with this stuff in his head, what would he actually make of modern money? Concretely: (a) Bitcoin -- a money with no issuer, fixed supply, value purely by agreement, but designed specifically to resist any state or bank managing it. Does that vindicate his "it's all just counters" view or does it break it, since he thought the whole point of money was to be *administered* toward productive ends? (b) Stablecoins and CBDCs -- issuers promising redemption, reserves somewhere, claims of stability. I can imagine him liking these but I'm not sure whether he'd treat the peg as the real thing or the reserves as a relapse into thinking value needs some "real" backing, which he was against in the coin case. And (c) would his underlying motivation even transfer? He cared about money as a tool for developing an exploited colony -- Ireland's wealth draining to England. What's the modern analog he'd latch onto, maybe developing countries that keep getting dollarized, or capital flight, or something else entirely? Try to actually reason from his positions rather than just saying "he'd probably like X" -- and where his commitments conflict (e.g., conventionalism about value vs. wanting centralized control), tell me which one you think wins and why.
