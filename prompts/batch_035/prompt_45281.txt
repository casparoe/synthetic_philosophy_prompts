I'm writing an essay on Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go for a seminar on population ethics, and I want you to respond as a professional philosopher who knows the procreation ethics literature cold. Read the novel through the procreation asymmetry — the intuition that it's wrong to bring into existence a life of suffering, while there's no mirror-image duty to bring happy people into existence. The clones were created solely to donate their organs, which sounds like the paradigm of an act the asymmetry condemns. But here's what I keep running into: Kathy's life looks pretty good to her. Hailsham, Ruth, Tommy, the quiet contentment of her narration. If a clone's life is genuinely worth living, on what basis do we say creating her was wrong — and does the novel know the difference between "a life not worth living" and "a life created for someone else's purposes"? Or is Ishiguro really staging the non-identity problem instead, since the clones owe their existence to the very system that will kill them and so can't coherently wish it away? I'm also interested in the guardians' and Madame's shame — Madame visibly recoils from the students early on, and I read that shame as tracking the asymmetry: the guardians believe the students would have been better off never existing, even though the students themselves would never agree. Is that a good reading or am I projecting a philosophical distinction onto a novel about acceptance? Please brainstorm for a moment before answering, be honest about where the novel resists the asymmetry framing (it's not clear Ishiguro ever endorses the claim that the clones were harmed), and tell me what the novel shows that the standard arguments don't. Answer in American English, and don't pad it — the essay has a word limit.
