Bit of background so you know where I'm coming from: I've been hanging around the rationalist corner of the internet since the Sequences days, mostly as a lurker (LessWrong, the EA Forum, occasional dips into the population ethics blog wars that flare up whenever someone mentions the far future). Earlier this year a friend and I finally caved and started a small podcast doing long-form interviews with philosophers, and honestly people are shockingly willing to talk to a nobody with a decent mic. Next week's guest is a philosopher who defends the procreation asymmetry, and I want to be sharper than the usual back and forth you see in blog comment sections.

Here's my understanding of her view, so you can aim the questions at it. Her version of the asymmetry is NOT the claim that adding happy lives makes the world better and adding miserable lives makes it worse, nor even the claim that the badness of the one outweighs the goodness of the other. It's about deliberation: when you're deciding whether to bring a child into existence, that child's prospective welfare can count as a consideration AGAINST creating her (if her life would go badly enough), but the very same prospective welfare cannot count as a reason FOR creating her, even if her life would go wonderfully. It's a asymmetry in the reasons available to you, not in the value of outcomes. She claims this gets the intuitive verdicts (don't create miserable people; creating happy people is at most a nice-to-have, not something you owe anyone) without swallowing the person-affecting restriction wholesale, and, she says, without running headfirst into the impossibility results in population ethics.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions. Requirements:

- At least one question pressing on whether the deliberative version really escapes the impossibility results or just changes the subject — she now owes us not a value ranking of outcomes but a story about which deliberations are permissible, and I want to ask whether that's escaping Arrhenius-style problems (Sadistic Conclusion and friends) or just declining to play. The likely reply is that the impossibility theorems only bite views that make claims about betterness, so build in a follow-up that asks what her view then says to someone choosing between two possible future populations — can she give them ANY guidance, or only veto certain options?
- One on the non-identity problem. If the reasons don't come from outcome value, where exactly do they come from in a choice between two different possible children, one of whom would have a somewhat worse life than the other (the classic case is choosing, via timing or other means, between bringing about a child with a disability and a different child without)? The standard move is to say the worse-off child's prospective welfare is a reason against that choice — but then it looks like it IS functioning as a positive reason for the alternative, which seems to give back the symmetric reason she denied. Follow-up keyed to however she's most likely to wriggle out.
- One question from our side of the fence, so to speak: my audience is full of people who work on x-risk reduction on the theory that future people's welfare counts NOW as a reason to act. On her view, does saving the lives of never-yet-conceived future generations generate any reason at all? She'll probably distinguish making people exist from helping people who will exist anyway, so the follow-up should be about whether that distinction does any work once you're deciding under uncertainty about whether humanity even survives — is there a version of her view that isn't just quietly hostile to longtermism, and would she bite that bullet on air in front of an audience that mostly does EA?
- Each main question needs its follow-up written out.

Also, one tactical note: she is reportedly very good at the move "that's a different question — that's about the ethics of our attitudes, not about reasons for action." If you can, structure at least one question so that this deflection is harder to deploy (e.g., by forcing both halves of the issue into a single concrete case where the answer to one obviously constrains the answer to the other).

No "what drew you to this topic" fluff, please — we can do the origin story over the intro music ourselves.
