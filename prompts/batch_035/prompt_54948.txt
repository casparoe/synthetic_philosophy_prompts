I am a retired physical chemist who spent the better part of four decades on problems of measurement, instrument calibration, and the reporting of results, and I have taken up philosophy seriously only since leaving the laboratory. I have lately been working through Kant's short essay on the supposed right to lie from philanthropy, in which he argues that you may not lie to a would-be murderer at the door who asks where his intended victim is hiding. The standard treatments I have encountered seem to me to talk past the argument rather than engage it: critics denounce the conclusion as monstrous, while defenders mostly restate it, and neither side examines the actual structure of the claim Kant makes about what lying does.

Here is the thought I want pursued, and it comes from my own background. In the sciences we maintain something close to an exceptionless norm against fabricating data, and we hold to it even though one can easily describe cases in which a single undetected fabrication would avert some grave outcome. The justification, as I understand it, is not that anyone has calculated the consequences of each individual act of falsification and found them bad. It is that the entire practice of reporting measurements rests on the reliability of assertion as such, and a norm riddled with case-by-case exceptions is not one that anyone can depend on at the point of use, precisely because a fabrication is normally invisible to the person who later relies on it. Kant's claim that a lie, even where it wrongs no particular individual, wrongs humanity generally by destroying the trustworthiness of statements strikes me as a very early statement of this kind of thought. So the question I want the essay to answer is this: can an argument of this systemic type — an argument about the practice of assertion rather than about the consequences of the individual act — actually ground an exceptionless prohibition? In the scientific case the norm works, I suspect, because of a verification asymmetry: since fabrication is undetectable downstream, the only point at which the rule can bite is the moment of assertion itself. But in the murderer-at-the-door case the potential cost of the lie is so immediate and so concentrated that the analogy is under real strain, and the essay should say exactly where it breaks, if it does.

The essay should therefore weigh, at minimum, three things. First, whether the systemic reading of Kant is exegetically plausible or a modern retrofit; I gather that scholars dispute how the essay relates to the rest of his ethics, and that some read Kant's other texts as permitting carefully chosen truthful statements designed to mislead — if any of this is contested, please present it as contested rather than settling it by fiat. Second, whether the systemic argument, stated in its strongest form, in fact smuggles consequentialism back in: it appears to weigh a diffuse risk to the practice of assertion against the immediate death of a person, and a rule whose justification is a ledger of risks looks as though it ought to be revisable at the margins — which is exactly what Kant denies. Third, whether the comparison with data-integrity norms in science holds at the level of enforcement, given that the scientific community can expel a falsifier after the fact while the door-case must be decided in an instant. Throughout, please separate what is settled among people who know this literature from what is genuinely contested, and do not present one interpretive school's reading as the consensus. At the same time, do not end on a merely diplomatic note: I want a defended verdict on whether the data-fabrication analogy vindicates Kant's position, refutes it, or reveals that the real dispute lies somewhere else entirely.
