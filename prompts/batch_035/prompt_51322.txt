My flatmate, a committed Quinean, has produced an argument whose conclusion I can't accept even though each step looks defensible on its own:

(1) By the indeterminacy of translation, all possible behavioral evidence about a remote tribe is compatible with several rival translation manuals that render their sentences into English in systematically different ways — their word "gavagai" comes out as "rabbit" on one manual and as "undetached rabbit part" on another — and nothing in nature makes one manual the correct one.

(2) The same holds at home: our own word "rabbit" could, consistently with everything we would ever say or do, be reinterpreted as applying to undetached rabbit parts, with the rest of our scheme adjusted so that no possible observation or behavior distinguishes the reinterpretation from the familiar reading — this is just Quine's inscrutability of reference, which he extended to our own language without reservation.

(3) By Quine's criterion of ontological commitment, what a theory says there is amounts to what its bound variables must range over when the theory is optimally regimented.

(4) But by (2), what the variables of the regimented theory range over is itself fixed only relative to a background scheme; a proxy function can shift the domain from rabbits to rabbit-parts while preserving every consequence we could ever check.

(5) So there is no fact of the matter, not even a shaky one, about what our own theory says exists — whether it is committed to rabbits or to rabbit-parts — any more than there is about what "gavagai" means.

(6) Therefore Quine's criterion of ontological commitment, the doctrine he is most famous for, delivers no determinate answer to the very question it was designed to answer.

The conclusion seems to prove too much: it would follow that we cannot so much as state what our best science says there is. My suspicion is that the slide occurs in (2), which treats reinterpretation of our own words as on a par with translation between languages — inside English, after all, we can ask whether this is the same rabbit as that one, and rabbits and undetached rabbit parts seem to answer that question differently. But my flatmate replies that Quine himself endorsed (2), so rejecting it means rejecting Quine rather than repairing him; and he adds that even if (2) fell, the foreign case in (1) would leave the natives' ontology as open as ever, so the collapse in (5)–(6) would just relocate. If I must give something up, I would rather keep (1) and surrender the absoluteness of ontological commitment than keep absolute commitment and lose (1) — but I want to know what that purchase costs. Which step should go, why did it look fine, and what does each available way out pay? Or is the argument in fact sound, with the "too much" merely apparent — relative ontological commitment being all the commitment there ever was?
