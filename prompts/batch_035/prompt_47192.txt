I run a small monthly discussion group on philosophy of mind — two academic philosophers, the rest of us motivated amateurs — and next month's topic is whether conscious experience does any causal work. For the session I want a decision tree handout, something like "Where do you actually stand on the causal status of experience?", that starts from a commitment anyone in the room will grant and walks them, question by question, to a named position.

Build the tree from the logical space first, then locate the positions in it, rather than the other way around. I'd expect the load-bearing questions to include some version of the following, though you should adjust or add as needed:

1. Whether there is something it is like to have an experience over and above all the functional and physical facts.
2. Whether the conceivability of a physical duplicate of you with no inner life says anything about its possibility.
3. Whether physics is causally closed (no gaps for anything nonphysical to do work in).
4. And the one I care most about: what causes your sincere utterance "I'm having a red experience right now." If experience is not physical and physics is closed, that utterance has to be fully caused by physical events — which makes experience look both idle and somehow still needed to explain what the utterance is *about*. This is the knot the tree has to honor rather than dissolve, so at least one node must force the reader to face it directly, e.g. by asking whether a zombie duplicate of them would report its experiences the same way they do.

The leaves should include: type-A or eliminative physicalism, type-B or phenomenal-concept physicalism, reductive representationalism, interactionist dualism, epiphenomenalism, Russellian monism (split it into panpsychist and neutral/protophenomenalist leaves if you think the questions actually separate them), and illusionism — but only if you can route someone to that leaf without having begged one of the earlier questions.

Constraints on the instrument itself:

- No more than five binary questions and no more than eight leaves total.
- Every node must be a concrete case or thought experiment a motivated amateur can answer without knowing which position it feeds — Mary before and after release, blindsight, a perfect actor, a duplicate reporting its inner life — never a question about doctrine or labels. Technical names may appear only at the leaves.
- Identify at least two combinations of answers that no one occupies or that are unstable under reflection, and diagnose each in a sentence: incoherent, self-undermining, or merely unexplored.
- Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate given the questions available, and say what further question would pull them apart, if one exists. Don't paper over it.

Finally — and this is what I actually want from you — commit. After presenting the tree, tell me which leaf you judge to survive the problem of explaining our judgments about experience best, and defend that verdict in a paragraph: give the strongest objection to your choice, say whether it succeeds, and don't hedge into a tour of the options. The group is going to spend two hours arguing with whatever you pick, so pick something.
