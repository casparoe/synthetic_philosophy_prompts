I serve on the professional conduct panel of our national licensing body for structural engineers, and our current case has quietly turned into a philosophy seminar about what the word "intentional" is actually doing in our bylaws. The case: a licensee, under severe budget pressure on a hospital project, switched the curtain-wall specification to a cheaper glazing system after being told in writing that the change would have a side effect — the building would exceed the energy code's performance targets by a wide margin. His reply, which the panel has in the record, was that he didn't care about the energy numbers, he only cared about cutting facade costs, and he signed off. The bylaws direct us to impose the severe track of sanctions for "intentional breaches" of the sustainability standard and a milder track for breaches that were merely foreseeable. Half the panel holds that this was plainly not intentional: his aim was cost, the energy overrun was foreseen but not sought, so it belongs on the milder track. The other half holds that knowingly driving the building over the limit in order to get something else just is an intentional breach, since nothing about the outcome was accidental.

A third colleague has complicated everything by pointing at the experimental philosophy work on side-effect intentionality — Joshua Knobe's chairman studies from 2003 and the literature they sparked, where laypeople's judgments about whether a foreseen side effect was brought about intentionally flip depending on whether the side effect is harmful or helpful. Her worry is that our shared concept of intention may already be partly a verdict of blame wearing a factual label. And she has a wrinkle she insists we confront before voting: the same glazing change also incidentally improved daylighting in the patient rooms, which unexpectedly helped the project earn a wellness credit — and none of us, on either side of the split, is even slightly tempted to say the member "intentionally" earned that credit, even though his mental state toward it was exactly the same as his mental state toward the energy overrun. Her argument is that if our application of the word "intentional" tracks whether we like the outcome rather than anything in the member's head, then the finding as drafted is unfair — to him, or to the public, depending on which way the asymmetry cuts — and the panel should say so in the opinion rather than launder a blame judgment through a technical-sounding mental-state term.

Draft the written opinion I'll circulate before the hearing. It must recommend a concrete disposition — which track the breach belongs on, what range of sanction follows, and whether to recommend that the council clarify or amend the bylaws language — and it must lead with that recommendation in its very first sentence, followed by the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the facts of the case. Please do not turn the opinion into a survey of the experimental philosophy literature; invoke the harm/help asymmetry only insofar as it bears on how the panel should locate the line the bylaws draw between "intentional" and "foreseeable," and take seriously the possibility that the honest conclusion is that the bylaws as written cannot do the work the panel wants them to do. The opinion should give a fair and serious treatment of the strongest argument on the side it rejects, not a strawman of it, and it should be a document my colleagues on both halves of the split could sign without feeling their position was misrepresented — even the ones who lose.
