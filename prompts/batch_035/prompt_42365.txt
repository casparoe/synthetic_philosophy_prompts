I'm a co-founder of a worker-owned platform cooperative — we're a software services firm, about forty people, structured as a multi-stakeholder co-op with an internal labor market and a revolving equity pool. I'm fluent in the vocabulary of cooperative economics: patronage dividends, member buy-in and exit equity, surplus allocation, the old debate between labor-managed and capital-managed firms, degrowth-era critiques of the kibbutz wage compression problem, Mondragón's tiered membership, and the trust-economics literature on why cooperative banks underperform shareholder banks on growth but outperform them on survival through downturns. I also know my Sidgwick from my Bentham, so you don't need to baby me on utilitarianism itself.

Here's what I want. John Stuart Mill spent his last decade or so drifting from classical liberal political economy toward something he thought of as a friendly, qualified socialism — roughly, the view that worker cooperatives should outcompete capitalist firms once capital barriers fell and workers acquired the habits of cooperation, that the moral test of any economic arrangement is how it develops the capacities of the participants, and that while the distribution of property was a matter of social convention rather than natural right, he remained deeply wary of state-owned, centrally-directed production and of equality imposed by fiat. He sketched all this in his late writings on labour, cooperation, and property — never fully systematized, and in some cases published after his death.

Restate Mill's late position on socialism, as precisely as you can, in the vocabulary I actually use. That means, among other things:

1. Translate his prediction about cooperative firms outcompeting capitalist firms into the language of organizational form competition — selection pressures, access to capital, incentive alignment, the labor-managed firm's underinvestment and underemployment problems. Say clearly which of his claims you'd cash out as a testable hypothesis about firm performance and which are normative claims wearing empirical clothing.
2. Translate his distinction between the "distribution question" (a matter of social choice) and the "production question" (a matter of natural constraint) into the vocabulary of cooperative finance: is this anything more than the distinction between how we allocate patronage and surplus internally versus what our cost curves and the market let us do? Note where his premise that the laws of distribution are entirely conventional looks too strong in a co-op's actual accounts — e.g., the way capital requirements discipline our nominal freedom to distribute surplus as we like.
3. Translate his worry about state socialism — his preference for cooperation from below over administration from above — into the language of governance and control rights: one-member-one-vote, board composition, the degree to which our members' interests actually align, and the principal-agent failures that cooperative governance is prone to. His fear was that state ownership produces passivity and dependence; restate that as a claim about how control-right allocation shapes member engagement, and assess whether that translation holds.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Which of Mill's claims translate cleanly? Which survive only distorted — e.g., does his idea that cooperation develops the participants' characters, his real reason for preferring cooperatives, have any honest expression in terms of incentive alignment and engagement metrics, or does that translation quietly gut it? And which parts have no expression at all in my vocabulary — for instance, his progressivist assumption that competitive selection between organizational forms is also moral progress, and his view of history as an educative process. For each piece of residue, tell me whether it points to something genuinely missing from cooperative economics as practitioners frame it, or to something Mill himself never made clear and that his position was better off without.

Do not launder his views through mine: if a Millian claim only sounds natural once you've added an assumption that no cooperative organizer would accept — say, that efficiency and character development must coincide in the long run — flag it as an addition rather than presenting it as a translation. And keep his reservations honest: he was a qualified sympathizer, not a cooperator, and I want the parts of his position that would embarrass us at a general members' meeting to survive the translation as well as the parts we'd put on the website.
