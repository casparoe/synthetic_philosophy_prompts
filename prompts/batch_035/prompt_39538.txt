I've held the factored cognition thesis for years, and I want a proper adversarial audit of it. The claim, roughly: for any question an idealized, unboundedly patient and careful human could eventually settle given unlimited time, a tree of ordinary humans on short time budgets -- each handed a question and the ability to consult other humans via decomposed subquestions, recursively -- can approximate the settled answer arbitrarily well. This is the idealization behind HCH, and the reason I think oversight-by-decomposition could scale: if deliberation factorizes, then a step we can't evaluate directly can still be checked by breaking it into pieces we can, all the way up.

Tell me what would count against it. I want the real defeaters, ranked by decisiveness, including ones that have arguably already fired: the decades-long failure of hand-built expert systems to make knowledge decompose cleanly; the mixed record of the Delphi method; the fact that forecasting teams beat their members working alone -- does that confirm factorization, or only aggregation? Take Polanyi's tacit-knowledge point seriously: if some of what an expert knows cannot be rendered as the content of a subquestion, the difficulty recurses at every node, and I want to know whether that is fatal or merely an engineering problem the idealization is entitled to wave away. I also have a candidate defeater of my own and want it evaluated rather than dismissed: in mathematical practice the crucial move is sometimes a global restructuring of the problem, such that no decomposition you could find without already seeing the whole decomposes it. Relatedly, the time budget seems to make decomposition-finding itself the bottleneck, so that the thesis quietly assumes an oracle for exactly the skill it was supposed to factor out.

And the falsifiability question, answered honestly: HCH idealizes away motivation, bandwidth, and coordination failure, so it's unclear what observation could ever touch it. Is it a Coase-theorem-style claim -- true of an idealization we measure our distance from rather than confirm -- and if so, is that acceptable for a thesis that is supposed to do real load-bearing work in arguments about whether amplification can reach capabilities we cannot directly inspect? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, distinguish the ones a committed defender would concede from the ones they'd dismiss, and say what observation, if it existed and came out the wrong way, would most move you.
