I'm taking a philosophy of art course, and we've spent the last two weeks on the forgery problem: why the value of a painting collapses when it is exposed as a forgery, even when the work is physically indistinguishable from what the artist would have produced — indeed sometimes indistinguishable, under blind viewing, from the original it imitates. My roommate, who works in currency trading, thinks the whole issue dissolves once you see the right analogy, and I've found it hard to shake. His claim is that an original canvas is like a banknote. Neither is worth what it's worth because of its physical properties: the engravings on a note are irrelevant to its value, and by the same token the pigment, composition, and brushwork of a painting are irrelevant to its market value or its standing in art history. What matters in both cases is an institutional fact — backing in the one case, provenance in the other. On his mapping, the artist's hand plays the role of the mint, the catalogue raisonné plays the role of the central bank's ledger, and a perfect forgery is simply a counterfeit note: physically indistinguishable, so whatever worth it has rests entirely on a social fact that never actually obtained. From this he draws three conclusions: first, that the value of original artworks is conventional through and through, closer to the value of money than to the value of, say, an engineering achievement; second, that the rational response to discovering a perfect forgery is exactly the rational response to discovering a note is counterfeit — you stop valuing it, and there is nothing left over to value; and third, that reports of a felt aesthetic difference between an original and its perfect copy are like reports that a counterfeit note "feels wrong" — confabulation after the fact.

I tried to push back with a case from his own world: if every note circulating in some country were counterfeit and nobody knew, or if an economy simply ran on openly counterfeit notes, the notes would work perfectly well, which suggests that money's value is acceptance all the way down and that "backing" does no work. To my surprise, he said this strengthens his side rather than weakening it: the same holds of the museum that hangs a perfect fake Vermeer — if it functions fine as an object of aesthetic attention, then the provenance was doing no work there either, and the "backing" was a polite fiction in both domains. Now I'm unsure what the analogy is actually supposed to settle, and our seminar discussion didn't help.

So here is what I'd like from you. First, lay out the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of the central bank, the mint, the serial number, legal tender status, the economy in which the note circulates, and the moment of exposure — and, on the art side, what each of those is being paired with. Second, identify where the mapping breaks, and sort the disanalogies into those that matter for his conclusions and those that don't (for instance: money is fungible and a painting is a particular; a note's physical properties are causally irrelevant to its value in a way the surface of a painting arguably is not; notes are issued, paintings are made — does the artist's causal responsibility for the appearance, which a copy cannot share, give the original something like a "tender" status money has, or rather something money lacks?). Third, examine the paired cases where the domains seem to diverge in opposite directions: counterfeit notes in a closed economy functioning fine, versus known forgeries that retain or even gain standing once their own history becomes the object of attention (the "workshop of" label, the forgery admired as a feat of deception). My roommate claims these cut the same way, as he says; assess whether the asymmetry instead shows the analogy was carrying his conclusion the whole time — in particular, whether "the value is conventional, so exposure rationally reduces the work to nothing" survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether it should be replaced by something weaker (e.g., that exposure rationally reduces the work's value by the value of its history of making, rather than to nothing). And finally, tell me whether there's a better analogy available — is an original more like a mined diamond against a lab-grown one, where value tracks origin despite identical properties, or more like being the first to make a discovery, where priority rather than any intrinsic feature does the work? Defend a verdict: I want to know what, if anything, the banknote analogy can legitimately carry.
