In my decision theory seminar we've spent three weeks on cases like the one where Death tells you he will be waiting for you tomorrow, you can stay in Damascus or flee overnight to Aleppo, and reliable evidence suggests Death has already anticipated whichever choice you'll make, so that fleeing to Aleppo delivers you to the Damascus appointment while staying put would have meant Death expected you in Aleppo. The class has split into two camps that no longer engage: the causal decision theorists, who say that given Death's prediction tracks your choice, both options have the same expected value, so there's nothing better to do than toss a coin, and the critics, who insist that going to Aleppo is transparently the better thing to do and that a theory of rational choice which forbids ranking it above staying put has mislabeled something important. Every argument now is a restatement of a position; nobody has moved. My working hypothesis is that this isn't a substantive disagreement about any case at all, but a verbal one about the word 'rational': the causal theorists are answering the question of what to do given your evidence, while their critics are answering something like which act you would advise a friend to choose, or which acts you won't end up regretting, and the two questions come apart only in these prediction-heavy cases where the standard theories were built never to look. Is that diagnosis right, or is there a real disagreement in disguise — perhaps about whether an act's value can depend on facts you cannot causally influence? And if my diagnosis is correct, does that mean progress is possible by simply distinguishing the two questions and assigning each a name, or does something remain unsettled even after the verbal dispute is dissolved? Please give reasons tied to what each side actually claims, not a survey of the literature, and be honest about whether you think the impasse reflects an ill-posed question or merely a hard one.
