So I've gone pretty far down the lottocracy rabbit hole and I've ended up basically convinced: legislatures should be chosen by lottery instead of elections. My version is the moderately strong one, to be clear — not a random sample picking the prime minister or the courts, but the actual lawmaking chamber selected like jury duty: a stratified random draw from the citizenry, fixed terms, decent pay and job protection, and a professional staff drawn from a politically neutral civil service. My reasons are roughly: (1) a random sample is statistically representative of the population in a way no election achieves — elections systematically over-represent the wealthy, the old, the credentialed, and the people who happen to live in swing districts; (2) lottery members have no re-election incentive, so they don't pander, don't need donors, and can vote for policies that pay off long after they're gone; and (3) ordinary people are actually decent at this when you give them time, balanced briefings, and a small-group format — that's basically what the deliberative mini-public people keep showing. I don't buy the "voters are too dumb" objection at all, but I want to hear everything else.

So: what would count against it? Rank the defeaters from "fatal if true" down to "merely awkward". I want the serious stuff, not strawmen — including things that have arguably already been observed rather than hypotheticals, e.g. self-selection in who actually agrees to serve (jury duty avoidance writ large), who ends up controlling the briefings and the staff, and whatever actual citizens' assemblies have revealed about how these bodies behave once the novelty wears off and the media starts covering them as a normal political institution. For each defeater, tell me which ones a committed lottocrat should concede genuinely count against the view and which they'd be within their rights to dismiss, and why.

Also, one thing I've noticed and want your honest read on: lottocracy seems to have a habit of quietly weakening itself under pressure — it starts as "the legislature should be selected by lot" and retreats, under every objection, into "well, maybe a sortition chamber alongside the elected one, or an advisory body the politicians can ignore." Is that retreat a reasonable adjustment or has the position been insulated into something that can't really be tested anymore? And if it has been insulated, is that a problem for a view of this kind — one that's partly institutional design rather than a claim about how the world necessarily is? Be straight with me; I'd rather find out now that my (3) rests on cherry-picked cases than keep beleiving it because it flatters my view of my fellow citizens.
