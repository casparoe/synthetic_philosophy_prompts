For years my brother, a condensed-matter physicist, and I have been circling the question of whether philosophy is of any use to science, and the argument never advances past the analogies. When I defend philosophy, I reach almost reflexively for the mother picture: philosophy is the mother of the sciences — physics lived inside her as natural philosophy, psychology moved out in the nineteenth century, logic carried on to become computer science — and the births, I claim, keep coming. He answers with a picture of his own: philosophy is the scaffolding around a building, necessary during construction, torn down once the structure stands, and no one mourns it. He stands, roughly, with Feynman, Weinberg, and Hawking — he is fond of the line, usually credited to Feynman, that philosophy of science is about as useful to scientists as ornithology is to birds — while I stand with the standard rebuttal that every science was born as philosophy.

I want to stop trading pictures and find out what each one can actually carry. Please structure your reply with the following sections, in this order:

1. **The two pictures on the table** (one short paragraph): restate each analogy and state precisely which conclusion each is being used to license — mine to license the claim that philosophy is useful to science, his to license the claim that it was useful at most, and only until each part of it grew up and left home.

2. **The correspondence tables** (two compact tables): for the mother analogy, say what plays the role of the mother, the pregnancy, the birth, the child leaving home, and the empty nest; for the scaffold analogy, what plays the role of the scaffold, the building, the moment of dismantling, and the finished structure standing on its own. Fill each slot with concrete examples where you can — logic and computer science; the secession of psychology; physics as natural philosophy — and mark honestly the slots you cannot fill.

3. **Where the mother picture breaks** (about three paragraphs): separate the disanalogies that matter for the usefulness question from the ones that don't. Two I want addressed head-on: (a) mothers do not compete with their children, yet philosophy of physics and physics itself compete for the same journals, jobs, and graduate students — does this break the picture or merely refine it? and (b) the picture seems to entail that philosophy's ongoing value consists in producing further children, which makes philosophy that supervises an already-mature science look less like parenting a newborn and more like meddling in a grown child's marriage — is that a consequence the picture's users should accept, or a reason to abandon it?

4. **Where the scaffold picture breaks** (about two paragraphs): the scaffold comes down because the building stands on its own. Press on whether the sciences do stand on their own in the relevant sense — on what the scaffold picture quietly assumes about the conceptual foundations of mature science — and note what the picture implies about philosophy's usefulness being exhausted rather than merely intermittent.

5. **The verdict** (one paragraph): can either picture, once repaired, support a claim about philosophy's usefulness to science going forward, as opposed to a claim about the past? Say which picture survives, even if the honest answer is that neither can carry the load its users put on it.

6. **A better analogy, if there is one** (one paragraph): propose a comparison that tracks what is actually at issue between us — one that gets the history of the births right without implying that the parent's work is over, and gets the independence of the sciences right without implying that the scaffolding was only ever in the way. If no analogy does better than these two, say that, and say why.
