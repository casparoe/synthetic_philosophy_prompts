I am a novelist by trade, and I have come to hold the view that the death penalty can never be justified — broadly on the grounds Camus pressed in his 1957 essay against the guillotine: that in a world without absolute authority no one is innocent enough to pass absolute judgment, so no sentence may be absolute; that the state's killing, prepared in cold blood over months, is more premeditated than almost any private murder; and that the deterrence evidence never carries the weight it is asked to carry. My own craft pushes me the same way. I spend my working life narrating from inside people who have done terrible things, and I have never yet managed to write a character who was a monster all the way down; so I distrust any penalty that treats a person as if comprehensibility ended at the prison gate. But a conviction that merely coheres with my work has not been tested, and I would like it tested. Tell me what would count against this position: which observations or findings would be decisive — spell out, for instance, what a deterrence result would actually have to show, in lives saved per execution, before it could outweigh the rest — which would be merely awkward, and which a committed defender of the view would concede as counting against it rather than explain away. I am especially troubled by one case I cannot resolve and will soon have to settle in my current novel: a lucid, uncoerced murderer who asks the court for death, arguing that any lesser penalty understates his crime. Does that request put pressure on the claim that no one deserves absolute punishment, and if so, exactly where — or does it merely expose a gap between what a person may deserve and what the state may do? Finally, assess honestly whether the non-empirical parts of the view have been insulated into unfalsifiability, and whether that insulation is a defect in a view of this kind. Begin with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble, and do not restate my question.
