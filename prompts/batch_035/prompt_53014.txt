When U.S. regulatory agencies evaluate a rule, they routinely monetize the mortality benefits using a single "value of a statistical life" — an estimate derived mainly from revealed preference, i.e., the wage premiums workers accept for riskier jobs — and then sum those dollars together with compliance costs across all affected parties into one net-benefit figure. Yet the neoclassical economics that grounds this practice insists that utility is ordinal only, that it cannot be compared across persons, and that one person's gain cannot be stacked against another's loss; the revealed-preference method itself adds that choices under constrained options reveal welfare. What theory of value does cost-benefit analysis actually presuppose here? Is it that dollars, not utils, serve as a common currency (a potential-compensation story in the Kaldor–Hicks tradition), that market choices are authoritative even about life and health, or something else? Reconstruct the implicit view, say whether it is coherent in light of the objections economists themselves raise — that willingness-to-pay tracks ability to pay, that risk wages reflect desperation as much as valuation — and tell me what would have to change in the practice if agencies instead treated preferences as one input among several rather than as the measure of welfare.
