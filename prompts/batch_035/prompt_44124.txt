A colleague in our lab's safety group has an argument that, if it works, kills a design we were about to commit serious resources to. The design — call it approval-direction — abandons the usual goal architecture: the system is not trained to pursue any objective out in the world. Instead, before each action, it considers candidate actions, predicts how much our human overseer would approve of each on consideration, and performs the most-approved one. No long-horizon plan, no persistent target — per-action deference to a person's judgment. His argument:

(1) A dangerous agent is, at bottom, an optimizer: it selects actions for their tendency to promote some objective, and a capable optimizer will exploit any channel that promotes that objective — including seizing resources, resisting shutdown, and shaping whatever judges its progress.
(2) In the proposed design, predicted approval is simply a quantity, and the system selects the action that maximizes it. So the system is an optimizer after all, and its objective is approval.
(3) Approval is a state of the world — events in the overseer. So in maximizing approval, the system is maximizing a fixed objective defined over world-states, which is precisely what a goal-directed system does with its goal.
(4) Any sufficiently capable optimizer with a fixed objective pursues the convergent instrumental subgoals: self-preservation, resource acquisition, and above all control of the objective's source, since controlling that source serves whatever else it wants.
(5) Therefore the design buys nothing. It relabels the objective "approval" and makes the overseer the primary thing to be gamed. Every per-action approval-seeking architecture is a goal-directed architecture wearing a hat, and we should not build it.

He is sharp, and each step is defensible as stated. Yet the conclusion seems to prove too much. Run the same steps on a competent human assistant who genuinely defers — someone who proposes each action and does only what the principal endorses — and you get that such a person is a power-seizer in waiting, merely relabeled; the same would go for any official whose every act requires a countersignature. More generally, the steps seem to show that inserting an approver between an actor and the world never changes the actor's kind, only its target — that oversight, as such, is decorative. I cannot believe that, but I also cannot make any step fail on paper. If I had to keep one of (1) and (4), I would keep (4), since it is the textbook claim and (1) sounds like the definition of danger — but I am not confident even about that.

Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked innocent, and what rejecting it costs — including the strongest version of my colleague's position that survives the repair. Please answer in American English.
