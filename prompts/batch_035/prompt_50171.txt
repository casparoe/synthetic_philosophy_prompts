I've never studied philosophy, so forgive me if this is all well-trodden ground, but I've been arguing with myself about whether guilt can be inherited by people who weren't alive when the wrong was done, and I'd rather find the principle behind my own reactions than keep circling. Here are my honest verdicts.

My grandfather's generation in my country took part in, or at minimum looked away from, a great historical crime. I would not say that I am guilty of it — I wasn't born, and if someone my age said "we did this" I'd wince. But when my family's house turns out to have been bought well below value from a family forced out in the 1940s, I feel I owe restitution: a real debt, payable by me, even though I committed no wrong. When I watch my country win something — Olympic medals, a scientific first from before my birth — I happily say "we did that," and it doesn't occur to me to refuse the pride. And yet when the state is asked to pay for the historical crime seventy years on, part of me wants to say that's unfair to current taxpayers, while another part says the debt doesn't expire just because the debtors died. A friend of mine from a formerly colonized country tells me her whole nation is owed something by mine; my instinct is to agree about the debt and to balk at the word "guilt" — but I can't articulate what the difference is between owing and being guilty that makes me so sure there is one.

Is there a principle that captures all of these, or close? I've toyed with one myself: that nothing attaches to you just by birth, but something attaches to what you keep — the house, the institutions, the unearned starting position — and guilt proper belongs only to what a person actually did or chose not to do. That principle handles the house, but I'm not sure it handles the pride: I didn't keep the Olympic medals, I just inherited membership in the group that won them, and I still say "we." It also seems to imply that a country whose last participant in the crime dies, and which has returned all the property, owes nothing further — and I'm not sure I believe that either.

What I'd like from an answer: the principle or small set of candidate principles, stated precisely, with the verdict of mine that motivates each part; what it says about cases I haven't mentioned — for instance, someone who immigrates and naturalizes the year after the crime, or a person who inherits nothing at all; and a frank statement of which of my verdicts simply can't all be kept, since I suspect the pride one has to go. I'd also like you to say where your own reasoning here is most likely to fail — what consideration or case, if someone pressed you on it, would make you give up the principle you recommend.
