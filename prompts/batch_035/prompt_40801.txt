I've been keeping a mental ledger of my gut reactions to generalizations about peoples — stuff from family dinners, travel writing, online arguments — and I want to know if there's actually a principle behind my verdicts or whether I'm just tracking how rude something sounds.

Here's the ledger. Saying the Dutch are blunt to the point of rudeness, based on my coworkers in Rotterdam: fine. A travel writer from around 1900 saying the English are reserved and can't express feelings: fine, even kind of charming. My uncle saying the Spanish are lazy: not fine — though this bugs me, because he'd also happily say the Spanish are laid-back, and I think THAT one is fine, and I can't find the difference. Saying Germans follow rules to explain why nobody jaywalks in Munich: fine. Saying you can't do a straightforward business deal in Egypt without someone trying to fleece you: definately not fine, and it wouldn't matter to me if the speaker had actually been fleeced twice there. A friend telling me, to my face, you're German, of course you're on time: kind of not fine? But the same friend saying the Swiss are punctual in the abstract: fine. A novelist writing her Irish characters as melancholy and funny at the same time: fine — though if a politician said the same thing in a speech I'd wince. And I think it's fine to say the Japanese bow, but not fine to say the Japanese are conformist, even though those feel like pretty close cousins.

So: is there a principle that captures all this? I have a half-formed one — generalizations are OK when they describe practices and habits, not OK when they attribute character traits or fixed essences. But that can't be right as stated: lazy and laid-back are both traits, so my uncle case slips through it, and it says nothing about the novelist/politician flip or the to-my-face one. So repair it, or replace it with something better. I want you to actually pick: which of my verdicts does the best principle have to overturn, and I suspect it's the novelist one, since fine-in-fiction seems like the place where I'm most obviously just being persuaded by tone. Also, please give me the repaired principle's verdicts on two cases I haven't listed — whether it's OK for a hiring manager to hold a stereotype about some nationality in their head while consciously never acting on it, and whether it's OK for a history book to call some past people a nation of merchants (I flip-flop on that one constantly). And if you think the honest conclusion is that there's no single principle and my verdicts are just noise arranged by rudeness, say that too.
