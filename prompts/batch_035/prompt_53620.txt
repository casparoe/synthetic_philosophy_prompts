I sit on the clinical ethics committee of a long-term care facility, and Thursday's docket includes a case I expect to divide us. The resident is a woman in her early eighties with advanced mixed dementia; she is no longer verbal, needs full assistance with feeding, and cycles between long stretches of apparent blankness and episodes of severe agitation — pulling at her IV line, crying out, striking staff — that we currently manage with low-dose antipsychotics that blunt but do not eliminate them. She also has brief, unmistakable windows of what looks like contentment: she relaxes when a particular Chopin nocturne is played and she tracks her granddaughter's face across the room. Her son, who did graduate work in Buddhist philosophy before medical school, is her healthcare proxy, and he has formally requested continuous deep sedation — essentially keeping her unconscious for the remainder of her life. He grounds this in a view he calls tranquilism: the position that the only thing that ultimately matters in a life is the absence of craving and suffering, so that a sedated existence free of suffering is strictly preferable to a conscious one mixing brief pleasure with recurrent distress, and her moments of contentment carry no independent weight that could justify keeping her awake to experience them. Her daughter, who visits daily and is the source of the music, argues the opposite: those windows, however small, are the last of what her mother has, and deliberately erasing them to pre-empt distress is closer to a slow, medicalized death than to comfort care.

Our facility's policy permits "comfort-focused care, including proportionate sedation, for refractory symptoms" but is silent on sedation as a standing condition rather than a response to an acute episode, and silent on sedation that would foreclose experiences the patient does not appear to mind. Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the meeting. It should take a definite position — continue current management, adopt a time-limited trial of scheduled deeper sedation with defined criteria for stopping, or accede to the son's request — with a concrete implementation plan and time frame. It should engage the son's tranquilism as a serious moral position rather than dismissing it as idiosyncratic, saying precisely where it does and does not apply to a patient who can no longer report her own trade-offs between calm and experience. And it should give the daughter's side its strongest form, not merely as sentiment but as an argument about whose interests are actually served by each option. If you think the deciding consideration cuts across both positions — for instance, something about the evidence we do and don't have about the resident's current inner life, or about what can be inferred from her settled preferences before the dementia — say so explicitly rather than burying it inside the framing of the family dispute.
