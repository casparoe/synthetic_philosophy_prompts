I write historical fiction and over the years I've argued myself pretty firmly into the position that we owe the dead fidelity of spirit rather than fidelity of fact — basically the Amadeus school: Salieri didn't murder Mozart and never confessed to it, but the film gets at something true about envy and mediocrity that a straightforward documentary about Mozart couldn't touch. My working rule is that fabricated scenes are fine as long as they're true to the overall shape of the person AND the audience knows what genre they walked into — a drama, not a docudrama with a "based on true events" card. If both conditions hold, I say the dead have gotten what they're owed, maybe more.

Lately though I keep losing this arguement at dinner parties and I want to stress-test the view properly instead of just defending it. So: what would actually count against it? Not the standard "you're slandering real people" complaint — I want the cases and principles that should genuinely worry me. Like, does the view survive the victim-of-the-biopic scenario, where the subject died recently enough that their enemies and heirs are still alive and the film is doing real work in how the person is remembered by people who actually knew them? Does it survive scale — is there a real difference between what a novelist with a small readership can invent and what a streaming release watched by tens of millions can, and if the view only holds at small scale, is that a fatal problem or just a boundary the view needs?

And here's the wrinkle I keep chewing on: AI reconstruction of dead performers. Once you can put words in a dead actor's mouth with their actual face and voice, convincingly, my whole "the genre signals the fidelity level" defense seems to do a lot less work, because the genre line blurs — the audience isn't choosing between a drama and a documentary anymore, they're choosing between real-seeming footage and other real-seeming footage. Does that break the view or just extend it to a new case?

I want your actual verdict here, not a menu. Commit to which of these objections is the strongest one, and commit to whether my position survives it intact, survives it with surgery (say what gets cut), or dies. Rank the rest from genuinely dangerous to merely annoying. And be blunt about the thing my friend keeps accusing me of: that "true to the overall shape of the person" has quietly become "fabrication is fine when it produces art I like," which would make the view unfalsifiable and self-serving at the same time. If it has, tell me — and tell me whether a view like this can even be more than taste dressed up as principle.
