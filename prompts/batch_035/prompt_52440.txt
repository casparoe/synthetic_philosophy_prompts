My partner and I are trying to decide whether to conceive, and the decision has turned, for both of us, on a question I now realize is a piece of philosophy rather than a piece of medicine. Severe, treatment-resistant depression runs in my family; after genetic counseling, we understand our prospective child's risk of a major depressive episode to be several times the population baseline, though everyone involved — the counselor, my partner, and I — would still judge the child's expected life to be comfortably better than worth living: the sort of life the child, on reflection, would very likely endorse. What I keep noticing in myself is that the elevated suffering risk feels like a genuine consideration against conceiving, while the substantial happiness the child would very likely have does not feel to either of us like a corresponding consideration in favor — as though the ledger is asymmetric. My partner, who holds a broadly hedonistic and symmetrical view, thinks this is a mistake: on her picture, expected happiness counts in favor roughly as expected suffering counts against, so the decision reduces to the expected balance, which is clearly positive.

So here is the proposition I want your credence on, stated as precisely as I can: (P) in deliberating about whether to create a new person, the predictable suffering that person would endure counts against creating them — pro tanto — even when their life is expected to be, by their own later lights, well worth living; whereas the predictable happiness that person would enjoy does not count in favor in any comparable way, and in particular generates no pro tanto reason to bring them into existence. My own credence in P is around 0.7; my partner's is around 0.35, and understanding that gap is what I most want out of this.

Please give me your own number for P, but more importantly show the derivation. Decompose P into the sub-claims it rests on — I would expect terms along the lines of: (a) an axiology of absence, i.e. whether a world in which a suffering-containing but on-balance-good life never exists is better than the world in which it does, while the parallel claim about a happiness-containing life is false; (b) the wronging question, i.e. whether creating the child wrongs anyone at all, given that the alternative is not a better-off child but no child, so it is unclear who the victim is; (c) whether the worth-living threshold does any real work, or whether intense suffering has a kind of priority that swamps the aggregate balance; and (d) whether giving up the asymmetry commits you to the total view, with its familiar costs — obligations to procreate whenever expected welfare is positive. Add further terms if you think they belong. For each, give a rough probability with a one-line justification, show how they combine into your credence in P, tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to, and what argument or evidence would move it most. Finally, identify which sub-claim is the most likely locus of the disagreement between my 0.7 and my partner's 0.35, and say whether you think one of us is right.
