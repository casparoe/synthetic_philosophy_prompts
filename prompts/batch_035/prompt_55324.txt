In three weeks I am presenting to the grantmaking committee of a longtermist fund, proposing that they commit roughly a tenth of next year's portfolio to reducing s-risks — risks of outcomes in which the future contains suffering on a scale that rivals or exceeds the badness of extinction — as against their current near-exclusive focus on extinction risk. The committee is about ten people: mostly AI-safety and biosecurity people with strong expected-value instincts, a moral-uncertainty theorist, and one veteran animal-welfare grantmaker who is sympathetic but believes tractable suffering work stops at factory farming. None of them are suffering-focused ethicists; most are symmetric consequentialists who take the expected value of the long run to be strongly positive.

My case, briefly: (1) even on a symmetric axiology, the expected suffering of the long run plausibly dwarfs present suffering, so s-risk reduction is not a niche axiology's concern but theirs; (2) a sign-asymmetry point — reducing extinction risk raises the probability of good and bad futures alike, so if the far future could go either way in expectation, extinction-risk reduction is directionally neutral, which means their current portfolio has an unacknowledged hole rather than my proposal having a fanatical one; (3) a person-affecting asymmetry of the sort associated with Narveson, if anyone on the committee holds it, protects work against future suffering while undermining the case for creating happy lives, so under moral uncertainty s-risks deserve a hedge; (4) several interventions — avoiding great-power war, preventing the lock-in of careless attitudes towards digital minds — reduce both kinds of risk, so the allocation is not zero-sum.

Prepare my Q&A sheet: the eight or so objections this committee will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they would do, each phrased in the expected-value vocabulary they would use. I anticipate the Pascal's-mugging flank (astronomical suffering at vanishing probabilities is just the mugging), a tractability complaint, and a suspicion that suffering-focused ethics tracks temperament rather than argument, but do not limit yourself to those. For each objection, give a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks — I suspect it will be the one demanding that I state my credence that the expected long-run future is net negative, since any number I give either torpedoes my case or makes me look like the mugger; tell me if there is a worse one I have not spotted. Finish with a candid closing section on where my overall reasoning is most likely to fail, and on what argument or piece of evidence would realistically move me to a different verdict — including what would lead me to conclude that the correct allocation is zero. Please write the whole response in British English.
