I'm putting together materials for a graduate seminar on idealization in theories of well-being and machine ethics, and I've been struggling to build a thought experiment that isolates a specific worry I have about coherent extrapolated volition (CEV) as a proposal for what a benevolent AI should aim at. As I understand it, CEV holds that we should satisfy not people's current preferences, but our collective wish if we knew more, thought faster, were more the people we wished we were, and had grown up farther together — with coherence doing real work in determining what counts as the volition that gets satisfied. What I want is a case in which a subject's coherent extrapolated volition conflicts with what that very subject would choose after fully informed, uncoerced, and unimpaired deliberation, so that the divergence cannot be chalked up to ignorance, cognitive bias, or manipulation, which are the easy ways to get idealized and current preferences to come apart.

Constraints: the scenario should be realistic and mundane — a dispute over an inheritance, a neighborhood conflict about housing, a school admissions decision, or something of that sort — with no superintelligence in the picture and no running of simulations of the person. It should fit in a single paragraph. And the divergence must be generated by the structure of the extrapolation procedure rather than stipulated; a case that simply declares that extrapolation would reverse the person's preference is cheating. My hunch is that the right way to do this is to exploit the interpersonal and coherence elements: because CEV extrapolates a collective volition, a subject's own fully informed and perfectly reasonable preference can be overruled by the demand that it cohere with everyone else's extrapolated preferences — perhaps a preference the subject would regard as entirely legitimate, but which becomes incoherent with the volition of the wider group once everyone is idealized together. If that's right, then the feature doing the work is aggregation-plus-coherence rather than individual idealization, and I'd want you to confirm whether that's the only way to satisfy the specification, or whether the the-people-we-wished-we-were clause could generate the same divergence in a purely individual case (say, by idealizing someone into a person whose values she reflectively refuses to endorse).

So: construct the case to these specifications, and then demonstrate that it meets each one. Walk through the verdict that a straightforward current-preference satisfactionist would give about what should be done for the subject, the verdict of a Railton-style full-information ideal advisor view, and the verdict of CEV, explaining precisely where the third diverges from the second. Then identify exactly which feature of the case produces the divergence, and tell me what happens to the case if that feature is removed — for instance, if the volition is extrapolated for the individual alone rather than collectively. Finally, give the strongest objection a defender of individualist idealization could raise against the case — ideally something in the spirit of the worry that idealization is supposed to inform a person's existing preferences, not transform them into someone else's — and assess honestly whether that objection defeats the case or merely shows that CEV is doing something different in kind from standard full-information views.
