The exam question, as printed on the paper:

"When governments weigh policies whose costs and benefits fall in different centuries, they must choose a social discount rate, and one contested component of that rate is pure time preference: the discounting of well-being merely in virtue of its occurring later. (a) Explain what pure time preference is, and distinguish it from the other reasons an analyst might have for valuing future goods less than present ones. (b) Set out the strongest objection to a positive rate of pure time preference, drawing on at least two authors from the course. (c) Evaluate the best reply available to the defender of a positive rate, and reach a verdict."

The candidate's answer, in full:

"Pure time preference is discounting utility simply because it arrives later — not because the future is uncertain, and not because resources invested today could earn a return and do more good elsewhere. It has to be kept apart from the other components of a social discount rate. An analyst can legitimately value a future benefit less because it is improbable, because future people are expected to be richer and so will get less welfare from a marginal unit of consumption, or because of the opportunity cost of capital; those adjustments can be sound even if pure time preference is not, so conflating them with pure time preference is the first error to avoid. Ramsey set out the classical objection in his 1928 paper on how much a nation should save: he declared discounting later enjoyments in comparison with earlier ones ethically indefensible, a mere weakness of the imagination. The deeper thought is older still. Parfit's principle of the point of view of the universe holds that the good of any one counts no more than the like good of any other, no matter where or when they live; from that standpoint temporal position is as irrelevant as spatial position, so a benefit loses no moral weight by being remote in time. Parfit sharpened this in Reasons and Persons: our bias towards the near mirrors our bias towards our own interests, and both are self-favouring distortions that impartial ethics corrects rather than accommodates. One qualification is legitimate — if humanity may not survive to enjoy later benefits, a small discount reflecting extinction risk is compatible with impartiality, which is roughly how the Stern Review arrived at its near-zero rate.

The best reply on behalf of discounting has two parts. First, uncertainty. We inevitably treat the future as less certain: benefits a century out are contingent on institutions persisting, on forecasts holding, on people existing at all. If we may weight a benefit by its probability — and everyone agrees we may — then a positive discount rate is simply the honest accounting of that uncertainty, and once this is conceded a modest rate of pure time preference is defensible after all, since what the objection condemns is preferring the near as such, which is not what a practising analyst does. The alternative reply, that market behaviour reveals society's true rate, as Nordhaus argues against Stern, is weaker: what people do is not evidence of what they are entitled to do.

Second, a formal argument. Strotz showed in 1955 that only exponential discounting is time-consistent: any other schedule gives an agent standing reasons to overturn its own earlier plans. Since time-consistency is a requirement of practical rationality, the exponential form is rationally compulsory, and once the form is fixed the rate itself is a matter for each society to set in the light of its own values. So any exponentially applied rate, including one embodying pure time preference, is rational. The upshot is that the objection is aimed only at the crude preference for whatever is nearer, not at principled discounting, and a small positive rate of pure time preference is permissible."
