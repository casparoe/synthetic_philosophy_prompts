I'm a second-year PhD student in functional analysis, and I've gradually noticed that my attitude toward the axiom of choice varies wildly from case to case, in a way I can't quite articulate and that my coursework has never forced me to make explicit. I'd like to work out whether there's a principle behind my reactions, or whether my mathematical instincts are just an inconsistent mess. Here are my verdicts on specific cases. Every vector space has a basis, even for vector spaces so large that no basis can be written down or used: I invoke this freely and sleep well. A countable union of countable sets is countable: I use this constantly in analysis, and it feels like it ought to be a theorem of bare set theory, not an extra assumption. Every infinite set has a countably infinite subset: feels obvious, almost analytic. Given a countable family of nonempty sets, picking one element from each in the middle of a proof: fine in practice, though if pressed to justify the move I'd hand-wave. Now the ones where I get uneasy. The reals can be well-ordered: I don't accept this on sight, and when a proof needs it I always look for a detour. Any two sets are comparable in cardinality, one way or the other: feels like it must be true, but I can't see why, and that combination makes me suspicious. Every equivalence relation has a set picking one representative from each class: stated in that bald form it looks like a naked appeal to choice, and I distrust it — even though I gather it's just the axiom in disguise. Finally, the pair I keep going back and forth on: choosing one sock from each of finitely many pairs strikes me as needing choice, in a way that choosing one shoe from each pair (take the left one) does not; and Zorn's lemma I trust completely in, say, a Hahn–Banach proof, even though I could not myself reconstruct the derivation of Zorn from the axiom of choice.

What I'd like from you: tell me whether there is a principle — perhaps something about the size of the family (countable versus uncountable), or about the existence of a definable way to make the choice, or something else entirely — that captures most or all of these verdicts. State the principle or candidate principles as precisely as you can, and be explicit about which of my verdicts each one must overturn to be coherent. I suspect the finite-socks case is where my gut is simply wrong, but I'd like you to say so directly rather than diplomatically, and to explain what the correct verdict there is and why. Then test the surviving principle on cases I haven't listed — for instance, whether every connected graph admits a spanning tree, whether every Dedekind-finite set must be finite, whether a countable product of compact metric spaces is compact, and whether a well-ordering of the reals could ever be exhibited rather than merely posited — and tell me what the principle predicts for each, flagging where the prediction itself is uncertain.

One request about how you present all this: please keep separate what is mathematically settled from what is genuinely contested. Which of these statements are provable in ZF alone, which require countable choice or some other fragment, and which are equivalent to full choice — those are facts, and I want them stated as facts. But claims like whether countable choice is "harmless," whether the countable/uncountable boundary marks any real epistemic difference, or whether "a definable selector" can be made precise in a way that does the work I want it to — those are contested among people who know the subject, and I'd like them labeled as such rather than smuggled in as the consensus view. If my own framing of the cases assumes something controversial, say that too.
