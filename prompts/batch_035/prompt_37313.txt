I've got a bunch of gut reactions about when "culture did it" explanations are legit and when they're lazy, and I want to know if there's actually a principle behind them. Japan's low gun violence being partly down to a taboo strong enough that even owning one feels off: fine by me. The spread of literacy in Scandinavia being pushed by the Lutheran idea that everyone should read scripture for themselves: fine. A historian saying that as clocks spread, Europeans' sense of time changed and that mattered for labor discipline: fine. But "this country is poor because its culture doesn't value entrepreneurship": sounds like a dodge. "People in East Asia save so much because of Confucian thrift": I roll my eyes. And "the Industrial Revolution happened in Britain partly because of a culture of practical tinkering and improvement" leaves me genuinely on the fence — same for explaining fertility declines across countries by "secular individualist values spreading," which sometimes seems fine to me and sometimes empty.

So: is there one principle that sorts the good from the bad here? My hunch is it's something about whether the values are evidenced independently of the behavior they're supposed to explain — with the poverty and savings cases, the only real evidence for "doesn't value entrepreneurship" or "values thrift" is the very behavior being explained, which makes it circular. But I'm not sure that works for all the cases I'm fine with. So please state the principle precisely, tell me what it says about the tinkering case and the fertility one, what it would predict for cases I haven't listed, and be honest about which of my verdicts it just can't fit. If the right answer is that my "fine" group and my "lazy" group can't be split by anything principled, say that too.
