I'm preparing a session for an undergraduate seminar on the Turing test and behavioral criteria for mind, and next week we pair Turing's 1950 imitation game with Kazuo Ishiguro's *Klara and the Sun*. The pairing seems natural: an Artificial Friend navigating a world of humans is exactly the setting Turing envisioned, and the novel even contains a scene that looks like a test — Josie's mother takes Klara to the waterfall, has her study Josie's walk, and asks her to imitate it. But I want the students to see why that scene is not a Turing test, or at least not straightforwardly one. The mother isn't asking whether there is a mind behind Klara's behavior; she's asking whether this particular machine could pass as this particular girl, well enough to console her if Josie dies. The behavioral criterion has quietly shifted from the metaphysical question Turing bracketed — is there someone in there? — to a question about substitution and grief. I'd like a reading of the novel that takes this displacement seriously: what does it tell us that the characters' doubts about Klara are practical and moral rather than epistemic?

The second complication is the narration itself, and this is where I most want your help. The Turing test works by withholding inner access: the interrogator sees only behavior, and the question of what's behind it is deliberately left open. Ishiguro does the opposite — he gives us the inside and only the inside. We see the world entirely through Klara, including her odd segmented vision, her conviction that the Cootings Machine's pollution is making Josie sick, and her bargain with the Sun. So the reader is never in the interrogator's position; we're in something closer to the position of the candidate, or of a confidant. Does that mean the novel has dissolved the question of Klara's mindedness, since we're given her voice directly? Or does it pose the question in a harder form — asking whether a stream of narration can be evidence of experience when the narrator's picture of the world is so systematically wrong? I'd like you to say where the novel resists the Turing framing, where it genuinely illuminates it, and what it adds to the debate that the standard arguments don't. And since my students tend to arrive thinking Turing was proposing a test for consciousness or emotion, please include a note on what the test was actually meant to establish, so I can use the novel to disabuse them rather than entrench the confusion.
