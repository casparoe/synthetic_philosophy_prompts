At a reading group on game-theoretic aspects of AI bargaining, a presenter introduced the idea of a 'safe Pareto improvement' roughly as follows: when two agents are headed for a costly conflict — each would rather concede than fight, but each most prefers that the other concede — they can sometimes arrange in advance that the dispute be settled by a cheap surrogate contest instead: a coin toss, say, with the loser of the toss conceding in the real interaction. Because the surrogate contest is cheaper than the real fight, and because, from each party's perspective before the toss, the toss is at least as likely to go their way as the fight would, both parties expect to do better than under the default of fighting it out — an ex ante Pareto improvement over conflict, whatever either party privately believes about who would have won.

The presenter then said this is nothing new: 'SPIs are just what litigants do when they settle out of court — you replace an expensive fight with a cheaper procedure, and both sides expect to gain.' When another member demurred, the presenter doubled down: 'If it works between plaintiffs and defendants, it will work between AIs. The formalism is bookkeeping; the courtroom shows the mechanism arises on its own wherever there are costly disputes, so we needn't engineer anything special into AI bargainers.'

I would like an audit of the settlement analogy. Please begin by setting out the correspondence explicitly, as a table: what plays the role of the trial (the default conflict), the settlement (the surrogate contest), the lawyers, the judge, the enforceability of the bargain, and each side's beliefs about prevailing at trial. Then identify where the mapping breaks. I would particularly like your views on three candidate disanalogies. (i) In an out-of-court settlement the parties bargain over the terms — the outcome is negotiated, whereas in an SPI the surrogate contest is meant to stand in for the real one, with the winner taking what the winner of the real fight would have taken; does the analogy quietly import a bargaining stage that SPIs lack, and a neutrality (the judge) that surrogate procedures need not have? (ii) A settlement is Pareto-improving only given the parties' beliefs and risk attitudes, and can fail to be attractive to a sufficiently confident or risk-loving litigant; the SPI idea is advertised as robust to private beliefs — or does that robustness itself rest on assumptions (risk neutrality, the surrogate being a fair model of the real contest) that the analogy smuggles in along with the courtroom furniture? (iii) Settlements require enforcement and a modicum of trust; SPIs are proposed precisely for settings where trust is absent. Finally, please distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the presenter's conclusion — that the courtroom precedent shows SPIs will emerge naturally and need no special engineering — from those that do not, and, if the courtroom is the wrong source domain, say what a better analogy would be.
