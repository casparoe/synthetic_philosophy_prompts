My brother-in-law and I got into it over Christmas dinner about the weather, of all things. He's a proper butterfly effect guy — he says forecasters can't see more than a couple weeks out because a butterfly flapping somewhere screws everything up, and he takes this to mean the future is genuinely open, not fixed. His argument, as best I can reconstruct it: if the future were determined, then a big enough computer could in principle work it out ahead of time; but chaos theory shows prediction is impossible even in principle, since any measurement you start from carries tiny errors and those errors blow up; so determinism must be wrong, or at least physics has quietly given up on it. I have a nagging feeling he's smuggling something in — I seem to remember someone telling me that chaotic systems are usually fully deterministic, and that the chaos comes from the rules being non-linear rather than from anything random going on. But I couldn't pin down where he goes wrong on the spot, and worse, when I pushed back he came back with what I think is actually a decent point.

He used a pool table (bear with me). Say the balls obey plain old Newtonian rules, no quantum stuff, nothing fancy. Even a machine that measured the balls' positions to a hundred decimal places couldn't tell you where they end up after a dozen collisions, and to get it right for a few more collisions you'd need more decimal places than there are atoms in the universe to write them down with. Apparently the same holds for something as dumb as a double pendulum. So his challenge to me was: if no possible observer, even in principle, could ever calculate the future, what does it actually mean to go on calling it "determined"? Is that a fact about the world or just a bookkeeping entry? So I suppose my question is twofold: where exactly does the "unpredictable, therefore not determined" move go wrong — spelled out slowly enough for someone who dropped physics at 16 — and then, honestly, does the determinism part matter for anything at all, if the prediction part is dead in the water either way? Sorry if this is a bit rambly, we'd had a lot of wine.
