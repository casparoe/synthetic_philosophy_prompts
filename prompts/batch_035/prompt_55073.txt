here's roughly where I've landed on supererogation after a term of moral philosophy and I want to know if it actually hangs together before I write the essay:

(1) supererogation is real — the soldier throwing herself on the grenade and the saint giving away nearly everything are doing something genuinely good that they're nevertheless not required to do.
(2) what makes those acts beyond duty is cost to the agent: duty runs out at the point where the sacrifice demanded of you gets too big.
(3) the threshold is indexed to your circumstances — my duty to give runs out sooner than a billionaire's does, because the same donation is a bigger cost to me.
(4) but I'm an impartialist about all of it: when we work out what duty demands, my interests count exactly as much as everyone else's and not one bit more. none of this "it's my life so my costs get special weight" business.
(5) and I think the demandingness objection is just right: morality couldn't require you to give until you're no better off than the worst-off person — that would be too much to ask. someone who does give down to that level is supererogating, not finally doing their duty.

go through these and tell me which bits pull against each other and how badly. I have a feeling at least one of them can't survive contact with the others but I genuinely can't see which. rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as all equally fatal, and for each one say what would have to give and what the cheapest fix is. also flag anything that only looks like a tension but isn't — I'd rather not gut a commitment that was fine all along.
