My friend and I watched Hacksaw Ridge the other night (his pick, I'd never seen it) and it turned into a two hour argument about whether the movie is actually pro-pacifist. His take is that it's secretly one of the most pro-war films ever made: Desmond Doss refuses to carry a rifle, sure, but Gibson's obvious affection is for the combat — the Okinawa sequences are filmed with real relish — and Doss's heroism only gets its meaning from serving the men doing the killing. By the end, he points out, the battalion won't even go back up the ridge until Doss finishes praying with them, so his pacifism has literally become a morale resource for the war effort. On that reading the movie only respects pacifism because it's paid for in blood and because it helps win. I pushed back — the whole arc is that the conviction costs him everything (the beating in the barracks, the court-martial, his marriage almost) and it holds anyway — but honestly I couldn't tell if the film is on Doss's side or just on the side of him being inspiring, and we kind of ran out of steam.

Give me a committed reading of the film through actual pacifist thought. As I understand it there's a distinction between pacifism as a personal absolute (I will not kill, whatever happens) and pacifism as a political thesis (war as an institution is unjustified and should be abolished), and Doss is a strange case on both axes — he enlists voluntarily, he wants to be in the fight, he just refuses to touch a weapon. Is that pacifism at all, or is it closer to a purity constraint on his own complicity while remaining a loyal participant in the machine? And related: does his work as a medic make him complicit in the killing (the standard objection that nonviolent service just makes the shooters more effective), and does the film ever confront that or does it dodge it? Use specific scenes — the barracks hazing, the trial and his father's intervention, the night lowering wounded men off the cliff with the rope sling, his whispered prayer to let him get one more, the prayer scene before the final assault — but don't give me a both-sides reading where the film is "complex" or "rich and ambiguous." Take a verdict and defend it: does Hacksaw Ridge vindicate pacifism, domesticate it, or betray it? And if any part of your reading leans on a contestable interpretation of a scene, flag that rather than smoothing over it. I want to bring this back to him and finally win, or at least lose for the right reasons.
