So I'm in a jurisprudence course and we just did the Hart–Fuller debate, and I'm tired of the textbook paragraph everyone recites about it, so I want an actual case analysis instead. The episode is the grudge informer case: in 1944 a woman, wanting to be rid of her husband, reported him to the Nazi authorities for derogatory remarks he'd made about Hitler while home on leave, and testified against him; a military tribunal sentenced him to death under the statutes making it illegal to make statements inimical to the Third Reich, though after some prison time he was sent to the front instead. After the war, both the wife and the judge who had sentenced him were indicted for unlawful deprivation of liberty under § 289 of the German Criminal Code of 1871, and in 1949 a court of last resort in criminal matters acquitted the judge but convicted the wife, on the ground that she had made free use of a Nazi "law" that was contrary "to the sound conscience and sense of justice of all decent human beings." This is the case Hart and Fuller then fought over in their 1958 exchange. Don't hang anything on any other historical details — treat the facts as I've given them and work from those.

What I want is the analysis run in both directions, like we'd do with any case study. Map the episode onto each position: what positivism says the court faced and should have done, what Fuller's view of law as a purposive enterprise says, where each one cleanly accounts for the outcome, and where each is silent or has to strain. Hart himself said the court had only two honest choices — let her go, or punish her and admit frankly it was retroactive law — so I want you to take seriously the wrinkle both men circled: the court got to its "natural law" result by reaching for something like retroactivity, and banning retroactive punishment is normally a positivist talking point. So is this episode really the natural-law win it's always presented as, or is it an anomaly for Fuller's side that gets talked around? And if you think it's a win for Hart instead, explain how "at least we're honest about doing something ugly" is supposed to be a recommendation of a theory of law rather than just candor about its absence.

Then one more thing. Consider a variant that nobody debates: suppose the informer had been a low-level clerk whose official job included processing exactly these denunciations, and who reported the husband's remarks in the ordinary course of duty rather than out of spite — no grudge, no testimony of her own, just a man doing what his job description said. Does the Hart/Fuller mapping change at all, or does it collapse into a question about excuses and duress that neither theory was ever built to handle? Be honest about where the theories stop and the judges' ad hoc moralizing starts — I don't want the victory-lap version of any of this.
