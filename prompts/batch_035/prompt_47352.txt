I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've been trying to sort out what I actually believe about death being bad for the person who dies — the old Epicurean puzzle, as I understand it: when you're dead, you're not around to be the subject of any misfortune, so how can death harm you? As an engineer I naturally model a person's life as a stream of experience, something like an event log, and death as the log simply terminating, but I've noticed my own verdicts on cases don't line up with that model, and I'd like help extracting the principle behind them, if there is one. Here's the raw material. A healthy 30-year-old killed instantly and painlessly in a car crash: very bad, clearly worse than the same person losing a leg. A 95-year-old who dies in her sleep after a good final year: barely bad at all, maybe not bad. The eternity before my birth: not bad for me in any way; I have no intuition at all that I was harmed by missing the Pleistocene. Yet I still think the 30-year-old was harmed by missing her next fifty years, and I can't articulate what makes the two directions of nonexistence different. A friend who reads my private journals aloud at my funeral and mocks them: I feel this wrongs me somehow, even though I never experience it, which already sits oddly with the "stream of experience" picture. Finally, the pair I really can't reconcile: person A is knocked into a permanent, dreamless coma at 30 and dies at 80; person B is killed instantly at 30. I judge B worse off than A, even though both have exactly nothing in the experience log after 30.

For what it's worth, my tentative principle is that death is bad for you roughly in proportion to the value of the experiences you would otherwise have had — a deprivation story. But it seems to predict that dying after a miserable, pain-filled year would be good for you, and it seems to make the instant-death case a tie with the fifty-year coma, and I don't fully accept either of those. There's also something in Lucretius's symmetry argument that I find much more persuasive than I want to: nobody thinks the time before birth was bad for them, so why is the time after death?

So my questions: is there a principle — yours or a repaired version of mine — that captures most or all of my verdicts? State it as precisely as you can, tell me which verdict motivates each clause, and say honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why. I'd also like its predictions on cases I haven't listed: the person whose remaining life would have gone badly; someone who is warned six months in advance of a painless death versus someone who dies without warning; and whether it matters, on the resulting view, whether death is annihilation rather than some permanent dreamless sleep, given that the two look identical from inside the stream. If the honest answer is that my verdicts are genuinely inconsistent and something has to go, I'd like to know which one you'd throw out first and what the more coherent position looks like from there. Please write in American English, and assume no access to sources: explain any philosophical terms you use rather than citing where they come from.
