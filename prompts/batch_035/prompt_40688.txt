Here's something I keep bumping into at work and can't quite square. Our org (about 200 engineers) makes big shared decisions -- which framework we migrate to, what the quarter's priorities are -- by ranked-choice voting: everyone ranks the options, and losers get eliminated until something has a majority. Last quarter we ended up with option A. This quarter, basically the same people and the same options, except one unpopular option got added to the ballot, and the winner flipped to B. As far as anyone can tell nobody changed their mind about A vs B at all -- the extra option just changed the elimination order. A staff engineer said something like "yeah, that's that impossibility theorem, Arrow's thing, every ranked system has problems like this" and everyone shrugged and moved on.

I can't stop thinking about the philosophy part though. When we say "the team decided B," what does running the decision through this procedure actually presuppose? Like, does it presuppose there's a real thing out there -- "the team's preference" -- that the vote is discovering, the way a thermometer discovers temperature? Or is the vote creating that preference, or is it just a procedurally fair way of landing on something everyone can live with, with no fact of the matter underneath? If Arrow's theorem really shows that every ranked-voting scheme can be jerked around the way ours was, does that mean the presupposition is false -- there just is no coherent "team preference" for the procedure to track -- or only that we're imperfectly approximating one? And if the collective preference might not exist, should I stop saying "the team wants B" in standup? That phrasing suddenly feels like it's doing metaphysics I never signed up for.

One more thing I'm unsure about: a lot of people here say the reason the result is legitimate is that we all agreed to the procedure in advance, not that it tracked anything. So maybe the commitment is to the procedure itself and the presupposed "team preference" talk is just leftovers. But that feels weird too, because if the procedure is all there is, why did everyone get so annoyed that adding a spoiler option flipped the result? Their annoyance seems to presuppose that the vote was supposed to reveal something, and it failed.

So basically: reconstruct for me what this practice presupposes, which of the views it quietly rules out, whether its parts are even consistent with each other, and what would actually have to change (in the procedure, or in how we talk about it) if we took the alternative view seriously. Explain like I'm an engineer who's read zero philosophy.
