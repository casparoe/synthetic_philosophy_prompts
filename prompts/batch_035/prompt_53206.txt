i'm in a small reading group on AI safety (we're a mix of ex-ML people and one philosophy grad student, so things get heated) and we keep circling back to worst-case vs average-case guarantees. Last week one of the guys was adamant that a model's safety is like a chain: only as strong as its weakest link. On his story, if there are a bunch of distinct failure modes and you can only red-team by sampling situations, you end up with an average-case estimate of how often things go wrong, but a chain with one broken link is broken no matter how strong the average link is — so average-case evals are basically theater, and we should demand worst-case guarantees before deployment, full stop. He also leaned on a comparison from algorithms: quicksort being fast on average doesn't help you when the input is adversarial, and no one would use it where someone else gets to pick your input.

I half buy this but the analogy is clearly doing a lot of work and I can't tell whether his conclusion is actually supported or whether the chain picture is just smuggling it in. Can you audit it for me? Like, what exactly is a link — a failure mode, a model property, a deployed safeguard? And are the failures even arranged in series, the way a chain's links are, such that any single failure sinks the whole thing? I can also imagine defenses being more like redundancy or defense in depth, where one weak component doesn't automatically lose you the game, which would make the system less like a chain and more like a net or a scaffold or something. And the quicksort comparison feels different in a way I can't articulate — the worst case exists but you rarely land in it, whereas the whole worry about misaligned models is that whatever exploits them is adaptive and will actively search out your worst case. So maybe: give me the correspondence table for the chain analogy (chain, links, load, breaking, tensile strength → what in the alignment setting), where the mapping breaks, which of those breakages actually matter for his conclusion vs which are just noise, and whether "average-case evals are useless, only worst-case guarantees count" survives once the disanalogies are on the table. also curious if there's a better analogy for the average-case side — something that shows why some people think average-case robustness is genuinely enough in certain regimes and not just a cope.
