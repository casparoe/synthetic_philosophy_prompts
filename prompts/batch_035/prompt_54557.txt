A friend and I spent the better part of an evening arguing about the thermodynamic arrow of time, and I've come away with an argument neither of us could refute that seems to prove far too much. (1) The fundamental dynamics we know of are, to a very good approximation, invariant under time reversal. (2) Our best explanation of why entropy increases toward the future is that the early universe was in a spectacularly low-entropy macrocondition. (3) But we never observe that macrocondition directly; the present macrostate of the world is all we have to go on, and the low-entropy past is an inference from it. (4) The inference runs through records and memories, which are themselves just present macrostates. (5) A present macrostate counts as a reliable record of a low-entropy past only on the assumption that record-bearing macrostates are not what you'd typically get from equilibrium fluctuations -- which is to say, only on the assumption that the past was lower in entropy than the present. (6) So the low-entropy past is justified by records, while the reliability of records is justified by the low-entropy past. (7) A justification that is circular in this way gives us no reason at all to believe its conclusion. (8) Therefore we have no reason to believe entropy was lower in the past and, by parallel reasoning, no reason to expect it to be higher in the future: the thermodynamic arrow rests on nothing. My friend concluded that there is simply no fact of the matter about whether the past was low-entropy, which I take to be a reductio; I'd most like to keep (5), since it seems to capture why a photograph of spilled milk is evidence the spill happened rather than fluctuated into place. Where does the argument go wrong -- which step should I give up, what does giving it up cost, and is the trouble invalidity or a premise that only looked innocent? Please say how confident you are in each part of your diagnosis and single out the point you'd be least sure of.
