And here is the answer I have to grade:

> Putnam asks us to imagine a planet, Twin Earth, exactly like Earth except that the clear, potable liquid in its oceans, lakes, and rain is not H2O but a different compound, abbreviated XYZ. Every Earthling has a twin there, so there is an Oscar and a Twin Oscar — though notably not molecule-for-molecule identical twins, since their bodies are mostly made of water and XYZ respectively. The scenario is set in 1750, before chemistry could tell either planet what its 'water' was made of, so Oscar and Twin Oscar are in identical psychological states: they have the same thoughts, the same experiences of drinking, sailing on, and testing 'water', and exactly the same descriptions in mind when they use the word. Yet 'water' as used by Oscar refers to H2O, while 'water' as used by Twin Oscar refers to XYZ. Since the twins are psychologically identical, the meaning of their words cannot be fixed by anything in their heads; as Burge famously put it, 'meanings' just ain't in the head. Putnam's conclusion is that in 1750 'water' was ambiguous: it had two different senses on the two planets, one meaning H2O and one meaning XYZ, even though nobody could have known this. The word gets its reference from the experts in each linguistic community rather than from the ordinary speaker — the division of linguistic labor — since most speakers could not tell water from XYZ. An internalist who wanted to resist this would have to insist that both twins mean the same thing by 'water' — the watery stuff, whatever it turns out to be — and that reference is not part of meaning, only the sense or descriptive content is.
>
> The code analogy works well, and in a way strengthens Putnam's case. Take two source files that are byte-for-byte identical: a call to `sort` will do different things depending on whether the build links against library A or library B, so what a given token of `sort` contributes to the program is fixed not by the file but by the environment it runs in — just as Oscar's token of 'water' gets its reference from his environment rather than from his internal state. The analogue of the 1750 condition is that the programmer often does not know which implementation the build will resolve to, especially with transitive dependencies, and the analogue of the division of linguistic labor is the package maintainer, who plays the role of Putnam's experts: the ordinary programmer defers to the maintainer for what `sort` does, just as the ordinary speaker defers to chemists for what water is. So the analogy supports Putnam's conclusion in an even stronger form than his own example: not only is what a token means not in the head, it is not even in the source code, and the programmer does not know what their program means until the build and its full dependency tree are fixed. Indeed, the internalist here is like someone claiming the source file alone fixes behavior, which we can disprove empirically by running the same file in two environments and observing different output — something Putnam could never do with Earth and Twin Earth. Code thus gives us an externalism we can actually test.
