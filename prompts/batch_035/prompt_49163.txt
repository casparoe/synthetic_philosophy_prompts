Write a dialogue between two philosophers of pain — call one the Evaluativist and the other the Imperativist — who run into each other at a conference coffee break, having just attended the same talk on pain asymbolia. Give them equal dialectical skill, and make sure they engage with each other's actual points rather than trading past positions; each should land at least one genuine hit on the other, and concede at least one point.

Here is the seeding for their views and the shared case that sets them off. The Evaluativist holds that what makes a sensation pain, rather than mere bodily information, is that it phenomenally presents a bodily condition as bad — as something wrong with the part — so that the badness is built into the feel itself. The Imperativist holds that pain's phenomenal character is instead constituted by imperative content: the sensation, when felt, is a command to do something about the body part — to protect it, withdraw, favor it — and the sense that the body part is bad is a downstream effect of being subject to that command. Both have just heard a description of pain asymbolia: patients (classically with insular damage) who report that they have pain, describe its location and intensity in apparently normal terms, can attend to it and grade it, yet are strikingly indifferent to it — they do not withdraw, do not grimace, sometimes smile, and when asked say the pain does not bother them. Each walks into the conversation convinced that asymbolia is a decisive argument for their own view: the Evaluativist because the patients seem to have retained the pain while losing a separable attitude of minding, suggesting the badness is not an attitude laid on top of the sensation; the Imperativist because the patients seem to retain the sensation while becoming unresponsive to the command, suggesting that the command, not the sensation's content, was doing the work of making pain matter.

Over the course of the dialogue they should push on concrete questions, for instance: whether the asymbolic's avowal "it's painful but I don't mind it" reports an introspective separation between two components or merely a failure of avoidance that the patient glosses in those terms; whether the two theories even make different predictions here, or whether each can redescribe the case as a lesion on its own side of the line (an uptake defect after intact representation versus command resistance after intact imperative content); whether evidence that asymbolics still show some residual conditioned avoidance to pain-paired stimuli, or still rate their pains as intense on a scale, favors either side; and what the analogous dissociations elsewhere — the placebo analgesia cases where unpleasantness drops faster than intensity, or the athlete who registers an injury but defers concern — are supposed to show. Near the end, have them stop competing over the case and jointly articulate what they now take the real crux to be: something like whether "minding" one's pain is an extra attitude directed at the sensation, or is partly constitutive of what the sensation is. Let the dialogue end with them sketching, and disagreeing about, one further experiment or clinical observation that would discriminate the views — not with a winner declared.
