In my graduate seminar on utilitarianism I have come to defend, and now genuinely hold, a version of negative utilitarianism with a lexical threshold, and I would like a candid map of the view's exposure. The view, as I hold it, has two clauses. First, below a certain severity of suffering — call it the threshold — pleasure and pain are fully commensurable, and the ordinary hedonic calculus applies: experiences aggregate, trade off against one another, and a sufficiently large sum of modest pleasures can outweigh a modest pain. Second, above the threshold, lexical priority takes over: no quantity or quality of pleasure, however vast or refined, and no avoidance of below-threshold pains, can compensate for a single instance of above-threshold suffering. Such suffering is to be minimised first; only once it cannot be reduced does the first clause become operative.

Tell me what would count against this view. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal through serious to merely awkward, and for each a brief account of what the world, a formal result, or a well-constructed case would have to be like for it to bite. Some candidates I already suspect, though I do not know how to weigh them. The location of the threshold looks arbitrary, and prescriptions seem to change discontinuously as it is nudged; the view appears committed to preferring a world containing one instance of just-above-threshold agony over a world containing many times as much just-below-threshold discomfort, and I would like to know whether that is a genuine absurdity or a bullet a consistent defender should simply bite. There may also be a collapse worry: as the threshold is pushed to one extreme the view seems to degenerate into plain negative utilitarianism, and at the other extreme into plain hedonic utilitarianism, so I would like to know whether what I hold is a stable intermediate position or merely an unstable way of sitting on the fence — and, more generally, whether intermediate positions of this shape tend to be defensible or are usually symptoms of ad hoc qualification.

I would also like to know what the view predicts that its rivals do not, so that I can see what it actually risks. Does it imply, for instance, that preventing a brief episode of above-threshold suffering takes strict priority over any distribution of below-threshold benefits to any number of persons, and if so, is that a commitment that could in principle be shown untenable, or one its defenders should welcome? Which of these difficulties would a committed defender of the view concede as counting against it, and which would be met with a further qualification — and, honestly, has the threshold device already insulated the view against refutation, so that no possible case could move someone who has adopted it? If it is insulated in this way, say whether that is a defect in a view of this kind or simply what should be expected of positions in normative ethics generally. Finally, if you think the two clauses are in tension in a way I have not noticed — for instance, that the commensurability claim below the threshold and the incommensurability claim above it cannot both be maintained without an argument I do not currently possess — say so directly rather than charitably.
