quick context: I write about science and ideas for a mid-size outlet, and I'm working on a piece about where the actual risk lives in AI systems. At a conference last month the same analogy came up in like four different panels, always from the "just keep it a tool" side: tool AI is a forklift, agent AI is a new hire.

The picture they push is this. The forklift has enormous power and zero discretion — it lifts exactly what the operator points it at, nothing more, and any damage it does is bounded by what the operator intended. The new hire takes on goals rather than tasks, fills in unspecified details however they see fit, acts while you're not watching, and slowly develops priorities of their own. So the argument goes: safety work should concentrate on agents, and a well-designed tool basically can't misbehave on its own, the way a parked forklift can't.

But one panelist said something I haven't been able to shake. A forklift doesn't get better at lifting the more you use it, and it has no representation of what it's carrying. Meanwhile the exact same model weights can be an autocomplete-style tool on Monday and the brain of a fully autonomous agent on Tuesday — the difference isn't in the AI, it's entirely in the scaffolding around it (the loop structure, the memory, what APIs it's plugged into), and that scaffolding gets built by whoever ships fastest. So on her view the forklift/new-hire line isn't a line between two kinds of AI at all, it's a line between two kinds of setups, and calling one of them "the tool" is doing sneaky work in the argument.

I want to actually understand this before I write the piece instead of just quoting people, so can you audit the analogy properly for me? Specifically I'd love:

A correspondence table with exactly these columns: (1) "source (forklift or hire)" — the element on the human side; (2) "AI counterpart" — what it's supposed to map onto in current systems; (3) "mapping holds?" — yes / partially / no; (4) "why" — one or two sentences; (5) "matters for the safety conclusion?" — yes/no, since some of the disanalogies probably don't affect the conclusion the analogy was recruited to support. Try to cover at least: the operator, the fixed capability of the machine, discretion over unforseen details, acting without supervision, and where accountability lands when something goes wrong.

Then, in prose after the table: what the forklift half quietly smuggles in (e.g. that "tool-ness" is a stable property of the system rather than of the deployment), what the new-hire half smuggles in (e.g. that an agent's goals are legible and correctable the way an employee's are), and — this is the part I care about most — whether the original conclusion ("tool AI is basically safe, safety work should focus on agents") survives once you lay the disanalogies out, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion the whole time.
