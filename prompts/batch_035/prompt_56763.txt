so I've got myself into a bit of a position on this and I want someone to stress-test it. My view: what everyone calls "the alignment problem" isn't one problem at all, it's at least three loosely related ones that only look unified because the same small world of labs, blogs and conferences talks about all of them. Roughly (a) a control/engineering problem — getting a capable system to reliably do what you intended; (b) a value-specification problem — working out what we *should* be intending in the first place, which is basically old-fashioned ethics plus formalisation; and (c) an institutional problem — who gets to decide, and what stops whoever holds the keys from deciding badly or selfishly. On my picture the unity is mostly sociological, not deep: nothing about (a) makes it the same problem as (c), they just happen to share a vocabulary and a community.

What would count against it? I want the things that should move me, ranked from "would force me to give this up" through "merely awkward", including stuff that has arguably already happened. The kind of thing I have in mind: if it turned out there's a single underlying failure mode — say, optimisation for imperfect proxies — that generates the whole cluster, that would be real evidence for genuine unity and I'd have to weaken the view. Conversely I'm aware the view is at risk of being insulated: since it's a claim about how to carve up a problem space rather than about the world directly, someone could say there's no fact of the matter at all and I'm just pushing a framing. If you think that's right, say so straight — and if it is right, tell me whether it makes the whole question trivial or whether framings like this still have consequences worth arguing about (e.g. for how research funding or org responsibilities get divided).

Also happy to hear what a defender of the "one problem" view would themselves concede counts against them — I don't want only the case for their side. And what I'd personally find most persuasive: a concrete case where progress on one subproblem made another one worse in a way that only makes sense if they're really the same problem deep down. If you can't think of any such case, that's data too.
