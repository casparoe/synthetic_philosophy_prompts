I write long-form pieces for a science-and-ideas section, and I am at work on a feature about what our physical theories could still be telling us when the things they talk about keep turning out not to exist — phlogiston, caloric, the luminiferous ether. The standard escape, I gather, is structural realism: do not believe in the entities, believe in the structure; Fresnel's equations, for instance, survived the death of his elastic-solid ether into Maxwell's theory, and it was on the strength of that case that the position was advertised as the "best of both worlds" — realist enough to avoid making the success of science miraculous, restrained enough to survive the pessimistic induction. I had assumed this was a sensible middle position. Then, over coffee, a philosopher of physics walked me through an argument that purports to show it is no position at all. It seems to prove too much, and I cannot locate the faulty step. Here it is, more or less as he gave it to me.

(1) The pessimistic induction gives us reason to expect that the entities posited by our best current theories will go the way of the ether; yet something did survive from Fresnel to Maxwell. So, the structural realist concludes, what we know of the unobservable world is its relational structure, not the intrinsic natures of whatever stands in those relations.

(2) But, as Newman observed in 1928 against Russell, a claim to purely structural knowledge conveys nothing beyond cardinality. Any collection of objects of the right size can be equipped with relations that instantiate any given structure; so if all we know is structure, then knowing that the theory's structure is instantiated in the world requires no more than knowing that the world contains enough things to carry it — which we knew already. The structural claim thus collapses into a triviality about how many things there are.

(3) The usual reply is to require that the relations in question be the ones our physics actually invokes, or natural relations in some metaphysician's sense, rather than any gerrymandered set of the right shape.

(4) But a restriction of that kind is itself a piece of non-structural knowledge: knowing which families of relation are natural, or which of them our physics is genuinely committed to, is knowing more than structure. So the epistemic structural realist either settles for the triviality of (2) or quietly helps herself to exactly the sort of knowledge she forswore in (1).

(5) The natural retreat is ontic structural realism: deny that there is anything more to the objects than their structural position, so that there is no extra non-structural fact for us to be ignorant of.

(6) Yet a structure is indifferent between its isomorphic copies: relabel the nodes and nothing structural changes. If an object just is a node in a structure, then which particular thing occupies which position is settled by nothing more than labelling — the world itself cannot distinguish an assignment of objects to positions from its permutation.

(7) But science does not read like that in the laboratory. A cloud-chamber photograph is described as showing this particle scattering from that one; detectors register individual events; a count of events is a count of individual occurrences, and theories are tested against them.

(8) So structural realism, in any of its forms, either says something trivially true (2), or rests on non-structural commitments it officially renounces (4), or cannot underwrite the individuative judgements of everyday experimental practice (6)–(7). There is no stable middle position; the best of both worlds was never on offer.

He finished by saying that this shows the whole structuralist programme was a confusion — realism trimmed until it fits the history of science is realism trimmed until it fits anything. That conclusion is more than my feature needs, and I would rather not accept (8): the survival of Fresnel's mathematics into Maxwell seems to me to demand some non-instrumentalist explanation, and the piece depends on that. But I cannot see whether the argument fails at (2), at (4), at the move from (5) to (6), or somewhere in (6)–(7), and I have no ear for where an argument of this shape usually slips. Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked innocent, and what each available rejection costs — including, if you think it can be done, the price of denying (7) outright.
