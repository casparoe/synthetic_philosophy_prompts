I'm taking a seminar this term on the philosophy of animal minds and animal ethics, and we've spent several weeks on the question of whether sentience is all-or-nothing or a matter of degree, with moral status tracking the richness of a creature's phenomenal life rather than flipping on at some threshold. For my final paper I want to analyze the UK's Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022 through the lens of this graded picture. In case the details matter: the Act was passed partly to replace the recognition of animals as sentient beings that the UK had previously carried through its EU membership, and it established an Animal Sentience Committee to scrutinize government policy for its effects on animal welfare. As originally drafted it covered only vertebrates, but after an independent review of the scientific evidence published in late 2021, it was amended in the House of Lords to extend coverage to cephalopod molluscs (octopuses, squid, cuttlefish) and decapod crustaceans (crabs, lobsters, shrimp) — while the same review considered and declined to recommend covering bivalve molluscs such as oysters and mussels, judging the evidence of sentience there insufficient.

What I'd like is a genuine case analysis that runs in both directions: what the degree-based view of sentience and moral status would say about this legislative episode, and what the episode in turn shows about that view. Some questions I keep circling: the Act draws a bright legal line exactly where the graded view says nature has only a gradient — vertebrates and these two invertebrate groups inside, everything else (insects, fish relatives like... well, everything from bees to bivalves) outside — so is the line-drawing a fatal embarrassment for the graded view, a foreseeable pragmatic concession, or actually evidence that law can handle thresholds even when the underlying property is continuous? And the selectivity is striking: octopuses and crabs in, oysters out, insects not seriously on the table, even though on many graded accounts insects sit somewhere in the same murky middle region as decapods. Does the degree view explain the inclusion list better than a rival binary view would, or does the episode reveal that public intuitions were tracking something else entirely (intelligence, similarity to us, cuteness)?

Please organize your answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion. I'd suggest (but leave to you) that the five points cover roughly: how the graded view reads the episode's basic structure, what it explains well, what it misdescribes or is silent about, how a binary-threshold rival fares on the same facts, and what the episode ultimately teaches us about the graded view's practical viability.
