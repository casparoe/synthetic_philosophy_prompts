Every spring I teach the intro epistemology course, and every spring I hit the day where I admit to the students that some of our "shared" intuitions aren't shared across cultures, and every spring I realize I have no principled story for when that variation actually matters. I've written down my gut reactions to five cases where intuitions apparently differ between cultures. I want to know whether there's one principle that generates all five verdicts or whether I'm just inconsistent. I don't need a reading list or the names of studies — assume the survey results I describe are real; I want the principle.

One: the Gettier setup, Smith believes the man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket, and it turns out Smith himself gets the job and, by coincidence, has ten coins. Suppose surveys show participants in one culture mostly say he knows, and participants in another culture mostly say he doesn't. My reaction: this barely moves me. I hold onto "that's not knowledge" and tell myself the variation is about people's willingness to say "knows" out loud, not about the concept. Two: the reference case where the community has only ever heard the famous name attached to a theorem, believes he proved it, but in the story he stole it from some obscure person nobody has heard of. Asked who a speaker using that famous name is talking about, people in one culture mostly say the famous guy, while in another culture a lot of them say the obscure real discoverer. My reaction: also barely moves me — reference isn't up to a vote. Though I feel a little shakier here, because if usage drifted within a single community over a few generations I'd happily say the reference shifted, so why can't it just differ between communities? I don't have an answer and its starting to bother me.

Three: the harmless-taboo moral cases, the victimless-but-disgusting stuff. Huge variation across cultures in whether people judge it wrong. My reaction: here the variation DOES defeat the intuition for me; the diversity feels like evidence that there's no fact of the matter waiting to be intuited. Four: free will. Suppose one culture is much more comfortable than another with "he's still responsible even though he was determined to act that way." My reaction: the variation makes me MORE confident that there was never a pre-theoretic fact of the matter, so it's a free pass to theorize rather than either holding firm or conceding. Five: beauty. Cultures disagree about whether a given thing is beautiful. My reaction: no defeat, no alarm, this is just what I'd expect — which makes me suspicious, because it suggests the case is doing no work and I'm only comfortable where I'd expect to be.

Cases three and four are the ones I can't square. Both are normative, they cry out for seperate treatments, but in three I let the variation kill the intuition's authority while in four I take the variation to show the intuition was never tracking anything. One versus three is almost as bad: epistemic and moral judgment both look normative, but the variation only bothers me in the moral case. So: is there a single principle here — maybe something like "variation defeats an intuition when it tracks a factor irrelevant to whether the concept applies" — or does any such principle have to smuggle in a prior assumption about which domains have facts independent of our attitudes, which would be circular and I'd want to know it? Which of my verdicts would you overturn, and in which direction — and be honest about case one, because I keep going back and forth on whether that one survives. Then tell me what the principle predicts for two cases I haven't given you: whether a culture's differing intuitions about the moral status of a sophisticated AI system should defeat those intuitions, and whether cross-cultural disagreement about a Ship of Theseus-style case should defeat the intuition that it's still the same ship.
