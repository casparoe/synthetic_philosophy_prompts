I would like an analysis of a real historical episode through the lens of a theory: Japan's postwar economic miracle and subsequent stagnation, read through Max Weber's Protestant ethic thesis and the broader question of whether culture can be a cause of economic outcomes.

The facts to work with are the familiar ones. Between roughly 1950 and 1990, Japan went from wartime devastation to the world's second-largest economy, with sustained growth rates unmatched at that point in history. The era was associated with the "salaryman" ethos — lifetime employment, deep company loyalty, long hours, work as the center of identity — often traced to Confucian-inflected values. Then, after the asset-price bubble burst in the early 1990s, Japan entered decades of negligible growth while its work culture, at least initially, changed very little. Note also that Weber himself extended his argument in comparative studies of Chinese and Indian religion, contending that Confucianism in particular lacked the inner tension with the world that had driven Calvinist asceticism.

Analyze the episode through Weber's thesis, and be disciplined about the mapping. In Weber's original mechanism, the sequence runs roughly: predestinarian anxiety, then innerworldly asceticism, then methodical conduct in a worldly calling, then the accumulation of capital. Identify what, if anything, in the Japanese case occupies each slot. Is the salaryman ethos a functional equivalent of innerworldly asceticism with the theological driver removed — and if so, what does that do to Weber's claim that the religious component was essential? Or is the ethos better read as a consequence of development, something that lifetime employment and corporate institutions produced, rather than a cause of it — in which case the case is a caution against reading any observed work ethic as causal?

Then run the analysis in the other direction, using the stagnation as a test. If culture explains the miracle, what explains the collapse of growth under an almost unchanged culture? Consider the responses a culture-explainer might offer — that institutional failures in banking and policy overwhelmed a still-functional culture, that culture did in fact shift in the younger generations, that the very conformism which once produced growth later inhibited restructuring — and say which, if any, of these responses are principled rather than ad hoc. At what point does a causal thesis that can be suspended, diluted, or counteracted at will stop being an explanation at all?

Conclude with a verdict on the culture-as-cause thesis: does the Japanese case support a generalized version of Weber shorn of its specifically Protestant mechanism, does it support only the original and narrow version, or does it show that culture is largely a marker of economic life rather than a motor of it? Where the theory is genuinely silent on some part of the episode, say so rather than forcing it to speak. Please write your answer in American English.
