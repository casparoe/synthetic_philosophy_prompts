For the past few weeks I've been going back and forth with my brother-in-law about a strange little sentence, and I've finally managed to lay out his whole position as a numbered argument, but now that I've written it down, it seems to prove far more than he could possibly want, and I can't locate the exact point where it goes off the rails. The sentence is "It's raining, but I don't believe it." My position is that there's nothing wrong with it as a piece of information: it could be raining while I, having trusted an hour-old forecast for clear skies, confidently believe it isn't. Every part of the sentence could be true at once, which is what I thought you need for a sentence to be sensible. His position is that the sentence is still, in effect, a contradiction, and he's given me a chain of reasoning for that which each looked unobjectionable to me when taken alone.

His argument, as I finally got him to write it out: (1) When you assert something, you automatically present yourself as believing it — that's just part of what asserting is, not an extra claim you're sneaking in. (2) So when someone says "It's raining," they've implicitly said "I believe it's raining." (3) A statement that contradicts itself can never be true, no matter what the weather or anyone's beliefs are actually doing. (4) "It's raining, but I don't believe it" explicitly denies what the speaker, in speaking at all, has implicitly committed herself to. (5) So the sentence contradicts itself, in the same way "It's raining and it's not raining" does. (6) Therefore the sentence cannot be true in any situation, even one where it is in fact raining and the speaker does in fact disbelieve it. He thinks this also explains why the sentence feels so absurd to say, and he mentioned he'd once read that some philosopher named Moore noticed this oddity decades ago.

Here's my problem. If step (6) is right, then the same reasoning seems to apply wherever a speaker asserts something she doesn't believe — which means every lie, and every sincere assertion by someone confused or self-deceived, comes out as automatically false. But that can't be correct. If my neighbor, wanting to make me go outside for his own amusement, tells me the mail has arrived, and it really has arrived, he has lied to me, and he has done it with a true statement. It even seems possible for someone to genuinely believe the whole strange sentence at once: a person who trusts the weather service more than her own eyes might believe that it's raining while not believing that it's raining. So my brother-in-law's argument seems to prove both too much and too little at once. Yet I can't deny that his step (1) sounds right, and I can't put my finger on the exact step where something gets twisted — it feels like the word "contradicts" quietly changes meaning somewhere between (3) and (5). Please identify precisely which step or inference is the mistake, explain why that step looked so natural in the first place, and tell me what my brother-in-law would have to give up if he accepts your diagnosis. Also, if there's a sense in which he's still partially correct about the sentence, I'd like you to say exactly what that sense is and where it stops. Please answer in American English.
