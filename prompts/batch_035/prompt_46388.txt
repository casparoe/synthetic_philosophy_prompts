I run a small blog mostly read by rationalist-adjacent folks who've heard of Newcomb but havn't gone deep into decision theory jargon, and I want a "which response to Pascal's mugging do you actually hold?" quiz. Everyone I know just says something vague like "I'd laugh at the mugger," and I want to pin down the *reason* they laugh, because I suspect my friends are quietly split between like four different incompatible reasons.

The quiz should have around eight questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options. Cases I'd like in there (reword or extend as you see fit): the classic street mugger demanding $10 backed by a threat of 3^^^3 units of disutility; a mirror version where the mugger *offers* a huge reward instead of making a threat, since I've noticed people react differently to huge promised pleasures vs huge threatened pains; a version where the mugger first demonstrates some mildly impossible-seeming ability, so the claim's credibility ticks up but nowhere near enough to make the expected value work out; a case where you're mugged twice in a row and have to say what happens to mugger number two; and a case where you've already donated your entire altruistic budget to what you sincerely believe is the most credible enormous-payoff cause, and a new mugger shows up anyway. Please also include one question built off the dust-specks-vs-torture thing, since half the internet already has opinions on that and I want to reuse them.

Some requirements:

- Every question has to be answerable without knowing any position names, so nothing like "do you favor bounded utilities?" — sort people by what they say about the cases, not by which label they like.
- The scoring key should map answer patterns onto at least these positions: bullet-biting (shut up and multiply, you pay the ten dollars), bounded utilities, the probability response (the prior on a claim should fall at least as fast as the claimed stakes rise — the Hanson-flavored move), the policy response ("I follow a standing rule of not updating on arbitrary verbal claims, and I accept this loses me a little expected value on average"), and the soaking response (my budget is already spent on the most credible huge-payoff thing, so there's nothing left for the mugger to collect).
- At least two leaves should be combinations almost nobody actually defends — flag those and say whether you think they're unoccupied because they're incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored.
- Flag any pair of positions the quiz genuinely cant separate, and if you can, add one question specifically designed to pull them apart.
- Include one question that distinguishes people who abandon expected utility maximization *only for mugging-shaped cases* from people who've abandoned it everywhere. I suspect my readership splits cleanly there and I want to actually measure it.

Keep the whole thing light and fun to take — like a magazine quiz, not an exam. And the scoring key should be honest about messy answer patterns, i.e. it should be willing to tell someone their answers don't add up to a coherent position.
