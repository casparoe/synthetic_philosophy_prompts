You are a professional moral philosopher with particular expertise in aggregation problems, population ethics, and the formal representation of welfare. Please think carefully and methodically: begin with a short brainstorm of candidate principles before committing to any, and be explicit about which verdicts each candidate can and cannot save.

Some background on who is asking. I am a recently retired pharmacologist. I spent forty years thinking about dose-response curves, thresholds, no-observed-adverse-effect levels, and hormesis, and I have lately been reading ethics and noticing that the deepest puzzles all seem to be about whether and how small harms add up. My instinct, trained into me by a career in toxicology, is that the body in which a harm lands matters: the dose makes the poison, and the poison must land somewhere in particular. But I am no longer sure my instincts are coherent, and I would like your help finding the principle behind my own judgments — or being told that there isn't one.

Here are my verdicts on a set of cases, as honest as I can make them. Take a large number — so large it dwarfs the number of humans who could ever live — of people each getting a dust speck in the eye, versus one person tortured for fifty years: the specks, without hesitation, and I have never wavered on this. One dollar taken from each of a hundred million people, none of whom will notice, to build something trivial they'll never use: fine, and I would not even call it a wrong. A city contaminating its reservoir with a chemical that raises each of two million residents' lifetime cancer risk by one in a hundred thousand, expected value twenty extra cancer deaths, to save the city money on filtration: this strikes me as very bad — close, in fact, to killing twenty people, even though no individual's risk change is detectable to them or to any physician. A factory exposing each of a thousand workers to noise that shaves a small, individually imperceptible amount off their hearing, versus a single worker's hearing destroyed outright by a preventable accident: I think the diffuse case is the worse one, though I can see someone going the other way. Fifty people each living a year with chronic moderate pain versus one person living fifty years with it: the fifty people, clearly. And a vaccination program in which each of two million informed volunteers runs a one-in-two-million risk of a fatal reaction — expected value one death — versus conscripting one person for a certainly fatal task to achieve the same public benefit: I accept the vaccine program and absolutely reject the conscription.

My tentative principle, offered for demolition as much as for endorsement: harms do not aggregate across persons until they cross some perceptibility or physiological threshold within a single body — a dose below the threshold in a given organism is, for that organism, nothing, and you cannot add nothings. I already suspect this cannot survive contact with my reservoir verdict, since each resident's risk increase is individually below any threshold I would recognize in the lab, and yet my judgment there treats the case as involving real victims with real harms. And my factory verdict seems to point the same way. Meanwhile my vaccine-versus-conscription pair seems to be tracking consent rather than anything about the size or distribution of harms, which makes me worry that the whole principle, even repaired, only governs some of the cases and something else entirely governs the rest.

So, my questions. Is there one principle, or a small set, that captures all or nearly all of these verdicts? Please state each candidate precisely, tell me which clause each of my cases motivates, and say honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate — I would rather overturn a verdict than adopt a principle stitched together ad hoc to save all seven. Test the surviving candidate or candidates against two further cases I have not decided and am genuinely torn on: a new analgesic that would relieve a serious condition in each of two million patients while certainly costing each of them one day of life expectancy — an aggregate of nearly 5,500 years of life — against the alternative of doing nothing; and a social media feature that adds, with certainty, one second of mild annoyance to each of a billion users daily, in exchange for a benefit concentrated on a few thousand people. Finally, tell me whether my toxicologist's instinct — that the organism, or the person, is the natural unit below which nothing counts — is doing any real work here, or whether I am merely smuggling the habits of my old profession into a domain where they do not belong.
