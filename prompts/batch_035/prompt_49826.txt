I'm in a decision theory elective, and we just spent two weeks on Hofstadter's superrationality stuff from the old Metamagical Themas columns, and now my TA and my flatmate have this ongoing feud I'd love to see properly fought out. Quick setup so we're on the same page: in the Platonia dilemma an eccentric trillionaire gathers 20 people and announces that if exactly one of them sends him a telegram by noon the next day, that person gets a billion dollars, while if zero or more than one telegrams arrive, nobody gets anything, and no communication between players is allowed. Hofstadter's answer was that a superrational player sends a telegram with probability exactly 1/20, since if all twenty reason identically, the symmetric mixed strategy is what maximizes everyone's expected payoff. And in the Luring Lottery he actually ran in Scientific American — where the prize was a million dollars divided by the total number of entries, so more entries meant better odds of winning but a smaller prize — the superrational recommendation was similarly probabilistic: roll a (simulated) die with one side per expected entrant and only enter if you roll a 1. In reality readers submitted entries in the googolplexes and the prize would have been worth a miniscule fraction of a cent, which my flatmate brings up constantly.

Here's the twist our professor added, which is where the seminar exploded: suppose the trillionaire announces beforehand that the 20 players are NOT all identical reasoners — exactly ten are ordinary causal decision theorists who will play the symmetric Nash equilibrium no matter what, and ten are committed superrationalists, and everyone knows these facts but nobody knows who is which. My TA, Tara, initially said superrationality dies here, since the whole premise was "everyone facing this problem will arrive at my answer," but then she pivoted: maybe superrationality just needs to be renormalized to the subgroup — the ten superrationalists reason identically with each other, so they should jointly pick whatever symmetric-among-themselves strategy maximizes expected payoff given that the other ten are playing the equilibrium. My flatmate Marco thinks this is just doing game theory with extra steps and a flattering self-image, and that the famous 1/20 answer was never anything more than the symmetric mixed equilibrium dressed up as cooperation — the die-roll advice for the lottery was likewise just equilibrium selection, so Hofstadter smuggled in standard theory and called it a revolution. Marco also claims he can prove that whatever the ten superrationalists end up doing in the mixed-population variant, a plain expected-value calculation gets them there, so superrationality is adding nothing but vibes.

Could you write a dialogue between Tara and Marco arguing this out over coffee? Some asks: both should be genuinely smart and neither should be a strawman, and they should respond to each others actual points, not lecture past each other. I'd specifically like them to hit (1) whether superrationality is just equilibrium selection in disguise, using the Luring Lottery die-roll answer as the test case, (2) what the superrationalist should actually do in the mixed-population variant — is "renormalize to the subgroup" coherent, or does Tara have to admit she can't rely on mirroring even within her subgroup, since each of the ten superrationalists might reason differently about how to handle the heterogeneity (recursion alert), and (3) whether the googolplex carnage from the real lottery says anything about superrationality as a view of actual humans, or just about Scientific American readers. They don't need to resolve everything, but by the end I want them to have located the real crux — the deepest premise one of them would have to give up for them to agree.
