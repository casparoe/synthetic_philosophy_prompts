My best friend and I had a four hour fight last night about whether I should get tested for Huntington's — it runs in my family, my uncle has it. We were fine on the practical stuff but it got weirdly philosophical. He started with this principle: information can never hurt you, only what you do with it can. So getting tested is always at worst neutral. Then I pointed out there's basically no treatment that changes the course of the disease, so if I test positive there's nothing medical I'd do differently, which by his own logic makes the test worthless, so he flipped to "then don't get tested." But I said wait, I WOULD do things differently — quit the job I hate, spend a year traveling, actually say the stuff to my sister that I keep putting off. And he said those don't count as "real" decisions because I'd be rearranging my life around dread instead of around anything treatable, which felt like cheating somehow. So we went in circles: does knowing something make you better or worse off in itself, or does knowledge only matter through the actions it changes? I remembered someone telling me once there's an actual result in decision theory that free, accurate information can never make you worse off in expectation, like it's been proven, but that can't be the whole story because so many people say they'd rather not know certain things and it seems rational of them. So my actual questions: is there ever a rational reason to refuse a costless, perfectly accurate test? If the answer has to do with the fact that finding out changes how you feel and not just what you do, then what exactly is my friend's "only actions matter" principle missing — is it just wrong, or is it right for some clean idealized version of a person and wrong for actual people like me? And does any of this actually settle whether I should book the appointment, or does it just relocate the question? I'd take the philosophy but honestly I also just want to know what to do.
