I want to practice defending guilt-centered moral cultures against a genuinely sophisticated opponent, since most objections to shame-based ethics I encounter strike me as caricatures — shame makes you hide, guilt makes you repair, end of story. Please play a committed defender of shame as the better moral emotion. Give her a real position with real resources, roughly along these lines: (1) shame is the more honest emotion because it registers that wrongdoing damages who one is, whereas guilt merely updates a ledger of acts; (2) the "repair" behavior guilt is praised for is really an accounting transaction — settle the debt, wipe the slate, remain unchanged — whereas shame demands transformation of the self; (3) the classic objection that shame needs a live audience and therefore collapses in private mistakes the essentially social nature of persons: internalized shame is the imagined audience of the people whose esteem one actually cares about, which is a more demanding and more specific tribunal than guilt's faceless, anonymous rule. She should be sharp rather than a strawman, willing to concede small empirical points (for instance, that proneness to shame correlates with avoidance and defensiveness), but she must never concede the main claim that a culture can, and should, deliberately cultivate shame over guilt.

Here is my opening move. I'll grant you the internalized audience — but I think that concession costs you more than you admit. Guilt, at its best, is answerable to the act itself: I can ask whether the rule was actually broken, and that check is available to the wrongdoer alone in the dark, which is why guilt survives anonymity without becoming anything uglier. Your imagined audience, by contrast, is answerable to no one: it is simply a picture in my head of whoever happens to matter to me, and nothing in shame itself tells me whether that tribunal is tracking anything moral rather than merely conventional. The honor-culture tough feels precisely the shame you describe when he is disrespected in public, and feels none at all while brutalizing someone weaker in private; his internalized audience is exquisitely calibrated — just not to morality. So my challenge is this: how does your view distinguish the shame that moralizes a person from the shame that merely domesticates that person to their local prestige hierarchy, without smuggling in an independent moral standard that quietly does all the work shame was supposed to do?
