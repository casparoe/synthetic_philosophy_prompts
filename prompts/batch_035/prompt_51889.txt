I trained in philosophy of language and now work on interpretability of language models, and an argument from my graduate department has followed me into the new job. My engineering colleagues keep telling me that a language model trained on text alone is Davidson's radical interpreter made flesh: it starts with nothing but the behavioral evidence, and it settles on whatever meaning assignment makes the corpus coherent — charity as a training objective, more or less. My philosopher friends insist this is a category error, but when I press them on what the radical interpreter has that the model lacks (or vice versa), the answers get vague. Before I referee that dispute, I realize I need something neither side has written down: a proper specification of what is even being claimed. So here is what I want from you: the desiderata for a theory of meaning for a natural language that is adequate for radical interpretation — where "adequate" means good enough to serve as the output of interpreting an agent from non-semantic evidence alone, with no shared language, no dictionary, no prior meanings to lean on.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it. Some seeds, though I want you to add to and prune this list rather than just tidy it: whatever forces such a theory to be compositional rather than an unbounded lookup table; whatever forces it to be testable against the interpreter's actual evidence — patterns of assent and dissent in observable circumstances — rather than against paraphrase intuitions the interpreter cannot have; whatever forces it to keep separate what a speaker believes from what her words mean, so that interpretation can survive massive false belief; publicity, in the sense that the theory must be recoverable from behavior available to any suitably placed observer; coverage of the indexical and demonstrative apparatus, which Davidson's own Tarskian machinery handled awkwardly at best; and the late-Davidson complication from the malapropism paper (A Nice Derangement of Epitaphs), where speakers communicate successfully while violating every standing convention — whether that sinks the requirement that meaning attach to a shared prior theory, or just relocates it. My own hypothesis, which you should treat as a hypothesis and test rather than endorse, is that the charity requirement and the evidence requirement are in tension: charity looks like it smuggles the interpreter's own semantics in as a premise, doing epistemology where the theory promised to do semantics.

Beyond the list itself, tell me which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely prejudices of the Tarskian program that happened to come bundled with it, and — the part I care most about — flag any subset of the desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, since an inconsistent specification would itself be a result about what radical interpretation can deliver. Then, briefly, score three candidates against the list: a Tarski-style truth theory as Davidson originally deployed it; the interpreter as actually characterized in the field-linguist thought experiment; and the distributional learner as a rough engineering approximation. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; the scorecard can be terse. Close with the single requirement that does the most work excluding candidates (and one sentence on why), stated at the very end in the form \answer{short label}, where the short label names that requirement.
