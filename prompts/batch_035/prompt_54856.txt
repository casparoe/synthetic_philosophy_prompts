My grandfather's wooden fishing boat turns 100 next year, and the local maritime heritage society gives an original-vessel plaque to boats that old, which has caused an absurd amount of family drama, so I've been trying to figure out what I actually think "the same boat" means, going by my gut on the cases. Here are my verdicts, honestly reported:

Granddad maintained her himself for 50 years, replacing a plank here and a plank there as things rotted, like you do. Same boat the whole time, obviously, right up to the end.

In '87 a hurricane smashed her up and the shipyard basically replaced half the hull in one go over a winter. Still the same boat, though I admit she feels slightly less "hers" after that than after the slow maintenance.

That same winter (bad year) he also had her fully taken apart once for a refit, every plank numbered and stacked in the barn for six years, then rebuilt using the exact same planks, nothing substituted. And weirdly this is the one I hesitate on?? Nothing was even replaced. But some part of me goes "hmm" at it, and I can't tell if that's a real intuition or just me being confused.

Meanwhile my uncle, who is not a popular man at Thanksgiving, saved a bunch of the planks that got swapped out over the decades of normal maintenance, and has been slowly building a second boat out of them in his garage. It's nearly finished. My gut says his garage thing is NOT the boat — but I can't give you a reason, and an annoying voice in my head says it's somehow more "the boat" than the one in the water, made as it is from original material.

Same question with materials: swapping rotted planks for the same species of wood is fine, but if you replace with fiberglass section by section, at some point it stops being the boat. I don't know where the point is, and I suspect "when it's more than half fiberglass" is me making stuff up.

And a sister ship built from the same plans at the same yard two slips over: never the boat, trivially.

So: is there one principle that actually captures all of this — spatiotemporal continuity, continuity of form, best candidate, whatever — or am I forced to drop some verdicts? Because 1 and 4 together look straight up contradictory to me (slow replacement preserves the boat, but rebuilding from the original planks doesn't make one?), and 3 is a mess on any story. Tell me which of my verdicts a consistent principle would overrule and why, what the principle says about whether we should apply for the plaque, and then apply it to a case I haven't decided: my uncle's garage boat gets finished and launched in another harbor while the original is still afloat and still being maintained — if both are candidates for being "the" boat, is there any determinate fact about when, or whether, the original stopped being it? And is it a problem if my answer turns out to be "there's no fact of the matter, it depends what the plaque is for"? That's actually allowed by the heritage society rules, weirdly, which just makes it worse.
