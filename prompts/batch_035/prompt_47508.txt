I run a small independent classical label, and the topic has just become awkwardly practical for us: last year we released an album of sonatas presented as rediscovered eighteenth-century works, and a musicologist has now established — with the composer's own confession — that they were written in 2019 and deliberately passed off as historical finds. Our listeners still love the record; some feel betrayed. Before deciding whether to withdraw it, repackage it with the honest attribution, or leave it be, I want to understand what people actually think the forgery took from them.

Please act as a professional philosopher of art with deep expertise in aesthetics and the ontology of art. Think carefully, and begin with some private brainstorming about which questions genuinely divide the positions on the value of originals before writing anything. Then write, for the label's newsletter, a "which view of originality do you actually hold?" quiz for our subscribers — educated listeners with no philosophy background.

The quiz should have eight to ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three short, first-person answer options. No question may name any philosopher, use theory jargon, or ask directly which "view" the reader holds; the point is to sort people by their reactions to cases, not by their loyalty to labels. The cases must include: (a) our own situation — a forged sonata that was praised before the attribution collapsed; (b) a Vermeer-style painting forgery that critics genuinely admired until the reveal; (c) a perfect physical duplicate of a painting secretly swapped into a museum frame, with the original destroyed and no one ever the wiser; (d) a Kreisler-style situation — pieces composed in an old style and performed for years under false attributions to long-dead masters; and (e) a digitally native artwork whose "original" and "copy" are bit-for-bit identical. Add whatever further cases you find diagnostic.

Then provide a scoring key built from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first identify the two or three questions that actually split the positions (does the reveal retroactively change the value of the same sounds or surfaces? is the locus of that value the object's causal history, our experience of it, or the state of our knowledge about it? is musical composition even the kind of thing that can be faked, given that a painting is one physical object but a sonata is a pattern instantiated in performances?), and only then locate the named positions in that space. The leaves should include at least: aesthetic empiricism (nothing post-reveal changes the work's value); historically grounded accounts on which value depends on the object's relation to a tradition; the view of forgery as misrepresentation of a performance of making; the Goodman-style point that fakes are possible only in autographic arts, so a forged "sonata" is a fraud but not a fake in the painter's sense; epistemic-contamination views on which what is spoiled is the reliability of our evidence rather than the experience itself; and scarcity- or pedigree-based economic accounts. Flag at least one combination of answers that no named position occupies, and diagnose whether nobody holds it because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; flag any pair of leaves your cases cannot reliably separate. Close with one paragraph applying the key to our album: what each leaf implies we should do with it. Do not include citations or a reading list — I'll add any references myself.
