I spent forty years in geophysics, and the textbook cases of successful theory in my field were things like plate tectonics, which won acceptance largely by explaining facts everyone had long known — the fit of the continental margins, matching fossil distributions across the Atlantic. Since retiring I have been reading Bayesian confirmation theory, and I gather there is a "problem of old evidence": an agent who is already certain of a fact assigns it probability 1, and so no hypothesis can raise that probability, meaning the fact can never confirm anything. If Bayesianism really says that degrees of belief are the whole story of confirmation, it follows that the discipline I practiced never once saw a theory confirmed, because all of our evidence was already old the moment a theory arrived to explain it — Mercury's perihelion could not have confirmed general relativity any more than a sunrise could. Why do philosophers treat this as a puzzle to be patched rather than a decisive refutation of the Bayesian approach?
