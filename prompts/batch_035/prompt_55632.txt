"The fair play account says political obligation arises from participation in a cooperative scheme. When the state provides public goods — security, roads, courts — that everyone benefits from, those who accept the benefits incur a duty to do their part, and obeying the law is what doing one's part consists in. In this respect it works like Locke's tacit consent: by going on enjoying the benefits without protest, citizens are taken to have agreed to obey, so at bottom the duty is a form of consent. Rawls, who originated the fair play account in A Theory of Justice, accepts it, and it follows that in a reasonably just society citizens must comply with the laws whatever their content, since the benefits keep flowing to them regardless; the duty lapses only when a law is so unjust that complying would breach the limit of fidelity. Rawls defines civil disobedience as lawbreaking done from conscience, and since it is sincere and nonviolent it is automatically compatible with fidelity to law; it can even be carried out privately, for example by quietly refusing to comply, which Rawls treats as the paradigm case."
