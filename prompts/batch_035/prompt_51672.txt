I sit on the grantmaking committee of a regional conservation trust, and at next month's meeting we must decide on the largest single project in our history: roughly 800 hectares of drained agricultural bottomland that the trust has already purchased, to be restored to seasonal wetland by re-meandering the channel, removing field drains, and replanting emergent vegetation. Our consulting ecologists project a roughly tenfold increase in animal biomass within a decade, dominated numerically by midges, mayflies, and amphibians — on the order of hundreds of thousands of small, short-lived individuals per hectare per year, the great majority dying as larvae or juveniles — along with waterfowl nesting habitat and a modest fishery.

The problem is that the committee has fractured along a line none of us anticipated when we bought the land. One trustee argues that natural ecosystems are essentially Malthusian: population growth runs up against resource limits, so most individual animals are born only to die young of starvation, predation, or desiccation, and the smaller and more fecund the organism, the more of its life is spent in these conditions. On that view, converting sparse farmland into a teeming wetland doesn't create habitat so much as manufacture suffering at industrial scale, and our proper constituency is ecosystem function and biodiversity, not the welfare of individual sentient beings — goals he claims the project serves and the suffering objection simply does not touch. A second trustee replies that we have no more warrant for assuming average wild-animal welfare is negative than for assuming it is positive; that the "idyllic" and "Malthusian" pictures are both guesses projected onto data we do not have; and that if we accepted the first trustee's reasoning, we would be committed to applauding the draining of wetlands and the paving of meadows, which none of us will do. A third has proposed a compromise redesign: fewer ephemeral, insect-producing shallows and more deep permanent pools, which would support far fewer but larger, longer-lived animals — fish, turtles, herons — on the theory that if we cannot estimate welfare well, we can at least reduce the number of deaths per hectare. Finally, our director reminds us that the purchase is sunk, the state matching grant expires in fourteen months, and a public refusal to restore land we campaigned to buy would be a reputational disaster.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before the meeting, in American English, no more than about 1,000 words. It should recommend one of four outcomes — approve as proposed, approve with the K-selected redesign, approve contingent on a specified monitoring or welfare-research program (say what it should actually measure), or reject — and defend it with reasoning the losing trustees could recognize as fair rather than as a restatement of our mission statement. In particular it should confront, not dodge: (1) whether "ecosystem health" and "aggregate welfare of inhabitants" are genuinely different goals that can come apart, or two descriptions of the same value; (2) how a funding body should act when the decision turns on quantities — the average welfare of a mayfly larva — that no one can estimate with any confidence, and whether the precautionary logic cuts toward creating more habitat or less; and (3) the strongest version of the reductio, that a principle implying we ought to destroy habitat to reduce suffering has lost contact with any ethics the trust can avow in public. The final line of the memo must be machine-readable, exactly of the form \decision{...} containing one of: APPROVE-AS-PROPOSED, APPROVE-REDESIGN, APPROVE-CONTINGENT, or REJECT.
