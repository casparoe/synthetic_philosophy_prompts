In a short 1997 paper, David Lewis asked whether penal substitution is something we can coherently believe in: whether an innocent, devoted volunteer can be justly punished in a guilty offender's place, and whether that punishment can satisfy the demands of retributive justice, as the doctrine of the atonement says Christ's suffering satisfied the demands owed for our sins. Lewis's own answer, as I understand it, leans on the volunteer's consent and on some relation of identification between the substitute and the offender — the substitute is punished not as a stranger but as, in some sense, the offenders themselves — while conceding that human criminal law, with its mixed aims of deterrence and reform, could never work this way.

Write a satirical policy op-ed, around 650 words, in the voice of the director of an invented criminal-justice think tank modestly proposing that the state adopt penal substitution outright: a program of vetted, consenting volunteer substitutes who serve prisoners' sentences in their place, complete with an identification procedure to make substitute and offender co-offenders in the eyes of the law. The target of the satire is the retributivist logic of punishment, not Christianity or any religious group; the theological doctrine is only the program's stated inspiration, mentioned by the director as the successful pilot project. The director must be deadpan, sincere, and internally consistent throughout: every objection a reader would raise — that desert doesn't transfer, that punishment communicates censure that misfires when aimed at someone else, that deterrence collapses the moment substitution is known, that the volunteer's consent can't make the state's demand for suffering respectable — must be anticipated and answered with the moves actually available in the literature (voluntariness, satisfaction of the retributive demand, identification), never conceded. The deterrence problem should appear only as a fine-print implementation detail the director cheerfully waves away. A reader familiar with the philosophy of punishment should finish the piece having felt the real question — whether suffering simpliciter is the sort of thing that can be assigned, like a debt, to whoever will consent to bear it — without the piece ever stating it.
