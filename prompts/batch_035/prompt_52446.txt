This term I am teaching a graduate seminar on scientific realism, and in our unit on underdetermination a student produced an argument that I find difficult to answer cleanly. It is constructed to show, not merely that current evidence fails to decide between our theories, but that no possible evidence ever could, and it proceeds as follows. (1) For any theory T and any body of evidence E accumulated up to a given time, one can construct a rival that disagrees with T about some unobservable or unobserved matter while agreeing with T in every prediction it makes about E — for instance, the disjunction of T with the supposition that the relevant part of reality differs from what T says while being made to appear exactly as T predicts. Such rivals are cheap, so at any moment the evidence then in hand underdetermines theory choice. (2) As evidence accumulates, the tie could be broken only by an observation about which T and its rival disagree outright; but (3) whenever such an observation threatens, the rival can be patched with a small auxiliary — the discrepancy is masked, the instruments were interfered with, the underlying difference is arranged not to show itself — and patches of this kind are always available. (4) Therefore, for every theory T there exists a rival T* such that no possible evidence, however extensive, could ever favor T over T*. (5) If no possible evidence could ever favor T over T*, then rational belief cannot favor T over T*, since warranted belief must answer to evidence. (6) So we are never warranted in believing any theory over its gerrymandered rival — not general relativity, not the germ theory, and not, by the same maneuver, the hypothesis that the world has existed for more than five minutes.

It is the last extension that I cannot accept, and the student, naturally, treasures it. My first instinct was to object that something illicit occurs between (3) and (4): that the argument slides from "whenever the evidence changes, the rival can be changed accordingly" to "there is a fixed rival that all possible evidence fails to discriminate," trading the order of the quantifiers over evidence and over rivals. But the student has a reply that gives me pause: the patches in (3), he says, are not improvised one by one; they are all instances of the single standing supposition already built into the rival in (1), namely that reality behind the phenomena differs from T while appearing not to. If that is right, then the rival was never a moving target, and my quantifier complaint may be aimed at a step the argument never takes. I am therefore no longer certain whether the fault is a bad inference, a bad premise, or instead a premise that is true but weaker than it looks. Before I respond in seminar, I want a diagnosis I can defend rather than a suspicion I can only assert.

So I would ask you to tell me where the argument stands or falls. Which step should be rejected, and — this matters to me as much as the identification — what does rejecting it cost, both within the philosophy of science and for the student's wider conclusion? For my own part, I should like to keep (1), which strikes me as a boring truth about the momentary state of inquiry, and some version of (5), since a view on which evidence is altogether irrelevant to warranted belief scarcely deserves the name of epistemology. But I would rather learn where you think the trouble genuinely lies, and what each candidate rejection obliges one to give up, than be confirmed in my original suspicion. Please be specific about why the offending step looked innocent in the first place; that is the part my students will not let me hand-wave.
