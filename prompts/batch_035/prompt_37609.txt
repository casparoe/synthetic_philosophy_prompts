I help facilitate a reading group for adults with about a semester of political philosophy behind them — enough to have opinions, not enough to have allegiances. Our closing session is on the "equality of what?" debate, and I want to end it with a diagnostic quiz rather than a lecture. Please write me a "which view do you actually hold?" instrument: ten to twelve questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options phrased as verdicts about who should get what or who bears which cost, never as labels or slogans.

The cases I want included, phrased as short vignettes: a person who develops an expensive taste for rare wines after joining a circle of friends who glamorize connoisseurship; a person born with a chronic condition requiring lifelong, costly treatment; a person with the same condition living where a cheap generic drug fully neutralizes it; someone whose mild but untreated depression drains the pleasure from everything they can afford; a devout person whose religious observance makes ordinary labor markets expensive for them to participate in; a contented grass-counter who lacks most things we'd normally call opportunities but reports perfect life satisfaction; a highly talented surfer who chooses leisure over any contribution; two citizens with identical income of whom one faces much higher prices for the basics because of where a boundary was drawn.

Structure the scoring key on two axes plus an exit. Axis one: the metric — welfare (however measured), resources, capabilities. Axis two: responsibility — whether voluntarily acquired traits, tastes, and commitments change what justice owes you, or whether the whole choice/luck cut is a mistake. The exit is the relational position: someone who answers several cases by rejecting the framing itself — that the question was never how to divide a pie among separable individuals but what kinds of standing people should have to one another. So the leaves should include at least: a straight welfare view; a resource view with an envy-test or auction flavor; a capabilities view; a responsibility-sensitive (luck-egalitarian) reading that can cross-cut the first three; and the relational exit. Locate the well-known proponents in that space rather than letting their names do the classifying, and mark clearly where, say, a responsibility-sensitive resource view and a responsibility-sensitive welfare view actually come apart in your answer patterns — or fail to.

Three further requirements. First, no question may ask about a position; every option must be something someone with no theory of the case could still sincerely pick, so the instrument sorts by verdicts, not vocabulary. Second, for each question note in the key which pair of leaves it separates and which pairs it leaves untouched, and if after drafting you find two leaves no question can pull apart, say so at the end rather than inventing a fake diagnostic — including whether that indistinguishability reflects a real convergence in the literature or a gap in the quiz. Third, include a closing note on the genuinely unoccupied or unstable combinations: e.g., someone who compensates for the congenital condition yet also for the acquired wine taste, or someone who compensates for neither yet still calls the result equality — say for each whether nobody holds it because it's incoherent, because it's unattractive once stated, or just because no one has bothered. The point of the session is for participants to discover what they believe from cases before they learn what to call it, so the quiz must be answerable — and mis-answerable — without any of the terminology.
