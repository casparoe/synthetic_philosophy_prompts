So I teach a moral psychology course at a mid-sized state school (about 20 students, mostly sophomores, no stats background, 75-minute seminar sessions), and next week we hit the part of the syllabus on experimental philosophy and moral judgment. I've been reading the rationalist-sphere long enough to know that the chairman-and-the-environment side-effect asymmetry is the canonical example, but that's exactly the problem: a chunk of my students have absorbed enough internet philosophy that if I just present the case and lecture on it, half the room will nod along to whatever framing I give them instead of noticing anything in their own heads. What I want is an activity where they are the subjects before they are the theorists. My vague idea is some kind of split-room setup where each half of the class reads a slightly different version of the same vignette, argues about what the protagonist did and why, and only then discovers the versions differed by a single word -- so the asymmetry shows up as mutual incredulity between the two groups rather than as something I assert. But I'm open to a better mechanism if you have one.

Design the full thing: materials (I can print handouts but want minimal prep), the actual vignettes or instructions for writing them (they can be new cases, not just the environmental one, as long as they're the same shape), a timeline for the 75 minutes, and how to handle the reveal. After the reveal I want a second phase where small groups design their own variant cases to try to break the effect -- e.g. swapping in a non-moral side effect, or changing who the agent is -- and test them on the other groups, with a running tally on the board. Then a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the actual interpretive dispute (is moral judgment penetrating the folk concept of intentionality, or is it just conversational pragmatics about implied blame), rather than just announcing "and this is called the Knobe effect" at the end -- though I do want the name withheld until the very end.

Also, and this is the part I actually need help with: tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this. My guess is some students will see through the split-room gimmick early because someone will mention the handouts at lunch, and I don't know how paranoid to be about that. And be frank about what a classroom version of this genuinely can't reproduce -- I'm aware that a noisy show of hands in a room of 20 who've all been chatting with each other is not a between-subjects experiment, and I'd rather know which parts of the real finding the activity can carry and which parts I have to smuggle in during the debrief, so I don't oversell it to them.
