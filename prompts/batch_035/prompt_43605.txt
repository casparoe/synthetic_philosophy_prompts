In my animal ethics course we've been discussing insect sentience, and I keep getting stuck on the pattern I see in actual regulation, so I'd like help figuring out what it presupposes. Consider two practices. First, research regulation: in the EU, the rules governing animal experimentation cover all vertebrates and were extended to cephalopods (octopuses, cuttlefish, squid) on the grounds that there is credible evidence these animals feel pain — but insects, crustaceans, and other invertebrates were left entirely outside the system. A researcher needs ethics approval and justification to run a study on a single fish, but none whatsoever to run one on ten thousand fruit flies. Second, food production: industrial insect farming, which raises billions of crickets and black soldier fly larvae for feed and food, currently operates with essentially no welfare standards, and routine killing methods like boiling or shredding would be illegal if applied to pigs or chickens. At the same time, the same broader legal culture is happy to act on mere plausibility of harm elsewhere — chemicals are restricted when danger is suspected, not proven.

What I want to understand is what theory of moral status this pattern implicitly rests on. Does it presuppose that sentience is an all-or-nothing threshold rather than a matter of degree, so that uncertain cases simply fall on the unprotected side? Does it presuppose that moral protection should track the balance of scientific evidence rather than expected harm — and if so, why does that principle apparently not apply to the far larger number of individuals involved, given that even a low per-insect probability of sentience, multiplied across numbers that dwarf all vertebrate livestock combined, might be expected to matter enormously? Or is the real presupposition something about institutional tractability or about a bright line being administratively necessary even if philosophically arbitrary?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory behind these rules, distinguish what the pattern strictly presupposes from what it is merely compatible with, and explain what would actually have to change in research oversight and in farming practice if we replaced the current approach with a precautionary, expected-value-based one.
