i'm a philosophy PhD student working on formal epistemology, and after another seminar shouting match about the surprise exam paradox I've decided the honest way forward is to lay out my gut verdicts on a bunch of variants and see whether one principle sits under them, because right now they look inconsistent and I want to know which of them has to go.

Setup for all of them: a teacher announces an exam, the students know the teacher is truthful, and "surprise" means that at no point before the exam starts do the students know which day it is. Here are my verdicts. One-day version: "exam tomorrow, and you won't know in advance that it's tomorrow." Verdict: the teacher cant pull this off — if the students trust her, that night they know it's tomorrow, so the announcement defeats itself; if they don't trust her it can come true, but that's winning by being disbelieved, which feels like cheating. Two-day version: "exam Thursday or Friday, you won't know in advance which." Verdict: Thursday works, the students can't know Wednesday night; and Friday is genuinely impossible, since by Thursday night they'd know. So the first step of the students' elimination reasoning is sound here. Five-day version where the exam turns up on Wednesday: my verdict is that the students are surprised, so the iterated elimination must break somewhere between step one and step two, but I can't say where, because step two is the same reasoning as step one run one day earlier.

The case I keep going back and forth on: five-day announcement, the students run the whole elimination, conclude there can't be an exam, don't study, and the exam happens on Friday. My gut says that counts as a surprise exam — they're flabbergasted. But that seems to contradict my own verdict that Friday was impossible in the two-day case, and it can't be that whether the last day works depends on whether the earlier days got used. Last one: an unreliable teacher. If the students know from experience that her announcements come true only about half the time, no day can be eliminated and every day is a surprise. Verdict: the announcement is easiest to satisfy when its least believed, which is bizarre but I think it's right.

So, is there a single principle that gets all or most of these — some account of what the students know and when — that makes the first elimination step valid in the one- and two-day cases but blocks the iteration? What does it say about the case I waver on? And what does it predict for a variant I haven't mentioned: the teacher announces the exam for a two-week window and it lands on day thirteen? Please tell me which of my verdicts your principle cant save, and be honest if the answer is that the two "sound" ones — the one-day announcement defeating itself, and Friday being impossible in the two-day case — are the ones that have to go. Write it as continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; I want to read it like a letter.
