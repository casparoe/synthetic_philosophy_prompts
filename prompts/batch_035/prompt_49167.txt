In three weeks I give the opening address at our town's banner festival — the one the guidebooks call medieval, the one our own tourism brochure dates to 1307. Last year the county archivist established what a few of us had long suspected: the whole ritual was concocted in the 1880s by the vicar and the railway company, complete with a forged charter and an entirely fictitious ancient feud with our continental twin town, whom we ceremonially reconcile every summer. The town knows now — the archivist gave a talk, the local paper ran a series — and knowing has done nothing to dampen enthusiasm; if anything the ritual feels more ours than before, something we made ourselves. My difficulty is what can be said from the podium. The audience will include the archivist, who flinches at every anachronism; the twin-town delegation, here to be reconciled over a feud that never happened; a coach party of tourists who came for the medieval; and several hundred residents who want the speech to feel the way the festival feels, which is old. I am not willing to say anything I know to be false, and I am equally unwilling to turn the occasion into a lecture on Victorian fabrication — that would be a small cruelty dressed up as honesty. Please write the five-minute address. It should honor whatever it is the ritual actually does for this town — and honestly, I would like to understand that better myself — without asserting that the past it claims ever happened.
