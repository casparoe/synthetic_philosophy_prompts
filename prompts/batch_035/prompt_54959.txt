I'm a PhD student in bioethics, and I've been asked to run a 90-minute session of our MA seminar on autonomy in the clinic — fifteen students, no lecture component budgeted, so the session has to work as structured activity rather than exposition. The topic is the authority of the earlier self: whether, and why, an advance directive written by a competent person should bind the later self who no longer endorses it, and what to say when the later self appears to be flourishing by lights the earlier self never shared. I want the participants to collide with the problem in role before it is named, so please design the activity so that the tension surfaces in what the rules force them to do, not in anything I announce. My sketch, to be corrected where it won't work: a first phase in which small groups write directives for a persona they have just built — both a sheet of specific instructions and a separate sheet of underlying values — without being told which sheet anyone downstream will see; a second phase in which other students take up dossier-constructed later selves, and I would like the dossiers deliberately varied so that some later selves are contentedly confused, some are lucid but have plainly and stably changed their minds, and at least one lives a life that better realizes the directive-writer's stated values than literal compliance with the directive would; and a third phase in which surrogate committees must decide, with the twist that half the committees receive only the instruction sheet and half only the values sheet, so that we can see whether participants treat the earlier self's authority as attaching to the specific choice or to the values behind it. I want a payoff scheme that creates genuine stakes without real money, and a deliberate role for the directive-writers themselves during the decision phase — my thought is that they should sit visible but silent behind their later selves, but you tell me whether that works or backfires. The debrief should map the moments of the activity onto the standard positions: the Dworkinian line that critical interests can trump experiential ones, the objection from those who hold that a presently flourishing patient's current interests should govern, and the competence-based story that the directive's authority runs out where the later self lacks only the capacity that made the earlier self's word count, versus the identity-based story that it runs out where the later self is no longer, in the relevant sense, the author. I'd also like you to flag what will predictably go wrong on a first run — I expect the dementia roles to drift toward caricature and the committees to start negotiating as students rather than as surrogates, so please tell me how to pre-empt each failure you foresee — and, finally, be frank about which feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce no matter how I stage it: what the participants in role can never actually have, and what I must therefore supply in the debrief by hand.
