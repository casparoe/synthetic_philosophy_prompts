In my political philosophy seminar we spent several weeks on luck egalitarianism — the view, associated with writers such as Dworkin and Cohen, that inequalities are unjust when they trace to brute luck rather than to choices for which the person is responsible, with Dworkin's distinction between brute luck and option luck supposed to mark exactly where the line falls. A friend has produced an argument that he thinks shows the whole position is unstable, and although I'm confident it fails somewhere, I cannot locate the failure. He states it as follows:

(1) Luck egalitarianism holds that an inequality between two people is unjust unless it reflects a difference in choice for which the worse-off person is responsible. (2) Every choice a person makes is made as it is because of the person she is when she makes it: her values, her temperament, her capacity to weigh reasons and resist impulse. (3) What a person is at any moment is not up to her: it is the product either of factors entirely outside her control — genes, upbringing, the culture she happened into — or of earlier choices by which she shaped herself; but those earlier choices were themselves made because of what she then was, which was likewise not up to her. So, by regress, everything about a person that issues in her choices traces, step by step, to factors beyond her control. (4) If a choice traces, in this way, to factors beyond the chooser's control, the chooser is not responsible for it. (5) Therefore no one is ever responsible for any choice. (6) Therefore no inequality ever reflects a difference in responsible choice; so, by (1), luck egalitarianism must condemn every inequality — including the inequality between someone who spent a decade in training and discipline and someone who spent it surfing, which the theory's own proponents insist it should tolerate. (7) So the distinction between brute luck and option luck does no work, the theory collapses into flat outcome egalitarianism while its defenders keep claiming it doesn't, and a theory that refutes its own proponents' paradigm cases is in trouble.

I have two worries and no resolution. If I reject (4), it looks as though I'm saying a choice can be responsible even though it traces through and through to luck — and then I can't see what remains of the "luck" in luck egalitarianism, since the same will be true of the negligent and the imprudent, everyone the theory wants to hold responsible, so my reply seems to prove too much for my side as well. If I instead reject (6) or (7), I concede that the theory has no room for the surfer, which its defenders will not do, and if I reject the regress in (3) I seem to need some notion of pure origination that no one has supplied. Where does the argument go wrong? Tell me which step to reject, explain why that step looked fine in the first place, and say what rejecting it costs — including what the least costly rejection leaves the theory owing. I'd also like to know whether the argument is valid as it stands, so that the whole question is which premise to pay for, or whether some step only appears valid under a reading its premises do not support.
