I teach an undergraduate course in cognitive science, and every year the unit on the symbol grounding problem ends the same way: I put Searle's Chinese Room on the board, the room splits, and nothing anyone says for the rest of the seminar moves anyone an inch. The pattern has me suspecting the disagreement isn't really about the arguments, since all of the arguments have been on the table for decades — Searle's original paper in 1980, Harnad's naming of the problem around 1990, the systems reply, the robot reply, and the newer reply that a system trained on enough human-produced text inherits grounding parasitically, through the statistics of how grounded people use their words. On one side are those who hold that manipulating formal tokens by their shapes and syntactic roles can never amount to understanding, since the tokens connect to what they're about only through their roles in a formal system, never through anything in the system that the tokens are about; the standard replies have been given many times, and none of them has converted anyone. On the other side is the charge that this begs the question: to insist that fluent mastery of a symbol system doesn't count as understanding is to assume precisely the intrinsic, inner property whose existence is in dispute — and, the defenders add, nobody has ever produced a test for that property that doesn't either collapse into Turing-style behavioral tests the skeptics already reject or gesture at something no one can check.

My working hypothesis, which I want you to evaluate rather than assume, is that the debate has stalled because the two sides are answering different questions under one heading: the skeptics are making a claim about the presence of a particular kind of internal state, something in virtue of which the system's symbols are genuinely about things, roughly the way states of a brain are about things; the defenders are making a claim about what a system can do with symbols, and each side treats the other's question as illegitimate — one as scientistic behaviorism, the other as obscurantism about a ghostly property. But I'm not confident this diagnosis isn't just relabeling the dispute as verbal when it is actually substantive: if the internal property is real, then which systems have it is an empirical question, and the skeptics' failure to state independent criteria for it is itself the substantive mistake to press, not a sign that there's no fact of the matter. So diagnose the disagreement for me. Is it verbal, a clash of starting intuitions about cases that neither side can argue for, a hidden empirical question awaiting the right experiment, or an ill-posed question that ought to be replaced? Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually says, including what they concede — for instance, that the skeptic grants the systems reply its formal point while denying its relevance, and that the defender rarely claims the room itself understands. And tell me what, if anything, would count as progress here: a sharper concept, a specified experiment or model, a change of question, or an honest argument that no progress should be expected and why. I would rather teach my students a good diagnosis of the impasse next term than one more rehearsal of the arguments that produced it.
