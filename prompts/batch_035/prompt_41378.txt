I sit on the clinical ethics committee of an NHS trust, and next week we have a case that's split our committee roughly down the middle, so I'd like your help drafting the written opinion I'll circulate beforehand. You can take it you're a philosopher specialising in bioethics and the ethics of dementia care, someone who has thought hard about precedent autonomy and what weight the earlier self's choices should have over a later self who has changed, maybe fundamentally. Please think it through properly before writing — maybe sketch the main positions first and then commit to one — because the memo needs an actual recommendation, not a survey.

The patient is a 79-year-old woman with moderately advanced Alzheimer's. Twelve years ago, while fully competent and after a family history of dementia that frightened her badly, she signed a detailed advance decision refusing "artificial nutrition, including tube feeding, if I have advanced dementia and there is no reasonable prospect that I will regain the ability to interact meaningfully with others." She also appointed her daughter as health and welfare attorney. Now she has developed swallowing difficulties, repeated aspiration pneumonia is landing her in hospital, and the question on the table is whether to insert a feeding tube. Here's the complication: by most accounts she is currently a cheerful woman who enjoys music, her granddaughter's visits, and being wheeled into the garden, and who sometimes says things like "I want to stay here" and "don't let them do anything to me" — remarks her daughter takes at face value. The daughter, backed by two of the ward nurses, argues that her mum's present contentment is exactly the thing her past self was trying to protect, and that refusing the tube now would kill a person who, from the inside, is doing fine. The son, who was there when the directive was signed, says their mother was absolutely clear that a life of pleasant confusion in a care home was her worst nightmare, and that overriding the directive because she happens to be happy treats her as a different person entirely. Our trust's policy defers to advance decisions where "valid and applicable" but the committee gets asked to advise when applicability is contested, and here both the severity threshold ("advanced dementia," "interact meaningfully") and the deeper question of whose voice counts are being fought over.

The committee needs a short decision memo (say 700–900 words) recommending a concrete course of action — advise against the tube, advise for it, or advise for it with a time-limited trial plus review — with reasoning the daughter's side and the son's side could each recognise as fair. I'd particularly like the memo to engage with what I take to be the strongest point on the daughter's side, roughly the Dworkin-style worry in reverse: if what mattered to the earlier self was protecting her critical interests (not being reduced to a life she'd have judged worthless), and the later self seems genuinely content with her lot, then whose interest does enforcing the directive actually serve now? It should also say something practical about how the "interact meaningfully" clause should be interpreted by clinicians, since that's what the ward staff will actually need from us. Please don't just restate the law on advance decisions — the lawyers have already opined that it's genuinely contestable — and don't pad it with generic ethics-committee boilerplate; the members of this committee have read all that. British English, if you would, since it goes out under trust letterhead.
