I read a lot of popular history, and I've started noticing a pattern in how historical explanations work that I can't quite put my finger on. When a historian explains why something happened — why the western Roman Empire fell, why the Soviet Union collapsed, why Germany unified when it did — the explanation is assembled out of facts that only became salient after the fact. So far, so familiar: I know about hindsight bias, the thing where people feel after the event that they would have seen it coming. But what bothers me is a step beyond that. I've started applying a little test of my own: take the explanation, flip the outcome, and ask whether the same raw material could have been rearranged into an explanation of the opposite result. Rome fell because of overextension, fiscal strain, and pressure on the frontier — yet had it limped on for another three centuries, those very facts would be redeployed the other way: overextension would show the empire had learned to live within its limits, fiscal strain would show institutions resilient enough to absorb shocks, and so on. The explanation seems to be available on both branches. It's as if the question "why did X happen?" has a guaranteed answer no matter which X actually occurred, which makes me suspect the answer carries less information than it feels like it does.

The nearest concepts I know of don't quite capture this. Hindsight bias is about the psychology of forecasters, not about a structural property of the explanations they later accept. Survivorship bias is about which data survived, not about the shape of the story told with it. And the narrative fallacy, as I've read about it, is about our appetite for tidy stories in general, not about the specific symmetry I'm describing — that the mirror-image explanation of the non-occurred outcome can be generated just as fluently, from the same facts, with no sense of strain. What I want to know is whether there is an established concept, or a literature in philosophy of history or historical methodology, for this outcome-independence — the property of explanations that are guaranteed to be available whichever way things go — and whether anyone has formalized my mirror test as a way of auditing explanations.

If there's no established name for exactly this, I'd like you to coin one and work out its edges. Which cases should count and which shouldn't; how it differs from hindsight bias, survivorship bias, narrative fallacy, and the old idea that history is written by the winners; whether some kinds of explanations are supposed to be immune (say, explanations citing physical constraints or resource exhaustion, where the mirror explanation really can't be built); and, most importantly, whether failing the mirror test is actually damning. Could there be a legitimate reason why a good explanation of what happened would look unavailable as an explanation of what didn't — or does the symmetry really show that the explanation is doing no real explanatory work?
