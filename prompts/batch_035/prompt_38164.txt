I teach an upper-level seminar on theories of distributive equality, and I want a case study that isolates the disagreement between resourcist and capability-based views over responsibility-sensitive inequalities — without cheating by invoking disability or brute bad luck, which is how this wedge is usually forced open.

Construct a thought experiment meeting the following specifications. Two citizens emerge from a fair resource-equalising scheme — assume something Dworkin-style, with whatever hypothetical insurance market you like — holding bundles that pass the envy test outright: neither would trade. Both are equally satisfied with their lives, and neither has expensive tastes; what it costs one to flourish costs the other exactly the same. Yet one of them ends up with a strictly smaller capability set in Sen's sense: a narrower range of doings and beings genuinely open to her, not merely fewer options she happens to enjoy. The contraction must trace entirely to a voluntary, ongoing, wholeheartedly endorsed commitment — a vocation, a faith, a settled form of life — such that she would refuse any compensation that came bundled with pressure to reconsider it. The scenario should be realistic and ordinary, drawn from the textures of normal social life rather than science fiction or exotic institutional machinery.

Then demonstrate that the design works. Set out what welfare egalitarianism, resource egalitarianism, and the capability approach respectively imply about whether anything remains to be equalised, and why. Identify precisely which feature of the case is doing the work — here, that responsibility attaches to a contraction of freedom rather than of welfare — and show what happens to each theory's verdict if you toggle that single feature while holding everything else fixed: for instance, if the form of life was entered under conditions of adaptive preference formation rather than clear-eyed endorsement. Finish with the strongest reply from each side: how would a resourcist argue that the case poses no genuine problem for her view, and how would a capability theorist respond without either dismissing the person's own endorsement of her life or abandoning the thought that equality of capability matters?

A few epistemic notes. Please keep clearly separate three things: what is settled by stipulation within the case itself, what actually follows from the theories as their defenders argue for them, and what remains genuinely contested among people who know this literature — whether capability views can accommodate responsibility for chosen capability losses is an open question, not something with a received answer, so present neither side's reply as knockdown. And please write the answer in British English throughout.
