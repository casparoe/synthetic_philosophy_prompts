I've been around the rationalist blogosphere since the old LessWrong days, and lately the phrase "stochastic parrots" keeps getting deployed in arguments about whether large language models understand anything. Most recently, when I suggested that an LLM could be genuinely misled by a false premise in a prompt — that it forms something like a working model of the situation it's asked about, which the false premise corrupts — I was told this is impossible because a stochastic parrot doesn't have a situation, only continuations, and a parrot can't be misled, it can only squawk. But I've noticed the same phrase doing much lighter work elsewhere in these threads: sometimes it just means the models are trained on next-token prediction, sometimes that they interpolate over their training data rather than doing anything new, sometimes almost a Kantian claim that there's semantic machinery but no one home. Please lay out the distinct precise theses that "stochastic parrots" could be expressing, ordered from the near-trivially true to the most radical, and for each reading say whether it is plausible, whether it is actually false, and what considerations bear on it — then tell me specifically which readings, if any, would support using the phrase to dismiss my claim about an LLM being misled by a false premise, since the weakest readings seem clearly compatible with it. If the reading people most likely intend when they use the slogan in arguments like mine differs from the most defensible reading, I'd like that flagged too, since it bears on whether the phrase is earning its keep or trading on ambiguity.
