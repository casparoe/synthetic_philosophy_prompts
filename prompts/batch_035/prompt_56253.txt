I'm taking a graduate philosophy of mind course and writing a term paper on eliminative materialism, and I've realized I keep going in circles because I have no fixed standard for what success would even look like. The literature I've read mostly trades in broad gestures — that folk psychology is a stagnant research program, that neuroscience will eventually speak a language too alien for "belief" and "desire" to survive translation — but nobody stops to specify the bar. Before I take a side in my paper, I want the specification first.

So: writing as a professional philosopher of mind who thinks carefully before answering, please give me the desiderata that any adequate eliminativism about the propositional attitudes must meet — what a genuinely successful elimination would have to deliver. For each requirement, give the motivating case or consideration, mark whether it is non-negotiable or a prejudice of one camp, and flag any subset of the requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — that tension is the part I most want to see. To push you past the usual talking points, here are some candidate desiderata I've been chewing on that seem under-discussed, which you should evaluate (accept, reject, or refine) rather than merely list: (1) a no-miracles-analogue — folk psychology has predicted everyday behavior reasonably well for millennia, and just as a radically false scientific theory demands an explanation of its predictive success, the eliminativist owes an account of how a theory with no belief-like states in it did so well; (2) a retention condition — phlogiston was eliminated, but oxygen chemistry preserved what the phlogiston theorists were mostly right about; an eliminativist must say what, if anything, belief-talk was tracking, and whether the successor theory preserves it; (3) a kitchen-resolution condition — the successor vocabulary must be usable by ordinary people for real-time social prediction, since a theory that only a scanning lab can apply doesn't eliminate the attitudes so much as abandon everyday practice; (4) an autobiography condition — if beliefs never existed, then I was radically wrong about my own mind for thirty years, including the very years I spent thinking the eliminativist's paper was persuasive, and the theory must say whether my past first-person reports were error, or something else; and (5) a scope condition — whether desires, intentions, and belief must fall together, or whether a mixed verdict (eliminate some, keep others) is coherent. Feel free to add desiderata I've missed, but argue for their inclusion rather than padding the list.

Then, briefly, since my paper has to engage the contenders: score Paul Churchland's original neurocomputational eliminativism, Stephen Stich's syntactic-revisionary version, Dennett's instrumentalist deflation, and a quietist refusal to play, against your list — and tell me which single requirement does the most to knock out the most candidates. Start with some brainstorming before you commit to the final list, and keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scorecard.
