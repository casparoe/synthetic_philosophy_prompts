Compare the self-sampling assumption (SSA) and the self-indication assumption (SIA) as competing approaches to anthropic reasoning. Explain, in your own setup, what each assumption says about how a reasoner should condition on the bare fact of their own existence, and how the two assumptions yield different posterior credences in cases where the size of the population containing you is itself uncertain.

Then apply both assumptions to the following case, which I call the Manifest. A colony ship leaves Earth carrying colonists in cryosleep. Either it carries ten colonists, each with a distinct mind, or it carries one trillion colonists who are all exact psychological duplicates of a single original colonist — the crew-filled manifest was lost, and each outcome had prior probability one half. Everyone wakes with amnesia, and you find yourself awake with no indexical information beyond your own existence. Before considering your evidence, you would be unsure whether you are one of the ten originals or one of the trillion duplicates. Now consider, in order:

1. Under plain SIA, how should you update toward the trillion-colonist branch, given that your existence is "easier" to find in a larger population of observers? What payoff does this reasoning carry over — for example, if you were asked to bet on the size of the crew?

2. Now suppose that the number of observers is counted by distinct experiential perspectives rather than by psychological tokens — that is, the trillion duplicates jointly constitute only one distinct "slot" an observer could occupy. Does SIA still boost the trillion-colonist branch, or does the update collapse back toward the ten-originals branch? Argue for your reading of the reference class.

3. Turn to SSA. Does the duplication twist make SSA's reference-class problem easier or harder? In particular, does the case give SSA a principled way to answer why one should reason as if randomly sampled from one's reference class, or does it merely relocate the arbitrariness?

4. Finally, relate your analysis to the presumptuous philosopher: the reasoner who, on merely reflecting that they exist, becomes confident in theories on which vastly more observers exist. Does the Manifest case strengthen the presumptuous philosopher's position, defuse it, or show that the phenomenon is entangled with the metaphysics of personal identity — with how we individuate minds — rather than with probability alone?

Aim for a self-contained treatment; assume the reader knows elementary Bayesian reasoning and the general idea of the presumptuous philosopher, but define SSA and SIA explicitly before using them. Where the two assumptions give the same verdict, say so, and focus your effort on the points where they genuinely diverge. Conclude with a considered judgment: does the Manifest case favor one assumption over the other, and if so, on what grounds beyond mere elegance?
