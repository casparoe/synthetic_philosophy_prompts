Please rank the following five approaches to the symbol grounding problem into tiers (A, B, C, or D) according to how well each one handles what I'll call the "first symbol" objection: any account that explains a symbol's meaning in terms of other symbols or inner representations presupposes that something already has meaning, so an adequate response must say something about how content gets started at all. The five approaches are: (1) Harnad's original hybrid bottom-up proposal, on which a system's iconic and categorical representations must be grounded in sensorimotor capacities before symbolic combinations can mean anything; (2) causal and informational theories of content in the Fodor–Dretske tradition, on which a symbol acquires meaning by standing in the right lawful causal or informational relation to things in the world; (3) Quinean stimulus-response accounts, on which a term's meaning is fixed by the sensory stimulation that prompts assent to it; (4) embodied and enactive robotics in the Brooks tradition, which tries to dissolve the problem by replacing explicit symbol manipulation with continuous sensorimotor coupling; and (5) conceptual-role semantics, on which a symbol's meaning is constituted entirely by its inferential role within the whole system. Give a one-sentence justification for each tier assignment that engages the "first symbol" objection specifically rather than generic strengths and weaknesses. Then, in a closing paragraph of a few sentences, state which single approach you would defend if forced to choose, given that a text-only system that has never perceived anything now appears to use words quite meaningfully. Please respond in American English.
