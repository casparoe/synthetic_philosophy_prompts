I spent the better part of four decades as a clinical researcher in endocrinology and, later, as an epidemiologist designing disease definitions for registries, so the word "disease" paid my salary without my ever once having to say what it meant. Now that I have retired and am working my way into the philosophy of medicine, I find the naturalism-versus-normativism debate (Boorse's biostatistical theory, Wakefield's harmful dysfunction analysis, the normativist line running through Nordenfelt) genuinely fascinating but also premature in an odd way: the parties seem to argue about theories before anyone has written down what a theory of health and disease is a theory *for*. In my old line of work, a case definition was always built to a purpose — surveillance, treatment eligibility, insurance compensation, trial enrollment — and different purposes tolerated different amounts of fuzziness at the edges. So before I try to adjudicate the theories, I want the specification they are supposed to satisfy.

Please write out the desiderata: what any adequate account of health and disease must deliver, with the motivating case or consideration behind each requirement, and with an explicit verdict on which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely the prejudices of one camp (I suspect "value-freedom" itself may be one of those prejudices, and I would like your candid view on that). As seeds, here are cases that a good list has to handle, though you should add your own: sickle-cell trait, which in malarial regions is a *selected* state and yet the homozygous disease is unquestionably pathological — what does that do to any requirement that dysfunction be read off evolutionary selection? Presbyopia and the other accompaniments of aging, which are species-typical and yet treated as defects worth correcting; hypothyroidism that is fully compensated by replacement therapy, where the dysfunction is present and the harm absent; infertility, where the biological part fails and the harm depends entirely on what the person wants; the dispute over whether deafness is a disease or a minority culture; and the boundary-policing work I remember from my registry days, where classifying a condition as a disease opened or closed access to treatment. Also tell me which distinctions a theory must respect rather than collapse — disease versus illness versus sickness, dysfunction of a part versus reduced adaptedness of the whole organism, having a condition versus being harmed by it — and which results from biology, medicine, or psychiatry it cannot contradict. If some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, say so and show me why: that would itself be a result worth having, the way I take a no-go theorem to be.

Keep the weight on the requirements and their motivating cases rather than on scoring particular theories; still, I would appreciate a brief closing passage on how the biostatistical theory, the harmful dysfunction analysis, and a normativist account in Nordenfelt's style fare against your list, and especially which single requirement knocks out the most candidates — my suspicion, from the naturalist side of the fence I used to occupy, is that it will be the requirement that the account license the *practical* work disease concepts do in clinical settings, but I am prepared to be told I have that backwards. A table for the desiderata is fine, and please write in American English.
