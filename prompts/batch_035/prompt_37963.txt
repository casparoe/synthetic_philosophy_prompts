I host a small podcast on decision theory and the philosophy of rationality, and next week's guest is a decision theorist who has spent much of her career defending superrationality in the tradition of Hofstadter — the idea that perfectly rational agents facing a symmetric problem will converge on the same strategy, and so should each reason as if selecting the strategy for all of them, cooperating in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma where standard game theory says to defect. I would like you to draft eight to ten interview questions for her, and I want them to press on the actual content of the position rather than invite her to restate it. Please make sure the set includes at least one question on each of the following: (1) the analogy Hofstadter draws between two rational agents solving the same strategic problem and two mathematicians solving the same equation — in arithmetic, the sameness of the answer doesn't change the answer, so why should it change the answer in a game; (2) the charge that superrationality smuggles in magical thinking, since my cooperating seems in no way to cause the other player to cooperate, and her anticipated reply that the shared logic, not causation, is doing the work — with a follow-up probing whether that reply still holds when the two agents' deliberations are physically independent events; (3) whether superrationality is really just evidential decision theory wearing a costume, given the resemblance to one-boxing in Newcomb's problem and to cooperating with an evidentially linked twin, and whether she must therefore take a side in that dispute; (4) the probabilistic extension of the view, using the Platonia dilemma as the test case — Hofstadter's scenario in which twenty would-be heirs each may send the billionaire a telegram, with a billion dollars going to the sender only if he is the sole one to send one, where the superrational answer is to telegram with probability one in twenty — and specifically whether allowing mixed strategies undermines the founding assumption that all agents will end up doing the same thing, since here they almost surely will not; and (5) the empirical aftermath of the Luring Lottery that Scientific American actually ran in 1983, in which readers could submit postcards standing for as many entries as they liked, the million-dollar prize was to be divided by total entries, and the outcome was a deluge of astronomically large submissions (googolplexes among them) that rendered the prize a rounding error of a fraction of a cent — I'd like a question on whether that widespread failure to think superrationally has any bearing at all on the normative claim, or whether it merely measures the readership.

For each question, please append a brief follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, so that I can keep the conversation moving rather than letting her settle into rehearsed material. It would also be welcome if one or two of the remaining questions explored the view's fragility — for example, the fact that a superrational player defects against a merely game-theoretically rational opponent, and that no one has settled how to extend the concept to asymmetric games — since I suspect these are the places where her position is least secure. Please write all of the questions and follow-ups in British English.
