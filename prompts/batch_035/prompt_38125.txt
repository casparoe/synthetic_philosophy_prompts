I take part in a reading group on the origins of the First World War — mostly historians, but a couple of political theorists as well, which is probably why our last session on the Fischer controversy turned into a four-round argument between two members about what it would even mean to hold Germany responsible for the war. The exchange was genuinely close, and I'd like a careful adjudication of it. It went as follows. (1) One member, let's call her Anna, defended a Fischer-style position: the German leadership deliberately risked a general war in 1914 to break out of perceived encirclement and secure great-power status, the blank cheque to Vienna gave Austria-Hungary a free hand, and the September Programme showed expansive war aims drafted almost as soon as the war began — the sleepwalkers framing, she said, is a way of diffusing a responsibility that the documents concentrate in Berlin. (2) Ben replied that Fischer's own evidence concerns what German leaders wanted once the war had started, not proof that they engineered its outbreak, and that the crisis involved decisions by every major power — Russia's early support for Serbia and subsequent general mobilization, France's encouragement of Russia, Britain's ambiguity about whether it would fight — so shared responsibility is the honest conclusion, not a dodge. (3) Anna answered that shared responsibility tends to slide into no responsibility: someone had to convert a Balkan quarrel into a continental war, and Berlin made that decision twice over, first by unconditionally backing Vienna and then by choosing the Schlieffen escalation rather than restraining its ally once Russia mobilized; the other powers were reacting to those choices. (4) Ben then pivoted to the conceptual level: even granting primary causal weight to German decisions, Germany didn't decide anything — a few dozen men in Berlin and Vienna did, without consulting the population, so national blame is a category mistake, and Article 231 of the Versailles Treaty was essentially a liability device dressed up as a moral verdict, one whose political consequences, he noted, included handing German nationalists a grievance to exploit. At this point the group's theorist, who had stayed quiet, said both of them were assuming that responsibility must attach either to Berlin's elite or to nobody, and that neither had noticed the possibility that responsibility can be distributed across institutions and structures rather than located in a single agent — but she declined to elaborate further, and we ran out of time.

I would like you to adjudicate this exchange for me. Please reply as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. The first four items should each give a verdict on one of the four moves in the argument between Anna and Ben, saying who has the better of that specific exchange and why — I want you to judge the arguments as actually made, not to reconstruct the historiography from scratch. The fifth item should briefly note what, if anything, the quiet theorist's remark changes about the adjudication, and then state who, if anyone, comes out more right overall.
