Two friends of mine — one a fairly committed presentist (thinks historians can and should evaluate historical figures by the best moral standards available today, and that refusing to do it is cowardice dressed up as method), the other a dyed-in-the-wool contextualist (thinks importing our standards into the past is a category error that produces moralizing rather than history) — have been going at it for years, mostly past each other. Write the joint statement they'd sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration: a numbered list of claims both can accept (about what sources can establish, about the difference between explaining an action and evaluating it — I suspect they agree on more than either admits); then a table of the exact residual disagreements, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording; then the cruxes. My guess is the big ones are (a) whether "understanding an agent on their own terms" and "evaluating an agent" are two separable acts, or whether every evaluation smuggles in a standpoint that can't be neutralized, and (b) whether there are any standards that we and, say, a 12th-century crusader could coherently share, or whether the very idea of shared standards across eras is itself a presentist fantasy. For each crux, give a concrete historical case both sides agree would bear on it (the kind of case I mean: a reformer who was unusually good by the standards of his own time and still pretty awful by ours — a abolitionist who was also a colonialist, that sort of figure), and record each side's prediction in advance about what examining the case would show. And if any part of their dispute turns out to be verbal — e.g. about what "judging" even means — say which part and why. Keep it honest: no both-sides mush where every disagreement dissolves into a misunderstanding, and no crux that only one of them actually cares about.
