I work on the deployment-safety team at a mid-sized AI lab, and a question from my day job has started keeping me up at night, so I'd like the help of someone who is both a philosopher of science and fluent in ML interpretability. Our team runs what's called mechanistic anomaly detection: before a model is deployed, we run it on a large corpus of benign traffic and build statistical baselines over its internal activations — which features fire, how attention heads and intermediate representations are distributed, what "normal" looks like on the inside. At deployment, we flag any input whose internal signature doesn't fit those baselines. The whole practice rests on the thought that dangerous behavior is produced by mechanisms, and that a harmful mechanism — whatever it is — will register as unusual against a baseline of benign mechanisms, even if the input text looks innocuous.

Last month we caught a jailbreak that slipped past every text-level filter, and it triggered an argument I can't stop thinking about. The monitor flagged it because the internal pattern was far outside the benign distribution: a strange coalition of features. But when our interpretability people looked, they said, roughly, that this was just the model's ordinary storytelling machinery being recruited into a new context — the same features that fire during harmless roleplay, now doing the jailbreaker's work. The monitor called it novel; the interpreters called it the same old mechanism in a new job. And on a different day the reverse happens: something that looks like a mere stylistic novelty gets flagged, and it turns out a genuinely new computational circuit has formed that we'd very much want to know about. So the practice seems to presuppose an answer to a question nobody on the team can articulate: what does it take for two episodes inside a network to count as involving the same mechanism? Is sameness a matter of shared parts (the same features and heads doing the work), of shared function (the same input-output role performed, however it's implemented), of causal organization (the same arrangement of components), or of provenance (the components were shaped by the same training signal)? These criteria come apart constantly, and each one draws the alarm boundary in a different place.

Here's what I'd like you to do, and please think carefully and structure your answer rather than free-associating. First, treat our monitoring policy as an object of philosophical analysis, the way one might analyze a courtroom's rules of evidence: reconstruct the theory of mechanism individuation it implicitly presupposes, and say which rival theories of sameness it quietly rules out. Second, note the apparent inconsistency I described: interpretability research celebrates the fact that a model reuses one circuit across superficially different tasks as a sign of abstraction and generalization, while our monitoring practice treats exactly that kind of cross-context recruitment as anomaly-worthy — do these two subfields of the same discipline presuppose compatible accounts of what a mechanism is, or does "mechanism" function as an equivocal term of art? Third, consider whether philosophy of science has anything useful here — debates about how mechanisms are individuated in biology and neuroscience, and functional analysis of the kind that asks whether the same heart mechanism is present in an octopus and a human — and say which account, if imported, would actually license our deployment policy as it stands. Finally, be honest about where the practice is philosophically underdetermined: which theories of sameness would all permit the monitoring as we've built it, and what concrete design change (in the baselines, the flagging rule, or the escalation criteria) would distinguish between them?
