I'm a graduate student in animal-welfare policy, and there is a pattern running through the law that I can't articulate the philosophical underpinnings of, so I'd like your help reconstructing them. Consider how protections are tiered. Research-ethics law in Europe and elsewhere requires approval and humane-treatment rules for work on vertebrates, and the EU's 2010 directive on animals used for scientific purposes extended that to cephalopods, while leaving other invertebrates — insects, worms, spiders — outside the scope entirely, needing no approval at all no matter how the experiment turns out. On the sentience side, the UK's 2022 sentience legislation covers all vertebrates plus decapod crustaceans and cephalopod molluscs, on the basis of a commissioned review of the evidence for sentience in those groups. Meanwhile, general animal-welfare law across most jurisdictions governs the keeping, transport, and slaughter of vertebrate farm animals, and only a scattering of provisions — boiling lobsters alive, selling live crabs to untrained handlers — touches the newly included invertebrates, while nothing at all touches the insects farmed in growing numbers for feed and food. So the law protects the mouse, the octopus, and eventually the crab; it ignores the bee and the shrimp... except that decapods include shrimp, so the line runs through the arthropods as well as between phyla.

Here is what I want to know, and please go at it as an analysis of what the practice presupposes rather than as an argument for expanding or shrinking it. What theory of moral status does this tiered structure only make sense under? The obvious reading is that the tiers track something like brain size or neural complexity, taken as a proxy for sentience, taken in turn as the mark of moral considerability — but I want to know which links in that chain the law strictly presupposes and which it merely fits comfortably with. Does the tiering actually commit anything to brain size being morally relevant in itself, or is it just an epistemic screening device for sentience, and would a sentience-first theory, a relational or contractualist view, or even a view resting duties on the human-animal relationship generate the same or different tiers? Notice the awkward data points: the bee is excluded despite cognitive achievements that rival some vertebrates, the cephalopod is included despite a radically distributed nervous system that doesn't look like the vertebrate template, and the precautionary instincts that justified including crustaceans seem not to have been applied downward to insects despite their astronomical numbers. Does that show the practice presupposes an inconsistent test, or just that it is underdetermined — that several moral-status theories would license roughly these tiers for different reasons?

Finally, do the reconstruction and the diagnosis together: say what is strictly presupposed, what is optional, and where the practice is philosophically up for grabs; then tell me what would actually have to change, concretely, in welfare law and research-ethics practice if the implicit theory were replaced by one on which insects' inner lives count — is it a redrawing of the line, a replacement of line-drawing with graded or context-sensitive protections, or something that makes the whole idea of a phylogenetic cut-off look confused? Keep the whole answer under 700 words.
