My brother-in-law and I have this running argument about whether the other side (in our case, the other party's voters and its cable-news layer, but pick your own country's version) argues in good faith or not. Last Thanksgiving he shut me down with Hanlon's razor -- you know, "Never attribute to malice that which is adequately explained by stupidity." I was claiming that a certain pundit obviously knows his central talking point is misleading and repeats it anyway; my brother-in-law said that's malice-attribution, and since incompetence explains the same behavior, I'm forbidden from concluding bad faith. Something about that move felt too easy, and it's not just him -- I keep seeing the razor deployed in online polarization arguments as if it's a theorem that ends the good-faith question. There's even a dark mirror version people bring up (any sufficiently advanced incompetence is indistinguishable from malice) which just muddies things further.

Here's what bugs me. In the contexts where I actually encounter the razor, "stupidity" seems like a weirdly narrow alternative to malice. The explanations on the table for why the other side says what it says include motivated reasoning, tribal signaling without belief either way, audience capture and grift, filter effects where nobody chose anything, and just genuinely different starting values. Some of those aren't stupidity and aren't malice either, and some of them (like motivated reasoning) seem kind of adjacent to bad faith even though there's no scheming villain. Also, when people say "the other side" does that mean a voter, a pundit, a politician, or some collective entity that doesn't have a mind at all? I suspect the razor is doing different work depending on who it's applied to, and maybe it was designed for a different problem entirely -- like a programmer flaming customer support over a buggy product, where the malice hypothesis really is paranoid.

So what I want is the full menu: lay out the distinct precise claims the razor could be making, from the trivially true to the substantive to the actually false. For each reading, tell me whether it's plausible, and -- this is the part I care about most -- whether it actually supports my brother-in-law's move of forbidding the bad-faith conclusion about a political pundit, or whether that use smuggles in assumptions the slogan by itself doesn't earn (e.g. about base rates of malice among commentators, or about whether "explained by stupidity" needs the agent to actually be stupid rather than strategically indifferent). If some readings support the move and some don't, say which ones and why. Lay it out as a list I can literally bring to the next argument, with a short verdict for each reading (defensible / contentious / false / inapplicable here). Then end with one final line in this exact format, so I can quote it at him:

Verdict: [the single reading of Hanlon's razor, named briefly, that best captures how it's being used in polarization arguments, and whether that use is legitimate]
