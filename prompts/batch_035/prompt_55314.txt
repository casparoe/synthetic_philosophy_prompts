I have no academic background whatsoever — I'm a retired insurance adjuster who reads a lot of popular math and philosophy books — but for the past six years I've been quietly obsessed with the argument, going back to a 1961 paper by the philosopher J. R. Lucas and later developed in Roger Penrose's two big books from 1989 and 1994, that Gödel's incompleteness theorems show the human mind cannot be a machine. I've brought this up so often at our monthly dinner club that two of the other members have now been arguing it with each other for almost two years, without me even prompting: Marcus, a retired high school math teacher who is fully convinced by the Lucas–Penrose side, and Dana, a software engineer who thinks the whole argument is a parlor trick that trades on confusing truth with provability. They've never once gotten anywhere, and I recently read that when researchers on opposite sides of a dispute agree to a structured "adversarial collaboration," they can sometimes produce a joint document that both sides sign, laying out exactly what they agree on, exactly where they differ, and the specific sub-questions that would actually settle things for at least one of them. I asked Marcus and Dana if they'd be willing to try this, and to my surprise they both said yes — on the condition that I produce a fair first draft for them to mark up.

So I need that draft, and I want it written by someone who genuinely knows this material. Please take on the role of a professional philosopher of logic who has followed the Lucas–Penrose debate and its responses closely, and begin with a short brainstorming pass before writing anything final: map out what the two sides can each accept without giving up their position, so the final document is neither vacuous nor slanted toward either side. To make sure you know the terrain as Marcus and Dana fight over it: Marcus runs the classic argument — any machine is, in effect, a formal system for arithmetic; Gödel showed that any consistent such system has a true sentence it cannot prove; but the human mathematician, looking at that machine from outside, can see that its Gödel sentence is true; so the human mind outruns every machine. Dana counters that this all hinges on whether the human is assumed consistent, which Marcus cannot prove any more than a machine can prove its own consistency; that Dana never has to say which machine Marcus is, only that there is one, since Marcus's claim is about machines in general; and that Marcus is also sliding between "the machine can't prove it" and "the machine can't ever output or act on it." Marcus, meanwhile, says Dana's consistency objection proves too much (it would mean no one is ever rationally certain of anything), and he insists his seeing-the-truth claim is not itself a proof or computation at all. I've also read that even Gödel himself drew a more careful two-pronged conclusion — either minds surpass machines, or there are mathematical problems whose solutions are absolutely beyond human methods — which both of my friends seem to invoke when convenient, and that Penrose's version leans on claims about consciousness and non-computable physics that may or may not be doing independent work.

The document itself should have: (1) a numbered section of claims both Marcus and Dana could sign — including about what Gödel's theorems actually establish, what the simple argument needs as extra premises, and what does not follow from them; (2) a disagreements table in which each remaining disagreement is stated in wording that the side holding that view would accept as fair; (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them, and I suspect the consistency assumption and the "which machine?" question are two, though you may find better ones; and (4) for each crux, a concrete argument, case, or line of reasoning that both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance, as they would state it. If any part of their dispute strikes you as merely verbal — for instance, over what "outstripping" a machine means, or over whether "the mind is a machine" means being simulable versus being a formal system whose output is exactly what the mind sees as true — please identify it and explain why. Write the whole thing in polished American English, keep it self-contained without any bibliography or reading list, and make it a document two people who have argued for two years could genuinely put their names on.
