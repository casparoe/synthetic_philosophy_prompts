Me and a bloke from my football team have been arguing for ages (usually over post-match beers) about what you're allowed to flat-out assert. He says: knowledge only — you can assert that p only if you actually know that p; a true belief you got lucky on isn't enough. I say: truth is the rule — if what you say is true and you believed it on decent grounds, the assertion itself was proper, and whether it "counts as knowledge" is beside the point. Cases that keep coming up: him telling his mate "your ticket lost" the day of the lottery draw before any numbers were checked (true, and near-certain, but he agrees he didn't *know* it); the guy at the station who says "the 6:15 is the fast one" off a timetable that was misprinted but, by pure luck, right about that one fact; and whether saying "p, though for all I know not-p" is a broken assertion or just a weird one. Neither of us has read philosophy since school, and I work in software, so we want this written down properly instead of another circular pub argument.

Draft the joint statement we should end up with: a numbered section of claims we can both sign about what makes an assertion proper or improper; then a table of our residual disagreements, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording; then the cruxes — the sub-questions where settling one would move at least one of us; and for each crux a concrete case or argument we'd both agree bears on it, with each of our predictions written down in advance. And if you reckon part of our dispute is just verbal (say, over the word "know"), say which part and why. Please write the whole thing in British English.
