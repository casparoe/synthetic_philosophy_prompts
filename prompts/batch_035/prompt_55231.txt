Quick bit of background: I'm a second-year law student taking a sentencing module, and something has been bugging me for weeks. In England and Wales (and a lot of other jurisdictions do something similar), if you plead guilty at the earliest opportunity you can get up to a third knocked off your sentence, whereas if you plead not guilty, go through the whole trial, and then get convicted, you serve the full sentence for the exact same offence. The official justification is that the discount rewards you for sparing victims the ordeal of giving evidence and for saving court time and money, and officials insist it is absolutely not a penalty for daring to have a trial. But seen from the outside, it looks like the person who exercised their right to a trial got punished harder for the identical crime, and I can't shake the feeling that this sits badly with all the proportionality talk we get elsewhere in sentencing, where judges are told to calibrate punishment to the seriousness of the offence and the offender's culpability.

My criminal law tutor frames everything as a contest between retributivism (punishment should track desert for what you did) and consequentialism (punishment is justified by future benefits like deterrence and efficiency), and I can see straight off how a consequentialist would explain the plea discount: trials are expensive and traumatic, so make pleading guilty attractive. What I can't work out is whether the practice *presupposes* consequentialism, or whether a retributivist could still sign off on it. Is the early plea itself part of what determines desert — does accepting responsibility before being proven guilty reduce how much blame you deserve — or is that just a convenient story? And is the 'it's a discount, not a trial tax' framing philosophically respectable, or is it spin? Basically: does this practice quietly rule out retributivism, is it consistent but underdetermined (several theories could each make sense of it), or is it genuinely in tension with itself given that the same system elsewhere insists punishment must be proportionate to the completed offence?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory of punishment behind the guilty-plea discount, distinguishing which parts of the practice strictly need consequentialist commitments, which parts a retributivist could accommodate (say, by treating the early plea like the cooperation or remorse credits some systems give), and which parts are simply underdetermined. Then tell me what a strict retributivist running the system tomorrow would have to change: scrap the discount, shrink it, or keep it under some redesription. Answer in British English please, and end with a machine-readable final line of the form \answer{...} containing a short phrase naming the theory the practice most presupposes — e.g. 'consequentialist', 'retributivist', 'both', or 'underdetermined'.
