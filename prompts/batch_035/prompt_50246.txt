quick background on me: i'm a software engineer, no formal philosophy training, but a while back i fell into a rabbit hole about whether "knowing" something can depend on what's at stake, and i've gotten stuck on what i think is a distinction that actual philosophers care about a lot and i can't fully reconstruct from the pop-level glosses.

as far as i can tell there are two different views that both say the standards for "knows" move around with practical circumstances, but for different reasons:

- one where the word "knows" itself is context-sensitive, like "tall" at a basketball tryout vs at a kindergarten. the sentence "she knows the deploy is safe" can be true as said by one person and false as said by another in a different conversation, and that's fine because the two utterances literally express different things
- one where the meaning stays fixed but whether a person satisfies it depends on how much is riding on it for *them*, the subject. same evidence, but if it matters a lot to her, she doesn't know, even though i, to whom it matters nothing, would know on the same evidence

here's the concrete setup that got me confused, from work. we have a rule that a deploy to prod needs signoff from someone who "knows" the change is safe. i reviewed a patch, tests pass, the diff is small and i'm pretty sure i understand every line, either on its own or in combination. for me the stakes are low: worst case my pager buzzes and i fix it. but the person who has to sign off is the one on call over the weekend while i'm away, and for her the stakes are high. so on the first view, i think the story is: when i say at standup "i know it's safe", that's true in that conversation, and when she later says "i don't know that it's safe, let's hold", that's *also* true in hers — even though it's the same change and basically the same evidence, because the two "knows" mean slightly different things. and on the second view, she just doesn't know and i do, nobody's utterance means anything different, and if she simply didn't care about her weekend she'd have known.

where i keep getting stuck: in every case i can cook up, these two views seem to hand out the same verdicts, and yet people write as if they're night and day. so what i want, explained for someone with no philosophy background, is what the actual *disagreement* is about — what would make one of them right and the other wrong, not just how they sound different. use my deploy thing as the running example if it helps, and please specifically address three things:

1. do the two views really always agree on verdicts, or is there a case where they come apart? i suspect the action is somewhere around who's doing the asking, or what happens when the stakes change mid-conversation (e.g. someone walks in and says actually there's a customer demo on monday, now the standup that was casual is tense) — does anything different happen on the two views there?
2. the disagreement thing. when she says "i don't know it's safe" right after i said "i know it is", is she contradicting me, correcting me, or neither? it feels like the two views should give different answers here and that this maybe matters more than the verdicts do, but i can't work out exactly how.
3. the payoff question. from the outside, the second view looks like it just makes "know" weird (your knowledge would flicker in and out as your practical situation changes, even with fixed evidence) for no benefit. its defenders obviously think there's a benefit. what is it meant to be? for what it's worth, the thing i'd want preserved is something like: most of what a team "knows" about its systems counts as knowledge even when the stakes are enormous, and you can accumulate knowledge under low stakes and keep it when the stakes rise — if the second view is in tension with that, say so honestly rather than smoothing it over.
