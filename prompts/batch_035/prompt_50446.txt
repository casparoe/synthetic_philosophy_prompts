I am a philosophy professor, and I need a seminar handout that takes apart a piece of economic folklore.

At last week's departmental workshop on intergenerational justice, I presented a paper arguing that the present generation has weighty obligations regarding people who do not yet exist. A senior colleague from economics closed off the discussion with the Keynes line — "In the long run we are all dead" — delivered, as these lines always are, as if it were a proof rather than a remark: since the long run belongs to no one, no argument that appeals to it can have any purchase on us. It carried the room, and I confess it briefly carried me as well. What I want now is a disambiguation, because I am confident the sentence is doing several different jobs at once and that only some of them are legitimate.

Please lay out, as a menu rather than an exposition, the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing, ordered from the trivially true to the substantive and, if it comes to that, the false. I would expect candidates along the following lines, though you should add, refine, or discard as the analysis requires:

1. A decision-theoretic claim about horizons: deliberation is indexed to the deliberator's own lifetime, so costs and benefits falling after the relevant agent's death cannot rationally enter that agent's calculations.
2. An epistemic or methodological claim: long-run equilibrium reasoning is a useless guide to policy, because it abstracts from the transition, and the transition is where all the harm occurs.
3. A psychological claim: no one now living has interests that survive their own death — no concern for bequests, no posthumous ambitions, no attachment to projects that outlast them.
4. A normative claim about discounting: the welfare of future persons counts for less in present deliberation, or our obligations run no further than our own interests do.
5. A purely rhetorical use: an aphorism whose function is to end a discussion without ever stating a premise capable of doing the work.

For each reading, tell me whether it is plausible, whether it is true, and — the reason the handout exists — whether it would actually support the dismissive use my colleague put the line to. Then I need two framing sections.

First, the original context. The line comes from chapter 3 of Keynes's A Tract on Monetary Reform (1923), where the passage reads: "But this long run is a misleading guide to current affairs. In the long run we are all dead. Economists set themselves too easy, too useless a task, if in tempestuous seasons they can only tell us, that when the storm is long past, the ocean is flat again." Say which of the readings on my list that passage actually supports, and whether the supported reading transfers from monetary policy — where the target was economists offering long-run reassurance about price levels — to the ethics of obligations to future people.

Second, the tension within Keynes himself. The colleague's aphorism borrows prestige from a man who, in his 1930 essay "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren," spent it at length on the long run: on what people a century hence would do with abundance, on how the love of money as an end in itself would come to strike them as a mildly disgusting preoccupation, and on the question of how to live once the economic problem was solved — which is to say, on his conception of the good life for people not yet born. Address whether the colleague is entitled to the slogan at all, given the use its own author put long-run thinking to.

Conclude by telling me, first, which reading my colleague most probably meant, and second, which reading he would have to defend — out loud, as a stated premise — for the dismissal to amount to more than a flourish. If that reading is false, say so plainly; the handout should equip my students to say, the next time the line is deployed in a seminar, exactly which premise is missing.
