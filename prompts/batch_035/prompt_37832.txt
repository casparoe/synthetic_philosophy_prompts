I sit on the interpretive committee overseeing the redesign of our museum's gallery on the ancient Americas, and our last meeting deadlocked over a single word on a single panel — a deadlock I've since re-fought, at length and unsuccessfully, with a friend. I'd like your help drafting the written opinion I'll circulate before the next meeting.

The case: the draft panel covering the late eighth through tenth centuries states that Maya civilization "collapsed" between roughly 800 and 900 CE, and attributes it to drought, escalating warfare, and the abandonment of the southern lowland cities. A companion interactive exhibit titled "Why Civilizations Fall" would place the Classic Maya alongside Easter Island as case studies in societies that destroyed their own foundations. One faction of the committee wants the panel to say the Maya "transformed" rather than collapsed, and the interactive retitled or dropped. Their grounds: millions of Maya-speaking people are alive today; the northern cities, Chichen Itza among them, flourished right through the period in question; and a community liaison has told us plainly that the current draft presents her people as if they were extinct. The other faction holds that "collapse" names something real: within a few generations the southern lowlands lost, by some estimates, most of their population, the dynastic monuments cease, and the cities stood empty. On their view, softening the language purchases a courtesy toward the living at the price of denying them an accurate history — and if "collapse" can't be said here, the word has no use anywhere.

The argument with my friend is why I'm unsure the deadlock is really about wording. She is a graduate student in Mesoamerican archaeology, and over a very long dinner she argued that "collapse" compresses two centuries of events across a region far larger than most visitors imagine into one verb, and imports an old frame in which societies are organisms with natural life spans that either fall or adapt — a frame, she says, that does the interpretive work while looking like a description. My reply, which I still half-believe, was that a region losing most of its population and its political institutions in two centuries is as close to the paradigm case of the word as history offers, and that if a word can't be used there, it should be retired rather than rescued. But I noticed that this concession, which I offered as a reductio, is actually compatible with her position, and I couldn't say why it shouldn't be. We parted without either of us moved.

Please draft the written opinion I'll circulate. It should recommend a concrete course of action the committee could vote on: the specific wording the panel should carry, whether "Why Civilizations Fall" should proceed, be retitled, or be dropped, and how the exhibit should handle the descendant-community concern. It should reason at a level the losing faction could respect rather than merely accommodate — treat the strongest point on the other side as the thing to answer, not an obstacle to note. Two further requests. First, if you think the question as we've framed it is confused — that "collapse versus transformation" is a false binary no wording choice can resolve, and the real decision lies elsewhere — say that plainly and recommend accordingly; I'd rather circulate a memo half the committee dislikes than one that splits the difference politely. Second, please distinguish what the panel can responsibly claim from what the underlying scholarship actually supports, and tell me if the evidence I've described is too thin to carry a confident recommendation — flag anything in it you'd want corrected before it goes in writing.
