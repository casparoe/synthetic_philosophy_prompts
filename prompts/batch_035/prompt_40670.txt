I'm a second-year medical student who has somehow got roped into a philosophy of medicine elective, and something about the way clinical language uses the word "function" has been bothering me. When a nephrologist says renal function is declining, or a lab prints "thyroid function tests," or a lecturer says the heart's function is to pump blood, the judgement seems entirely about the here and now: an organ functions well if it is currently making its usual contribution to the organism's capacities, and badly if it isn't. The reference ranges on the report are set statistically from current populations, with no appeal to any history at all. And surgeons will happily replace a diseased aortic valve with a mechanical one, and nobody hesitates to say the mechanical valve performs the heart's function — despite the fact that a manufactured disc of carbon and titanium has no evolutionary history in the patient whatsoever.

But in other corners of medicine, function talk seems to be historical through and through. Our psychiatry teaching leans on the DSM's requirement that a disorder involve some failure of a mental mechanism to do its job, and some philosophers of medicine argue this only makes sense if the "job" is read evolutionarily — a panic response is pathological because the threat-detection system isn't operating as natural selection shaped it, not merely because it is unpleasant or statistically unusual. Meanwhile, advocates of "Darwinian medicine" tell us that fever, cough, diarrhoea and pregnancy sickness should often be treated as functional — as defences doing exactly what they were selected to do — rather than as malfunctions to be suppressed, which is a claim that seems to turn entirely on ancestral selection pressures. So the same profession seems to be operating with two different pictures of what a part's function is: one where function is about present contribution to the organism, and one where it is about what the part is "for" in some deeper historical sense, presumably set by natural selection.

My question, then: what theory of function does clinical practice actually presuppose, if any? Can one of these two pictures cover the whole of medicine, or do different bits of the practice quietly rely on different accounts — and if so, is that a genuine incoherence or something the profession can live with? I'm especially interested in the cases where the two pictures come apart, like the chronically stressed office worker whose cortisol system seems to be firing exactly as it was selected to fire in a world of acute physical emergencies, just in an environment it was never built for: is that system functioning or malfunctioning, and does the answer depend on which account of function we adopt? Please also tell me what would actually change in practice — say, in how we classify disorders or assess disability — if the medical profession were forced to adopt one of the two accounts wholesale. And since the essay goes to a British supervisor, please write in British English.
