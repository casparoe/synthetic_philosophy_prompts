I am a graduate student in bioethics, and three weeks before the end of term our introductory course's instructor went on sudden medical leave, leaving me responsible for writing the final exam — my first time setting assessment for a large class rather than grading for someone else. At the same time, the topic I have been assigned to examine has become unexpectedly personal: I was recently prescribed modafinil for hypersomnia associated with my overnight hospital shift schedule, and my roommate, a law student, takes the same medication off-label before exams. The pills are chemically identical, our prescribers share a building, and yet under the familiar therapy/enhancement distinction his use is an enhancement and mine a treatment. He argues over dinner that this shows the distinction is nothing but paperwork, and I have not been satisfied with my own replies — which is exactly why I want to see whether our students can do better than I have.

Write me one essay question on the therapy/enhancement distinction that cannot be answered by reciting the standard line that treatment restores normal species functioning while enhancement goes beyond it. The question should give students two seeded cases and force them to work through where the standard criteria give conflicting verdicts: first, my own modafinil situation (same drug, same dose, one user impaired relative to their own baseline and one not); and second, a hypothetical gene-editing therapy that corrects a mutation causing early-onset retinal degeneration but, as an unavoidable side effect of the delivery mechanism, raises visual acuity above the population average in everyone treated. Students should have to decide whether the distinction is best drawn relative to the individual patient's prior baseline, the statistical norm for the species, or the presence of a dysfunction in some stronger sense, and then confront the harder question: even if the descriptive distinction cannot be cleanly drawn, might a version of it still be needed for rationing and coverage decisions — i.e., can the distinction fail as biology yet survive as a policy convention? The exam is closed-book, in-person, and students will have ninety minutes, so no question that invites name-dropping without argument.

Alongside the question itself, please provide a model answer of roughly 600 words that a well-prepared student could plausibly produce from memory, and a marking scheme totaling 20 marks built around five must-hit points, with the marks for each and a one-line note on the way students typically fumble that point. Most importantly for my purposes, the scheme should explicitly separate an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves only the A answer makes — I want to be able to tell the difference between a student who has memorized the labels and one who can wield them under pressure. Please write everything, including the exam question and model answer, in American English.
