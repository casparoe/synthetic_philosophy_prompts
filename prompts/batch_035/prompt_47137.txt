At Thanksgiving my brother-in-law Dave and my cousin Marcia got into it over a headline from some science news site saying intelligence is about 50% heritable, and I definately couldn't follow who was winning. I have no academic background (my last biology class was high school) but I've been turning it over on my own since, and I still can't sort out alot of it, so I want you to referee.

It went like this. (1) Dave said 50% heritable means half of each person's intelligence is baked into the genes and the other half comes from upbringing — that's why his son takes after him, and why he figures his son's ceiling got set at conception, give or take. (2) Marcia said that's not what the number measures at all: heritability is about differences within a population — the share of the variation between people in some group that lines up with genetic differences between them. It doesn't say how much of any one person is genetic, and the same genes in a different environment would give a different number. (3) Dave said then it's a mushy statistic, because she'd have to admit that if every school and every meal were made identical, heritability would go up, since whatever differences were left would be mostly genetic — so the number climbs exactly when we do right by kids, and then it gets quoted as genetics is destiny, meaning pretty much the opposite of what it shows. And anyway, he said, dog breeders never needed a population caveat: they picked the parents they wanted and got the traits they wanted, generation after generation, which is just genetics working, plain and simple. (4) Marcia said the height story is exactly what you'd expect: once malnutrition stops explaining why some kids are shorter, the remaining differences are mostly genetic, so heritability rising is the signature of environments getting more equal, not of genes getting stronger. And the breeder point, she said, is heritability doing its one real job — predicting how a population responds to selection — which only applies where someone is actually breeding, and says nothing about any individual. (5) Dave said fine, but then look at what got printed: a bare 50% in a headline that every reader will take the way he took it, and if the scientists know that, leaving the caveat out is on them. And he said she still never answered what the number is even for — if it can't tell him anything about his own son, and it changes whenever we improve things, why does anyone compute it in the first place. By then the pie was out and the whole thing dissolved.

Please go through their exchange move by move and tell me who's right at each step — I'm especially curious whether Marcia's first correction was really as airtight as she made it sound, and whether Dave's point about the headline is fair or just grousing. Give it to me as exactly five numbered items, one for each of the five moves above, each item a sentence or two saying who's right about what in that move. No intro or conclusion, just the five items.
