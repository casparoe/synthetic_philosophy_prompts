I'm a PhD student working on social epistemology, and my department's reading group has spent an entire term on testimony and gone precisely no where. The shape of the debate as we've been running it: the reductionist side (Humean, Fricker-flavoured) says testimony deserves no default epistemic credit — you're entitled to trust an utterance only insofar as you can subsume it under an inductive track record for the speaker or speaker-type, plus a perception-based read on their competence and sincerity. The anti-reductionist side (Coady, Burge's acceptance principle, Reid-ish veracity principles) says no such reduction can avoid circularity, because the very data you'd build your track record from is itself testimonial — so testimony has to be a basic source, with defeasible default entitlement. Everyone in the room agrees about every actual case: we trust the weather app, we trust the historian, we don't trust the chain email. And yet nobody has moved an inch all term.

My working hypothesis, which I'd like you to take seriously but also push back on: the disagreement isn't about entitlement at all, but about what counts as a "source" of justification. The reductionists are answering a question about the structure of an individual subject's justificatory evidence, while the anti-reductionists are answering a question about the reliability and norm-governedness of a social practice, and both sides have just been calling these two different things "testimony". If that's right, the debate is partly verbal — but not merely verbal, because it still matters which question you answer when you go off and do applied work. So: what kind of disagreement is this really? Verbal, a clash of background epistemologies (internalism vs externalism wearing a different coat), a hidden fight about how much epistemology should lean on division of cognitive labour in science? And is there anything that would count as progress — a discriminating test case the two views actually disagree about (do anonymous forum posts get prima facie warrant on anti-reductionism? what about a language model?), a conceptual distinction, or should we concede the question is ill-posed and get on with our lives?

One thing I want you to engage with specifically: when I floated the "two different questions" line in the group, the resident reductionist said it couldn't be right, because anti-reductionism makes a real prediction — it says default entitlement is present even when the subject has no evidence about the speaker whatsoever, e.g. a small child hearing her first testimony — and that's substantive, not verbal. So either my diagnosis is wrong, or the disagreement is verbal at the level of the slogans but substantive further down. Which is it? I want an actual diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of who said what, and an honest answer about whether anything could end this debate or whether we're stuck with it.
