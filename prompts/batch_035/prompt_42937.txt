I'd like your credence on a claim about acausal trade, decomposed into its moving parts rather than delivered as a bare number.

Here is the claim, sharpened so it has a determinate truth value: There exist circumstances in which two agents with no causal interaction — say, civilizations in causally disconnected regions of an expanding universe, or an agent today and a hypothetical agent whose existence it can never causally influence — can each rationally carry out a costly act that benefits the other, purely on the expectation that the other is reasoning the same way, such that both do better than if either had reasoned causally and declined. Call this the coherence of acausal trade. And a second, decision-relevant claim layered on top: that the coherence of acausal trade can, in principle, impose real obligations on an agent like a present-day person or organization — for instance, to weight the interests of causally unreachable hypothetical counterparties in its decisions.

My overall credence in the conjunction is roughly 0.55. A colleague of mine puts it below 0.15 — he thinks the whole apparatus rests on decision theories that are interesting formalisms but not correct norms, and that even granting them, the mutual predictability and Schelling-point coordination the trades require are fictions at the level of precision needed for anything to hang on them.

Please give me your own number, but more importantly decompose it. At minimum I'd want separate probabilities for: (1) that decision theories on which decision-theoretic identity matters — acting as if choosing the output of your algorithm rather than your particular token action — are the correct norms rather than merely consistent ones, and which of the family you'd condition on; (2) that mutually sufficient predictability between causally disconnected agents is achievable in principle, given that each side must simulate or model the other well enough to verify the exchange rather than defect or extort; (3) that the resulting trades are net-positive rather than dominated by darker relatives — the blackmail cases, where each side threatens to inflict costs conditional on the other's non-compliance, and the question of whether trade and extortion can even be kept apart in the formalism; and (4) that all of this ever cashes out in guidance for bounded agents like us, rather than only for idealized agents with precise models of each other. Add whatever further terms you think the conjunction needs — for example, whether the whole framework survives if personal identity or algorithm-identity turns out to be vaguer than the theories assume — with a number and a one-line justification for each, then combine them.

Two further requests. First, tell me which single term of your decomposition the final number is most sensitive to, and what argument or consideration would move that term — and hence your overall credence — the most; I'm asking you to flag where your own reasoning is likeliest to fail. Second, address where you think my disagreement with my colleague actually lives: is it in the decision-theoretic term, in the predictability term, or somewhere in the bridge from idealized agents to us? If you think the sharpened claim above conflates separable questions, say so and give your credence on the repaired version.
