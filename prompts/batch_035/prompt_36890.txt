I write historical fiction, mostly set in the nineteenth century, and I've hit a problem I can't solve by craft alone. My current novel has a protagonist who, by the standards of the 1840s, is a genuinely good man — he's kind to his tenants, horrified by cruelty, gives to charity — and he also owns people. My first draft let the narration stay inside his moral world, and my editor said it read like an apologia; my second draft kept nudging the reader to condemn him, and my agent said it read like the narrator was winking at us over his shoulder. Both feel like cheats, and I suspect the real issue isn't style but that I have no worked-out account of how anyone — novelist or historian — should evaluate people by moral standards at all, when the standards keep moving.

So before I do another draft, I want the spec, not a verdict on my guy. Write me the desiderata: what an adequate account of how to morally evaluate historical figures (and characters standing in for them) has to deliver. Which cases must it get right — the slaveholder decent by his era's lights, the abolitionist who was ahead of the country but horribly sexist about everything else, the "product of his time" defense, the presentist who ends up condemning basically everyone before 1950 and so cheapening condemnation itself. Which distinctions can it not collapse — explaining versus excusing, what the agent could reasonably have known versus what we know in hindsight, judging the person versus judging the world that made them. For each requirement give the case or consideration behind it, and say which ones are non-negotiable versus which are just the tastes of one camp (people who want history to vindicate the present versus people who want it left alone).

The part I most want: tell me if some pair of the desiderata can't be jointly satisfied, and where the list forces a choice. My instinct is that "don't judge people by standards that didn't exist for them" and "don't treat morality as whatever happened to be locally accepted" pull against each other, but I can't tell whether that's a real tension or just me being muddled. And flag which single requirement does the most work — the one that, once you accept it, decides most of the disputes. Then, briefly and only if it helps, say how three familiar positions do against the list: pure presentism (judge everyone by today's standards), relativism (judge them by their own era's), and whatever you'd call the middle view people gesture at when they say "he was good for his time" without explaining what that could mean. I don't need citations or a reading list — I need the list of requirements, with your reasoning, in terms I can actually apply at the level of a sentence while writing.
