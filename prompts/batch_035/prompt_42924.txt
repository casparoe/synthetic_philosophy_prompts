I'm a retired radio astronomer (32 years, mostly pulsar timing, if that matters) and I've gotten deep into philosophy since I stopped working. My daughter got me a used copy of Hume's Enquiry and I've been chewing on the problem of induction ever since, and I think I've stumbled onto something. I've written up the sketch of an essay below and I want comments on whether the central argument actually works, because I honestly can't tell anymore.

The essay sketch, working title "The Free Lunch We Never Had to Refuse":

The starting point is something I only found out about last year, from a machine learning textbook my grandson left at my house: the no-free-lunch theorems. As I understand them, if you average an optimizer's performance over ALL possible objective functions, every optimizer does exactly as well as random search, and this includes whatever fancy search procedure you like. No structure you can find in a dataset means anything unless the world was already biased toward having that kind of structure. When I read this I nearly fell out of my chair, because it seems to just BE Hume's problem of induction with equations. Hume in prose, Wolpert in math. Uniformity of nature can't be justified without circularity; equally, no learner beats random guessing averaged over all possible worlds. Same theorem, different century.

The usual escape from both is identical: restrict the hypothesis space, or assume the world's objective functions are not drawn uniformly but from a "nice" distribution, or whatever. And that's where everyone (as far as I can tell) stops, and where I think something interesting starts.

My claim: the restriction is not an extra POSTULATE we make. It's forced on us, and I don't mean by convenience. Here's the point I want comments on especially. I want to call it the load-bearing coincidence. Every instrument we use to detect regularities in nature is itself a piece of nature, built by the same physics it's probing. Our eyes evolved under starlight; my old telescope's receiver chain is governed by the same quantum electrodynamics as the pulsar signal it was built to catch. In signal processing we'd say the detector is matched, over a long shared history, to the source. So induction doesn't work because nature is simple. It works because we are made of the same stuff, and any inductive bias our instruments carry was itself deposited by the world's own regularities. Science is not an outside observer cracking nature's code, it's one part of a noisy universe listening to the rest of it, and the "prior" over worlds isn't borrowed, it's inherited. On this picture the no-free-lunch theorem stops being an embarrassment and becomes a bookkeeping identity: the prior is like a loan Hume says we can never repay, except it was never a loan, we're downstream of the collateral.

The objection I can already hear, and can't answer: the load-bearing coincidence is itself an empirical claim, established by induction, so I've just drawn a bigger circle and stamped my name on it. My attempted reply is that the coincidence is not being used to JUSTIFY induction in general (which would be circular) but to EXPLAIN why our particular methods track our particular world, and explaining why a method works is different from showing it must work. Whether that distinction between justification and explanation can carry this much weight I genuinely don't know. It feels like it might just be the ordinary is/ought slope in a lab coat.

So, comments please. Specifically: (1) Is my identification of the no-free-lunch theorems with Hume tight, or am I glossing over real differences — the theorems are about optimization over finite domains, Hume is about projecting patterns forward in time, and maybe those come apart somewhere that matters? (2) Does the justification-vs-explanation move do any real work, or does the bigger circle swallow it? (3) Do you recognize the shape of the load-bearing-coincidence argument from anywhere (it smells faintly like some things I've half-remembered from anthropic-principle arguments, and something about our cognitive faculties being selected to fit the world), and if so, where is it most likely to break? I don't want a reading list — I want to know if the argument stands or falls on its own terms.

Please lead with your bottom-line verdict on whether the central argument works, right there in your first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restating my argument back at me — I've read it, I wrote it.
