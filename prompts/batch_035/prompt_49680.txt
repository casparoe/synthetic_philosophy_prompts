Write an essay on whether it is ever rational to honor sunk costs. Take "honoring a sunk cost" as follows: an agent honors a sunk cost when the mere fact that resources have already been irrecoverably spent changes what she does, holding fixed her beliefs about the future payoffs of her options and her risk attitudes. This stipulation matters — the textbook principle that rational choice ignores sunk costs says that spent resources should be causally and evidentially inert — so part of your task is to classify carefully whether the cases below involve genuine honoring of sunk costs, or merely cases where past expenditure carries information about the future.

Weigh at least the following considerations. First, the standard decision-theoretic argument: rational choice is forward-looking; at the moment of decision, the past expenditure exists in every available history, so it cannot discriminate among the options; and empirically, sunk-cost sensitivity correlates with escalation of commitment and post-hoc rationalization in organizations. Second, the commitment defense: in settings where an agent's resolve is being watched — a union leader continuing a strike, a government pressing on with a war — visibly honoring sunk costs can make threats and promises credible where a known cost-ignorer's would not be. Press the question of whether this shows sunk costs are rational to *honor*, or only that they can be rational to *create* in advance (burning one's bridges before the opponent is watching), which is a different claim. Third, a case where the sunkness itself is claimed to do work: in a proof-of-work blockchain, the ledger's integrity is commonly said to rest on the sheer cumulative quantity of already-sunk energy — prospective attackers are deterred not merely by what rewriting the chain would cost going forward, but by the mountain of past expenditure they would have to have matched. Determine whether this is a genuine counterexample to the textbook principle or only forward-looking incentives dressed in sunk clothing. Fourth, a policy consideration: for a computationally bounded agent who cannot re-derive, at every decision point, the value of every course of action from scratch, a standing policy of finishing what one starts may outperform a policy of perpetual re-deciding — even though each individual application of that policy will look, taken alone, like honoring a sunk cost.

Do not settle for a survey of positions. Reach and defend a verdict — for example (but not necessarily) that honoring sunk costs is irrational at the level of individual acts but can be rational as a policy, or that the commitment and blockchain cases are genuine exceptions once properly described, or that the textbook principle survives all of the above under the right description. Then close with a section stating what would change your mind: the specific argument, class of cases, or kind of empirical finding that would move you to the opposite verdict, and the step in your own reasoning that you judge most likely to fail.
