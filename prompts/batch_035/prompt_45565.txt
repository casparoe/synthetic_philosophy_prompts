I've taught Williams's integrity objection to utilitarianism for years now — the Jim and the Indians case, the thought that utilitarianism treats my projects and commitments as just one more set of items on everyone's ledger, the one thought too many point about the man who can save his wife or a stranger and would somehow need an extra justifying thought to make saving her permissible — and I've basically signed on to it. But something uncomfortable has been happening in my seminar lately. Every utilitarian reply we consider — indirect utilitarianism, the distinction between a criterion of rightness and a decision procedure, Railton-style sophisticated consequentialism where alienation from your projects is itself consequentially bad — gets waved away by me or by my students with something like "but that's just consequentialism conceding the objection and relabeling its concession as a reply." And I honestly can't tell anymore whether that response is a refutation or a cheap trick.

So here's what I want: what would actually count against the integrity objection? Not a survey — a ranked list of defeaters, from fatal to merely awkward. A few specific worries I'd want you to take seriously rather than dodge:

First, does the objection depend on reading utilitarianism as a decision procedure? If a utilitarian happily grants that agents should deliberate from within their own projects, and holds that the theory only tells you which states of affairs are better, has the objection been survived, or merely dodged, or has the utilitarian given up something that made the view worth holding? I want your actual view on that, not both sides restated.

Second, does the one thought too many point even belong in the integrity family? It's always struck me as doing different work than the negative responsibility stuff in the Jim case — it seems to be about the phenomenology of justification, about which thoughts can play which role in deliberation, rather than about integrity as such. If I'm right about that, does the utilitarian have an easier time explaining the intuition away than the standard presentations assume?

Third, and this is the one I really want an honest answer to: has the objection been qualified into unfalsifiability? When the utilitarian says "fine, my theory isn't in the business of structuring an agent's practical life" and the Williamsian replies "then your theory has no distinctive role in an agent's life at all," I genuinely dont know whether the objection still has a live target or whether both parties are just gesturing at a picture of what moral theories are for that neither has argued for. Is there some formulation of the utilitarian reply, or some case, or some result from moral psychology, that a committed Williamsian would have to concede lands a real blow? And tell me which of my likely dismissals above I'd probably be making unfairly.
