My friend and I have been arguing about where corruption actually begins, and I've been keeping a list of her verdicts so I can pin down her underlying rule, but I can't do it. Her calls so far: a patient giving her surgeon a box of expensive chocolates after a successful operation, not corruption; a contractor handing a city procurement officer an envelope of cash after winning a bid, obviously corruption; wealthy donors paying to attend dinners where the mayor takes questions in private, not corruption — her view there is that everyone knows this is how politics works, and nobody is guaranteed anything; in the small town where her grandmother grew up, everyone gave gifts to a well-connected local man who used his relationships to get their paperwork unstuck from the bureaucracy, and she insists that was not corruption either, just how the place functioned; but she called it corruption when a ministry official hired his own brother for a job the brother couldn't do. What strikes me is that all of these seem consistent with several different principles at once: maybe she thinks corruption requires an explicit trade of money for a specific official act; maybe she thinks it requires an official abandoning impartial duty; maybe she thinks it's whatever the local norms condemn. Now she has a new case to judge, and I want your prediction before I show her your answer: the principal of a selective public school admits a donor's academically weak child shortly after the donor funds the school's new gym, with nothing going to the principal personally and the gym benefiting every student. Which rule is my friend really running, what will she say here, and what further case should I put to her that would actually distinguish the rules from each other? I'd also like your honest view on whether any of the three rules gets at what corruption really is, or whether all of them miss something her intuitions are tracking.
