So here's my situation: I'm a journalist on the science-and-ideas beat, and I'm writing a feature about philosophers borrowing the adversarial-collaboration format from the psychology replication wars. The organizers of the pilot project asked me to draft the template document for them, and the pairing they picked is as old as it gets: an epistemicist and a supervaluationist, on vagueness and the sorites paradox. So I need you to write the joint statement these two could actually put their names on, readable enough for a general magazine audience (no unexplained jargon, but don't dumb it down to nothing either).

Concretely, I want:

1. A numbered list of the claims both sign. It should cover at least: that words like heap, bald, and tall really do generate sorites reasoning; that the tolerance principle -- the thought that a single grain can never tip you from heap to non-heap -- is where the trouble starts; that the cheap exits (just refuse to apply induction along the chain, or call the whole thing a fallacy of equivocation) don't fully dissolve the puzzle; and that this isn't just a word game, it infects law, medicine, benefits thresholds, whereverver a decision turns on a vague predicate.

2. A disagreements table. For each row: the precise formulation of the disagreement, worded so that side itself would sign it; what each side thinks is the other's strongest point; and anything they concluded is a merely verbal dispute. My hunch is the question is there a sharp boundary? partly dissolves into sharp relative to what? -- one of them cares about whether a sentence containing a vague term has a truth value, the other about whether the world itself has a cutoff, and I'd like the document to say explicitly which parts of the fight are about that distinction and which aren't. Don't quote me on this though, put it in their mouths only if it survives their scrutiny.

3. The cruxes, and for each one the argument or thought experiment both agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. The organizers want at least one crux that's quasi-testable, so I suggested a forced-choice study: thousands of people shown a sorites series of sand piles and asked heap or not, then look at the shape of the aggregate response curve. My epistemicist source says a steep S-curve in the population is what his view expects and a totally smooth gradient would embarrass him; my supervaluationist source says she isn't even sure what her view predicts there, and honestly that asymmetry feels like a result the piece can run with. Do something with that, and add whatever second crux you think the document needs (higher-order vagueness? whether a plain statement like there are heaps has a determinate truth value? your call).

One more thing: no reading lists and no citations -- the fact-checkers cut that from the template, and I can't afford another fight with the lawyers. The document should stand on the arguments alone.
