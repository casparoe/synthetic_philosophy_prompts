I'm a retired materials scientist who's gotten properly into philosophy since I stopped doing real work, and last weekend I had two old colleagues round — Dave, ex-cosmologist, and Marion, who did statistics in pharma — and they got into a right row about anthropic reasoning that I couldn't referee and has been bugging me ever since. Can you go through their exchange move by move and tell me who's right about what?

Dave opened with fine-tuning: if there's just one universe, the constants landing in the narrow band that permits stars and chemistry is a collosal fluke; but if there are trillions of universes with different constants, it's no surprise that somebody finds themselves in a lucky one — and from our own point of view, that somebody had to be us. So our existence is strong evidence for a multiverse. (1)

Marion said that proves far too much. She'd been reading about the "presumptuous philosopher" problem: take two rival physics theories that fit all current data equally well, one implying 10^100 observers across all spacetime and the other 10^120. By Dave's rule we should be nearly certain of the second before running a single new experiment, which seems flatly wrong — evidence about the world shouldn't come from counting people who might have existed. (2)

Dave replied that it isn't armchair evidence, it's ordinary Bayesian surprise-minimisation: on a single-universe theory we should be flabbergasted to exist, and a theory that makes our data unsurprising gets a big likelihood boost. That's just how evidence works. (3)

Marion then brought up a cleaner case she'd cooked up herself: a lab flips a fair coin — heads, they create one conscious brain in a vat; tails, they create a billion copies. You wake up as one of the brains, knowing the setup. Dave's rule, she said, makes you about a billion-to-one confident the coin landed tails, and it's the same rule that lets the presumptuous philosopher magic up trillions of observers. His only escape is to count just observers in a "reference class" like yours, but nobody can say what fixes the reference class without hand-waving. (4)

Dave bit the bullet: in the vat case he'd definately bet on tails, and he didn't think that was crazy — if the procedure were run a million times, most resulting brains really would be on the tails side. But he said her alternative is worse: reason his way, then condition on finding yourself early in human history (as the doomsday argument does), and you conclude mankind probably ends soon — which he assumed she wouldn't accept either. (5)

Marion said that just shows the whole framework is broken rather than that either of them is right, and maybe self-locating evidence simply doesn't work like ordinary evidence at all. (6)

Who's right at each step? And is there any position that gets out of the whole mess without either conjuring observers from the armchair or predicting imminent doom?
