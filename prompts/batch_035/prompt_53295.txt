I run a little "film & philosophy" night at my neighborhood cinema — maybe 25 people, mostly non-philosophers who like arguing afterwards — and next month we're screening Grave of the Fireflies (Takahata, 1988), the one about the brother and little sister, Seita and Setsuko, who lose their mother in the firebombing of Kobe and slowly starve in the countryside at the end of WWII. I want to pair it with the paradox of tragedy and I need you to help me build the reading. Take on the role of a professional philosopher of art who also seriously knows film, and please brainstorm a bit before committing to one line — I want to see the candidate readings, not just the winner.

The paradox, as I'll frame it for the group: we avoid real suffering but we seek out artworks that make us feel grief, dread, horror — and we call that a good night out. The standard moves I know of: Aristotle's catharsis, Hume's idea that the artistic treatment somehow converts the unpleasant feeling into pleasure, Kendall Walton's make-believe framework where the emotions are "quasi-emotions" aimed at fictional worlds (so they were never really pain in the first place), and Susan Feagin's meta-response idea, where what we enjoy is noticing ourselves respond with compassion — we're pleased by our own sadness, at a second level. What I want is a reading of this specific film through those lenses, and I have a hunch the film is a hard case for all of them. For one thing it announces Seita's death in the opening minutes, so the whole thing is anticipated grief with no suspense to hide behind. For another, it's a famous "one watch" film — people call it a masterpiece and say they will never sit through it again, which seems to break the "we seek it out" premise the paradox rests on. And Takahata himself reportedly resisted calling it an anti-war film — he said it was more about a boy failing, out of pride and stubbornness, to keep his sister alive — which makes the moral structure murkier than a straightforward tragedy of war.

Some things I'd love you to dig into: does any of the four theories actually fit our relationship to this film, or does it just relocate the paradox (if "enjoyment" can't be the word for what this film gives us, what is the word)? What do you do with the beauty — the fireflies, the sea, Setsuko digging a little grave for the dead insects and asking why they die so soon — beauty placed right up against starvation and death, the thing the title points at? Is that Hume's "artistry converts pain to pleasure" doing real work, or is it something else? And where does the film resist all of these readings — be honest with me, because my group will notice if I'm forcing the film into an illustration. Finish by telling me which scenes I should rewatch with the group in mind and what you think this film adds to the paradox-of-tragedy debate that the philosophical papers on their own don't.
