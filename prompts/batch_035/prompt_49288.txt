I'm a software engineer at a fintech company, and my day job is full of net present value calculations: given some rate r, a cash flow in 2200 is worth less to us today than the same cash flow now, and this feels like second nature to me. Recently I've been reading about the economics of climate policy, and I keep running into an argument that looks like my work: just as a dollar received in 2200 is discounted relative to a dollar received today, so a life saved or a case of suffering averted in 2200 should count for less in our cost-benefit analyses; the same mathematics that justifies discounting payments justifies discounting welfare, and anyone who accepts NPV in finance should accept a substantial social discount rate in climate policy. My philosophy-major friends bristle at this — one said we're "discounting the people, not the payment" — and I gather there was a famous fight in the climate economics literature where one camp used a pure time preference rate near zero and the other insisted on something closer to market returns. But since the arithmetic on the two sides of the analogy looks identical to me, I can't pin down why the philosophers are upset, and I'd like a serious audit rather than a vibe.

Please take the role of an expert working at the intersection of welfare economics and moral philosophy, think carefully, and treat this the way you'd treat a proof someone claims transfers from one domain to another. Begin with a short brainstorm of everything that might differ between the two cases, then set out an explicit correspondence table: what in the financial case is supposed to map onto what in the welfare case — the payment to the life saved, sure, but also the rate of return on alternative investments mapping onto what, exactly, and whose preference for consuming now is supposed to map onto whose preference for present welfare? Then work through where the mapping breaks. In particular: is discounting a future cash flow a claim that the future dollar matters less, or a unit conversion that reflects opportunity costs — the fact that a dollar in hand can be invested and compound — and is there any comparable compounding asset that turns present resources into averted future suffering, so that "invest now and help more later" could be scored against "help now"? Where does pure time preference come in, and is a policymaker's impatience the same thing as an individual saver's time preference, given that the "patient" party here is a different person altogether — someone in 2200 who never got to vote on any of this? Distinguish carefully between the disanalogies that matter for the policy conclusion and the ones that don't.

Finally, render a verdict. Does the conclusion — that market-derived discount rates should be applied to future lives and suffering in cost-benefit analysis — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy quietly carrying the conclusion the whole time? If some defensible form of discounting survives, say precisely which part of the finance picture it inherits and which it must give up; and if none does, sketch what a more honest way of comparing welfare across generations would look like, even if it's messier than a single number in a spreadsheet. I'd rather hear that the problem is genuinely hard than get a tidy answer that only works because the analogy was never checked.
