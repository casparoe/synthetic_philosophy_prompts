I spent my working life in atmospheric science, much of it near the edges of policy modeling, and since retiring I have been reading systematically in philosophy, including the intergenerational literature. I would like your help thinking through a claim I now suspect is half right and half muddled.

The standard story I absorbed was this: from Sidgwick onward, philosophers have argued that the mere temporal position of a benefit makes no moral difference — that a welfare gain lying in 2150 rather than 2025 is, in itself, no reason to count it less — and that this case against "pure time preference" is what separates the high-discount from the low-discount camps in long-horizon economics. I have since come to think this mislocates the action. In the usual Ramsey-style decomposition of the discount rate, the disagreements people attribute to ethics about the far future often sit in a different parameter altogether: the one that converts consumption into utility, which simultaneously plays the role of risk aversion and of aversion to inequality across time. That parameter is at least partly empirical, and disputes over it look more like calibration quarrels than like philosophy.

Please describe the most important implications of the philosophical case against pure time preference for actual long-horizon policy analysis — the regulatory cost-benefit work and integrated assessment carried out for governments. I am less interested in the climate headline than in which implications genuinely change the numbers on the page and which are overrated. Specifically: does rejecting pure time preference alter conclusions once the wealth-growth and uncertainty terms are doing their work; is there any principled reason a public body, unlike a mortal individual, may discount the distant future at all; and should the same assumptions govern analyses whose costs and benefits change sign within decades rather than centuries?

If you think my framing is confused — that the philosophical argument has less leverage over real modeling practice than I am assuming, or that the distinction I draw between the two parameters does not survive scrutiny — say so plainly rather than working around it. I would rather be corrected than accommodated. And please confine yourself to reasoning; do not turn this into a reading list.
