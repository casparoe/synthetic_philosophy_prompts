I run trust & safety policy at a mid-sized discussion platform (about 200 moderators, all decisions appealable). The way new rules actually get made here is ugly: some incident happens, the mods dump forty or fifty example posts on me — some everyone agrees are violations, some everyone agrees are fine, and a bunch of maybes — and I have to turn that into a written rule that a moderator with two weeks of training can apply to posts nobody's seen yet, in about thirty seconds per post. This comes up roughly monthly and it's the part of the job I have zero training for.

I keep feeling like I'm doing the same thing a Bongard problem asks you to do: here's a small handful of things, figure out what separates them — except my boxes aren't hand-constructed to have one clean answer, and the first hypothesis that fits the examples is usally something shallow (they all contain a certain word) rather than the actual concept. Then a week later the rule overfits or underfits and mods come back to me with edge cases.

What I want is a procedure. Given a pile of labeled examples: what to do first, second, third. When to commit to a feature-based rule, when to accept that the category is family-resemblance-shaped and the honest rule is just "more like these, less like those," how to actually use the maybes instead of ignoring them, how to stress-test a candidate rule before shipping it, and at what point to conclude that the examples don't pick out one concept at all and push back on whoever's asking for the rule. Ground it in whatever philosophy and cognitive science actually know about inducing concepts from few examples — I don't want citations, I want the ideas, and I want you to be honest about which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedure costume.

Then run it on two cases from my last year: (1) we banned "astroturfing" based on about thirty example accounts, and it turned out half the examples were about coordinated behavior while the other half were really about being deceptive about who you are; and (2) a "cruelty" rule where the mods' violation examples were suspiciously consistent — every single one contained slurs, even though nobody thinks slurs are necessary for cruelty. I have a feeling the procedure should flag that second one as trouble before we ship anything, and I want to see whether it actually does.

Finally, tell me what the procedure can't do — where inducing a concept from a small, biased sample is just going to fail no matter how careful I am, and the honest move is to declare the rule provisional and let the appeals process catch the misses.
