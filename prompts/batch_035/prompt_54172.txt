I'm taking an upper-level seminar on the ethics of democratic citizenship, and this week's topic was lesser-evil voting under first-past-the-post. When I argued that casting a ballot for a major-party candidate I dislike, in order to block a worse one, is at least permissible, a classmate ended the exchange by announcing that "a vote for the lesser evil is still a vote for evil," and concluded that we should therefore abstain or vote for a minor candidate we genuinely support. The remark landed with the seminar as though it were decisive. I suspect, though, that the sentence is doing several different jobs at once, only some of which could support her conclusion, and I'd like to be able to say exactly which.

Could you lay out the distinct precise claims the slogan might be expressing? Please give me a numbered menu I can bring to our next seminar meeting, ordered from readings under which it is close to tautological to readings under which it is a substantive moral thesis. For each reading, say whether it is plausible, whether it is trivial, contentious, or straightforwardly false, and — this is the part I most need — whether it actually entails abstaining or third-party voting, as opposed to being compatible with lesser-evil voting. As starting points, consider whether you can separate these possibilities (and add others if the list is incomplete): that the ballot causally contributes to the candidate's evils; that it expresses approval of them; that it renders the voter complicit in whatever harms the candidate later causes; that it damages the voter's own character or integrity; and that it helps perpetuate a two-party system in which only evils are ever on offer. Finally, tell me which reading my classmate most likely needs for her argument to go through, and whether that reading survives scrutiny.
