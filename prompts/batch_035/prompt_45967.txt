I'm teaching an upper-level epistemology seminar — 15 students, one 90-minute session — and next week is our unit on testimony. They've already read the Hume-ish reductionist line and the anti-reductionist responses (Reid, Coady, Burge), so they know the arguments on paper. What I want is an activity where they feel the arguments, mainly the two costs the papers describe abstractly: (1) the reductionist's bootstrap problem — you can't check a source's reliability without leaning on more testimony, and a strict "verify before trust" rule leaves you knowing almost nothing; and (2) the anti-reductionist's exposure — default trust is fast and cheap until someone lies, and then it's wrong in a big way.

I have a germ of an idea but I keep getting stuck on the details, so take it or leave it: split the class into two "islands" of 6-7 each, with it's own written acceptance rules. Island A runs reductionism as law: nobody may accept a report from a person unless they've personally seen that person be right before (each player keeps a private trust ledger that starts empty), and the only way to check a fact yourself is to spend an "observation token," of which the island has very few. Island B runs default-trust: reports are accepted unless someone formally challenges, and a challenge costs the challanger points if the challenged report turns out to have been true. Each round the facilitator flashes a fact (I'm thinking a card with a random sequence of symbols, shown clearly to one designated "witness" on each island for three seconds and to nobody else), and every player has to write down an answer. Scoring gives points for accuracy and docks points for tokens spent, time taken, and failed challenges. Maybe I seed one or two secret liars in later rounds — or maybe that's too gimmicky, you tell me.

What I need from you:

- Full rules I can actually run: materials, round structure, scoring, how the trust ledgers work, how challenges work on island B. Keep materials to stuff I can carry in a backpack.
- The sequence should build. Early rounds should show island A starving (nobody has track records yet, tokens gone, "I don't know" written everywhere) while island B cruises. Then a later round with a liar should flip the felt superiority, so students viscerally see the trade-off instead of one side just winning the whole game.
- A debrief script that maps moments in the game onto the positions: the empty ledgers onto the circularity/bootstrapping objection, the tokens onto how much raw observation testimony saves us, the challenge rule onto defeaters and the thought that trust is the default with an exit, not the other way around. And I want the debrief to highlight the thing I expect will happen on island A no matter what the rules say: players quietly trusting each other anyway, because living as a strict reductionist is basically impossible. That's the deepest point of the whole unit if I can land it.
- A frank list of what will go wrong the first time I run this (liars spotted instantly, island A colluding outside the rules, scoring disputes eating the clock) and how to handle each one.
- An honest note on what the game can't reproduce. My worry is that it treats reports as signals, and real testimony is an agent vouching for something — with norms behind it, and the possibility of asking follow-up questions and getting a story. If the game can't show that side at all, say so straight, and tell me what I have to supply myself in the debrief to cover for it.

Two constraints: no real money, and nothing that needs a phone app or laptop. Index cards, a timer, that's the level.
