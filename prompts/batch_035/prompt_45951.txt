Last night my closest friend and his sister argued for three hours at my kitchen table about whether present-day Germans can be guilty of the Holocaust. I had brought it up after finishing Jaspers' 1946 The Question of German Guilt — his four-fold distinction between criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt, and his claim that political liability falls on all citizens of a state while moral guilt attaches only to individuals — and the two of them took it from there while I mostly listened. I want an outside ruling on the exchange, because both seemed to me partly right and I cannot sort out which parts. Call them Jonas (my friend) and Petra (his sister). The exchange, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Jonas began from the corporate side of Jaspers' analysis: nations outlive their members. The Federal Republic still services debts contracted by the Third Reich and still honors its treaties, so we already accept that a polity's obligations bind people who were not yet born when those obligations were incurred. If responsibility persists across generational turnover in the legal register, he said, it is arbitrary to insist it evaporates in the moral one. A German born in 1998 stands in the same relation to the state's history as a German born in 1908 stood to the Reich's debts.

(2) Petra answered that this conflates liability with guilt. Debt is a bookkeeping relation; guilt is a desert relation, and desert presupposes a culpable act — a choice to support, ignore, or knowingly profit. Someone born in 1998 made no such choice, and calling her guilty is not a stern moral judgment but a misuse of the concept that empties it. What the debt analogy actually supports is inherited responsibility: duties of memory, restitution, and vigilance, which can bind the uninvolved. Guilt and responsibility are being run together, and only the second transfers.

(3) Jonas replied that the liability/guilt distinction fails to deliver the asymmetry we actually feel. On Petra's picture, the descendant of a perpetrator and the descendant of a victim living in the same country inherit exactly the same duties — remember, restitute, stay vigilant — and that flattens something important. His proposal was a ledger view: heirs inherit the estate, assets and liabilities together; a nation whose wealth and self-image were shaped by enormous crimes inherits the liability entry along with everything else, and that liability entry just is collective guilt, inherited the way debts are.

(4) Petra pressed on the group, not the ledger. The ledger is kept by a state, a legal person with continuous existence; guilt, if it attaches anywhere, attaches to people. And the German people is not a continuous group across generations: today's Germany includes immigrants with no connection to the crimes, descendants of victims, descendants of resisters. Jonas's ledger has a creditor — the victims — but no stable debtor. What survives generational turnover is an institution, and institutions can owe; only agents can be guilty.

(5) Jonas then reached for Jaspers' fourth category, metaphysical guilt — the co-responsibility said to arise from human solidarity, from surviving where others were murdered, which by design has nothing to do with legal provability or personal acts. His point was that if that is a genuine form of guilt at all, then guilt already does not presuppose individual culpable choice, and the door is open to guilt that persists: crimes of that magnitude cannot simply be deleted from the moral ledger when the last perpetrator dies unrepentant; either the polity remains answerable or the ledger falsifies itself.

(6) Petra closed with what she called the Arendt point: that if everyone is guilty, no one in particular can be judged, so collective-guilt talk ends up shielding individuals rather than accusing them — a round of costless self-reproach that requires nothing of anyone. She added that Jaspers himself, who introduced the metaphysical category, tied it to being alive and standing by while others were destroyed, not to being born later; extended forward in time it becomes vacuous, since it would make everyone guilty of everything forever, and a concept that applies to everything applies to nothing.

They stopped from exhaustion rather than agreement. Please adjudicate the six moves in order — for each, say whether it is right, wrong, or right-but-not-for-the-reason-given, with a brief justification — then identify what, if anything, both of them are missing, and say who comes out more right overall. End with a final line of the form:

Verdict: <one short sentence stating which of the six moves survive scrutiny and, if either, which disputant is more right overall>.
