In my graduate seminar on emergence and distributed cognition, the analogy that keeps doing the heavy lifting is the one Hofstadter dramatized in the Prelude and Ant Fugue dialogues of Gödel, Escher, Bach: an ant colony as a brain, with individual ants playing the role of neurons. The point of the dialogue, as I understand it, is that no single ant is intelligent, yet the colony as a whole exhibits something like memory, planning, and even conversation — in the dialogue, the colony-level intelligence, Aunt Hillary, chats with an Anteater, and the running joke is that the Anteater can eat a few of her "neurons" without harming her mind at all. In my program, this picture gets put to two quite different uses, and I have started to suspect that neither use has ever been checked against the actual mapping.

One group of my colleagues leans on the analogy to argue that mind is purely a matter of organizational pattern. On their telling, if a swarm of individually oblivious insects can add up to something that converses and remembers, then intelligence is a pattern in the traffic of signals, not a property of the signal-carrying stuff — from which they conclude that substrate is irrelevant and that consciousness in machines is unmysterious in principle. A second group uses the same analogy in the opposite direction: they take the colony's apparent purposes to be real, emergent features that are over and above the ants, and conclude that reductionism about the mind fails — that when you get the right kind of organization, a new subject of experience appears at the collective level. It strikes me as suspicious that a single picture can be cited as support by people drawing incompatible conclusions, but I can't yet say whether that means the analogy is doing no work for either side or genuine work for both.

What I would like is a proper audit of the analogy. First, set out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the neuron, the synapse or connection, the pattern of connectivity, the persistent memory trace, the whole brain, and the person or self — a table is fine. Second, say where the mapping breaks. Some candidate disanalogies I have noticed but cannot evaluate: ants are agents under individual selection, with interests that only partially coincide with the colony's, whereas neurons are not candidates for having interests at all; the colony has no fixed internal architecture, with its "computation" running through the external environment via chemical trails rather than through dedicated wiring; and the colony's characteristic processes unfold on timescales of hours or days rather than milliseconds. Which of these differences matter for the uses my colleagues want to put the analogy to, and which are incidental? Third, render a verdict: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the substrate-independence conclusion survive, does the emergent-subject conclusion survive, or does the analogy turn out to have been carrying the conclusion the whole time? Finally, if the colony is not well modeled as a brain, tell me what it is better modeled as — an ecosystem, a market, a slowly reconfiguring computer — and whether that replacement analogy would support any of the original conclusions. I would rather have a defensible negative result than a comforting one.
