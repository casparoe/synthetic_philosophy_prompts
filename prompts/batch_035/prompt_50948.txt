quick gut-check before I present my "view profile" to a reading group — I work on hard choices and value comparisons, and I want to know whether what I've ended up commiting to actually hangs together. Here's the list:

(1) There are hard choices — philosophy career vs music career, city vs countryside — where neither option is better and they're not equally good either. I buy the small-improvement arguement: a small bump to one option (slightly better pay in the academic job) still leaves you with a hard choice, so the two can't be exactly level; they're on a par, a fourth relation besides better/worse/equal.

(2) In those cases there's no fact of the matter about which option I have most all-things-considered reason to pick.

(3) But when I commit to one of them — really put myself behind the choice — the commitment itself generates a reason to stick with it that wasn't there before I chose.

(4) In general I'm a realist about practical reasons: they're grounded in facts about the situation, not in my attitudes, desires, or acts of will. You discover reasons, you don't manufacture them.

(5) I also think a lot of values are incommensurable: no amount of money compensates for betraying a friend — calling some sum "adequate compensation" there is a category mistake.

(6) Yet I think it's perfectly rational to weigh money against time with friends when choosing a job — better pay in a less convenient city — and no category error happens there.

(7) And I hold that the structural requirements of rational choice theory, like completeness and transitivity of preference, bind you in every choice situation, hard or not.

Something in this set feels off to me but I honestly can't locate it. One person in the group swears (5) and (6) are flat-out contradictory; another says the whole thing is fine as long as I phrase stuff carefully. Go through the pairs, tell me which tensions are real and how bad, what the cheapest fix for each would be, and which single revision would clean up the most. Don't pull punches.
