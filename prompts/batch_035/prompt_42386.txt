I teach a seminar on translation theory organized around Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot — his dozens of self-constrained renderings of Marot's tiny three-syllable get-well poem, his translations of Jabberwocky, his anti-anti-translation stance between literalism and free paraphrase — and the thesis that runs through all of it: in a poem, form is not packaging for a meaning that could be shipped separately; the form carries the meaning, and a translation that keeps only the propositional content has kept the least important part. For the final unit I want to pair the book with Arrival (2016), both the film and its source, Ted Chiang's novella Story of Your Life. Please read the pair through Hofstadter.

The reading I'm after has three layers. First, the adaptation itself as translation under new constraints: the novella is, grammatically, a tense-machine — its second-person address to the daughter, interleaved with the account of learning Heptapod B, only reveals itself as foreknowledge rather than memory on rereading. But a shot of film has no grammatical tense; the medium cannot mark past against future except through convention. So the film has to translate the story's most load-bearing formal device into something else, and what it chooses — the flashback convention, which the film then weaponizes once what we took for memory turns out to be foresight — is Hofstadter's thesis in action: the twist is not a fact Louise possesses but a structure the viewer has been misreading. Second, translation inside the film: the semasiographic logograms with no linear order, the sign ambiguously rendered as a weapon or a tool that nearly starts a war, the kangaroo anecdote. Does Heptapod B, as the film imagines it, confirm Hofstadter's inseparability claim by constructing a language where the form/content split simply cannot arise — or is it a fantasy that dissolves his problem rather than solving it? Third, where the reading resists: the film drops the novella's physics (the least-time, variational reasoning that mirrors the heptapods' simultaneous perception) and trades it for a perceptual structure; is that a form-preserving translation of the story's deepest layer or a substitution of its effect for its content? I'd also like your view on whether the film's quieter choices — the daughter's name, a palindrome; her death changed from the story's accident to a slow illness — are formal decisions in Hofstadter's sense or merely sentimental ones. Conclude by saying what the film demonstrates about form and meaning that Hofstadter's own examples can't, and flag honestly any point where the analogy between adaptation and translation breaks down rather than papering over it. My students will have read the book and seen the film; don't summarize either for its own sake.
