I'm trying to work out what I actually think about picking people or outcomes by lottery, going just by my gut on cases. A criminal jury of randomly selected citizens deciding guilt: fine — in fact I trust that more than I'd trust career judges. A citizens' assembly of randomly picked ordinary people hashing out abortion policy, like Ireland did before its referendum: fine. A lottery for who gets the next transplant organ among medically matched patients: fine, and honestly I'd rather have the lottery than let a committee of doctors pick. Same for a lottery allocating subsidized housing among qualified applicants. But a randomly selected panel of citizens setting the central bank interest rate: clearly bad. And full-blown lottocracy — picking the whole legislature by lot, real power, no elections — leaves me uneasy, though I can't say why its any worse than the jury, which also wields real power over someone's life. So is there a principle underneath all this? My rough guess is something like: lotteries for allocating equally valid claims, random citizens for judgements ordinary people can make, experts for technical decisions — but I'm not sure that covers the organ case, where I prefer the lottery over the experts. Whatever principle you land on, tell me what it predicts for a case I haven't mentioned (randomly auditing tax returns vs. randomly choosing which companies recieve a scarce licence, say), and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't save.
