my book club just did a string of popular collapse books -- Diamond on Easter Island, stuff on the late Bronze Age, the classic maya "collapse" -- and the same argument keeps coming back every meeting. one guy insists a civilization is basically an organism: the economy and long-distance trade routes are its circulatory system, a megadrought is a heart attack, and when enough organs fail the thing dies, full stop. he uses this to say that scholars who talk about "transformation" or "reorganization" instead of collapse -- like the people who say the maya didn't die, the lowland cities just emptied and the population carried on in the north -- are basically doing euphemism, the way you'd call a death a "transition" to spare the family. another member says no, a society is more like a river changing course or a company that restructures: nothing dies, the water just moves.

i want to stop having this argument from vibes. audit the organism analogy properly. give me the correspondence table (what plays the role of metabolism, organs, immune system, the age of the organism, and whether there's even such a thing as a corpse), then where the mapping breaks, and please separate the disanalogies that actually matter for his conclusion -- that "transformation" is a euphemism -- from the ones that don't. like, does it matter that organisms die discretely while the "same" people usually walk away from a societal collapse speaking a related language and farming the same fields, or is that a detail? is there an analogue to reproduction or to the species boundary at all? then check whether the river/restructuring analogy is doing the same kind of work in the opposite direction and where it breaks too. finally tell me whether either side's conclusion survives once their analogy is deflated, and whether some third comparison would actually be able to carry the question of whether the late bronze age palaces, say, "died" or "changed." I suspect the whole fight is a fight about which metaphor gets to be the default but maybe that's too cynical.
