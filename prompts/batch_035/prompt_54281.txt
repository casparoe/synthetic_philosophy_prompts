I teach philosophy of science at a mid-sized university, and this year I was drafted onto the central panel that awards our internal seed grants: modest sums, roughly forty thousand euros each, three funding rounds a year, perhaps sixty proposals per round. The panel has nine members from across the faculties, none of us expert in more than a sliver of what crosses the table, and we are expected to get through each proposal in about fifteen minutes. What I have noticed, watching my colleagues argue, is that we keep reinventing an unsatisfactory version of the same distinction: proposals that promise to generate new and somewhat unexpected findings versus proposals that promise mainly to absorb anticipated difficulties into ever more elaborate auxiliary assumptions. This is, of course, uncomfortably close to Lakatos's distinction between progressive and degenerating research programmes, with his hard core, protective belt, and appeal to what a programme's history actually delivered rather than what its prospectus promises.

What I would like you to do is turn Lakatos's methodology into a procedure this panel could actually run under our constraints: an ordered set of questions a non-expert can ask about a proposal, guidance on what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points at which the procedure should deliver a no rather than escalate, and the points at which it must concede that the question is one of scientific judgment the panel cannot launder into a checklist. Two difficulties deserve special attention, and I want the procedure to confront them rather than pretend they dissolve. First, Lakatos's apparatus is retrospective: it tells you how to judge a programme's past track record, but our applicants are mostly asking us to bet on its future, and many are early-career researchers whose programmes have no track record at all. The procedure needs a defensible way to handle the absence of history without quietly becoming a proxy for seniority or fashion. Second, we get a growing number of what I think of as migration cases: groups importing an established framework from a neighboring field into ours, protective belt and all, and arguing that transplanting it counts as novel prediction because it is new to our domain. I genuinely do not know whether the procedure should score that as progress or as the degenerating case dressed up, and I would rather the procedure surface that question honestly than bury it in a scoring rule.

Please then run the procedure on three hypothetical proposals so I can see how it behaves: a laboratory studying memory consolidation whose past decade fits a familiar pattern of post hoc revision around a cherished mechanism; a young researcher with a genuinely unfamiliar model of collective decision-making and nothing but a pilot study behind her; and a migration case in which an economics group wants to bring a well-known rational-choice framework to bear on our institution's educational data, complete with a suite of auxiliary parameters fitted to each new dataset. I will admit that I privately want the third one funded, since I know the applicants and like the work, so please make sure the procedure treats that case exactly as it treats the others, and tell me plainly if its verdict goes against my preference.

Two further requests. I want the response to be candid about failure modes: where the procedure is most likely to misfire in practice, what a determined applicant could do to game it, and what evidence about a programme's actual history should be counted as sufficient to overturn the verdict the procedure delivers, since a rubric that cannot say what would change its mind is not worth fifteen minutes of a panel's time. And please write the whole thing as continuous prose, as if composing a memorandum you might send a colleague by email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. The panel members will print it and read it on a train; it should read like reasoned advice from someone who has thought about Lakatos longer than the rest of us.
