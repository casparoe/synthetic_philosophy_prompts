My ethics reading group has fractured into two camps that have been arguing past each other for a year, and I'd like us to end the year with a document instead of another shouting match. One camp — the optimizers — are committed consequentialists who believe expected-value reasoning remains action-guiding under severe causal ignorance: when we cannot trace the distal effects of an act, they say, we should act on the best estimates available over whatever horizon we can model, and they put substantial weight on the far future and on speculative, high-stakes interventions such as research into existential risk. The other camp — the skeptics — accept consequentialism as the criterion of rightness but insist that radical cluelessness about ripple effects (the sort of point made in the philosophical literature on cluelessness by Lenman and Greaves) means the theory yields no action-guidance at all: for almost any act, the expected value washes out to something symmetric around zero within decades, so the rational response is to prefer only robustly good, near-term options — or, at the limit, to concede something close to moral paralysis. Both camps are sincere, both have read the same papers, and both are tired.

Please draft the joint statement an adversarial collaboration between them should produce. I want four parts. First, a numbered section of agreed claims — and I'd like these to be substantive, not vacuous: things like which classes of effects both sides concede are tractable, whether the symmetry argument rests on empirical premises rather than a priori ones, and what both accept about the reliability of experts making long-range forecasts. Second, a disagreements table: for each live disagreement, a row giving the claim in wording both camps would accept as a fair statement of the dispute, plus a column for each side's version phrased so that its own camp would sign it. Third, a cruxes section: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp, and for each, a concrete case both camps would agree bears on it. As material for this last part, consider two test cases from history: the career of DDT (initial euphoria about malaria eradication, then bioaccumulation and bird-population collapse, then a ban followed by decades of argument about the malaria toll of restricted use) and the Haber–Bosch process (which feeds roughly half the world and also armed the wars of the twentieth century). For each test case, have each camp state in advance what it would have concluded given only the information available at the time — 1945 for DDT, 1910 for synthetic nitrogen — and what it predicts the other camp will say about the same case, so that the document records predictions rather than hindsight. Fourth, a short section saying which of the remaining disagreements are substantive and which are partly verbal, and why.

Two requests about the spirit of the document. It should be committed, not diplomatic in the empty sense: wherever the drafting itself must choose a formulation — where to draw the line between "tractable" and "intractable" horizons, or how to word the symmetry claim so the skeptics can sign it without giving up their position — make the choice, state it, and give the reason, rather than offering me options and leaving the selection to the group. And each side's formulations should be strong enough that its own camp would genuinely put its name to them; if you find that a disagreement dissolves once it is stated precisely, say so plainly rather than preserving it for the sake of balance. Please note at the end, briefly, where you had to lean on a contested characterization of either camp's position in order to produce signable text.
