Two fellow PhD students in my department and I co-run a reading group on testimony, and last week we drifted onto moral testimony via a hypothetical we invented: a startup, "Verdict," where users describe a decision they face — say, whether to fire a long-serving but underperforming employee — and receive a moral verdict from a panel of hired ethicists along with a short written rationale. There's no dialogue with the panel and no visible deliberation: you submit the case, you get the verdict. Assume the verdicts are highly reliable. The two of them got into a genuine disagreement about whether actually acting on Verdict's output would be morally defective, and since I couldn't referee it, I'd like you to.

The exchange, as best I can reconstruct it: (1) Anika said using Verdict is defective even when it gets things right, because it makes the user's action insensitive to the moral reasons: had the app said "fire him," she'd have fired him, and had it said "keep him," she'd have kept him. She added that moral judgment isn't merely a means to right action — exercising it is partly constitutive of responsible agency, so outsourcing it amounts to a kind of self-corruption. (2) Bastian replied that this proves too much: we all acquire moral beliefs from other people all the time — if a trusted colleague tells him a practice is exploitative, he believes it without re-deriving ethics from first principles, and no one calls that defective; forbidding it would make moral life impossible. (3) Anika conceded this but drew a line: learning general moral claims from trusted sources can build understanding over time, whereas Verdict delivers one-off case-specific verdicts that bypass understanding entirely. (4) Bastian said the line won't hold, because asking a wise friend "should I fire him?" and getting a bare "yes" would also be fine, even though that's exactly as case-specific and understanding-bypassing — and if the wise friend is fine, Verdict is fine. His closing shot was that Anika's view implies the more reliable the advisor, the worse it is to take the advice, which he took to be a reductio; Anika's was that some things become worse for us to delegate precisely as they become better done by others, and that delegation-friendly virtues aren't the only virtues.

You are a professional philosopher specializing in moral epistemology. Please think carefully, and begin by briefly mapping where the two actually disagree versus where they talk past each other. Then adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the four moves (plus each side's closing shot), say whether it is sound, what it gets right or wrong, and why. Finally, say who is more right overall, and identify the strongest consideration that neither of them raised.
