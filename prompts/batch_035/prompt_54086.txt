I'm a backend engineer with zero philosophy background, and I got into a long argument with my friend Dave (masters in econ) after a poker game about the Allais paradox. In case you don't have it memorized: pick between A = $1 million for sure and B = 89% chance of $1M, 10% chance of $5M, 1% chance of nothing; then pick between C = 11% chance of $1M and D = 10% chance of $5M, 90% chance of nothing. Basically everyone takes A over B and D over C, and Dave says that's definately irrational, because the two pairs differ only in what happens in the 89% of worlds where they give you the same thing, so by the independence axiom your preference can't legitimately flip between them.

I pushed back that taking the sure million in the first pair isn't crazy — if you gamble and hit the 1%, you have to live with knowing you passed up a guaranteed fortune, and that regret is a real cost. Dave then deployed what he calls his nuclear option, and I can't find the hole in it. (1) A theory of rational choice is only worth having if it forbids some preference patterns; a theory that permits everything is just a record of what people happen to like. (2) In expected utility theory, the independence axiom is what does the forbidding — it's exactly what the Allais pattern violates. (3) The standard rescue of the Allais chooser is to say the outcomes were described too thinly: getting nothing after you passed on a sure million is a different (worse) outcome than getting nothing when both options were gambles, and feelings like regret are legitimate parts of outcomes. (4) But once you let people fold their feelings about the choice itself into the outcome, there's no stopping: prefer the risky option? postulate a utility of risk-taking. Prefer the safe one? a utility of security. Any preference at all can be redescribed so that it maximizes expected utility. (5) So EU theory faces a fork — accept the redescriptions and it forbids nothing, becoming empty math with a theory-shaped sticker on it; reject them and every ordinary person who makes the Allais choices is irrational. (6) Either way the framework that half of economics runs on is in serious trouble.

I hate both horns, but I especially don't want to give up the intuition behind (1) — I really do think a theory that can rationalize anything after the fact is broken (we have a name for that at my job: writing the A/B test after you've already seen the numbers). At the same time it feels wrong to say your gut feelings about how you'd feel are somehow fake outcomes — they seem exactly as real as the dollars. So which step do I reject, and what do I pay for rejecting it? I have a vague suspicion something slides between (3) and (4) but I can't put my finger on it, and Dave just keeps saying "welcome to decision theory."
