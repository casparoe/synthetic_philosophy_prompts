I'm a PhD student in formal epistemology, and a version of an argument from our reading group on the logical omniscience problem has been troubling me for several weeks. Epistemic logic models knowledge with a normal modal operator, so agents come out logically omniscient: whatever follows from what they know, they know. Everyone agrees this is an idealisation, but a friend of mine claims that the idealisation quietly converts into a substantive thesis about actual mathematicians, via the following argument. (1) Knowledge is closed under known entailment: if S knows a set of propositions A, and S knows that A entails the particular proposition φ, then S knows φ — perhaps unable to recite it on demand, but knowing it implicitly; and this closure principle is one we rely on throughout epistemology, for instance in sceptical arguments and in our account of inference as a source of knowledge. (2) In 1980, every trained number theorist knew the axioms of Peano arithmetic, and knew the general fact that whatever follows logically from those axioms is true. (3) Fermat's Last Theorem follows from those axioms — this was so in 1980, as it is now; Wiles merely demonstrated it. (4) So in 1980 every trained number theorist already knew Fermat's Last Theorem implicitly, and by the same reasoning every theorem that will ever be proved is already implicitly known by anyone who knows the axioms; a schoolchild who has mastered Peano's axioms differs from Andrew Wiles only in the explicitness, not the content, of what she knows about Fermat's equation. (5) If all that happened in 1994 was the making-explicit of something already known, then nothing was learned in the sense in which we ordinarily say mathematical results are learned — the sense in which I learned something when I first worked through a proof of the incompleteness theorem as an undergraduate.

The conclusion seems to prove far too much: mathematical pedagogy, refereeing, and the very notion of an open problem all presuppose that there are arithmetical truths no one yet knows, and the argument appears to abolish that. Yet when I try to locate the damage, each premise looks respectable in isolation. The one I should most like to keep is (1), since abandoning closure under known entailment seems to cost us inference as a source of knowledge and would wreak havoc elsewhere in epistemology. The only other candidate I can see is denying that the general fact in (2) — knowing that whatever follows from the axioms is true — delivers the specific entailment knowledge that (1) demands, and when I gestured at this, my friend replied that the entailment was available a priori to anyone who understood the axioms, in the way anyone who understands the axioms can in principle verify any proof, and that this is all (1) requires; I could not see how to press the denial without its sounding ad hoc. Please tell me which step to reject, explain why that step looked fine, and price the alternatives: what each available rejection costs, both within the philosophy of mathematics and for the standard responses to logical omniscience. And if the argument is in fact valid, I want to know which premise is least costly to give up, and what that purchase leaves us unable to say.
