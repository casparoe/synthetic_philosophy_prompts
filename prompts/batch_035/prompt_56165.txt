I've been trying to work out what I actually believe about advance directives by going with my gut on a bunch of cases, and now I want to know if there's a real principle behind my verdicts or whether I'm just vibing. Here's the set:

A woman signs a living will: if I ever get advanced dementia with no hope of recovery, no feeding tube, no antibiotics, let me go. Decades later she's deep into it. She's not suffering though -- she's calm, likes music, lights up when her daughter visits, just can't feed herself. My gut: no tube. The directive holds, even though the current her, if you could ask her, would keep living and enjoying things.

But here's one that pulls the other way. A guy with untreated depression signs a directive refusing resuscitation, telling his brother his life isn't worth living anyway. He then gets treated, is doing well for a few years, glad to be alive, and then comes down with the dementia. The directive kicks in. Gut: don't honor it. And notice it can't just be because the demented him wants to live -- the woman in the first case presumably wants that too and I still say no tube. Something about WHERE the directive came from is doing the work, but I can't say exactly what.

Psychiatric version: a woman with schizophrenia, lucid at the time, writes an advance directive saying that when she's psychotic, give her the injection whether she fights or not, and that anything she says during an episode doesn't count. She then has an episode and fights the injection hard. Gut: inject. Easy call for me, even though it's the most violent override of a current self on this list.

Weird one: same as the first case, but instead of a signed document there's nothing on paper. Her daughter just testifies that she said, more than once, "if I turn into one of those people who just sits there smiling at everybody, don't let them keep me alive on machines." Gut: also no tube, only slightly less confidently. Which is odd, because I'd have thought the whole point of formal directives was that informal ones are so much shakier.

Religious one: a man refuses blood transfusions in his directive, for religious reasons he held all his life. The demented him, asked hypothetically, says sure, do whatever makes me better -- he doesn't really grasp the issue anymore. Gut: honor the refusal. It feels harsh but it feels right.

And then the one I keep going back and forth on. A woman writes the no-antibiotics directive when dementia means, to her, what it meant in her mother: fear, confusion, hours of screaming. But she turns out to have a different kind of dementia -- sweet, contented, no distress at all. The reason she gave for the directive just doesn't apply to her actual situation, even though the situation falls squarely under the words she wrote. Should the words govern or the reason? My gut here is honestly mush, and I want your help.

So my tentative principle is something like: the earlier self gets authority over the later one when the later one can't speak to her own settled values anymore, AND the earlier self was speaking from those same settled values rather than from a passing mood or a distorted state. But I'm not sure this handles the case where the daughter's testimony carried almost as much weight as the signed form, and I'm not sure what it says about a directive written in, say, 1970 about "extraordinary measures" when the extraordinary measures of today are ones the writer couldn't have imagined and might well have welcomed.

Is there one principle that captures all my verdicts? Which ones would it have to overturn? And please tell me what it predicts for a couple of cases I haven't listed -- say, someone whose directive refuses antibiotics but whose dementia is mild enough that she can still coherently discuss her care and insists she's changed her mind about the whole thing. Also happy to hear if you think the whole framework is confused and there's no principled way to do this, only a decision procedure.
