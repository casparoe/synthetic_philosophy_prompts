I've just finished the copyedited manuscript of my first book, a popular history of lighthouse engineering: roughly 400 pages and something like 3,000 discrete factual claims — dates, builders, tonnages, lens specifications, shipwreck names — each of which I have checked against at least one primary source. My publisher wants the preface finalized this week, and my current draft contains a sentence saying that, despite the checking, some errors are certain to remain, for which I take sole responsibility. At a small dinner to celebrate the delivery, my two friends spent most of the evening arguing about whether I can rationally publish that sentence alongside the book. They finally turned to me to referee, and I found I honestly couldn't, which is a problem, because the preface is due in days.

The friends: Marcus, who did his doctoral work in epistemology, and Dara, my acquiring editor. Their exchange went, roughly, as follows. (1) Marcus: I believe each of the 3,000 claims — I would stake my house on any one of them individually — and rational belief is closed under conjunction: if I rationally believe p and rationally believe q, I rationally believe p∧q. Iterating through the whole book, I believe the entire thing is true, and the preface sentence says otherwise. So my rational options are to delete the sentence or concede I don't really believe my own book; he added that the structure is that of the lottery paradox — a million-ticket fair lottery, where one believes of each ticket that it will lose yet believes some ticket wins — and said epistemologists apparently have a name for my preface situation too, which he found darkly amusing. (2) Dara: it is not the same disease. The lottery probabilities are uniform and all generated by a single random draw, whereas my claims were checked individually, on heterogeneous evidence of varying strength, and the preface belief isn't derived from the claims at all — it rests on the track record of books of this kind produced under exactly this checking regime, nearly all of which contain some error. The sentence has its own evidential basis, so no closure principle is implicated in forming it. (3) Marcus: that makes it worse, not better. However it is formed, the resulting set of 3,001 beliefs is inconsistent, and a rational agent shouldn't hold an inconsistent set; Dara's outside-view move is precisely the lottery believer's move, merely with a different flavor of probability. (4) Dara: there is a genuine difference in access. The lottery player has nothing to investigate — no ticket is more worth examining than any other — whereas with the book there is a next step: pull the sources again. Her preferred reading of the preface sentence is as something like a detective's warrant, an acknowledgment that inquiry remains open, not a settled disbelief in the claims; it is not obvious that rationality requires the consistency audit Marcus demands of beliefs formed from such different directions. (5) Marcus: then she has saved the sentence by changing what it says — if the preface means only "I am willing to re-check," it is not the belief that errors remain, and the paradox walks back in the moment the sentence asserts that; and as a flat belief it leaves an author asserting, of each thing she says, that it is true, while asserting that not everything she says is true — performative humility with a contradiction in its pocket.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: rate each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating; say who, if anything, is more right overall; and identify what, if anything, both of them are missing — in particular, whether the lottery and preface cases really do stand or fall together. Finally, if you can, tell me whether there is a formulation of that preface sentence I can sincerely stand behind when the pages go to press.
