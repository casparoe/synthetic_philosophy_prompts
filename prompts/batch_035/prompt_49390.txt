For most of my working life I ran experiments on reinforcement learning in the dopaminergic system — recording from midbrain neurons in macaques, measuring reward prediction error, watching learning curves steepen and flatten. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy of mind, and I keep encountering a claim that strikes me as suspiciously tidy: that the reward signal of an RL agent is, in effect, its valence, so that an agent undergoing negative reward prediction error is thereby suffering, or something close to it. Something in me resists this — I watched learning signals do their work in animals whose affective states I had independent reasons to doubt were engaged at that moment — but I also recognize that my resistance may be an unexamined prejudice of the laboratory, where one is trained to distrust any quantity one cannot plot.

Before I take sides, I want the specification. Please write out the desiderata: what any adequate theory of when a reinforcement-learning agent can suffer has to deliver. I have in mind, at minimum, that it should cover the cases it must get right (among them: nociception without learning, learning without affect, an agent trained by pure TD-error that behaves identically to one whose losses are driven by something we would independently call distress); the distinctions it must respect rather than collapse (learning signal versus felt valence; having a negative signal versus disliking it; functional role versus phenomenal character); and the empirical results from neuroscience and affective science it cannot contradict — including the dissociation between "wanting" and "liking," the fact that prediction-error signals appear in aversive learning as readily as appetitive, and the developmental case of infants who learn long before anything we would confidently call suffering. For each requirement, state the case or consideration behind it, and say whether you regard it as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp — I am particularly unsure whether the demand that the theory not render suffering ubiquitous (as the plain reward-as-valence reading seems to) is a genuine constraint or a bias toward scarcity.

Then, the two parts I most want to see. First, tell me whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, in the way that requiring both a tight link between reward and suffering and clean separation of learning from affect may be — and if so, say which requirement should give. Second, identify the single requirement that excludes the most popular candidate theories; my suspicion is that it is the one requiring the theory to keep suffering from being coextensive with the negative gradient of a scalar loss, since that requirement appears to rule out the reward-as-valence view on one side and at least some purely functionalist views on the other, but I would like your considered judgment. A table is acceptable. Please confine yourself to the conceptual work — no reading lists and no literature surveys; I can find the references myself, and it is the criteria, not the citations, that I want to test my laboratory instincts against.
