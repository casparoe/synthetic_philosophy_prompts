I'm a PhD student in philosophy with a logic background, about a year into a dissertation that drifted from anti-realism debates into the Church-Fitch knowability paradox, and now I'm trying to figure out what the actual live research questions in that pocket are before I sink three more years into it. The setup as I understand it: if every truth is knowable (KP), then since an unknown truth conjoined with its own unknowability is a truth, it would have to be knowable, and knowing it would entail knowing a contradiction -- so KP plus the claim that not everything is known collapses anti-realism into something trivial. The standard escape routes are restricting which truths are knowable (Dummett-style, Tennant's cartesian restriction), denying the distribution of knowability over conjunction, or biting the bullet on the temporal/actualized readings of the knowability operator. My advisor keeps hinting that the field has mostly re-litigated these same three moves for decades and that the interesting stuff now is elsewhere.

So here's what I want from you: a prioritized agenda for someone in my position -- two to three years, comfortable with modal logic and epistemic logic, not looking to invent a new logical system from scratch. Which open questions or lines of work around Fitch's paradox should I actually prioritize, and please defend the ordering rather than just listing them. For each candidate, I want to know roughly: how much downstream work depends on it (e.g., does settling the conjunction-distribution question make half the other debates moot?), whether it's tractable with existing formal tools or requires building new machinery, and whether it's genuinely unsettled or just under-communicated. I'm especially unsure how to weigh two things: on one hand, the "knowability for whom" question -- whether moving to a multi-agent or group-knowledge setting dissolves the paradox or just multiplies versions of it; on the other, whether the connection people draw between Fitch-style reasoning and other mild agnosticism results in the literature is a real research program or a grab bag of superficially similar theorems. Feel free to also tell me some popular angle is overrated, I'd honestly rather hear that now than in year four.

And since I don't want to anchor on one confident take: tell me where your own ranking is most likely to go wrong -- what argument or consideration, if you thought harder about it or if someone pushed back, would move something up or down the list.
