I've been following the debate over the doomsday argument for a while now -- the reasoning from Carter and Leslie that, roughly, if you treat your birth rank among all humans as a random draw, the fact that you find yourself only tens of billions of births into the story should shift your credence toward humanity ending sooner rather than later -- and what strikes me is how completely stalled it is. The standard defenses and attacks are all decades old: the appeal to the self-sampling assumption and the choice of reference class on one side, and on the other the objections that your birth rank isn't a random sample at all, that the argument commits a selection fallacy (Eckhardt-style), or that if you condition on there being at least one observer, the whole update washes out. Bostrom tried to systematize the whole mess by distinguishing self-sampling from self-indication, and people have noticed that the same dispute resurfaces in the Sleeping Beauty problem, where self-indicators tend to be thirders and self-samplers halfers. And yet, as far as I can tell, nobody on either side has ever changed their mind. Every party agrees on the ordinary facts, agrees on the probability axioms, agrees on Bayes' theorem -- and still the updates come out opposite, or get refused entirely.

My working hypothesis is that this isn't really a disagreement about probability at all, but about a conceptual question that neither side states explicitly: something like whether "finding yourself to be an observer" is evidence in the same sense that finding a coin on the table is evidence, or whether the case where your own existence is part of the conditioning event is categorically different from cases where it isn't. But I might be wrong; it could instead be a hidden empirical or methodological dispute about the reference class (who counts as "in your sample" -- all humans? all observers? all conscious moments?), or a clash of prior intuitions about typicality that nobody can argue for without presupposing the very principle under dispute. Diagnose this for me: what kind of disagreement is it, actually? Substantive, verbal, or two camps answering different questions under one heading? Support the diagnosis with what the various sides actually claim, not just a survey of positions. And then tell me honestly whether anything could end it -- a formal result, a better analogy, a decision-theoretic reframing like the move from updating to betting behavior, or a genuinely observed case of anthropic reasoning succeeding or failing -- or whether we should conclude the question is ill-posed and expect the stalemate to be permanent.
