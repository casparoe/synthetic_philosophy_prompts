I'm a junior philosophy major in a social epistemology seminar (20 students, 75 min sessions). Next week is my turn to co-run the session, and it's on the C. Thi Nguyen paper distinguishing epistemic bubbles from echo chambers. My professor will do the lecture part (~15 min), and then it's on me for the rest of the class plus the debrief, which is a little terrifying honestly.

Here's my problem. Most of my classmates already get the definition-level distinction: a bubble is where relevant voices are just left out by omission, so exposure to the missing info pops it; an echo chamber is where the structure actively discredits outside voices, so the same exposure gets absorbed and explained away. What they do NOT get is how different those feel from the inside, and why exposure fixes one and just makes the other defensive. I want the activity to produce that asymmetry live in the room: some participants end up in a bubble condition and others in an echo-chamber condition, on the same question, and then everyone gets exposed to the exact same outside information, and we watch who updates and who discounts it.

Design that for me. What I need specifically:

- The game itself: rules, printable materials, roles, and a timeline that fits my ~55-60 minutes. The hidden question should be something they can't just reflexively google or answer from priors but can in principle resolve from distributed clues — maybe a fictional scenario with a solution we can control, or a logic/estimation puzzle where clues are handed out unevenly. The bubble groups just never recieve some clues; the echo-chamber groups get those same clues but wrapped in a preamble that discredits their source. Then phase two: everyone gets the full outside information at once.
- A scoring scheme that works without real money, and setup that keeps groups from immediately realizing they've been sorted into conditions.
- The debrief: questions that map specific moments in the game onto the distinction, including a couple of short interviews with bubble-group and echo-chamber-group members about what the outside info looked like from where they sat, plus a runthrough of why the two conditions can hold the same evidence and update differently. Maybe a beat where the class tries to design a "de-programming" strategy for the echo-chamber groups and discovers how much more than information it takes.
- What will predictibly go wrong the first time I run it, and how to handle each.
- An honest note on which part of the idea the game can't actually reproduce (I suspect the trust-manipulation in real echo chambers builds up over years, not twenty minutes, but tell me whatever else the activity fakes).
- One constraint from my prof: the echo-chamber manipulation can't leave people feeling personally tricked or lied to in a way that sours the room — she wants the debrief to land on the epistemics, not on resentment. Design the reveal/debrief so that works.

Also, a short paragraph I can send my prof beforehand explaining what the activity is supposed to demonstrate, since she has to approve it by Friday.
