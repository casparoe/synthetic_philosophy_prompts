I teach an upper-level seminar on the ethics of procreation and population (14 students, one 80-minute session, no prior exposure to the literature). We're about to reach Benatar's asymmetry, and I don't want to open with a lecture — I want the students to walk into it backwards. My plan is to stage a scenario in which they, serving as the Ethics Board of a colony ship or remote settlement, have to make a series of binding decisions about creating future people and about the welfare of existing ones: whether to authorize additional births when the projected lives would contain more suffering than happiness, whether to authorize them when the projected lives would be wonderful, whether to fund pain-relief versus enrichment programs for the existing population, and what to say about potential people who are never brought into existence at all. I'd like you to design the full activity: the concrete scenario and materials, a decision-by-decision timeline with secret ballots or some other mechanism that keeps early answers independent, a way of recording and displaying the group's pattern of judgments (ideally so the 2x2 structure of the asymmetry becomes visible on the board only once all four cells have been filled in), and the moment where the students are confronted with the conclusion their own recorded answers seem to commit them to — that coming into existence is a harm — before Benatar's name or the term "asymmetry" is ever mentioned.

Please act as an experienced designer of philosophy classroom activities as well as a professional philosopher working on population ethics, and think carefully about the sequencing before you write: the whole point is that each individual decision should feel obviously right in isolation, and the trouble should only appear when the pattern is assembled, so the individual cases need to be framed in ways that don't tip the hand. For the debrief, I want a mapping from specific moments in the activity onto each of the four claims that make up the asymmetry (presence of pain is bad, absence of pain is good even if no one enjoys that absence, presence of pleasure is good, absence of pleasure is not bad unless someone is deprived), a structured discussion of exactly where a student could dig in and reject one of the cells they themselves endorsed, and what rejecting each one costs them elsewhere (e.g., if the absence of pleasure in a never-created happy person counts as a bad, what does that imply about the vast numbers of merely possible people?). I'd also like an honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce — my students' intuitions about four cases are evidence about their psychology, not an argument, and the debrief needs to make that gap explicit rather than papering over it — plus a list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: I'm thinking of the one student who has already read the book and tries to spoil the reveal, students who notice the pattern halfway through and start voting strategically for consistency, and the risk that the utilitarians in the room reject cell three immediately and thereby skip the experience I'm trying to create. Tell me how to handle each of those, and whether any of them is better prevented by scenario design than by facilitation.
