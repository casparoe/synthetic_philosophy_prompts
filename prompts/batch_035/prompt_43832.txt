I run a reading group that just did Dennett's Atlantic piece The Problem With Counterfeit People — the late-Dennett claim that systems like chatbots can have all the behavioral competence of a person with none of the real understanding underneath, so they're counterfeit people, and that minting them is closer to vandalism than to creating minds. We want a sorting quiz out of it, and I want the quiz to work on people who haven't read the piece.

Group is 12 people, mostly non-philosophers, one evening session. Please write a "which view of machine personhood do you actually hold?" quiz, 9 or 10 questions, each a concrete case with 2-3 answer options, plus a scoring key.

The cases should be built from the logical space, not from a list of schools. So think of it as: you take as your fixed starting point a system that passes every behavioral test you can throw at it — years of conversation, novel problem solving, apparent emotional life — and then the branch questions are things like: does its origin matter (grown up like a child through embodied interaction with the world vs. reverse-engineered from oceans of human-written text)? does the training history matter even when the behavior is identical? does it matter whether the competence came from comprehension or from something more like a lookup table that got good enough at faking comprehension? does it matter whether anyone loves it or relies on it? does it matter what it's made of?

Then locate the actual positions as leaves, mapped from answer patterns: Dennett's own view (perfectly convincing counterfeit people are possible and will probably exist, and that's the whole point — nothing real underneath, which is why the counterfeits themselves have no moral status even though creating them is a wrong against the rest of us); the straight functionalist leaf (passing the tests with no caveats is what there is); the origin/history leaf (there's a difference in kind between grown and synthesized minds regardless of behavior); the relational leaf (status comes from embedding in relationships and society, not from inside at all); the biological substrate leaf; and a deflationary leaf for people who think the question "is it REALLY a person" is confused in a way that no case will fix.

Two specific cases I want in there somewhere, because they cut across the standard lines: (1) a mind upload — a scan of a specific existing human, running on silicon — which pulls behavior-identical intuitions toward "genuine" for people who would say a de novo system with identical behavior is counterfeit, and I want to see whether the quiz can actually detect that tension rather than paper over it; and (2) a system with human-level competence that never saw a scrap of human-produced data, trained purely by reinforcement learning on some non-human task and then given a body and language late — the AlphaZero-raised-as-a-child scenario — which seems like the cleanest probe for whether someone's real commitment is "grown like a child" or just "not trained on our stuff."

For the scoring key: for each leaf, one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and for at least one pair of leaves admit the quiz can't separate them and say what question would have to be added to do it. Also flag any answer-pattern cell that's coherent but that you'd expect nobody to land in, with a guess about why nobody's there (incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored).

One more ask: Dennett's vandalism point runs orthogonal to personhood — you can think the fakes have zero moral status AND that flooding the world with them is still a serious wrong to real people. So add one final standalone question (not part of the sort) that maps where participants land on that, with the answer options phrased so that someone can consistently pick "no status, but building them is still wrong."

No question should just ask "do you believe AI could be conscious?" — cases only, nothing self-labeling. And please don't have every question resolve cleanly onto the same axis; if two questions are probing the same distinction, cut one.
