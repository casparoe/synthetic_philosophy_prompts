Then discuss one explanation of the effect that has been proposed, saying what it explains well and where it struggles."

---

Knobe's chairman case goes like this. The chairman of a company is told by the board that a new program will increase profits but will also harm the environment. The chairman replies that he doesn't care at all about harming the environment, he just wants to make as much profit as he can, and starts the program anyway. Asked whether the chairman intentionally harmed the environment, around 80% of people say yes. In the mirror version, the program will help the environment, the chairman again says he doesn't care about helping, and now a large majority say he did not help intentionally. So knowledge and indifference are held fixed across the two versions, only the moral valence of the side effect flips, and the intentionality judgments flip with it. This became known as the side-effect effect.

Knobe argued that this shows the folk concept of intentional action is not purely descriptive but partly moral: whether an act counts as intentional depends in part on how we evaluate it. This is a challenge to the traditional analysis of intentional action in terms of belief, desire, and skill, which leaves no room for moral valence.

Why does the asymmetry happen? The most natural explanation is that in the harm condition people infer that the chairman wanted the harm, and since wanting is part of intending, they judge that he harmed intentionally, whereas in the help condition they make no such inference. Knobe himself proposed something along these lines in the original paper, in the form of the "deep self" account: the harm flows from the chairman's core values (profit over the environment), so it expresses who he really is, while the help happens despite his values. The deep self account handles the basic asymmetry nicely, and it also explains why an agent who causes harm reluctantly, while genuinely caring about preventing it, tends to be judged less intentional. Where it struggles is that the effect shows up even when participants know basically nothing about the agent's values or character, which the account seems to need as input. On the other hand, the effect has now been replicated across many scenarios and populations, which is a point in the account's favor as tracking a real feature of the concept rather than an artifact of one vignette.

Overall, I think the studies establish that the concept of intentional action just is a moral concept — there is no neutral, value-free notion of doing something intentionally — so the traditional analyses are simply mistaken about what the folk mean by "intentional."
