I write long-form pieces on science and ideas, and for the past year I've been reporting on what the free-will debates imply for how courts and the rest of us assign blame. Somewhere between the interviews I developed a working position of my own, and before it goes anywhere near print I want to know whether it actually coheres. Here is what I currently believe:

1. Frankfurt's case does what it was meant to do. If Black, the standby neuroscientist, never has to intervene because Jones decides on his own to kill Smith, then Jones is morally responsible even though Black's readiness guaranteed Jones could not have done otherwise. The principle of alternate possibilities is not a necessary condition on responsibility.

2. What carries responsibility in that case is the actual sequence of practical reasoning: Jones reached his decision for his own reasons, through a deliberative mechanism that would have registered sufficient reason to abstain had such reason been present. Whether alternatives were genuinely open to him is beside the point.

3. Since reasons-responsive sequences like that could occur just as easily under causal determinism, I'm a compatibilist: even if the physics turned out to be fully deterministic, responsibility would survive intact.

4. Nevertheless, I think Pereboom's Plum is not responsible — the man who kills because neuroscientists rigged his motivational structure — even though, at the moment he acts, nobody intervenes and his action issues from desires that respond to what he takes to be reasons. And I confess the intuition survives the whole four-case progression, which unsettles me, because the progression ends with nothing but an ordinary agent in an ordinary deterministic world.

5. Separately, I hold something like a fairness condition on blame: it is unjust to hold someone responsible for a bad act if they never had, anywhere in their history, a fair opportunity to avoid becoming the sort of person who would do such a thing.

6. I also hold that coerced agents — the clerk at gunpoint who opens the safe — are not blameworthy, even though the clerk, like Jones, could not have done otherwise.

My suspicion is that some of these commitments are pulling against one another, but I genuinely cannot see where the trouble comes from, and I have no settled view about which one should give way. Please treat my position as the object of inquiry rather than as a prompt for a survey: go through the commitments, tell me which pairs are in real tension and which only appear to be, rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say what would have to give and what conceding it would cost me elsewhere in the set. If one single revision would clean up the most, say so and explain why. I would rather have a pointed audit than a literature overview — no reading lists, please, just the analysis of whether my package hangs together.
