According to Gödel's first incompleteness theorem, any consistent formal system strong enough to express arithmetic contains statements that are true but unprovable within it, the paradigm case being the Gödel sentence, which asserts its own unprovability and whose truth follows, given the system's consistency, from that very unprovability. The continuum hypothesis, meanwhile, is known to be unprovable in ZFC, our standard foundation for mathematics. By parity of reasoning, this should mean the continuum hypothesis is true but unprovable: ZFC is presumably consistent, it cannot settle CH, and incompleteness tells us such statements exceed proof while remaining true. Yet set theorists behave as though the independence of CH left its truth value genuinely open — debating whether it is true, false, indeterminate, or true only relative to a background model — rather than treating it as one more Gödel sentence whose truth is secured by the fact that we cannot prove it. Why does the unprovability of the Gödel sentence transfer so readily into a claim about truth, while the unprovability of the continuum hypothesis does not? Is there a principled asymmetry between the two cases, or are set theorists simply failing to apply the lesson of incompleteness uniformly? If the asymmetry is principled, what exactly grounds it, and does it extend to other statements independent of ZFC, such as large cardinal axioms?
