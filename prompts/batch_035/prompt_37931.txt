I co-run a student discussion group (mixed bag: a few philosophy majors, but mostly people from econ and bio who just like arguing), and our theme this term is population ethics. Next session is on suffering-focused views, and I want to close it with a poll slide ranking a few positions, so I need the ranking to actually be defensible when people push back. Here are the five positions I want compared:

1. Classical hedonic utilitarianism — positive and negative welfare both count, one-for-one.
2. A "weak" negative view — suffering gets extra weight compared to happiness, but no amount of suffering strictly outweighs any amount of happiness.
3. A lexical (or near-lexical) view — there's some threshold of intense suffering such that no quantity of happiness can compensate for it, while below that threshold tradeoffs work normally.
4. An asymmetry view about creating lives: adding a new happy life to the world adds no value (though it's not bad), while adding a new miserable life makes things worse. People sometimes pin this on Popper, though I gather that attribution is pretty shaky, so please don't lean on it.
5. Straight negative utilitarianism — positive welfare counts for zero, morally; only the reduction of suffering matters.

What I'd like from you: first, rate each of the five on a 0–1 scale for how plausible it is as something I could endorse after serious reflection (decimals welcome, and it's fine to cluster several views at the same number). Then give an overall ranking from most to least plausible with a sentence on why each lands where it does — I want to be able to read those sentences off the slide. After that, tell me what fraction of our 90-minute session to spend on each view, with the fractions summing to 1 — weighted by how much there is to say that our mixed audience would find genuinely interesting, not just proportional to your plausibility scores (I suspect view 5 deserves a chunk of time purely because of the painless-extinction objection, even if it ranks near the bottom).

Please write everything in American English. And since I'll be standing in front of a room that has definately read more philosophy than me, add a short note at the end saying where your own ranking is most likely to go wrong — what argument or counterexample would get you to reshuffle it, and which of the five ratings you're least confident in.
