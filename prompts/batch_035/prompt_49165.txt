I'm in a book club that reads one pop philosophy book a month, and this time we picked one where a chapter argues that Buddhist ethics is really just consequentialism that arrived early. The author's summary goes roughly like this: Buddhism holds that suffering is the only thing that's intrausively bad, and since every sentient being's suffering counts the same, the whole system reduces to a kind of proto-utilitarianism. He says karma functions as the utility calculus -- actions are good in proportion to the good consequences they ripen into across lifetimes, so karma is basically an ancient tracking mechanism for outcomes. He points to the bodhisattva ideal as proof of the aggregative spirit: a being who postpones his own nirvana until every last sentient being is liberated is obviously maximizing total welfare, just with a longer time horizon than Bentham had. And he says the precepts, like not killing or lying, are just rules of thumb for people who can't do the full calculation -- the Middle Way itself shows Buddhism rejects absolutist rules in favor of weighing things up. He even cites a story where the Buddha, in a past life as a ship's captain, kills a robber to save the five hundred merchants aboard, and takes this to show that Buddhist ethics explicitly endorses ends-justifies-means reasoning, so Buddhism already solved the organ-harvest objection before utilitarianism got around to having it.

Some of this sounds right to me and some of it sets off alarm bells, but I genuinely can't tell which is which. I remember hearing somewhere that karma in the early texts is defined by intention rather than consequences, which if true would undercut alot of his chapter, but maybe I'm misremembering. Go through his summary piece by piece and tell me: what would a scholar of Buddhist ethics accept as basically fair, what would they want to qualify, and what is just wrong? I'm especially unsure whether the ship-captain story is a trump card for his reading or a famously contested episode that he's presenting as settled, and whether the bodhisattva reading holds up given that not all Buddhist traditions even center the bodhisattva ideal. And at the end, tell me whether his overall claim -- that a Buddhist has to answer trolley problems the way a utilitarian does -- survives the corrections, or whether it depends entirely on the shaky parts.
