I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy background, and I serve on the committee that reviews production incidents at my company. Last quarter we had two cases that I can't stop thinking about. In both, a senior engineer bypassed our review process and merged a database change to production on a Friday evening without the full test suite running. The code changes were similarly risky, and the violations were equally deliberate shortcuts. In one case, nothing happened: the risky path in the code never got triggered. In the other, a race condition silently corrupted a batch of customer records, and we lost our biggest client over it. The committee has proposed a formal written warning for the engineer whose change destroyed data and no action at all for the other one. When I objected that the two did exactly the same thing and only luck separates them, my manager replied that blame naturally tracks outcomes — that's how it works in law, in sports, and everywhere else, so we shouldn't pretend otherwise. A third colleague offered an argument that's been bugging me more: even if the underlying acts are identical, the bad outcome is our only evidence that something went wrong at all, and an organization that punished only detected violations at least has a reason to keep engineers watching for near misses — though I can also see that this just incentivizes hiding the near misses we never detect. I want to know whether the two engineers deserve different treatment, or whether treating them differently is just the committee laundering luck into desert. Please give me your actual conclusion rather than laying out the options and refusing to choose, and tell me plainly if you think my framing of the problem is confused somewhere — I'd rather be corrected than politely agreed with. Please answer in American English.
