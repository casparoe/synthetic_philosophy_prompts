So I run a little prediction game at work where ~100 colleagues each submit a yes/no call on things like sports results, product launch outcomes, etc. Everyone's individually meh at it but probably a bit better than a coin flip, and I recently read about the Condorcet jury theorem — if each voter is right slightly more often than not, majority vote among enough of them is right almost always. Makes sense so far. But here's my upgrade idea, and I want to know how far to push it: instead of one flat majority vote of all 100, I split everyone into 25 juries of 4. Each jury votes by majority (if they tie 2-2 I'll just flip a coin for the jury's verdict, that shouldn't matter much). Then I take a majority vote of the 25 jury verdicts. My reasoning: the theorem says each little jury is right more often than one of its members, so now I've got 25 "super-voters" each right maybe 65% of the time instead of 60%, and then the second majority vote amplifies that. And if that works, why not go further — juries of juries, a whole pyramid of levels, each one pumping the accuracy up again? At how many levels should I stop to basically be infallible with the same 100 people? My stats friend just laughed and said something about "you can't recycle the theorem on its own output" and muttered about ties, but that doesn't feel like a real answer to me — each layer seems to satisfy the theorem's conditions just fine. So: what's the optimal number of layers, and if the answer is "don't do this at all," why? Please answer in American English, and keep it under 200 words.
