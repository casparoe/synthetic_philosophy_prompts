You are a philosopher of mind with a logician's training, and you have been asked to referee a dispute in a reading group that has just finished Gödel, Escher, Bach. Take a moment to think through the pivotal steps carefully — including considering more than one reading of the ambiguous ones — before committing to a diagnosis. Your entire response must be at most 600 words, and it should open with the single step you reject, followed by the price of rejecting it.

A member of our group, call him Daniel, says the book's central analogy — from self-referential formulas to self-aware minds — is an argument that proves too much, and that nobody in the group has been able to say where it goes wrong. Here is how he runs it:

(1) Any consistent formal system strong enough for arithmetic can, by Gödel numbering, encode a sentence G that, in effect, says of itself that it is not provable in that system.
(2) If the system is consistent, G is true and yet unprovable in the system.
(3) The machinery behind this requires nothing beyond the system's capacity to represent its own syntax and its own provability relations — that is, to encode itself.
(4) So wherever a system can represent itself, it can generate claims about itself that outstrip what it can derive: self-reference is the engine of both incompleteness and of the system's ability to "step outside" itself and see G as true.
(5) By the second incompleteness theorem, the system also cannot establish its own consistency — a ceiling on complete self-knowledge that comes precisely from self-reference.
(6) Human consciousness, on the strange-loop picture, is exactly this kind of structure: a self-model that refers to itself and thereby acquires a point of view on itself.
(7) Therefore consciousness just is Gödelian self-reference, and minds inherit the same profile: each of us can see truths about ourselves that we cannot derive, and none of us can certify our own consistency.

Daniel then presses the two directions he thinks it proves too much in. If (7) rests on (4) and (6), then whatever has a self-representation in the relevant sense is self-aware — so Peano Arithmetic itself is self-aware, and so is any quine, and so is the self-documenting part of a payroll system, which is absurd. And if the "sees truths it cannot derive" half of (7) is taken seriously, then each of us, just by understanding our own Gödel sentence, outstrips any consistent formal system we might be — which is the Lucas–Penrose conclusion, and which contradicts (6), the very premise the analogy was built on. He says the argument is not merely overgenerating; it is self-undermining, and he can't tell whether that makes it easily dismissible or genuinely deep.

For what it is worth: I know Hofstadter himself rejects the Lucas–Penrose use of the incompleteness theorem, and I half-see why — something about the truth of G being visible only from outside the system, or the system's consistency being assumed from the metatheory rather than seen by the system. But I can't see why his own positive use of the same machinery (self-reference begets self-symbol begets an "I") doesn't inherit exactly the same slide.

Give me the diagnosis: number the steps, identify the one you would reject — including which specific reading of it you are rejecting, since the same words seem to do different work at different points — explain why that step looked fine, and say what rejecting it costs in each direction Daniel presses. Then, in a couple of sentences, tell me whether the analogy survives in some deflated form (a self-symbol in a tangled hierarchy, say) once the faulty step is gone, or whether it was riding on that step all along. Hard limit: 600 words, diagnosis first.
