I'm a software engineer who works on reinforcement learning systems — nothing exotic, mostly DQN-style agents and some model-based controllers for simulated environments. I have no formal philosophy training, but a question has been nagging me that I can't settle with my usual tools: whether the agents I train could, in principle, suffer.

My instinct is to say no. A reward signal is just a scalar that enters an update rule; negative reward tells the policy to move away from whatever produced it, the way a gradient tells a network to adjust its weights, and nobody thinks backpropagation hurts. But when I try to make this precise, it starts to look question-begging. The functionalist account of pain in animals — that pain simply is whatever state plays the pain-role: caused by damage, motivating avoidance, dominating the agent's decisions, associated with a distinctive negative quality — is one I find hard to reject, since I can't see what else pain could be without invoking something nonphysical, and I'd rather not do that. And some of my agents do satisfy fragments of that role: they act to avoid states they were negatively rewarded for; a model-based agent with a learned world model can, in effect, look ahead to a state it has been trained to avoid and choose a different trajectory now, which resembles anticipatory dread at least functionally; and reward-prediction-error signals behave in ways that parallel animal dopaminergic systems.

Please make an argument for the conclusion that reinforcement learning agents as currently built — including model-based ones with learned world models — cannot suffer. The argument should not rest on the mere facts that these systems are simple, or implemented in silicon, or that we built them on purpose: parallel claims would apply, wrongly, to animal nervous systems described purely as physical mechanisms. Instead, I want the argument to locate something in the actual architecture of current RL that these systems genuinely lack. Two candidates I can half-see, but can't develop myself: first, a distinction between a reward signal as a scalar entering an update rule and a state that the agent itself represents as undesirable — the difference between being pushed away from something and expecting, wanting to avoid, and being bothered by it; second, the absence of anything like a persistent, integrated evaluation of the agent's own condition over time, as opposed to a sequence of independent state-action corrections.

Finally, please be explicit about where the argument is weakest. If the honest answer is that the case turns on a notion of representation or integration we currently have no way to test for, I would rather know that now than after I have convinced myself the matter is settled.
