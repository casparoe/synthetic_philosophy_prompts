I'm the founder of a small company heading into a second round of acquisition talks with a much larger firm. My advisor is pushing a specific tactic: before we sit back down, I should make my reservation price public and irreversible — for instance, tell my employees and a trade journalist that I will reject any offer below a certain number, and that I will resign and sell my shares on the open market if the board ever accepts less. His reasoning is that once the acquirer knows I genuinely cannot say yes to a lower figure, their lowballing will stop: faced with a take-it-or-leave-it floor that they can verify is truly binding, they will simply meet my number rather than lose the deal over a gap they no longer have the power to close.

The tactic sounds clever, but it makes me uneasy, and I want to think it through rather than follow it on charisma. First, where exactly does the advantage of tying my own hands come from? It seems paradoxical that destroying some of my own options could make me better off, and I'd like the logic of that spelled out, not just asserted. Second, under what conditions does it backfire — for instance, if my estimate of what they'll pay is wrong, or if their negotiator reacts badly to being cornered? Third, and this is what really nags me: my advisor frames this as a race, saying whoever commits first and most visibly wins. But the acquirer's lead negotiator could presumably do the same thing — announce to her board that she is authorized to offer at most some ceiling, in a way she can't walk back either. If we both did this, and our two locked-in positions didn't overlap, we'd have a deal that's impossible even though both of us would prefer some deal in the range to no deal. Is my advisor right that racing to commit first is generally wise, or is there something structurally self-undermining about the race itself — a reason why being the first to make myself inflexible can leave me stuck in a mutually destructive standoff that a flexible negotiator would have avoided?

A colleague offered a possible middle path I can't evaluate: make a commitment that is costly and credible to the other side, but revocable only by a third party — say, have my lawyer hold a signed resignation letter that he will deliver to the board unless the final offer meets some threshold, with instructions letting him waive the letter under conditions I don't fully control. Does this kind of delegated, partially-revocable commitment preserve the bargaining advantage my advisor is after while softening the standoff risk, or does it just reintroduce the flexibility (and thus the exploitability) that the whole tactic was meant to eliminate?

Please say how confident you are in each of your claims, and single out the point you are least sure of.
