I've read a little about pain asymbolia — people who, after certain brain injuries, say they still feel pain, can locate it and rate its intensity, but report that it doesn't bother them at all, sometimes laughing while describing a burn. I'm not a student of philosophy or medicine; I've just been chewing on this alone, because it seems to pull apart two things I'd always assumed were one: the feeling of pain and the badness of pain. If the sensation stays and the suffering goes, maybe the badness was never in the sensation itself but in something else — the recoil, the wanting-it-gone, some second-order hating of the feeling. But then I'm not sure what that recoil is, if not just more feeling.

Could you write a sonnet in the voice of someone with pain asymbolia? I'd like the octave to render the sensory side faithfully and concretely — the heat, the ache, the sharpness, located and rated — so the pain is unmistakably present as experience. Then I'd like the volta, at line nine, to be the place where the aversion should arrive and doesn't: the flinch that fails to come, the indifference where distress was expected. The closing couplet should turn outward to the question this raises for the rest of us, namely whether the rest of us are wrong to think the badness lives in the hurt itself — though I'd rather it raise that question than settle it. If you can, use the rhyme scheme itself to carry the split: for instance, let the octave's rhymes feel insistent and bodily, and let the sestet's fall strangely quiet or wrong-footed, so the form enacts the disconnection between intensity and mattering. I want someone who has thought about the badness of pain to read it and recognize the philosophical issue exactly, and someone who hasn't to simply feel how eerie it would be to touch a hot stove and care nothing.
