I teach an upper-level seminar called Truth, Validity, and Paradox, and I'm putting together the three-hour final. The students are comfortable with classical propositional logic, have worked through the Liar and Tarski-style biconditionals, and have spent the last month on dialetheism and the logic LP, including why the paraconsistent refusal of explosion lets them tolerate a few gluts without collapsing into trivialism. My worry, having graded their midterm, is that many of them now treat dialetheism as a swagger move: find the Liar, declare it both true and false, and consider the paradox problem solved. What they have not internalized is that this dialectic runs through negation at every step, and that Curry's paradox -- built from the naive truth schema and a conditional, with no negation anywhere in sight -- is a genuinely different threat, since simply tolerating a glut doesn't obviously help when the derivation ends in an arbitrary conclusion rather than a contradiction. I want the final to force this confrontation.

Please write one essay question, answerable in about ninety minutes, in which Curry's paradox is the centerpiece. The question should be constructed so that a student who has merely memorized "Liar sentence, glut, explosion blocked, QED" cannot pass it, while a student who understands the actual structure of the paradox can. It should require them to derive Curry's conclusion at least schematically, explain precisely why the dialetheist's standard maneuver from the Liar case is not automatically available here, and then evaluate what a blocking strategy would have to give up. I'd also like the question to gesture at a generalization we discussed in seminar: a form of the paradox that targets validity itself rather than truth, where the key premise is a sentence which says, in effect, that if that very argument is valid, then everything is true. Students should be pushed to see that any response to the truth-directed version has to say something principled about this validity-directed sibling, and that "both true and false" is of no obvious use against an argument that never produces a falsehood.

Alongside the question, provide a model answer of roughly 700 words and a marking scheme with five must-hit points, worth a total of 25 marks, with a one-line note on the mistake students typically make at each point -- for example, conflating non-explosiveness with a solution to Curry, or assuming that rejecting the conditional is costless given what the conditional was needed for in the naive theory of truth. One commitment: in the model answer, do not survey the candidate blocking strategies and leave the choice open. Pick one -- restricting contraction, weakening the conditional, or a substructural move you find most defensible -- defend it explicitly against the obvious objection that it cripples the conditional, and state honestly where you think the residual cost is largest. The marking scheme should reward students who make and defend a similar commitment, even one different from yours, and penalize those who list options without choosing.
