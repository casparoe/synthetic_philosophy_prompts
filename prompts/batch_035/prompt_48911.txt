My mom passed away three weeks ago and I'm giving the eulogy on Saturday. She taught philosophy for thirty-some years, and the hill she chose to die on professionally — the thing she'd corner people about at Thanksgiving — was Strawson's Basic Argument. You probably know it: to be truly responsible for what you do, you'd have to be responsible for how you are — your character, your motives — but to be responsible for how you are, you'd have to have made yourself that way, and for that you'd have had to already be a certain way, and so on back, until you hit the fact that nobody is causa sui, nobody made themselves out of nothing. So ultimate moral responsibility is impossible, and she meant it — not as a metaphor, not as a "science says free will is an illusion" thing, she thought the argument worked on its own, from any starting point. She liked to say nobody had ever refuted it and nobody ever would, which made her a real treat at cocktail parties.

Here's my problem: a eulogy is basically one long act of desert-attribution. "She was the most generous person I've ever met." She would have just blinked at that — generosity was how she was made, luck all the way down, no more to her credit than being tall. She didn't think this made praise meaningless (she'd talk about forward-looking reasons, what praise does rather than what it's owed for), but the whole genre of eulogy runs on exactly the deep sense of desert she spent her career saying is incoherent, and she asked me, when it was clear the end was coming, not to "undo her at the finish line." Also complicating things: half the room will be her old colleagues who will catch every fudge, and the other half is family, including my brother, who's been mostly estranged from her and once heard her deploy the argument in what he took to be a self-serving way — so any line that sounds like "she couldn't help it" will land as a cheap excuse with him, even though it's technically true in her own system. I need comfort in that room, not a fight over whether mom was a robot.

So: write me the eulogy. It should be honest to her actual view (no sneaking ultimate desert back in through a side door), warm enough that non-philosophers feel it, and defensible in front of people who read the argument before breakfast. And she'd hate a eulogy that was just a clever dodge — if possible, I want it to end by saying something real about why we go on praising and blaming and loving people anyway, something the argument by itself can't give you. Around 700 words? And don't put in anything about her being at peace now or watching over us — she thought that whole picture was confused, and I'd rather not win the room with a lie.
