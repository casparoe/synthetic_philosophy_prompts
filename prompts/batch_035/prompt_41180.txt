At my dad's 60th birthday dinner someone toasted that he's "officially old now," and two of my friends, Dana and Marcus, spent the rest of the evening fighting about what that sentence even means. Dana has a linguistics background and Marcus read a stack of philosophy of language at some point, so this escalated fast, and I only took one semantics course in college so I couldnt referee. Here's the exchange as best I remember it, in order.

(1) Marcus: either dad is old or he isn't, and if he isn't old today but is old in twenty years, then there's a last day on which he isn't old, full stop. Vagueness is just ignorance — the sentence has a truth value and we can't know it, the same way nobody can know the exact number of beans in a sealed jar, and the jar doesn't become vague because of that. (2) Dana: that's absurd — "old" is a word we made up, it's meaning is fixed by how the community uses it, and usage simply doesn't settle whether a healthy 63-year-old is old. Where usage runs out there's no truth left over, because truth about our own words supervenes on use; no use, no fact. (3) Marcus: supervenience on use can't give her what she wants — there are only finitely many usage facts and infinitely many candidate cutoffs, so if any cutoff exists, use alone can't be what singles it out; and it's no mystery that we can't locate the boundary, because knowing where the line is would require reliably telling apart cases that differ by a single day, and nothing in our practices can do that, so the cutoff is unknowable without being unreal. (4) Dana: a "fact" that no possible evidence could ever bear on — not just for us, but for a hypothetical being with total access to the entire history of English usage — isn't a fact, it's a metaphysical ghost; and the bean jar comparison cheats, because the bean count is anchored in physical reality while "old" has nothing outside our practices to anchor a line in. (5) Marcus: fine, but her view smuggles in the same sharp lines one level up — "old" comes out borderline when it's true on some admissible sharpenings and false on others, which requires a sharp boundary between admissible and inadmissible sharpenings, and "admissible" is itself vague, so she's bought the ghost and relabeled it. Also, she says a borderline 63-year-old is not old and also not not-old, yet she insists "he is old or he is not old" is true, and Marcus wants to know what that truth is even made of if both disjuncts fail. (6) Dana: higher-order vagueness is a cost every theory pays — his cutoff is unknowable, and the claim that it's unknowable is itself fuzzy on his own view, so he has ghosts too; and "old or not old" is true in the only sense that matters, namely it comes out true under every admissible sharpening, which is exactly what you'd want from a logical truth. Neither of them budged, and by the end I honestly couldn't tell whether they were disagreeing about anything they'd ever act differently on.

Please adjudicate this move by move — for each of the six moves say whether it's basically right, basically wrong, or right only in part, and justify each rating. Then tell me who comes out ahead overall, and whether there's something both of them are missing entirely. Start your reply with your overall verdict in one sentence, no preamble, and don't restate the whole argument back to me before getting into it.
