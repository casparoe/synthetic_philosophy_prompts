Last weekend my oldest friend, a condensed-matter physicist with strong reductionist instincts, and I had a four-hour argument on a hike about what is ultimately real. It started when I mentioned I had finally repaid a debt of gratitude to someone who had helped me through a rough year, and he said that was a charming way of talking, but what had actually happened was that certain patterns of neurons in my head had nudged other patterns into new configurations; gratitude as such doesn't exist, he said — it's a useful shorthand for creatures too limited to track the underlying physics. When I objected, he laid out his position: whatever exists at the bottom level, the things made out of it — debts, promises, tables, storms, perhaps even animals — are just arrangements, and arrangements aren't additional realities. He even claimed my repayment of the kindness wasn't real in the strict sense; only the particles involved were.

I tried a couple of counterarguments. First, I said his "just" was doing illegitimate work: that everything is made of particles tells you what things are made of, not that only the particles are real. He replied that if the particle facts are complete — if a being who knew every particle fact would thereby know everything — then anything else is at best a bookkeeping device. Second, I tried turning the regress on him: aren't his particles themselves "just" excitations of quantum fields, and couldn't the fields in turn be "just" structure in something deeper? If each "just" move eliminates a level, why does the move stop being legitimate precisely at his favorite level? He answered that the chain has to stop somewhere — reality must have a bottom floor — and that whatever occupies that floor is what is genuinely real; the higher floors are like furniture in a house: present in a manner of speaking, but not load-bearing. We ended at an impasse over a question I still find genuinely puzzling: his picture says there are particles, and that they are arranged table-wise and promise-wise, and that tables and promises are in no way additional to those arrangements — and yet they somehow fail to be real. I can't decide whether that is a profound insight or a confusion about what "real" means.
