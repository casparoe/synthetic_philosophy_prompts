As part of a graduate seminar on the interpretation of probability, I have been hosting a year-long working group with two colleagues who have argued past each other for some time: one a committed frequentist in the long-run relative frequency tradition, who holds that probability statements are statements about frequencies in repetitions of a kind of trial and that a probability assigned to a single, unrepeatable event is either a shorthand for such a frequency statement or a confusion; the other a committed subjectivist in the de Finetti and Savage tradition, who holds that probabilities are coherent degrees of belief, that there is no further fact of the matter about a single-case probability beyond coherence, and that the frequentist's "repetitions" are themselves held together only by someone's judgment of exchangeability. I would like the document the two of them should have produced by now: the joint statement they could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration, with the residual disagreement made exact rather than papered over.

Please draft that joint statement with the following components, in this order:

1. An agreed-claims section, numbered, covering at minimum: the mathematical apparatus both accept; the claims both accept about the role of calibration in evaluating a forecaster who issues probabilities on unique events; what both accept about the difference between assigning a probability to a hypothesis and assigning a probability to data given a hypothesis; and whatever they can jointly say about the cases in which either framework, applied with care, yields the same practical recommendations.

2. A disagreements table, in which each remaining disagreement is stated twice — once in wording the frequentist would accept as a fair statement of her view, and once in wording the subjectivist would accept as a fair statement of his — and never in a form that either would reject as slanted.

3. A cruxes section: the sub-questions such that settling one of them would move at least one party, and, for each crux, a concrete case or piece of evidence both parties have agreed in advance would bear on it, with each party's prediction about that case recorded before it is examined. For this section, please use the two test cases they agreed to focus on, summarized here:

The Last Forecast. A regional weather station issued, as its final act before permanent decommissioning, a forecast of a 30% chance of rain for the following day, for a region in which no forecasts will ever be issued again and the station's archives have been sealed. The question they agreed to confront: is there any fact of the matter about whether 30% was the correct probability for that day — a fact that could in principle be discovered later even though no further forecasts or repetitions of that trial will ever occur — or is the correctness of the number assessable only against the forecaster's broader track record? Their agreed evidence bearing on this: a post-hoc audit of the sealed archive, which shows that across several thousand prior forecasts on unique events, rain followed the forecaster's 30% announcements almost exactly 30% of the time. Each party should record, in advance of the audit being described, what they take the audit to show or fail to show about the single final forecast, and whether it settles anything about the day itself.

The Discontinued Die. An artisan produced a single die using a novel and now permanently discontinued process; the die has not yet been rolled and never will be rolled more than once. Before the roll, a probabilist announces that the probability of a six is 1/6. The agreed question: what, if anything, makes that announcement correct or incorrect, given that there is no sequence of trials of this die and no ensemble of dice from this process? For this crux, record each party's account of what would have to be true for the announcement to be wrong, and whether either party can specify such a condition without appealing to a repetition that will never occur.

4. A closing section in which, if either party believes part of the dispute is merely verbal — for instance, that probability is univocal and one tradition has misdescribed it, or that the two traditions are assigning probabilities to different subjects and talking past each other — they say which part and why, in language the other would accept as a fair characterization of the diagnosis. If they jointly conclude that some portion of the disagreement is verbal and some is substantive, the document should say which portion is which.

A few constraints on the drafting: the agreed claims should be substantive, not platitudes — both parties should feel they gave something up; the disagreement formulations should be precise enough that a reader could tell, from the table alone, what would count as one side conceding; and the predictions in the cruxes section must be genuine predictions, written as they would have been recorded before the evidence was examined, not retrofitted to it. Please do not resolve the dispute for them: the deliverable is a document both could sign, not a verdict on who is right.
