At a recent gathering of my graduate set theory seminar, two fellow students got into a sustained argument about whether the continuum hypothesis has a determinate truth value, and since they could not settle it, they asked me to referee — but I found myself unable to, both because I could not always follow the technical claims and because each of them seemed to me to score real points along the way. Let me reconstruct the exchange as faithfully as I can, move by move.

Elena speaks of sets as though they were remote but ordinary objects. (1) She opened by claiming that Gödel's constructible universe and Cohen's forcing prove only that ZFC is incomplete about the size of the continuum, not that there is no fact of the matter. She drew an analogy to the parallel postulate: when it turned out to be independent of the other Euclidean axioms, the right lesson was not that there was no truth about geometry, but that a further fact — ultimately, the structure of physical space — was needed to settle which geometry holds. Set theory's independence results likewise call for better axioms, not resignation.

(2) Daniel, who calls himself a formalist about sets, answered that the analogy is self-undermining: geometry got settled by something external to the axioms, namely empirical space, and set theory has no external tribunal. All we have is a menu of equally consistent extensions — adopt the axiom of constructibility and CH is true; adopt Martin's axiom together with the negation of CH and it is false — and asking which is correct is like asking whether the one true group is abelian.

(3) Elena replied that the menu is not symmetric. The constructible universe L is an inner model: it sits inside V, and it satisfies CH. Making CH false requires instead enlarging the universe from the outside, adding reals the constructible hierarchy does not contain. The iterative conception of set says that V contains every collection that can in principle be formed, so the universe should be maximal rather than minimal — and the large cardinal axioms already testify that V strictly exceeds L. Once you take richness seriously, she argued, the reals will not be confined to a minimal count, and pressure builds toward CH failing.

(4) Daniel countered that large cardinals are precisely the wrong witness to summon: the large cardinal hierarchy is the most justified extension of ZFC we have, and it does not decide CH at all — by forcing, one can flip CH either way over models carrying large cardinals. Seventy years of the search for new axioms has, at exactly the question at issue, produced nothing but further menu items. That the large cardinals do settle other questions — projective determinacy, for instance — shows they are genuinely restrictive elsewhere, which makes their silence on CH all the more damning.

(5) Elena's reply was that the silence is local, not structural: the same axiom-finding program decided neighboring questions, such as whether all projective sets of reals are Lebesgue measurable (yes under large cardinals, no under V=L), and forcing axioms like the Proper Forcing Axiom, motivated by maximality considerations, settle the continuum at aleph-two. Daniel's closing move was that once one's preferred axioms are justified partly by which way one wants CH to come out, one has conceded his point: that is taste, not truth, and the multiverse conception — many universes of sets, CH true in some and false in others, with no single privileged V — is the honest terminus.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move, rating each of the five numbered moves on a scale from clearly right to clearly wrong and justifying each rating. Along the way, please flag any mathematical slips in the claims as stated (I could not fully check the inner-model and forcing claims myself). Then tell me who, if anyone, wins overall, and what, if anything, both of them are missing. Please write your response in American English.
