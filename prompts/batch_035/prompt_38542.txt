My friend and I spent an entire evening arguing about the color phi phenomenon — the illusion in which two spots flashing in different positions appear as a single moving spot that changes color partway through its journey, sometimes seeming to take on an intermediate shade (a smear of yellow between red and green, say) that was never presented — and about the classic dispute it is supposed to generate: the "Orwellian" view, on which consciousness happens fast and unrevised, so that the brain rewrites the record afterward and we misremember having seen the smear, versus the "Stalinesque" view, on which the brain buffers the inputs briefly and does the editing before anything is experienced, so that we genuinely did see it. We agreed on the phenomenology and, as far as either of us knows, on what the experiments show; but by midnight neither of us had moved an inch, and we realized we couldn't tell whether we were disagreeing about an empirical matter, about the reliability of immediate memory, or about nothing at all. My working hypothesis is that the whole dispute turns on whether there is a fact of the matter about the moment-by-moment micro-timeline of experience — about when the intermediate color was "had," not just reported — and that if you deny that any instant in the stream counts as the definitive final draft, the two views collapse into each other, making the disagreement at least partly verbal; my friend insists that a substantive residue remains, having to do with what is actually happening in the second or two after the flash when the subject confidently answers questions about what she saw. Diagnose this disagreement for me: is it verbal, substantive but empirically inaccessible with current methods, or a difference in what the two of us are treating as the thing to be explained? Spell out what each side is really committed to once the rhetoric of "editing" and "revising" is stripped away, and tell me honestly whether anything — an experiment probing the time course of report, a conceptual distinction, a formal model of temporal representation — could settle or dissolve it, or whether the question about the timing of experience is simply ill-posed. I am not looking for a survey of the literature; I want the structure of the disagreement itself.
