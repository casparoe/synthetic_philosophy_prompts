This fall I agreed to TA the midterm grading for an evening intro-ethics course at the local community college (long story, involves a friend who teaches it and my having argued with him about consequentialism on a forum until he decided to conscript me), and I'd appreciate a second pair of eyes on one answer before I commit to a grade. The course is mostly non-traditional students, and the question was designed by the instructor, not me, so I have no ego invested in the answer being good or bad — I just want to grade it accurately, and I don't fully trust my own judgment on the subtle stuff.

The exam question was: "Is the moral difference between doing harm and allowing harm a real difference, or only an apparent one? Discuss with reference to the bathtub cases, the trolley cases, and the contrast between turning a trolley and harvesting a transplant patient's organs. Conclude by saying whether the doing/allowing distinction can bear the weight that the traditional medical distinction between active and passive euthanasia places on it."

And here is the student's answer, transcribed from the blue book: "The bathtub cases are designed so that everything is the same except whether the harm is done or allowed. Smith drowns his cousin in the bathtub; Jones, planning to drown him, instead lets him slip under and watches while doing nothing. Since the two men are equally malicious and the outcomes are identical, the cases show that killing and letting die are equally wrong in every case, and Rachels concludes that the doctrine holding otherwise is simply refuted. This is also the conclusion of the doctrine of double effect, which says the same thing about intending versus foreseeing. Turning to the trolley: Foot's bystander at the switch is permitted to divert the runaway trolley from five people toward one because the threat was already heading toward the five, so the bystander only allows the one to die rather than doing him harm — the same reasoning that permits the driver in the original case. The surgeon who harvests the one patient's organs, by contrast, does harm, which is why he may not proceed even to save five. So the trolley literature confirms the bathtub result. Still, something is right about the distinction's pull: we blame people more for harms they bring about than for harms they fail to prevent, so negative duties seem more stringent than positive ones, and that asymmetry explains our intuitions without the doing/allowing distinction itself carrying any moral weight. On euthanasia, then, the distinction between withdrawing treatment and administering a lethal injection cannot justify treating the two differently — doctors should be judged by the same standard in both cases."

I want to be able to attach your comments to my own and feel confident that I'm marking the student for what they actually wrote, not for the version of it I expected to read. If you think a competent grader could reasonably disagree about the grade, say where the slack is and which way it cuts.
