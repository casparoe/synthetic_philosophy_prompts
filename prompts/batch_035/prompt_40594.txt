I've been cataloguing my gut reactions to cases where one being's utility must be weighed against others', and I want to know whether any principle captures them. Nozick's utility monster, who converts every resource into vastly more well-being than anyone else, should not get everything: the rest of us keep a share. But a person who is merely temperamentally, say, thirty percent better at converting resources into well-being — I'm comfortable giving them a somewhat larger share, and my objection to the monster seems to be about scale, not the idea itself. Meanwhile a person born with a low hedonic baseline, who extracts less well-being from anything we give them, seems to me to deserve an equal or even priority share, which is in some tension with the thirty-percent case. A mind — engineered or naturally evolved — with a vastly wider hedonic range seems to me to count for more per experience, but not millions of times more; its ecstasies don't outweigh a city's ordinary satisfactions, and I can't say where the discounting kicks in. Within my own life I would gladly trade many pleasant years for a few ecstatic ones, but I would not impose that trade between persons. And I balk harder at an engineered monster than at a naturally occurring one, though intensity in an ordinary child who just happens to feel everything keenly moves me in favor of favoring them. Is there one principle that saves all or most of these verdicts, or must some go? If you can state one, tell me what it implies for a case I haven't listed: an uploaded person running at high clock speed who claims correspondingly vast total well-being.
