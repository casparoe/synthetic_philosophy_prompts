I've ended up on the board of a small regional charity that funds health stuff -- mostly unglamorous things like hearing aids, home dialysis support, respite care -- and this year we're finally trying to write down an actual allocation policy instead of deciding by vibes and whoever speaks loudest at the meeting. I've talked myself into prioritarianism as the backbone of it: benefits count for more the worse off the recipient is, and this is supposed to hold independently of any comparison between people, which is the part I like. I used to lean egalitarian, but the leveling-down objection wrecked that for me -- the thought that a situation where the worst off stay exactly as bad off and nobody gains anything could still count as in one respect better just seemed like a reductio of the whole view -- and prioritarianism looked like it gave me what I actually wanted (special concern for the worst off) without paying that price. My partner thinks I'm overthinking a charity that mostly buys hearing aids, but there's real money and real decisions riding on this now, so I want to stress-test the view before we enshrine it in policy.

Tell me what would actually count against it. Not just the standard complaints from critics, but the things its own defenders would wince and concede: I gather there's a live issue about where the priority weights even come from and whether any answer is available that doesn't amount to "we made them up," and something about cases where a risk is imposed on a single person over time, where I've heard prioritarianism comes apart from both the utilitarian and the egalitarian answer, and in the direction I find least intuitive. There's also a question I keep chewing on that I haven't seen adressed head-on anywhere: should the priority attach to how badly off someone's whole life is going, or just to their current state? The widow whose had a comfortable life but is in a horrific year vs. the person whose whole life has been one long slog -- on our board this matters concretely, and I honestly don't know what the view is commited to. And I want you to be blunt about whether the view gets insulated into unfalsifiability when defenders retreat to "it's a claim about what's good, not about what anyone owes anyone" -- is that a principled distinction or a bunker? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me which of them should actually change the wording of an allocation policy and which are just philosophy-seminar problems.
