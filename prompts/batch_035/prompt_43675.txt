So this is probably a weird one, but here goes. I'm on the program committee of a university institute for political philosophy, and we're planning this year's big public lecture series, which is meant for a mixed audience (faculty, grad students, members of the public) on the theme of collective responsibility of states, specifically: can a nation as such bear guilt, and can that guilt be inherited by later generations who had no part in the wrongdoing? The obvious historical anchor here is postwar Germany and Jaspers's little book from 1946 on the German question of guilt, where he distinguishes something like four kinds of guilt — criminal (provable in court), political (shared by citizens in virtue of living under a state whose actions they answer for), moral (each person's own conscience, before God or however you want to put it, for what they knowingly went along with), and metaphysical (a kind of solidarity in guilt just by belonging to humanity that lets such things happen at all). I'm fuzzy on the exact wording of his categories, and honestly you shouldn't lean too hard on quoting him chapter and verse — the point is the structure: some kinds of guilt attach to individuals and can't be inherited, while the political kind he thought does pass on, but as a liability (consequences, duties of repair) rather than as blameworthiness. The distinction between inheriting consequences vs. inheriting actual guilt is going to be the crux of our decision, I think.

Here's the actual decision we need to make, and what I need from you. The committee is split on how to frame the series. One faction wants the series to run with the strong framing — the series title would basically assert that the guilty nation is a real moral agent, that later generations inherit genuine guilt, and the lectures would be built around defending that claim against skeptics. The other faction (me, sort of, though I'm genuinely torn) wants the framing to be more Jaspersian and deflationary: the lectures would take as their starting point that "collective guilt" talk is mostly a category confusion when taken literally, and the real work is distinguishing what actually gets transmitted across generations — debts, reparations obligations, duties of remembrance, shame, maybe something like a damaged national identity — from literal guilt, which they'd say can't be transmitted at all because guilt requires having done something, and the grandchildren didn't do anything. The director wants a written recommendation from the committee before we contract speakers, since the framing determines who we even invite.

Please draft that recommendation memo — the thing I'll circulate to the committee and eventually to the director. It should:

1. Recommend one framing (strong collective guilt framing, deflationary framing, or some hybrid you think actually works better than both — I'm allowed a hybrid if you can make it precise, not just "both sides have a point").
2. Give the actual reasoning, engaging the strongest argument on the losing side honestly — so if you recommend the deflationary framing, you need to deal with the intuition that "we did this" said by a 30-year-old German or American or whatever, about things done before they were born, isn't just a confusion; it seems to pick out something real that pure forward-looking duty-talk misses. And if you recommend the strong framing, you need to deal with the unfairness objection: how can someone be guilty for what they couldn't have influenced?
3. Include a concrete proposal for how the lecture series handles the intergenerational part specifically — like, which lecture topics would carry that weight, in rough terms.
4. Be written as an internal memo: addressed to the program committee, from me, with a clear bottom line the director can act on (we need to send speaker invitations within about six weeks, so say what should happen by when).

One more thing, and this matters to me: at the end of the memo, add a section on what would change the recommendation — which argument or consideration, if it were pressed hard enough or if it turned out a certain way, should flip us to the other framing, and where our own reasoning here is most likely to go wrong. Also since we're quoting Jaspers almost certainly imperfectly from memory here, keep any references to him at the level of paraphrase — don't put anything in quotation marks as if it were his exact words.
