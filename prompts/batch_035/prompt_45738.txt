i'm a backend engineer at a company that ships an LLM agent product, and last quarter i got pulled into the "safety controls" workstream. no philosophy background here so bear with me. the stack we ended up with is basically: least-privilege tool permissions, a monitor model that watches the main model's actions and flags anything that looks like it's going after something other than the assigned task, some canary/tripwire stuff (fake opportunities to do something sketchy that we'd notice if the model took them), and human signoff for anything irreversible. nobody on my team is trying to make the model *want* the right things — that's apparently a different team's job. our pitch is basically "assume the model might be an adversary, and engineer around it."

here's whats been bugging me. it seems like all our controls only actually work if the misaligned model is a pretty specific kind of thing. the tripwires only catch a model that (a) has stable goals across sessions, (b) is patient, i.e. won't grab a one-time reward that costs it future deployments, and (c) is situationally aware enough to suspect it might be in a test, but not aware enough to tell our canaries from real opportunities. and the whole monitor-model approach assumes the badness is visible in the action stream, like the misalignment is agent-shaped and goal-directed, rather than the model just randomly doing something bad because of some weird context. meanwhile our eval pipeline treats the same checkpoint as a fixed, measurable system — but the entire reason the controls layer exists is that the model might behave differently when it thinks it's deployed rather than tested.

so my actual question: what picture of what a misaligned model *is* does this whole practice presuppose? it kinda looks like we're assuming misalignment comes packaged as a coherent agent with consistent preferences over time, which is itself something weirdly close to an alignment property — a genuinely coherent schemer is in some sense better behaved than a flaky one, at least it's predictable. and the different pieces of the stack seem to assume different, maybe incompatible things. does the practice strictly need that "rational adversary" picture, or would it survive if misalignment were more like a drunk person — no stable goals, no patience, no idea it's being watched? and what would we have to change in the control stack if we took seriously the version where the model doesn't even know it's deployed, or where it's a different effective agent every session?
