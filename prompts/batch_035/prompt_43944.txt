Retired geophysicist here, spent most of my career modeling contaminant transport and only lately got into the philosophy/economics side of things, so forgive me if this is something every grad student learns in week one. Over thanksgiving my nephew, who works at one of those trading firms, and I made a small bet about whether a rocket launch would slip to the next window. I offered him 2:1, he took it instantly, and afterwards I couldn't stop thinking about the fact that the speed and eagerness of his acceptance was itself information — if he'd hemmed and hawed I'd have believed my side more, if he'd tried to negotiate better odds I'd have believed it less, and so by the time the bet is actually struck I've already partly updated toward his view, and he's already updated toward mine based on what I was willing to offer. It felt like the bet was dissolving as it was being formed.

Now generalize that. If two people each treat the other's willingness to trade as evidence (and they should, since nobody bets for free), and both keep updating until their views meet, you'd think they'd converge to the point where no odds make both sides happy to accept — the trade vanishes in the act of agreeing to it. Any trade that survives should be because the two sides want different things from it, like insurance, where I want to be protected from a bad outcome and the insurer wants the premium and neither of us claims to know something the other doesn't. And yet the exchanges clear billions of shares a day, and I gather a huge fraction of it is exactly the kind of trade where both parties think they're the one who knows better, and both of them know the counterparty is out there and presumably informed too. So either the updating I'm imagining doesn't or can't happen, or the speculators are trading with somebody other than the idealized versions of each other.

I know the phrase "efficient markets" and I know about the winner's curse in auctions, which feels like one instance of this same mechanism, but neither seems to be the thing itself. So: does the general claim — that between agents who take each other's willingness to trade as evidence, speculative trade in the strict sense should dry up, leaving only hedging and liquidity-type trades — have an established name in the literature? And if it does, I don't just want the label, I want the carving: what exactly the claim assumes about the agents, which kinds of real-world trade survive it and why (I have my own guesses — different needs, different priors that people refuse to give up, plain noise — but I'd like to know how the field actually sorts all that), and whether the people who study this treat it as a description of markets, an idealization they know is false, or more of an epistemological point about what disagreement between rational agents even amounts to.
