Is there a name for this? I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums since the Overcoming Bias days, and somewhere along the way I started noticing a certain move that keeps deciding puzzles and arguments. I want to know whether the move itself has an established name and a literature.

One instance: I once spent a whole evening on a string-rewriting puzzle — an initial string, a handful of rewrite rules, a target string, derive the target. The trick, it turned out, was that the target was underivable, and the way to see that was to stop applying the rules and ask instead what they preserve: the rules happened to conserve a certain arithmetic property of the string (a count of one of the letters, modulo 3), the start string had one value of that property and the target needed another, so no sequence of moves could ever get there. The insight came not from being better at the game but from stepping outside it. A second instance: debates that only end when someone stops contesting the object-level question and instead attacks the shared framework assumption that generated the dispute in the first place. And a third is the mirror image of the first: sometimes the outside look shows you not that the task is impossible but that you're using the wrong representation, and the winning move is to change the rules you work under, not to follow them more cleverly.

So: does this general move — leaving the system in which a problem is posed, either to show it has no in-system solution or to replace the system itself — have a name? The nearest landmarks I know don't fit: Gödel's theorems are results about formal systems, not a technique for problem-solvers, and the "outside view" from the forecasting literature is about reference classes, not formal systems. I also have a nagging feeling that somewhere in the mathematical-recreations tradition there's a coined term for exactly this, some strained acronym, but I can't place it. If such a term exists, I'd want to know what it covers, because I can't decide whether my first instance genuinely counts: finding the invariant meant abandoning the puzzle's rules, but I was still doing ordinary mathematics, just within a wider system — was that a jump out of the system, or only a jump into a bigger one? A bare label won't settle that; I'm after the carving.
