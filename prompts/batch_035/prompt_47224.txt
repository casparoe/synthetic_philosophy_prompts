I am an undergraduate enrolled in a seminar this term titled "Collapse and Transformation in Pre-Modern Societies," which ranges over the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, the Terminal Classic Maya lowlands, and Rapa Nui. Over the semester the class has hardened into two camps, and our instructor has asked pairs of us to attempt a written exercise modeled on adversarial collaboration. My assigned partner holds the opposite view from mine, and I need to draft the joint document we could both put our names to before the final session.

The two camps are as follows. The "failure" camp holds that collapse is a genuine and catastrophic phenomenon: the rapid, involuntary loss of population, political centralization, specialized production, and literacy or administrative capacity, typically triggered by the intersection of environmental stress, warfare, and brittleness in highly optimized systems. The "transformation" camp holds that most cases labeled collapse are better described as reorganization: populations persist, identities persist, and what actually ends is a particular configuration of elite institutions — with the drama of the "collapse" narrative owing a great deal to a record-bias problem, since the archaeological signal for monumental construction, palace economies, and written administration is far more durable and visible than the signal for village-level life, so when a state dissolves its people may simply move into activities that archaeology is bad at seeing.

Our document must not be a debate recap, and it must not be a bland compromise. Please draft it with the following components. First, a numbered section of claims that both camps can sign without squirming — including, I suspect, agreement that "collapse" as commonly used conflates at least three distinct things (the fate of a political apparatus, the fate of a population, and the fate of a cultural tradition), and that the camps differ at least as much about how these are weighted as about what happened on the ground. Second, a disagreements table: each residual disagreement stated in wording that the camp holding that view would accept as fair to itself, with the rival camp's one-sentence gloss alongside. Third, a cruxes section. The ones I have identified so far, which you may revise or supplement, are: (a) the counterfactual baseline — is the relevant comparison the society at its complexity peak, or the trajectory a non-collapsed regional society would have followed; (b) the record-bias question — how much of the apparent sharpness of a collapse is an artifact of which activities leave durable traces, versus a real discontinuity in lived welfare; and (c) voluntariness — at what point does settlement dispersal and abandonment of monumental investment stop counting as adaptation and start counting as flight under duress. Fourth, for each crux, specify at least one class of evidence that both camps in the seminar have already conceded is probative — paleodemographic estimates, lake sediment cores for land-use intensity, settlement-survey data showing continuity versus dispersal, evidence about the timing of depopulation relative to foreign contact — and write down in advance what each camp should predict if its view is correct, so that the predictions are on record before the evidence is examined. Finally, a short closing note flagging any disagreement that the two of us have concluded is verbal rather than substantive, with an explanation of what the words were doing to keep us apart.

Please write the document as the polished text of the joint statement itself, with section headings as specified above, followed by a brief signature block with a date. Keep the tone scholarly and mutually respectful: neither camp is permitted to describe the other's position as a mere moral preference about the past.
