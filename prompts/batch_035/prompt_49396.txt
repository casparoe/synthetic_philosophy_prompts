So I've been going around and around on this thing called the raven paradox on my own and I can't tell if I'm stuck because it's genuinely hard or because it isn't a real question. Here's the setup as I understand it: seeing a black raven is supposed to count as evidence that all ravens are black. Fine. But "all ravens are black" is logically the same claim as "all non-black things are non-ravens." And a white shoe is a non-black thing that's a non-raven. So a white shoe confirms that all ravens are black?? Everyone agrees that sounds insane, but nobody agrees on where the trick is, and from what I can tell this has been argued about since the 1940s and people are still writing papers where they basically say the same three or four things to each other.

The moves I keep running into are: (a) yeah, actually the white shoe does confirm it a tiny tiny bit, get over it — the confirmation is so small it doesn't matter in practice; (b) it depends on what else you know — if you know roughly how many ravens there are versus how many non-black things in the world, checking a shoe and finding it's not a raven means almost nothing, but if you somehow knew there was exactly one non-black thing in the universe, checking it and finding a shoe would tell you a lot, so maybe the paradox only works because we're sneaking background knowledge in and then pretending we don't have any; (c) something is wrong with treating logically equivalent claims as getting the same evidence, which is uncomfortable because that equivalence feels like it should be untouchable; and (d) the whole thing turns on an ambiguity in the word "confirms" — like maybe a fact making your belief stronger is different from that fact being a good test of the belief, and the two camps are talking past each other.

My own pet theory, which I admit I might be in love with, is that it's mostly (d) plus a hidden disagreement about whether "evidence for" means how much a finding should move your belief, or which observations were worth going out and collecting. The tiny-bit people and the shoe-proves-nothing people aren't really disagreeing about shoes, they're answering different questions, and once you split the questions both answers come out kind of boring.

So what I want is a diagnosis of this arguement, not a recap of the positions. Is it verbal, is one side just wrong, or is there a hidden assumption about the size of the world doing all the work? Where exactly does each side think the other cheated? And be honest with me — is my (d) theory actually right, or is it one of those "it's just a disagreement about words" cop-outs that saves you from having to think? Also tell me what would settle it: is there some case or distinction or example someone could construct that would make one camp give up, or is this thing going to be re-argued in 2090 exactly like it is now? I'd rather hear it's unfixable and why than get a fake resolution.
