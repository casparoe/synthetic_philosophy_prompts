I'm writing an extended essay for school on whether images produced by artificial intelligence can really count as art, and I'd like to use Tolstoy's What Is Art? as my main lens. My understanding is that he judged art by whether it transmits a feeling the artist has genuinely experienced to the audience, and that he was scathing about polished, technically dazzling works made for the amusement of cultivated people rather than for ordinary ones, which he treated as counterfeits of art.

This leaves me with a puzzle. On the one hand, a text-to-image model has no feelings and has experienced nothing, so it seems it could never be sincere in his sense — perhaps the worst counterfeit imaginable, produced without even a counterfeiter's emotion behind it. On the other hand, plenty of ordinary people report being genuinely moved by AI-generated pictures, and Tolstoy appears to make the test the effect on the receiver rather than the facility of the maker, which suggests that whatever infects the audience is art regardless of how it was made. Where would he actually come down? Would it matter to him that a human prompted the machine — perhaps a human who genuinely felt something and used the model to express it — and would his verdict change for someone who types a few words and accepts the first output untouched?

A second question, which my teacher encouraged me to consider: Tolstoy thought the merit of art should be settled by the shared judgement of everyone, not by professional critics or experts. Does that mean he would approve of the way algorithmic feeds now decide what art the public sees, rewarding whatever attracts the most engagement? Or is mass taste shaped by optimisation for attention precisely the corruption of popular judgement he feared?

I should say that I won't be satisfied with a survey of possibilities — please argue for the single most probable Tolstoy on each question, drawing on the commitments he actually held, and tell me where his views would leave genuine room for doubt.
