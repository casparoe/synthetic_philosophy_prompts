I teach an intro metaphysics course and next week is our session on the special composition question -- you know, the van Inwagen thing of "when do several things compose one further thing?" I want a decision-tree handout, one page-ish, that starts students at a commitment basically everyone in the room will grant (they all believe in at least some composite objects when they walk in, or at least they think they do) and walks them branch by branch down to a named position on the SCQ. Think of yourself as a professional metaphysician who's taught this material for years and knows exactly where students wobble.

Some requirements so this doesn't turn into a wikipedia dump:

1. Every branch node should be a question a student can honestly answer before they know any of the labels -- test them on cases, not vocabulary. So instead of "are you a universalist?", something like: if the atoms in this room were arranged in exactly the same pattern but nothing was alive, would there still be a big object made of them? The cases I want worked in somewhere: the statue and the lump of clay, the trout-turkey (front half of a trout fused to the back half of a turkey), a heap of scattered pebbles on a hillside, a sentence token, and a living person. Use these or better ones, but cases like these, not just abstract questions.

2. The leaves need to include at least: plain nihilism, universalism, the view that only living things compose (van Inwagen-style), organicism's cousins among the restrictivisms, brutal composition, and -- this is the part my old handout always botched -- an exit for the deflationist/verbalist student, the one who wants to say the whole fight is verbal and there's no language-independent fact about how many objects are in the room. There's always one of those, and I want the tree to have a legitimate path for them rather than a dead end.

3. After the tree, I want a short section flagging where the tree fails: which pairs of positions it can't actually seperate (e.g., can any honest answer to your nodes distinguish universalism-plus-composition-as-identity from plain universalism? I suspect not, but you tell me), and which leaf-combinations end up unoccupied -- combinations of answers nobody in the literature holds -- with one sentence on whether that's because the view is incoherent, just ugly, or merely unexplored. The empty branches are honestly the most interesting part for my purposes.

4. Build the tree from the logical space, not from a list of famous names -- figure out the real questions and the answer combinations they generate first, then locate the actual positions in that space. Also please start with some actual brainstorming before you commit to a tree structure, and think carefully about the node ordering, since a badly ordered node can dump a student in the wrong leaf even when their intuitions are consistent.

Keep the language undergraduate-friendly, no more than about ten leaves total, and make the whole thing something I could actually print and hand out. If you think one of my case requirements is doing more harm than good, say so and explain, don't just quietly ignore me.
