My friend Dahlia is a cultural psychologist and insufferably opinionated about guilt vs. shame — her official line is that guilt is the better moral emotion and shame is mostly medieval residue that cultures should grow out of. But I've been collecting her verdicts about which response is "fitting" for people in various situations, and I can't for the life of me figure out the rule she's running. Case files so far: someone who secretly reads their partner's journal, and the partner never finds out — guilt. A guy who trips and dumps red wine down the host's dress at a dinner party while everyone watches — shame, and she's adament that guilt would be misplaced there since nothing wrong was done. Someone who tears into a cashier in public and later regrets it — guilt, because "someone was wronged." And a woman whose years-long lying about money finally wrecks her marriage — Dahlia says the fitting response there is shame, not guilt, because what haunts the woman isn't the individual acts anymore, it's what they made her. Which is a weird verdict for someone who claims guilt is the superior emotion, but whatever, she's consistent about it.

We're doing a predictions-over-dinner thing next week and I've got the new case ready: a normally careful driver, totally out of character, chooses to glance at a text and hits a cyclist, leaving permanent damage that no apology or payment can undo. One-off freely chosen act, harm that can never be repaired. Predict her verdict — but honestly I care less about the answer than about the rule. I can see at least two candidate rules that both fit her track record so far: something like "guilt for what can still be repaired, shame for what can't" versus "guilt for single acts you chose, shame for what the acts reveal you to be" — and maybe others I haven't thought of. Tell me what each candidate predicts for the cyclist case, give me your best guess with actual reasons, and tell me what further case would split the surviving candidates apart (I already have to bring a tiebreaker).

Bonus round, because she will absolutely bring it up: Dahlia also claims that a whole culture can more or less choose its guilt/shame mix, and that her verdicts somehow support this. Does the pattern she's running actually suggest anything about whether cultures can steer between the two emotions — like, if the rule is about repairability, is that even the kind of thing a culture can choose to emphasize — or is she overreaching from her own case judgments to a claim about entire societies?
