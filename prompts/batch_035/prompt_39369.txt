I'm drafting a short philosophical essay and would like your comments on it. The topic is whether death is bad for the one who dies. My thesis: the standard anti-Epicurean move — the deprivation account, on which death is bad because it deprives you of goods you would otherwise have enjoyed — collapses if one accepts tranquilism, the view associated with some suffering-focused ethicists, that only the absence of suffering has intrinsic value, with positive experiences counting as valuable only instrumentally, as relief from or protection against future suffering. If nothing is intrinsically good except the absence of suffering, then death deprives you of nothing intrinsically good; at worst it forecloses the relief of suffering you would otherwise have received, and if it also forecloses future suffering, it may be prudentially better than continued life.

My notes so far, in the order I expect the sections to run: (a) the Epicurean argument that death is not bad for the person who dies, on the ground that there is no time at which both the person and death coexist; (b) the Lucretian symmetry between pre-natal and posthumous nonexistence, and the question of whether that symmetry does any work once the deprivation account has been rejected; (c) the deprivationist's best reply I can construct — that the badness of death must be located in goods lost by an existing subject — together with my argument that tranquilism undercuts it; and (d) the unwelcome implication for the tranquilist: a death that is painless and forecloses only suffering looks prudentially better than a life containing any net suffering, which seems to commit tranquilism to something close to the notorious negative-utilitarian conclusion about painless extinction.

Please comment on three things specifically. First, is the core inference valid: am I entitled to conclude that death is not bad for you, or have I conflated that with the stronger claim that death is positively good, and does the distinction matter for section (d)? Second, given my thesis, is section (b) now dead weight that I should cut, or does the symmetry argument do independent work against remaining deprivationist replies? Third, would the essay as sketched be interesting to someone who already knows the Epicurean debate, or is it just a recombination? Please be blunt about the weakest point in the draft. Respond in American English.
