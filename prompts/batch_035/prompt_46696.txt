I'm a retired schoolteacher with no academic background, and for the past few years I've been working through, entirely on my own, the running quarrel between famous physicists and philosophers of science. Next month I'm presenting to my local philosophy discussion group: about fifteen curious laypeople, plus a retired particle physicist who takes Weinberg's side and a doctoral student in philosophy of science who works on scientific realism.

My thesis is that the physicists' complaint, from Weinberg's chapter titled "Against Philosophy" onward, really lands on only one kind of philosophy: rules that forbid, like the positivist distrust of unobservable entities, which I gather held back quantum field theory for a while. The kind of philosophy that actually guides physicists — an ideal of simple, symmetrical, beautiful laws — is one they practice constantly without acknowledging it. While researching I also found, to my surprise, that the famous quip comparing philosophy of science to ornithology was never securely Feynman's: Weinberg reported it anonymously in the 1980s, and it appears to echo an older line about aesthetics and artists. I think this supports my point, since the slogan targets the forbidding kind of philosophy and leaves the inspiring kind untouched.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections this specific audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they could do, each phrased the way the retired physicist or the doctoral student would put it, each with a short reply I could give in two minutes, a note on where that reply is soft, what I should concede up front, and the one question I'm hoping nobody asks.
