I'm the patient-safety officer at a mid-sized regional hospital, and part of my job is running root-cause analyses when something goes wrong for a patient — a medication error, a missed deterioration, a delayed escalation. Recently I had a long and oddly consequential argument with a friend who had agreed to water my plants while I was away and didn't, then insisted on philosophical grounds that he wasn't responsible for killing them, because not doing something isn't doing anything: an absence can't be a cause. We ended up arguing about the case where a person collapses on a crowded street and a hundred bystanders all fail to help: it seemed crazy to say all hundred caused the death, but also crazy to say nobody did, and my friend's move — pick the nearest non-helper by some rule — struck both of us as arbitrary. It was only afterward that I realized I face versions of this every month at work: our incident reports constantly assert things like the omission of the allergy check caused the reaction, or the failure to escalate overnight caused the arrest, and whether we write those sentences shapes which recommendations get made and which staff get named in the record.

I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher specializing in the metaphysics of causation, and specifically in the literature on causation by omission — the counterfactual approach and its failure in the many-omissions case, the norm-based solution associated with Honoré and Michael Moore under which an omission counts as a cause only if it violated a duty, and the known objections to that solution. Please think carefully and start by briefly brainstorming, before writing the procedure itself: what the commission/omission distinction actually turns on, what the profligate-omissions problem is, what the norms solution fixes and where it misfires (for instance when the purpose at hand is prevention rather than blame, or when the duty itself is contestable), and which of these distinctions must survive translation into a hospital procedure and which can be dropped. Then write the procedure.

The procedure itself should be something a small RCA team — nurses, a pharmacist, me, none of us philosophers — can run in about twenty minutes per incident, before any report is drafted. It should consist of an ordered set of questions about a candidate omission, with guidance on what counts as a sufficient answer to each, a clear statement of where the procedure returns a verdict of cause, contributor, or not-a-cause, and an explicit marking of the points where judgment cannot be eliminated — in particular where the question stops being metaphysics and becomes a value question about whose norms we are using and for what purpose, since our reports feed both prevention work and, sometimes, personnel decisions, and I suspect those two uses want different answers. Please also state honestly the known ways the procedure will misfire, including the worry that by folding norms in we make causation depend on facts about our own rulebook rather than about the world.

Then apply the procedure, worked through step by step, to three cases from recent months, all with identifying details changed:

1. A nurse administered a drug to a patient with a documented allergy. The allergy flag was in the electronic record, but the alert fired only on the ordering screen, which the nurse didn't open; she followed unit protocol as written. The candidate omission is her failure to check the allergy list herself, which unit policy describes as expected but not enforced.

2. A patient deteriorated overnight and was found unresponsive at 6 a.m. The covering resident who was formally assigned to the unit did not round on this patient, but neither did three other residents whose service agreements arguably covered the patient — our staffing rules are genuinely ambiguous here, and the family has asked us directly who caused this.

3. A patient's spouse, when asked at admission about home medications, did not mention a daily anticoagulant because she assumed the hospital already had it on file. The omission plausibly caused a serious interaction.

For the third case especially I expect the procedure to give an answer I don't like, or at least don't know how to use — please say so plainly rather than smoothing it over, and tell me what the procedure can and cannot settle for each of the three.
