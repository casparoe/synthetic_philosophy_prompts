My best friend and I spent most of a long weekend arguing about the origins of the First World War, and we've deadlocked in a way I'd like you to break with actual numbers. The proposition: the standard telling of the July Crisis — the assassination at Sarajevo channeled through the alliance commitments, the blank cheque, and the mobilization timetables into, more or less mechanically, a general European war — substantially overstates how inevitable the war was, because historians writing after 1918 know the ending and cannot help narrating the crisis as though everyone in it was already on rails. My friend thinks the "sleepwalkers" style of revisionism is itself a fashion, and that whatever it gets right, the deeper point stands: given the naval race, the Moroccan crises, the German war council of December 1912 (whose significance I gather has been heavily contested, though I can't remember by whom), and the mobilization schedules, a general war was close to overdetermined, and a competent narrator would converge on the same explanation even with no knowledge of the outcome. I hold roughly the opposite: the pre-1914 world was full of last-minute defusals, and the sense of inevitability is a hindsight artifact — selection on the dependent variable, dressed up as historiography.

Give me your own credence that the standard narrative substantially overstates the war's inevitability, but, more importantly, decompose it. I'd want at least these terms: your probability that a maximally well-informed observer sitting in, say, June 1914 would have assigned high probability to a general European war within five years; your probability that the July Crisis was genuinely a close call, in the sense that identifiable small choices — Grey's mediation proposals, a Russian decision to mobilize against Austria alone, an earlier and clearer British warning to Berlin — could plausibly have kept the war local; your probability that the narrative-fallacy mechanism specifically (narrators building a coherent causal arc because they know the ending) is doing real work in the historiography, as against the arc simply being true; and any further terms you think the decomposition needs, each with a rough probability and one line of justification. Then combine them in whatever way you think honest rather than mechanical, tell me which single term the final number is most sensitive to, and say what evidence would move that term most — for instance, whether an objective base rate of European crises that did not end in general war, estimated from the preceding decades, would help, and what it would show.

If you think my framing rigs the question — for example, that "inevitability" and "overdetermination" need to be pried apart before any probability over this proposition makes sense, or that the hindsight bias here operates as much on us, arguing about which historians were biased, as on the historians themselves — then say so, adjust the proposition, and re-derive. My own credence is somewhere around 0.7 in favor of the overstatement claim; my friend's is closer to 0.25.
