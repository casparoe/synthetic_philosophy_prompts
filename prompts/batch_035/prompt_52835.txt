My housemate Priya and I have been going around in circles on this since a political theory seminar where we read about the Athenian boule, and now she's basically got me half convinced that we should be picking judges out of a hat, which can't be right but I keep losing the argument. Help me find where the chain snaps. It goes:

(1) Political equality, the core of democracy, requires that no citizen's political input be systematically privileged over any other citizen's. (2) Elections privilege exactly the wrong people: the wealthy, the famous, the well-connected, the eloquent — they're the ones who can realistically hold office, and everyone else is reduced to choosing among them. (3) A lottery privileges nobody, since each citizen's chance of holding office is exactly equal no matter how rich, connected, or silver-tongued they are. (4) So selecting officials by lot is more democratic than selecting them by election — which, incidentally, seems to be how Aristotle saw it, he treats lot as the democratic method and election as the oligarchic one. (5) If that's right, and if democracy is the thing we care about, then we should prefer lotteries to elections for every public office where drawing names is administratively feasible — there's nothing special about judges, cabinet ministers, or central bankers that makes elections uniquely appropriate for them and nothing else. (6) So we should be selecting judges, ministers, and central bankers by lot. (7) But that's obviously insane, at least for some of those offices.

So one of these steps is rotten and I can't settle on which. When I try to reject (5), Priya asks what the principled difference is between an office where lottery-selection is fine and one where it isn't, and I flail. When I try to reject (3), she points out that "equal chance" sure sounds like the purest form of nobody being privileged, and I flail again. The one thing I'm sure of is I want to keep (7), and honestly I'd like to keep (1) too since it just sounds like what democracy is. Tell me which step to give up, why it looked fine when it was sitting there in the list, and what it costs to drop it — especially if the cost is admitting that something I want to call undemocratic (elections) isn't, or that something I want to call obviously crazy (a randomly chosen central banker) isn't either.
