"The dollar auction: a dollar bill is auctioned to the highest bidder, but the second-highest bidder must also pay their final bid and gets nothing. (a) With the two live bids at 85 and 90 cents, explain why the player who is behind does better to keep raising than to quit, and say how far up this rational raising actually goes. (b) Given that, where is the real mistake in this game made? (c) How does the game relate to the sunk-cost fallacy and to Staw's escalation-of-commitment findings — do the game-theoretic analysis and the psychology tell the same story?"

The student wrote:

"(a) The player at 85 has two options. If he quits, he's out his whole 85 cents. If he raises to 95, the leader has to either fold and lose 90 cents, or else bid $1.05 for a dollar — and nobody rationally pays more than the prize is worth, so he folds. The follower then takes the dollar for 95, a 5 cent profit instead of an 85 cent loss. The general principle is that quitting means losing everything you've bid so far, while overbidding means that if you end up winning, you lose only your bid minus a dollar, which is always less. So rational raising goes up to roughly the dollar and then stops — around $1.05 nobody sensibly continues, the leader just takes his 90 cent loss rather than throw good money after bad.

(b) The mistake is entering in the first place. That's the clever part of Shubik's design — the trap catches rational players: once you're in, every move that looks safe digs the hole deeper, and the only way to win is not to play. So the error isn't in the bidding, it's in the entry.

(c) Staw's finding was that people double down most when they were personally responsible for the earlier decision — a self-justification thing. But the auction shows that staying in is actually the rational move at each point. So the sunk-cost fallacy is kind of a myth: people who 'honor sunk costs' are playing it right, it just looks dumb from outside. Same story with the Concorde — the escalation was rational, just expensive."
