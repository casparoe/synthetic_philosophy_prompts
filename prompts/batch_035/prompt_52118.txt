I've joined a working group at my company that has been asked to redesign our job architecture — the leveling system, the titles, the promotion criteria. In our first meeting, one colleague argued that the whole system is "money-like": titles and levels exist only because everyone in the company collectively agrees to treat them as real, and that's exactly how money works — real only because we all believe in it. Since the company believes the current system into existence, she concluded, we can redesign it however we like; the only genuine constraint is coordination, getting everyone to update their beliefs at once, and the only real costs are transition costs. A second colleague pushed back hard, saying that this is not how money works at all — nobody can revise what money is by convening a committee — and that if the comparison fails for money, it fails for our titles too. I've been assigned to write up the framing document, and I'd rather not build it on a broken analogy.

Could you audit the comparison for me? I'd like a correspondence table — what plays the role, in our leveling system, of the collective belief, the issuing authority, the ledger, the counterfeits, the sanctions — and then the places where the mapping breaks down. I'm especially interested in which features of money the analogy quietly imports that our titles lack, or the other way around: enforcement and punishment, network effects and lock-in, the fact that money's "rules" are not codified anywhere a committee could amend, and whether belief is actually what sustains either system in the first place. A related question: my colleague's whole argument seems to rest on what I've seen called the Tinkerbell reading of money — real only as long as people believe in it. I gather some philosophers of money deny this, holding that money survives individual disbelief and even general crises of confidence in a way Tinkerbell doesn't. If that's right, the first crack in the analogy appears on the money side before we even get to titles.

Finally, tell me whether the "we can redesign it however we like" conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether a corrected analogy points somewhere else — say, toward some parts of the system being more belief-dependent and changeable by fiat, and other parts being anchored by power, precedent, or outside institutions (other employers, the labor market, people's CVs) in a way no internal meeting can touch. And if money is the wrong source domain for our situation, is there a better one — something whose rules really are up for grabs in the way my colleague assumes ours are, like the rules of a club or a game? I want the framing document to be honest about which parts of our system are which.
