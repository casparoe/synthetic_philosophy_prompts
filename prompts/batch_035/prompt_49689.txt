You are a professional philosopher with deep expertise in decision theory and the philosophy of probability, and I would like your help thinking through a question I have been turning over for several years, entirely on my own. I have no academic training in philosophy or mathematics, so please bear with me if my terminology is off, and correct it where it matters.

The St. Petersburg paradox itself is easy to state: a fair coin is flipped until it lands heads, and if it takes n flips you win 2 to the nth power dollars, so the expected value of playing is a sum of infinitely many halves, which never stops growing; yet almost nobody would pay even a modest amount to play. What puzzles me is not the paradox but the argument about it, which seems to have run in roughly the same shape for centuries without anyone conceding anything. As far as I can tell from my reading, the responses fall into a few clusters. One group says the problem is with the game, not the theory: no real casino could pay unbounded sums, so once you cap the payout at what a counterparty could actually deliver, the expected value becomes finite and modest, and the paradox dissolves. A second group says the problem is with the rule: expected value is the wrong guide when the payoffs are wildly skewed and the probabilities tiny, and we should care about something like the typical outcome or a risk-adjusted value instead. A third group bites the bullet: taking the game exactly as described, you should be willing to pay any finite price to play, and our reluctance is a human bias to be explained away by psychology rather than accommodated by the theory.

Here is my amateur hypothesis, and I want you to test it rather than flatter it: I suspect the disagreement is not really about gambling or even probability, but about idealization. The first cluster thinks a decision theory is about actual choices made by actual agents against actual counterparties, so a scenario no one could ever run is a defect in the setup. The other clusters think idealization is the whole point — the value of the paradox is that it isolates the pure structure of a choice. On my view, each cluster is answering a different question under one name: what should a person do here, versus what does our best theory recommend for a stylized case. If that is right, then no new theorem or experiment could settle it, and the centuries of stalemate are just two projects talking past each other. But I can also imagine I am wrong — maybe something technical is hiding in the infinite sum itself, since I have read that infinite sums can behave strangely (their value can even depend on the order in which you add things up), and the stalemate is genuine substance rather than crossed wires.

So here is what I would like from you. Please begin with some genuine brainstorming: set out at least three possible diagnoses of what kind of disagreement this is — verbal, substantive and formal, substantive and empirical, different-questions-under-one-name, or something else entirely — before committing to any of them. Then give me your best diagnosis, with reasons, engaging with what each cluster of respondents is actually claiming rather than with caricatures of them. Finally, tell me honestly what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a conceptual distinction, a formal result, a reformulation of the question — or whether no progress should be expected, and why. Take your time and be rigorous. I would much rather have a careful answer that overturns my hypothesis than a polite one that confirms it.
