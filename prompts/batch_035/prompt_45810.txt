I serve on the national examination committee of the board that licenses structural engineers, and I've held the seat long enough to have inherited our exam's assumptions rather than chosen them. By background I'm a longtime reader of the rationalist blogs, so I arrive with opinions about externalized cognition that my colleagues mostly do not share. Our licensure exam has always paired a closed-book section testing recall of code provisions and design formulas with an open-resource practical section. Two developments have pried the closed-book question open again. First, a group of candidates has petitioned to bring their personal engineering knowledge bases into the practical section — years of their own annotated notes, failure analyses, and worked examples, maintained daily on a tablet. One petitioner argued that his notebook is so reliably coupled to his daily practice that excluding it is like excluding a candidate's eyeglasses. Second, the committee is split over whether to permit a proctored AI assistant in that same section for everyone. One faction takes the extended mind line: if a tool is constantly available, trusted, and automatically consulted, then the person-plus-tool is the competent system, and a credential that ignores the tool certifies a fiction no one works under. The other faction holds that we license the practitioner, not the practitioner's apparatus, and that only the closed section measures what actually lives between the ears. My own half-formed view, which the memo should take seriously but need not endorse, is that both factions commit the same category error: parity considerations tell us what explains a person's performance in the moment, but a credential is a bet about performance across future environments, so the real question is not metaphysical but actuarial — which coupled systems can we trust to be present and functioning when it matters? Draft the decision memo I will circulate before the March sitting. It should recommend a concrete policy on both the personal knowledge base petition and the AI assistant question — permit, prohibit, or differentiate, with criteria examiners can actually apply at the door — and it must give the strongest opposing argument its full weight rather than a strawman. My fellow committee members are engineers, not philosophers, so the memo should make the philosophical stakes legible without name-dropping anyone, and it should be explicit about whatever our exam can no longer honestly claim to certify once the decision is made.
