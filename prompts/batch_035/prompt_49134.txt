My friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about luck egalitarianism. She thinks the view quietly collapses under pressure, and I found myself defending a version of it while making concessions I'm no longer sure I can hold together. Before I write up my half of the debate, I want a consistency audit of the following package, which is roughly the set of things I ended up asserting:

1. The core luck-egalitarian claim: inequalities are unjust when they trace to brute luck, but not when they trace to choices for which the person is responsible.
2. The currency of justice is resources, not welfare: there is no claim to equalize happiness or preference satisfaction, and no compensation for expensive tastes a person happens to have developed.
3. The right test for whether a distribution of resources is equal is the envy test: no one would prefer anyone else's bundle.
4. Someone born blind deserves full compensation for the costs of blindness, and this is compatible with (2), because what is being compensated is a shortfall in resources — the means and capacities others have and the blind person must buy back — not a shortfall in happiness.
5. Someone who develops a comparable disability through a fully informed, voluntary refusal to take precautions everyone else takes — say, a motorcyclist who rode without a helmet — has no justice-based claim to compensation for the outcome. That is option luck, and it should fall where it lands.
6. Nevertheless, I am unwilling to say that such a person should be left untreated. A universal healthcare system that treats them at public expense strikes me as required, not merely permissible, even though it pays for outcomes that (5) says are entirely the person's own responsibility.
7. Preferences, characters, and capacities for prudence are themselves heavily products of unchosen causes — genetic endowment and upbringing — and yet I want to hold people responsible for the choices that flow from exactly those unchosen dispositions.
8. It is permissible, maybe even good, for parents to confer advantages on their own children — music lessons, savings, connections — even though this generates brute-luck inequality between children of different parents.

My friend pressed hardest on (6) as flatly contradicting (1) and (5), and on (7) as a general self-undermining point: if characters are brute luck, it is unclear what is left for the "choices" in (1) to be. We also circled the question of whether (4) and (8) are problems or only look like ones. The argument never pinned down which of these commitments is actually load-bearing and which should give.

Please do not respond with a survey of positions or a menu of live options. Take a stand: identify which of my commitments are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, rank the real tensions by severity, and for each say what would have to give and what that revision would cost me — including, if I give up the wrong thing, how much of the original luck-egalitarian motivation survives. If you think the whole package is an unstable hybrid, say so plainly and tell me which single revision cleans up the most. I would rather be told I have been defending something incoherent than be handed a set of consistent options and left to choose.
