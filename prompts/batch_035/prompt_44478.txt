I'd like your help adjudicating a dispute that broke out in my reading group, which this term has been working through topics in collective decision-making. The proximate occasion was a decision by our department to switch a certain hiring committee from a consensus rule to a simple majority vote; a colleague had cited the Condorcet jury theorem in defense of the change, and two members of the group — I'll call them R. and J. — took opposite sides and were still at it when the room emptied. I'll reconstruct the exchange in numbered steps, as close to how it happened as I can manage.

(1) R. opened by claiming the theorem amounts to a near-mathematical vindication of majority rule: if each of n voters is even slightly more likely than not to judge correctly, the probability that the majority judges correctly climbs toward certainty as n grows. A hundred voters each right 60% of the time, R. said, produce a majority that is right roughly 97% of the time, which is why enlarging the committee and taking a vote should appeal to anyone who cares about tracking the truth rather than whoever shouts loudest.

(2) J. replied that the theorem is being sold under false pretenses: it assumes the voters' verdicts are independent of one another, and committee members plainly are not independent draws — they read the same dossier, absorb the same departmental folklore, and chat in the hallway before the vote. Citing the theorem for a real committee is, in J.'s words, like citing a frictionless-plane result to sell a particular car.

(3) R. answered that J. has the assumption wrong: the theorem doesn't require unconditional independence of verdicts, it requires independence conditional on the state of the world — given that the candidate is the right hire (or isn't), each member's error should be an independent event, the way jurors might each separately miss or catch the decisive fact. Shared good evidence is no threat to this; only shared causes of error are. That, R. claimed, is exactly why real juries are sequestered and instructed not to deliberate before voting: the practice is an institutional attempt to secure the conditional independence the theorem needs.

(4) J. said this concession gives the game away: for actual committees the dominant causes of error are common — everyone works from the same file, the same talk, the same parochial tastes — so the number of effectively independent judgments is tiny, and the twelfth member adds almost nothing. The theorem's asymptotic promise is idle when a fifteen-person committee is functionally five people. Worse, J. continued, the theorem is symmetric: if members run even slightly below 50% competence, the majority's accuracy gets worse as the group grows, so quoting the theorem as praise for majorities presupposes precisely what is in dispute — that the average member sits on the right side of chance.

(5) R. called the below-chance scenario a scarecrow: a committee whose members are systematically worse than a coin flip would be a bizarre institution, and in any case the theorem can be run with voters of differing competences — what matters is that the average exceeds one-half. The real point of the theorem, R. insisted, is not the specific numbers but the mechanism: majority rule is an error-correcting device, while a consensus rule can be held hostage by one stubborn member and never aggregates the private signals of the others at all.

(6) J. closed by questioning whether those private signals even exist in a committee setting: most of a member's judgment is constituted by the shared evidence plus a residual of taste, so there is less to aggregate than the mechanism requires, and the theorem is true of a world that R. merely wishes committees inhabited. R. retorted that if that were right, the department should abolish committees outright, which J. did not seem prepared to recommend.

Please adjudicate this move by move. For each of the six steps, say who is right about what, flag any errors of mathematics or of interpretation (including in the numerical claims), and note where either party is trading on an ambiguity. Then, when the dust settles, tell me: does the theorem, properly stated, give the department any genuine reason to prefer majority vote to consensus on this committee — and if so, what exactly is that reason, and what would have to be true of the committee for it to apply? I'd also like to know what, if anything, both of them missed.
