I'm a high school senior writing the extended essay for my diploma, in a subject area my school files under "Language and Literature," though it's really philosophy of language. My topic is translation under formal constraint, built around Douglas Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot (1997). The book grew out of Clément Marot's A une damoyselle malade, a very short poem Marot wrote in 1537 to a sick young woman: twenty-eight lines of three syllables each, in rhyming couplets, playful and tender. Hofstadter's project was to get hundreds of people to translate it into English under self-imposed constraints that mirror the original's tightness, and to argue from the results that form carries a large share of a poem's meaning — what he calls the "ton."

I have a full draft, but rather than paste all 3,200 words, I'll give you the notes for it, plus my own translation attempt, and then tell you what I want comments on.

My thesis, in one sentence: Hofstadter is right that form carries meaning, but he understates how, because he treats form and content as two channels whose losses can be traded off against each other — and that trade-off picture, I argue, is wrong.

Here are the sections.

Section 1 reconstructs Hofstadter's position as the trade-off picture: a translation has a fixed budget, spends some of it on semantic fidelity and some on formal fidelity, and every translation is just a different spending pattern. I don't dispute that he values form highly; I dispute the bookkeeping.

Section 2 makes my positive claim. The formal constraint doesn't merely add a channel of meaning; it prunes the space of possible lines. Given the 28-by-3 structure and the couplet rhymes, there is a definite (if enormous) set of things Marot's poem could not have said, and I claim that set — call it the poem's modal profile — is part of the poem's content. The reader's sense that the poem is effortless, that it is straining against its own cage, just is the reader's registering of the modal profile. A prose translation preserves the propositional content but not the profile, so it isn't a partial success at translating this poem; it is a different object that shares one component with it.

Section 3 is my test case: my own translation. I imposed twenty-eight three-syllable lines, rhyming couplets on a narrow set of English sounds, and a requirement that the closing line keep the poem's pivot from tallying to farewell. The first four lines of my attempt are:

"Dainty one, / Here's a sun / Sent your way; / Short your stay..."

My argument is that the moments where my version creaks — where I picked a word for its syllable count rather than its sense — are not losses in a content channel; they are evidence for my thesis, because the creak is what the original never lets you feel. Marot sounds unstrained inside the cage, and I claim the reader can hear that.

Section 4 confronts what I think is my best counterexample, and I'm not sure I survive it: the limerick. Its form is generic, shared by every other limerick ever written, so the pruning story seems to prove too much — it would make every limerick have the same modal profile, which is absurd, since limericks are mostly bawdy content wearing a rented costume. My attempted fix is to distinguish genre-level constraints, which are shared and carry nothing, from poem-level constraints, which are tuned to this content and carry everything. But I can't yet say what makes a constraint "tuned" without circularity: a constraint is poem-level when it's expressive, and it's expressive when it's poem-level. I know this section is hand-waving and I'd like help seeing whether the idea is salvageable or whether it sinks the thesis.

Section 5 takes up free verse and admits the thesis may quietly extend until it says nothing. Free verse has no audible constraint, so what is pruned? My fallback answer is that free verse prunes against a much larger and looser constraint set — everything prunes something — but if the modal profile idea covers both Marot's couplets and a prose paragraph, it has stopped being a criterion. This is, I think, the essay's weakest joint, and I'd rather be told that plainly than be encouraged.

What I'd like from you, then:

1. Comments on the central claim: does the modal-profile argument actually undermine the trade-off picture, or does Hofstadter's budget metaphor survive it? Take a position; don't give me both sides and no verdict.
2. Comments on the two weak sections: does the limerick fix have a non-circular version, and is there any principled way to keep free verse from swallowing the criterion?
3. Comments on my own translation: does it do the work Section 3 needs it to do, or does the fact that I wrote both the translation and the theory make the "you can hear it's unstrained in the original" claim something I couldn't check honestly?
4. On accuracy: I've characterized Hofstadter only through what I summarize above; if anything in that characterization seems off as a reading — too convenient for my argument, for instance — flag it.

Finally, and this matters to me: at the end of your comments, please say what would change your mind about your verdict on the thesis, and identify the single point in the essay's reasoning where it is most likely to fail. If you can name a kind of argument or example that would lead you to the opposite conclusion, name it.

Please write in American English, and please be candid rather than encouraging — the draft is due in three weeks, and I'd rather rebuild it now than defend a hole in the viva.
