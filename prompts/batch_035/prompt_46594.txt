Every year my applied ethics course does the commodification unit -- kidney sales, vote markets, price gouging after hurricanes, the greatest hits. And every year my students get scarily good at reciting the standard objections back at me: the corruption of goods stuff, the crowding-out worry (pay for blood and the altruists stay home), the semiotic objection about what selling *says*, exploitation vs mere unfairness, that whole cluster. At this point they can run the arguments on any repugnant market you name like a checklist, and honestly, so can I. The term papers have gotten boring for everyone involved.

So I'm changing the final assignment this year, and I want you to write me the model answer, sort of. Write the FAQ page for a fictional startup -- some suitably ghastly name like a "marketplace for life's unused assets" -- where users can sell kidneys and other organs, votes (with a convenient "we handle the paperwork" option), their place in line, apology rights (they draft and sincerely deliver your apologies for a fee), plus one or two other offerings you invent. About 700 words, headers included, in the flat chirpy customer-service voice of an actual startup FAQ page.

Here's the conceit, which is the whole assignment: the company has clearly hired a philosopher-in-residence, and the FAQ is obsessively, defensively pre-emptive. Every question is a real objection to repugnant markets, and every answer has to engage the objection's actual content -- not a strawman version -- while somehow ending in a sales pitch. So when a user asks whether selling a kidney says something bad about the value of human bodies, the company doesn't dodge: it explains the semiotic worry correctly, then explains that their "meaning-preserving premium listing" takes care of it. When someone raises crowding out, the answer should demonstrably understand the worry and then get it wrong in a specific, confident way. You get the idea. The humor has to come from getting the philosophy right, so that someone who's taught this stuff laughs instead of winces.

A few requirements, since my students always ask:

1. At least one answer must quietly concede an objection in full and then keep selling anyway. This is the joke I care most about -- the company is too honest to lie about the arguments and too committed to the marketplace to care. Don't flag it. Let the reader notice.
2. Include the "who actually ends up selling?" question, and let the answer be truthful about the demographics in a way that's funnier and darker for being deadpan about it.
3. Don't make the company evil morons. It should be smart enough that a decent defender of markets would sincerely endorse maybe half the answers. If it's a strawman, the satire lands on nothing. I want it to land on the whole discourse -- on how the debate itself gets metabolized into product features.
4. No real companies or living philosophers mentioned by name. The FAQ cites its "independent ethics advisory board," which readers should come to suspect is one underpaid adjunct on a retainer.

For the framing: deadpan throughout, no winking at the reader, but it should stay legible -- someone who hasn't taken my course should still get that something is wrong, even if they can't name which argument is being mangled. And one answer somewhere should raise the strongest *pro*-market reply (the desperation point, or the autonomy point about whose choices the paternalism overrides) so well that the anti-commodification side gets satirized too. Fair's fair.
