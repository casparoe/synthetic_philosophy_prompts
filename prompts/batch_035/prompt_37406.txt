I write about science and ideas for a general readership, and something in research ethics keeps nagging at me. Institutional review boards in psychology green-light studies that involve flat-out deception — a fake fellow participant, rigged feedback telling you that you failed, sometimes a whole staged scenario — so long as participants are debriefed afterwards and the risk is judged minimal. Clinical trial consent forms, meanwhile, go the opposite way: they can leave a lot out (you'll be told you may recieve either the active drug or a placebo, but not which), but nothing they assert may be false, and there's no debrief that makes an untrue statement to a patient okay. What view of lying does this whole setup presuppose? Augustine, in On Lying, held that every lie is wrong in itself — not even saving a life licenses one, and coming clean afterwards doesn't undo it. On that view the debrief can't launder the experiment: the statement was a lie when it was made, and that's that. Notably, he did allow concealing truth while never asserting falsehood, which fits the consent-form practice to a tee. So what's the implicit theory over here on the psychology side — that a lie's wrongness is cancellable after the fact, that the debrief turns it into something participants would've consented to in advance, or that these deceptions aren't really lies but a kind of temporary role-play both parties are in on? Can one theory of lying cover both practices, or is the psych-experiment part just a consequentialist loophole bolted onto an otherwise Augustinian scruple? Tell me what the practices strictly presuppose versus what they merely happen to fit.
