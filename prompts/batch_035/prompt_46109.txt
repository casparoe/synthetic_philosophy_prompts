So I'm TA-ing a philosophy of language course, and next week's seminar is on Quine's indeterminacy of translation — the gavagai stuff. After last year's session the professor complained that students walked out able to recite the positions but with no idea which one they themselves hold, so this time she wants me to prepare a decision-tree handout that takes a student from the field linguist's starting point (you're in the jungle, you have nothing but the natives' observable behavior to go on, and you have to write a translation manual) and ends at a named position, with every branch node phrased as a question a student could answer without already knowing where it leads. We argued about this at our reading group and I want the tree built from the logical space, not from a list of famous names — so please brainstorm the diagnostic questions first, then locate the actual positions in that space, and note any combinations of answers that nobody occupies (and say whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored).

For the angle, there are three splits I specifically want on the tree, because they're where students actually get lost. First: whether two translation manuals could both fit ALL possible behavioral evidence — including every future utterance, every点头, every interrogation — and still disagree about what "gavagai" refers to (rabbit vs. undetached rabbit part vs. rabbit stage, that whole business). Second: whether the same thing happens in physics, since Quine apparently claims translation is somehow worse off than ordinary scientific underdetermination, and a student's answer to "is gavagai underdetermined in the same way orbital theory is, or in some stronger sense?" is exactly what separates garden-variety underdetermination fans from the real indeterminacy thesis. Third: once all evidence is in and the manuals still tie, whether there's simply no fact of the matter about reference, or whether something outside the behavioral evidence does the fixing — innate structural constraints, rationality/charity constraints on interpretation, or the world itself somehow magnetizing the words onto objects. The leaves should include Quinean holism, the Chomskyan nativist escape route, Davidsonian charity, a Kripke-Wittgenstein style "there was never a fact to be found" resignation, and whatever the correct labels are for the remaining spots; write "unnamed" where nobody actually sits. Also please work in one invented test case along these lines: a manual that translates "gavagai" as "rabbit, except on days after the shaman has visited the village, in which case undetached rabbit part" — and have one node turn on whether the student thinks such a manual could be evidentially just as good as the boring rabbit one.

You can take the role of a professional philosopher of language who has taught this material for years and has seen every way students confuse themselves about it. Think carefully before committing to the tree shape, and be honest about its limits — for instance, I have a suspicion the tree can't cleanly seperate Quine himself from Davidson, and if that's right I'd rather the handout flag it than paper over it. Audience is undergrads who've read the gavagai sections of Word and Object but basically nothing else; aim for roughly six or seven nodes and something that fits on one page. Also, small thing, if a student's answers land them on a leaf that requires them to also accept something weird about physics or meaning they didn't sign up for, the handout should say what that awkward extra commitment is — one sentence per leaf is fine.
