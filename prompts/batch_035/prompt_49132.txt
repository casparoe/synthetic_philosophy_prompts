In our philosophy of mind reading group we keep hearing the line that 'consciousness is a user illusion', most recently invoked by a visiting speaker to brush aside a question about whether one's own introspective report about being in pain could ever be mistaken: if consciousness is a user illusion, he said, then there is nothing further for the report to be answerable to, and the question dissolves. The metaphor, as it gets thrown around, is borrowed from the graphical user interface: the icons on a desktop are not the files, but they are not nothing either — they are a simplification the system shows itself so it can get on with its work. Different people in the group seem to be using that one sentence to mean strikingly different things, and I suspect the speaker's deflection only works on some of the readings.

Could you lay out, as a structured list I can circulate before the next meeting, the distinct precise claims that 'consciousness is a user illusion' could be expressing? I'd like each reading to be stated as an actual thesis, not as a gesture — for instance it seems to matter a great deal whether the claim is that introspection is a fallible representational faculty, that phenomenal properties as we take ourselves to encounter them do not exist in the brain (though something else does the corresponding work), that the very concept of a phenomenal property is defective in the way an illusionist says it is, or merely that consciousness is less than the naïve picture assumes. For each reading, please say whether it is a platitude, substantive and defensible, or simply false, and whether it would actually support the speaker's use of the slogan to dissolve the question about incorrigible pain reports.

I don't want an even-handed survey that leaves the deciding to me. Commit to a verdict: which reading do you think is the best content for the slogan, which reading the people repeating it most likely intend, and — given the gap between those two, if you find one — whether we should retire the phrase or reappropriate it with a definition pinned to one reading. If the desktop metaphor itself is doing dubious work on your preferred reading (for example, if the analogy between icons and a system's self-model only goes so far), say so plainly rather than letting it ride.

Please write in British English throughout, and flag any point on which you are genuinely uncertain rather than papering over it with hedged phrasing.
