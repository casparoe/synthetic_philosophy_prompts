Write a villanell­e in the voice of the absent-minded driver from the decision-theory literature: he knows his route home crosses two identical intersections, and he knows the payoffs — exiting at the second gets him home best, exiting at the first is mediocre, and sailing past both leaves him stranded — but being absent-minded, he cannot tell, upon arriving at an intersection, whether it is the first or the second. Make the two refrains the two resolutions that keep competing for him: the plan he settled on before setting out (go straight at every intersection, since that plan is optimal from the start) and the reasoning that seizes him each time he actually arrives at a corner (if I go straight here, what should I infer about whether I already went straight once before?). Let the form do the work rather than the diction: a villanelle repeats its own lines without acknowledging the repetition, just as the driver re-encounters his decision with no memory of having made it, and the refrains — taking turns, neither ever answering the other, until they finally collide in the closing couplet — should enact the clash between the planning perspective and the acting perspective rather than resolve it. Someone who knows the absent-minded driver problem should be able to tell which refrain is which and why the poem cannot obey both; someone who doesn't should still get a good poem about a man who keeps arriving at the same corner.
