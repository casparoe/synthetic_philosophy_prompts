So here's my situation: I spent 35 years as a microbiologist and since retiring I've fallen hard for philosophy, been going through a local reading group like a man possessed. My friend Marta teaches an upper-level seminar on free will and moral responsibility at the university here, and she's got the idea that a scientist's outsider angle would make for a good exam question, so she asked me to draft one. The topic is the Dennett vs. Caruso debate on just deserts and punishment (you know, the little dialogue book they did together a few years back). I have opinions but I've never written an exam question in my life, so you're doing the heavy lifting.

Here's what I want the question to do. The easy version of this question -- "compare Dennett's compatibilism with Caruso's free will skepticism" -- could be answered by anyone who memorized the positions, and Marta would throw it out. So the question has to force students into the weeds. My own take (and tell me if you think I've got this wrong, I'm an amateur) is that the really interesting pressure point is this: Dennett's justification for punishment is supposed to be forward-looking -- blame as moral education, shaping people into responsible agents -- and he insists he's not a retributionist. Fine and good for the ordinary corrigible offender. But what about the incorrigible one? Somebody who fully understood what they did, knew it was wrong, and is simply not going to be taught anything by any amount of blame. For that person, the moral-education justification seems to collapse into pure incapacitation, which is more or less what Caruso's quarantine/public-health model says we're left with once basic desert is off the table. So either Dennett has to sneak some backward-looking desert back in to handle the hard cases (and Caruso will say that's the very thing in dispute), or he has to admit that for the incorrigible offender he and Caruso prescribe the same thing and only differ in the story they tell about it. That feels like a real crux to me, but you're the judge of whether it's the right one.

So: write me (1) the exam question itself, suitable for a 90-minute essay answer in an upper-level seminar, built around the incorrigible-offender test case, and requiring students to also address whether the Dennett/Caruso disagreement is substantive or partly verbal; (2) a model answer of around 600-700 words that takes an actual position rather than surveying both sides; and (3) a marking scheme worth 20 points total, naming the specific moves an A answer must contain and the specific things that should cost marks -- with a line on how B answers typically go wrong. Don't have the model answer lean on quoting page numbers or anything, Marta's students won't have the book in the exam room and the answers shouldn't pretend otherwise.

One more thing, and this matters to me after a career in science: after the marking scheme, add a short honest note saying where you think the model answer is most likely to fail or misrepresent the actual debate, and what argument or consideration would change your mind about the verdict the model answer reaches -- e.g., if there's a Dennettian reply to the incorrigible-offender worry that the model answer waves away too fast, flag it. I'd rather hand Marta something with its error bars visible. If you think my framing of the crux is off-base entirely, say so directly instead of politely building around my mistake.
