For the past few years I've kept up a steady forecasting habit — a Metaculus account, the occasional Good Judgment Open tournament, and the standard LessWrong-style calibration drills where you answer dozens of trivia questions with a probability and then plot your calibration curve. My question is about the philosophy this whole ecosystem quietly commits me to. The practice has a definite shape: you must commit to a number, not a qualitative opinion ("70%," not "probably"); your standing is determined by a proper scoring rule, so hedging toward the base rate doesn't save you on the questions you actually know something about; calibration as such is celebrated as an epistemic virtue, with whole training curricula devoted to it; and in Tetlock's research the aggregated crowd forecast was deliberately extremized — pushed away from 50% — because pooled judgments were found to be systematically underconfident. I've absorbed the folk view that this all just follows from "beliefs are probabilities," and I'd never questioned it, but I've lately been reading about accuracy-first epistemology and epistemic utility theory, where apparently even people who agree that credences are graded fight over whether accuracy is the only thing that matters, and I've realized I don't know what my hobby actually presupposes.

So here's what I'd like worked out. Reconstruct the implicit epistemology of the forecasting tournament: what view of belief, of evidence, and of epistemic value does the leaderboard only make sense under? Which rival views would license the same practice anyway — I take it the practice underdetermines quite a lot, and I'd like to know where the line is between what the scoring rule strictly requires and what merely fits. There's also what looks like an internal tension I'd want addressed: the community treats calibration as the virtue, but someone who answered 50% on every question all year would be perfectly calibrated and score terribly, so the scoring rule must be rewarding resolution as well as calibration, and I don't know whether that combination is coherent or whether the two pull apart on some kinds of questions. And a related wrinkle: Brier scores and log-loss are both proper scoring rules, but I've read they can rank the same two forecasters differently, which suggests the practice presupposes a choice of measure that the accuracy-first theory may not settle — is that right, and what does it mean for the claim that scoring rules "vindicate" probabilism? Finally, tell me what would have to change in the practice if we took seriously a rival like a binary-belief or safety-based view — or could a defender of that view run the same tournament with a clear conscience?

I'd like the response to lead with its bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question. Since I'm genuinely torn between "the practice just is Bayesianism operationalized" and "the practice is philosophically a mess that happens to work," please be explicit about which parts of your reconstruction are forced and where you could be wrong: what argument or consideration would flip your verdict on whether the leaderboard presupposes accuracy-first epistemology in particular, and where is your reasoning most likely to fail?
