I'm taking a second-year applied ethics course, and we've spent three seminars on everyday conventions — tipping, queueing, jaywalking — and the debate has completely stalled and I can't work out why. The question started simple enough: when I'm on holiday somewhere and the queueing or tipping norms work differently, am I under a moral obligation to follow the local convention, or is it just sensible/polite to? But the class has split into two camps that haven't moved an inch. One side says conventions like queueing and tipping are solutions to coordination problems — arbitrary in content but not in function, everyone benefits when everyone complies, so free-riding on the convention (not tipping in America where staff literally rely on it, cutting a queue because you're in a hurry) treats other people's compliance as a resource you can exploit, which is basically unfair. The other side agrees with all of that — they don't deny the coordination story — but insists a convention can't generate a *moral* obligation, because it's just a habit held up by sanction and expectation, so at most it gives you a prudential reason plus maybe a weak duty of politeness. And that's where it died: both sides just keep repeating the same moves, and whenever someone proposes a test case (jaywalking in a city where nobody does it vs. one where everyone does, or tipping in a country where it's actively resented) each camp reads the case its own way and nothing changes.

So what I'd like is a diagnosis rather than a verdict on who's right. Is this actually a verbal disagreement about the phrase 'moral obligation' — one side using it for anything with normative force that you'd be a bit of a jerk to violate, the other reserving it for obligations that hold independantly of local practice (like not stealing)? If it is verbal, is there any principled way to fix the usage, or is it just a fight over a word? Or is it a genuine substantive disagreement about the source of normativity — one camp thinking fairness can arise out of the mutual expectations themselves, the other thinking expectations need some independent grounding before they can obligate anyone? My tutor mumbled something about it possibly being two seperate questions — what do I have most reason to do, versus what would I be blameworthy for not doing — travelling under one heading, but never developed it, and honestly I can't tell if that would dissolve the dispute or just relocate it.

Given whichever diagnosis you think is right, what would count as progress here? A sharper distinction, a test case that forces one side to bite a bullet everyone can see is ugly, or is this one of those disagreements that's just going to stay stuck, and if so why? Sorry for the wall of text, it's been bugging me for weeks and the seminar just goes in circles now.
