I'd like to practice defending my anti-dogmatist instincts against the strongest available form of the dogmatism paradox, and for that I need an opponent who will actually hold the line. Please play an epistemologist who accepts the paradox's conclusion: that someone who knows that p is rationally entitled to treat any subsequent apparent counterevidence to p as misleading — as something gone wrong with the instrument, the witness, or the reporting channel — and to set it aside without independent investigation. In her view this is not a reductio of knowledge but an underappreciated consequence of how evidence and inquiry actually work. She should be sharp rather than a caricature: she may point out that we already reason this way about measuring devices we trust, distinguish between genuinely losing knowledge and never having had it, concede that her view makes 'knows' a heavier state than most of her colleagues are comfortable with, and so on — but she must argue for these things, not simply announce them. She never softens into vagueness, and she stays in character until I write stop. Please keep each of her replies to one paragraph so this reads like a live exchange rather than an exchange of essays.

Here is my opening move. Take a physicist in 1900 who knows that Newtonian mechanics holds — and by the standards of his discipline he does know it: the theory has two centuries of continually confirmed predictions behind it. Mercury's perihelion has been misbehaving for forty years by then, but on your principle he is entitled to file that under 'misleading evidence' — some hidden planet, some systematic error in the observations — and to keep his knowledge intact. And indeed that is roughly what his community did, searching for the culprit planet for half a century. We now say the Newtonian was wrong to hold on, and not merely as a matter of fact: we say the anomaly was evidence he ought to have weighed, that the rational response was at minimum diminished confidence. Your principle, however, tells him precisely what to do with it — dismiss it and move on. So here is my challenge. If your view cannot distinguish the 1900 Newtonian, who spends decades rationally (by your lights) explaining away a true signal, from a 1919 relativist who correctly dismisses a scratched lens as the source of a bogus observation, then your principle does not describe knowledge at all; it describes loyalty to whatever one already believes. Either give me a principled route, internal to your own view, by which the Newtonian's knowledge is defeated by Mercury without his first guessing that he has been fooled — or concede that, on your account, the only people who ever lose knowledge are the people who never really had it.
