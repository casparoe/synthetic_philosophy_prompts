I'm a backend engineer and, by accident of being the one person who didn't leave the meeting room fast enough, I now sit on the charity-giving committee at my company (about 400 people, based in Reading). Every year we hand out roughly £45,000 — a mix of what staff raised through bake sales and marathons plus a company match that gets topped up pound for pound. Historically it's all gone to local stuff: the food bank where Priya from finance volunteers, the air ambulance, the primary school one of the directors is a governor at. This year it's blown up. One of our data scientists, Sam, circulated a long internal post arguing we're basically walking past a drowning child every single day. His thought experiment: if you passed a kid drowning in a shallow pond on your commute, you'd wade in and ruin your £200 trainers without a second thought, and if you wouldn't, something is seriously wrong with you. So why does it matter that the kids who need help are far away, or that our money travels through a charity rather than through our own arms? He wants the whole pot to go to whichever charity demonstrably saves the most lives per pound — on his numbers that's malaria prevention work — and he thinks keeping any of it local is like only being willing to rescue the child if it happens to float past our office window.

Dave from facilities is furious. His side of it: the company isn't a person walking past a pond, it has roots here, half the staff gave precisely because of the local connection, and the morale effect of seeing the food bank van with our logo on it is part of what makes people donate at all. Someone floated a compromise — treat the staff-raised money as the sentimental bit that stays local, and point the company's own match at the most effective charity, on the grounds that staff have feelings about their bake-sale money but the company doesn't have feelings. Another wrinkle people keep tripping over: Sam's example is a one-off rescue, whereas what we're deciding is a standing policy, and a couple of members feel uneasy about locking the commitee into maximum-effectiveness forever, as if we'd never again be allowed to buy the scouts a new tent. Dave also keeps insisting it must matter that if we do nothing, no child drowns *because* of us — we're just not rescuing, not drowning anyone — which Sam says is nonsense but didn't manage to argue very well in the meeting. There was also a brief tangent about whether it changes anything that loads of other companies in town give nothing either, which nobody resolved.

I have to circulate a decision memo before Thursday's meeting and I'm genuinely out of my depth, so please take this seriously. Act as a professional moral philosopher who's spent a career on duties of rescue but who can write for engineers rather than for a journal. Before you write anything, brainstorm the strongest version of each side, plus the compromise — including the weirder questions: does it matter whose money it is and whether money can carry sentiment, whether distance should matter at all, whether a standing policy differs morally from a one-off rescue, and whether it changes things if everyone else also fails to give. Then commit to a concrete recommendation the committee can actually vote on, including a split and a rough process for next year. Handle the strongest point on the losing side seriously rather than smacking down a strawman — Dave is definitely going to read this. Around 700-800 words, plain English, and no philosophers' names or citations, just the arguments themselves. Thanks!
