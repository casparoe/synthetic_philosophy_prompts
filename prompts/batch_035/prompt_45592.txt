Each spring I chair the jury of a mid-sized regional visual art prize: five jurors, one weekend of deliberation, a public exhibition, and a modest but career-relevant cash award, and then a ranked shortlist that must be defended in a short public statement. The jurors are serious people — artists, curators, a critic — but every year we descend into the same quarrel: one juror insists that beauty or artistic merit is simply not the kind of thing that can be right or wrong about, that all we can honestly do is aggregate preferences and hope; another insists the opposite, that some judgments are simply correct and the rest are ignorance. I have been reading Hume's essay on the standard of taste, and it seems to promise a middle path — that the standard is the joint verdict of qualified judges, where qualification consists of things like delicacy of taste sharpened by practice, extensive comparison, freedom from prejudice, and good sense — a middle path I would like to actually use rather than admire.

Please write a decision procedure our jury could genuinely run during that one weekend. Specifically:

1. Operationalize each of Hume's qualifications for the ideal critic as a test a real jury can apply to itself and its members: what question we ask, what evidence counts as a sufficient answer, and what to do when the answer is thin. Address the obvious problem head-on: Hume's examples (the wine tasting where a taste of leather or of iron turns out to be traced to a key and a thong at the bottom of the cask) suggest that good taste reveals itself in vindication — but our jury has no way to open the cask. So what does "delicacy" or "practice" amount to when we cannot check a judge's discriminations against the object in the way Hume's story allows?

2. Be honest about the circularity worry: if the qualified judges are identified partly by their verdicts, and the standard of taste is just the verdicts of qualified judges, then the procedure risks rubber-stamping whoever is already on the jury. Say where you would break the circle — for instance, whether qualifications can be specified independently of verdicts about the works at hand, and how far that goes — and where the circle cannot be broken and must simply be acknowledged as a limit of the procedure.

3. Include a step for the case Hume built his essay around: the vulgar verdict (his parlor maid) versus the connoisseur's. Our public audience, the local press, and several funders will have loud opinions, and the prize statement must be written for them. Where does the procedure distinguish "the audience is wrong" from "our panel has drifted into an in-group taste," and where is that distinction itself a judgment call the procedure cannot settle?

4. Handle at least two complications Hume arguably did not anticipate. First, works in categories where no juror has deep practice — this year we expect submissions involving AI-generated imagery, and last year's fights over a durational video piece suggest the practice-based test will simply fail there; should the procedure import something like judging a work within its intended category, and how would a juror establish what the category is without circular appeal to taste again? Second, works engineered to manipulate — sentimentality, topical grievance-appeal — where Hume's freedom-from-prejudice test seems to point at the audience rather than at us, or perhaps at our own moral vanity in condemning the manipulation.

Then run the procedure on three concrete submissions of the kind we actually see: (a) a technically immaculate, deeply conventional landscape that every juror privately admits is beautiful and no juror wants to award; (b) a raw, formally rough piece about the region's industrial decline that two jurors find profound and three find inept; and (c) an AI-assisted piece submitted with a candid disclosure that the artist curated and edited rather than painted. The procedure should yield a defensible ranking or a principled refusal to rank in at least two of the three, and I want it applied honestly even where it gives an answer I will not like — I am the juror who finds the landscape beautiful and the AI piece troubling. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do: which questions before our jury are questions of value that no Humean machinery can remove from our hands, and where following the procedure carefully would still produce a verdict a reasonable person could call wrong.
