Last night my friend and I spent the better part of four hours arguing about Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals, and we reached a genuine impasse that I would like to see resolved properly. Some background so you know exactly where the disagreement lies. Lewis argued in the mid-1970s that Stalnaker's thesis — the equation of the probability of a conditional with the corresponding conditional probability, i.e. Prob(if A then B) = Prob(B given A) — cannot hold in general. The shape of the argument, as we worked through it, is this: apply the equation not just to an agent's overall probability function but also to that function conditionalized on A and on not-A; the ordinary laws of probability, particularly the law of total probability, then force Prob(B given A) to take only trivial values (0 or 1). Hence "triviality": if indicative conditionals are propositions with truth conditions, and their probabilities obey the thesis across the relevant range of probability functions, then almost nothing can be learned from almost anything.

Here is where my friend and I split. She concedes the mathematics but insists the result is much less dramatic than Lewis made it sound, on two grounds. First, she says, the proof requires the thesis to hold for a whole family of probability functions closed under conditionalization; if you read it as a claim only about an agent's actual credence function at a time — one function, no closure assumption — then nothing trivial follows, and the thesis keeps its intuitive appeal as an account of when conditionals are assertible. Second, she says the proof silently helps itself to the ratio analysis of conditional probability, treating Prob(B given A) as Prob(B and A) divided by Prob(A); on her view conditional probabilities are more fundamental than that and are not always expressible as ratios, so Lewis's manipulations simply don't get a grip. My reply was that both moves look like changing the subject rather than answering the argument: the thesis is supposed to be a semantic claim about what conditionals mean, not a psychological accident about a particular agent, and the ratio analysis is precisely what made the thesis intelligible in the first place. She then made what I found her strongest point: even if conditionals do not express propositions, that is a discovery about their semantic category — compare expressivism in metaethics — not a scandal, and the triviality results would then be doing constructive work rather than destructive work.

Write a formal, polished essay adjudicating this dispute. First, reconstruct the structure of Lewis's argument accurately enough to identify which assumptions are actually doing the work — especially the assumption that the thesis holds across a class of probability functions closed under conditionalization, and the reliance on the ratio analysis of conditional probability. Second, evaluate each of my friend's two escape routes on its merits, including what each costs: does restricting the thesis to a single probability function preserve anything worth calling a semantics, and does going primitive about conditional probability explain anything or merely refuse the question? Third, consider the constructive reading she offered — the no-propositions view of conditionals — and ask whether it can account for the things propositions were supposed to account for: embedding under negation and disjunction, nested conditionals, and the validity of modus ponens. You need not reproduce formal proofs, and please do not pad the essay with citations or surveys of the literature; I care about the reasoning. Reach a definite verdict on what the triviality results actually show — that indicative conditionals lack truth conditions outright, that Stalnaker's thesis is simply false, or that it survives in some restricted but defensible form — and defend that verdict against the strongest objection to it. My friend and I will read whatever you write, so do not soften the conclusion for the sake of balance.
