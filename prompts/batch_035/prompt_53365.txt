For the past few years I have co-taught an upper-level course on the philosophy of time, and every final exam has carried a question on the thermodynamic arrow that students effectively answer by recitation: they reproduce the Second Law, gesture at a low-entropy early universe, write "the Past Hypothesis," and collect their marks. This term I want to replace it with a question that separates students who understand what the low-entropy boundary condition is actually doing from students who have merely memorized its name. I have a design in mind, sketched below, but I want you to make the definite calls rather than offering me a menu of options.

The question should be built around a distinction my students consistently miss: that between explaining why the universe contains an entropy gradient at all, and explaining why the low-entropy end of that gradient is the one we call the past. The wedge I want to use is a case I will describe loosely here and ask you to state precisely in the question itself: consider a universe qualitatively like ours except that the boundary condition sits at the other end — a low-entropy condition in the far future and a high-entropy past. It seems that any creatures in that universe whose memories and records are thermodynamically reliable would call their low-entropy end "the future," so that no observation available to the inhabitants of either universe distinguishes the two. The exam question should then put before the student a claim I have only half-formulated and want you to sharpen: roughly, that if the physics cannot distinguish which end is objectively earlier, then the best-system move of promoting the boundary condition to a law gains nothing — it relabels a stipulation as an explanation. The student's task is to work out what, if anything, follows: whether the case shows that the direction of time is conventional, whether it shows only that the labelling is underdetermined while the gradient itself is objective, or whether the whole comparison trades on an equivocation between two senses of "past." A student who answers by repeating that entropy increases toward the future should be visibly unable to get started on the question.

Please produce three things. First, the question as it will appear on the paper, written for ninety minutes, presupposing familiarity with the Past Hypothesis and with best-system accounts of lawhood but not hinging on who proposed what, and not asking for citations or reading lists. Second, a model answer of about 700 words that commits to a definite verdict on the claim as you have formulated it. Third, a marking scheme that names the specific moves a top answer must contain and the specific errors that should cost marks, with a one-line note after each error on the misunderstanding it signals; I have deliberately not fixed the total, so choose a mark allocation you can defend. Throughout, make definite choices: one formulation of the claim, one verdict, one allocation. If you think my setup is flawed — for instance, that the two-universe comparison equivocates in a way that ruins the question — say so plainly and repair it rather than building the exam around a broken case. Close the whole response with a final line of the form \answer{...} giving only the total marks your scheme allocates, so I can check the paper's budget at a glance.
