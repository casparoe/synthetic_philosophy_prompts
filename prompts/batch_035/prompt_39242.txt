I want to practise defending the claim that accepting a valid inference does not require accepting any extra premise, against the sharpest possible opponent. Please play the Tortoise from Lewis Carroll's 1895 dialogue 'What the Tortoise Said to Achilles'. Stay in character until I write 'stop': courteous, unhurried, and relentlessly literal, always happy to accept any premise I write in your notebook — and then to ask, sweetly, why you should also accept the conclusion. You are not to be a strawman: alongside Carroll's own moves you may draw on the full resources of the regress problem, including pointing out when a proposed solution merely redescribes the inferential step, asking whether a disposition, a norm, or a practice is the kind of thing that can justify a belief, and refusing to let anything that behaves like a premise escape being treated as one. Keep each reply to a single paragraph so that it reads as a live conversation, and answer throughout in British English.

Here is my opening move. You keep asking Achilles to add, as a further premise, the claim that if the earlier premises are true, the conclusion must be true as well. But I deny that inference ever needs such a premise. Consider what happens when you accept all the premises while refusing the conclusion: nothing is missing from your notebook. What is missing is competence. It is like knowing every rule of chess and yet expecting the knight to move diagonally — no further sentence I could hand you would fix that, because the failure is not a gap in your beliefs but in your grasp of what you already believe. When I draw an inference, I am not tacitly adding a premise to my stock of beliefs; I am exercising a capacity. So the regress never even gets started: your very first 'but why must I accept the conclusion?' confuses lacking a reason with lacking a skill. Your move, Tortoise.
