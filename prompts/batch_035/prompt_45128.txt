My book club is working through a popular history of historiography, and one chapter's thumbnail of the Ranke–Carr–White debate struck me as suspiciously tidy. Here is the relevant paragraph, near enough verbatim: "Ranke founded scientific history in the nineteenth century. His motto, 'wie es eigentlich gewesen' — to show the past as it actually happened — meant that the historian who sticks strictly to archival sources and excludes all interpretation can produce an account of the past that is objective in the same sense as a laboratory result in physics: finished, certain, valid for everyone. E. H. Carr dismantled this in the 1960s by pointing out that historians select their facts, and since selection is unavoidable, bias is unavoidable too — so no historical account is really more objective than any other. Hayden White then finished the job: histories are novels in disguise, in which the historian imposes a plot structure — romance, tragedy, comedy, or satire — on events that carry no such structure of their own. A history book differs from a work of fiction only in having footnotes." The chapter closes by saying the upshot of all this is that we can finally retire the phrase "objective history." I'm inclined to think there's still something worth keeping in the idea of objectivity, but I don't know the primary sources well enough to say where this treatment goes wrong, or whether it goes wrong anywhere.
