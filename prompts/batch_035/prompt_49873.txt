I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and at a recent workshop on personal identity a cognitive scientist defending gradual uploading reached for the Ship of Theseus. The Argonauts, she said, replaced the ship's planks one at a time until none of the original wood remained, yet nobody thinks the ship ceased at some point and a distinct successor ship began — and by parity of reasoning, if my neurons are swapped out one at a time for functionally identical silicon implants, with consciousness and memory continuous at every stage, then there is no step at which I die, and the end-state upload is simply me. The analogy was doing real work in her argument: it was meant to establish that uploading is a survival technology rather than an elegant way of dying and leaving a copy, and that opponents are confusing numerical identity with psychological connectedness. I found myself unsure which parts of the ship story are load-bearing and which are decoration, and I want a rigorous audit rather than the usual standoff between "it's obviously you" and "it's obviously a copy."

Please structure your response as follows, in this order:

1. "The correspondence map" (~250 words): lay out explicitly what in the uploading scenario plays the role of the planks, the ship, the replacement steps, and the observers who keep calling it the same ship, and note where the mapping has to be stretched.

2. "Disanalogies that matter and disanalogies that don't" (~450 words): distinguish the disanalogies that are fatal to the argument's intended use from the ones that are harmless. In sorting these, please address at least the following: (a) whether artefact identity is partly conventional or stipulative in a way personal identity is not — consider Hobbes's variant in which the discarded planks are reassembled into a second ship and we must decide which one counts as the original; (b) that in the ship story the replacements are like-for-like wood, whereas uploading is more akin to replacing each plank with a plastic replica that behaves identically — a version of the case in which intuitions are much less settled; and (c) that the ship has no inside, so the analogy presupposes that everything that matters about a person is available from the outside, which is precisely what opponents of uploading deny.

3. "What the analogy smuggles in" (~250 words): identify the assumptions about function, substrate, and the causal continuity of memory that the ship comparison quietly takes for granted, including the "functionally identical" clause that was doing more work in her argument than "same-shaped plank" does in the ship case.

4. "Does the survival conclusion survive?" (~300 words): once the disanalogies are on the table, state whether her conclusion — that gradual uploading preserves the person, not merely a psychologically continuous successor — still follows, or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole time. Note explicitly whether the argument supports only the weaker claim that there is no sharp line where death occurs, and whether that weaker claim suffices for the survival conclusion she wanted.

5. "A better analogy, if there is one" (~200 words): if some comparison handles gradual replacement more honestly than the ship — a flame passing between candles, a river, a rebuilt radio relay, or something of your own devising — present it and say what it gets right that the ship gets wrong. If no analogy can carry the weight, say so and explain why.

Please treat the correspondence map as a genuine table if that is clearer, and keep the verdicts sharp rather than diplomatically inconclusive.
