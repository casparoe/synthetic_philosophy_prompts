Over the holidays my brother-in-law and I got into a long argument about whether it's ever acceptable to generalize about national character, and since then I've been trying to work out what I actually think by consulting my reactions to particular cases. So let me lay out my verdicts and ask whether there's a principle behind them, because I can't find one that fits. When a colleague who lived in Helsinki for six years says that Finns tend to be reserved and that silence at a dinner table isn't a snub, that seems fine to me — useful, even. When the same generalization shows up in a hiring manager's assessment of a Finnish candidate ("culturally unlikely to speak up in meetings"), it strikes me as wrong. When an economist writes that average differences in generalized trust help explain why some countries are richer than others, I nod along; when someone explains a country's corruption by saying that's just how people there are, I recoil, even though the two explanations don't feel that structurally different. My grandmother saying that we are a nation of talkers and God help anyone trying to finish a sentence at our table seems charming; a tourist blog warning that locals will talk over you seems mostly fine too, which is inconvenient for the member-versus-outsider distinction I was hoping to use. And a mid-century diplomatic cable explaining a policy failure by the excitable and unstable character of the people involved strikes me as false and offensive in a way I can't reduce to its falsity. The case that really ties me in knots is the sociologist's: survey data showing measurable average differences between countries in norms of politeness or directness. I believe the data, but I balk at calling it national character at all — even though it is, apparently, the best-evidenced version of the thing on offer.

So my question is this: is there a principle that captures all of these verdicts — something about descriptive versus explanatory versus predictive use, about whether the generalization posits an essence rather than a tendency, about what's at stake for the people described, or some combination — and if so, what is it, precisely? If a principle can't save every verdict, tell me which ones it forces me to give up and why. I'd also like to know what the principle says about the case I keep circling: my company's country-liaison training, which teaches incoming staff the local norms about directness, gift-giving, and how to decline an invitation. Is that just national character made respectable, and does it inherit the problems of the diplomat's cable, or is it different in kind? And does the principle predict anything about a case I haven't listed, so I can test it against my gut?
