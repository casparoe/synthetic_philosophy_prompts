I'm putting together a unit on moral status for an undergraduate bioethics elective, and instead of another paper about thresholds I want to teach Daniel Keyes' Flowers for Algernon as an argument about whether cognitive capacity is what makes a being owed moral consideration. The novel looks at first like it runs on a scalar view of personhood: Charlie's standing in other people's eyes rises and falls with his intelligence, in both directions. Early on, his bakery coworkers keep him around as a mascot — there's a whole idiom in the book, people at the bakery use his name to mean screwing something up, and he reports, ashamed, that he finally understands what they mean when they say "to pull a Charlie Gordon." After the surgery, the same people who mocked his ignorance hate him for his knowledge — he writes at one point that before, they had laughed at me, despising me for my ignorance and dullness; now, they hated me for my knowledge and understanding. So the regard tracks the intelligence in both directions, but what stays constant is that he's a specimen to them, never a peer — Nemur and Strauss even argue about whether the procedure created a person, as if Charlie wasn't one before they got involved. What I want is a reading that takes this seriously against the actual philosophical debate: the capacity-based views, and the standard point behind the argument from marginal cases, that any cognitive threshold loose enough to include all humans will include some animals and tight enough to exclude the animals will exclude some humans. Where does the novel dramatize that dilemma rather than just gesturing at it?

Here's the wrinkle I can't decide about, and where I'd like your honest judgment. The book's form cuts against its content. It's all Charlie's own progress reports, so the spelling and grammar degrade as he regresses, and the reader literally watches the voice come apart — yet my sense is our attachment to Charlie doesn't track his scores at all, which looks like evidence against the capacity view, or maybe just a trick of narrative sympathy that tells us nothing about moral status (I worry it's the second). And then the ending: the last progress report, written after he's checked himself into the Warren home, asks whoever reads it to put some flowers on Algernon's grave in the back yard, if they get a chance. A mouse. Tiny brain, no syntax, and the book closes on a memorial gesture for him — while the genius Charlie, mid-book, looked down on the pre-surgery Charlie as almost a different person, and the novel presents that as a kind of blindness on his part. So which is it: does the novel end up saying moral status sits somewhere other than cognition, or am I loading more weight on a graveside gesture than it can bear? I keep feeling like I'm overreading the flowers, and I'd rather find that out now than in front of a room of students.

Which scenes would you actually point a class to, and where does my framing strain — I'd like you to say so plainly rather than prop up my reading.
