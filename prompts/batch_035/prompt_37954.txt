I have been trying to work out, from my own reactions to cases, when the observation of instances confirms a universal hypothesis, and I would like to know what principle, if any, my verdicts add up to. The background is the old puzzle about grue: call something grue if it was examined before the first of January 2030 and is green, or was not examined before then and is blue. My verdicts, for what they are worth:

(1) Every emerald anyone has examined so far has been green. I take this to confirm the hypothesis that all emeralds are green, and not to confirm the hypothesis that all emeralds are grue — even though the two hypotheses are satisfied by exactly the same observations made so far, and differ only about stones examined later. Mere definitional availability of a predicate is not enough; if someone coins 'blem' tonight to mean green-or-own-a-scales, I do not think green emeralds confirm that all emeralds are blem.

(2) At the same time, I hold that whether evidence confirms a hypothesis is a fact about the evidence and the hypothesis, not about which words anyone happens to use. If a community across the river describes the situation with different vocabulary, their confirmations cannot come apart from ours unless something about the describers has somehow become part of confirmation itself.

(3) Yet I cannot bring myself to say that a community which, from the very beginning of their language, learned 'grue' and 'bleen' as their basic colour words — never having our 'green' and 'blue' at all — never managed to confirm anything about colours. Their miners find grue emeralds; their evidence confirms, in their mouths, that all emeralds are grue. Telling them their inductions never got off the ground seems absurd.

(4) But now the same reasoning pulls me towards saying that a long-established astrological community's observations confirm that all persons born under Mars are hot-tempered, since their zodiacal predicates are as entrenched in their practice as 'green' is in ours. And that I flatly refuse to say.

(5) Time-indexing cannot be the mark of the bad predicates. 'Everyone born in 1950 will die before 2110' mentions dates and is surely confirmable by the deaths so far, being just ordinary mortality restricted to a cohort. Meanwhile, I reach into a bag of coins and find the first fifty all silver; 'all the coins in this bag are silver' seems confirmed on the spot, though I invented the restriction to this bag five minutes ago, so a long history of past use cannot be necessary either.

(6) One more: I take it that the compound hypothesis 'all emeralds are green and all rubies are red' is confirmed by finding green emeralds and red rubies, even though the compound predicate was never used by anyone before I formed the hypothesis.

I have a tentative principle of my own — that a predicate is projectible when it plays a role in a practice of projection that has earned its keep — but I cannot make 'earned its keep' precise without either collapsing into past success (which the coin case refutes) or into present usefulness (which the astrology case refutes), and I cannot see how anything of this shape reconciles verdicts (2) and (3). So: is there one account that captures all of these verdicts, or close? Please state it precisely, clause by clause, with the verdict that motivates each clause; tell me what it predicts for a case I have not listed, namely what happens after 2030, when an emerald is finally examined and found green — which hypotheses, if any, does that confirm; and say honestly which of my verdicts your account cannot accommodate. I have accepted that something has to give; I want to know what, and what I should pay for keeping the rest.
