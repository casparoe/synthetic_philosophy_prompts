I'm a retired pharmacologist -- thirty-odd years mostly doing dose-response work on analgesics -- and since retiring I've got properly into moral philosophy, which turns out to be harder than anything I did in the lab. Here's the position I've ended up holding, and I want you to tell me what would count against it, because I'd rather find out now than defend it badly at my philosophy reading group.

The position is prioritarianism: benefits to the worse-off matter more, in themselves, not because of any further effect. My route in was honestly my day job. Everything in pharmacology has diminishing returns -- the first milligram does most of the work, the tenth barely registers -- and for a long time I just assumed morality would treat welfare the same way. First unit of welfare to someone at the bottom buys more moral value than the tenth unit to someone near the top. That still seems right to me, but I'm aware I imported it from somewhere it may not belong, so tell me if that origin story is itself a reason for suspicion.

The obvious thing I already know is coming is the levelling-down objection: if you can either leave two people at 10 and 90, or level everyone down to 10 and 10, my view says the second outcome has something better about it, and most people find that flatly absurd. Here's my honest position on it: I half feel the pull of biting the bullet and saying yes, there IS something better about equality even when no one gains anything, and I can't tell whether that's me being brave or me having confused myself. So walk me through it.

Specifically, I want the defeaters sorted by how much damage they'd do, and phrased for someone who knows the science but is still shaky on the philosophy. A few things I keep chewing on:

(a) I gather prioritarians insist their view is NOT just utilitarianism with a concave welfare function bolted on. Fine, but then what work does the extra bit do? If the only difference is which curve you draw on the same axes, I'm not sure what my position is over and above a utilitarianism I never signed up for. Is there a case -- realistic, not science fiction -- where the two views actually come apart in what they tell me to do, in a way that isn't just the concavity doing the work?

(b) Someone at the reading group said there are results showing prioritarianism and utilitarianism become behaviourally indistinguishable once the utilitarian's utility function is allowed enough wiggle. If that's right, does it count against my view, or does it just mean the label doesn't matter? I genuinely can't tell, and the answer changes whether I should bother defending this at all.

(c) The levelling-down thing again, but more precisely: is there a version of prioritarianism that simply concedes levelling down never makes anything better, and survives that concession? I've picked up that people distinguish between saying equality is good in itself versus something about how we ought to treat people, but I keep losing the thread of why the distinction helps.

Also: I'd like it if you'd seperate what's actually settled from what people who know this stuff are still fighting about. I'm new enough to the field that I can't always tell a genuine consensus from a well-argued minority position, and I'd rather be told "this bit is contested, here's roughly who's on which side" than handed contested claims as if everyone agreed. And if your overall verdict is that my position is so well insulated by qualifications that it can't really lose -- tell me that too, and whether that's a problem or just how ethics works.
