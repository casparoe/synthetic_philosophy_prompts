I hold a computationalist view about which systems matter morally, and before I rest anything on it, I want to know where it can break.

The position, stated carefully: what makes a system morally considerable is the information processing it actually performs — the functional organization of its internal states — not the medium that carries it. Neurons, silicon, or anything else are on equal footing so long as the same computations run. This is meant to cut three ways at once. Substrate is out: nothing about carbon as such confers standing. Provenance is out: a system does not matter more for having evolved by natural selection than for having been designed on purpose. And surface behavior is out: producing the right words or passing some test is at most evidence; the standing comes from the processing underneath, which is why an excellent actor with the wrong internals would lack it and a mute, immobilized system with the right internals would have it.

What troubles me is that this seems to leave the real question unanswered. Call it the attribution problem: a single physical system is describable as computing many different things at many levels of grain, and only some of those descriptions are supposed to be morally loaded. If nearly any mapping from physical states to formal states is permitted, then almost anything computes almost anything — including, on some perverse description, whatever it is that suffering is made of. Conversely, if the individuation criteria are drawn too tightly, we risk smuggling the answer into the definition. My position is only as good as its account of which computations matter, and I do not yet have one.

So what would genuinely count against the view, as opposed to merely annoying its defenders? I am interested in several layers of exposure. First, formal and metaphysical: the triviality results suggesting that an unrestricted notion of implementation collapses computationalism into vacuity — every open physical system realizing every finite-state machine — and whether the standard repairs (demanding counterfactual support, causal integration, or level-appropriate organization) rescue the position or merely relocate the gerrymandering somewhere less visible. Second, empirical: which discoveries about how biological minds work would show that the very same computation can matter or fail to matter depending on what realizes it, and, symmetrically, which findings would count as confirmation rather than mere consistency. Third, case-based: the thought experiments the position has to survive — fading and dancing qualia, the China brain, claims that even a human population linked by radio could instantiate the relevant organization — and, for each, what it actually shows about functional organization rather than about consciousness specifically. Fourth, moral: the costs the position commits me to that its own defenders would concede are costs. Wherever the individuation question gets resolved, some class of systems will fall inside the boundary that common sense wanted outside — simulations, partial emulations, systems where the morally loaded computation is distributed, duplicated, or run virtually — and I want to know which of those admissions are bearable and which poison the view.

I would also like an honest verdict on whether the view risks anything at all: does it predict anything that substrate-bound rivals (biological naturalism, say) and behavior-based rivals predict differently, such that some cross-species or cross-substrate finding could come out one way under my view and the other way under theirs? If the honest answer is that the position has been insulated — that every conceivable result gets absorbed as consistent with the right functional organization, properly individuated — say so plainly, and tell me whether insulation is disqualifying for a claim of this kind or simply the price of doing metaphysics. Rank what you give me: what should move a committed computationalist, what we would concede is awkward but survivable, and what we would refuse to treat as evidence at all. For what it is worth, the triviality result is the objection that actually keeps me up at night, and I would rather you press it hard than file it under standard worries.
