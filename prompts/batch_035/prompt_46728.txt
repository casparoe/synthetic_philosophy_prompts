So I run a small podcast on ML safety stuff, and next week I've got on a researcher who's spent the last few years arguing that debate between AI systems is how you get oversight of models that are smarter than you -- the setup where you have two agents argue opposite sides of a question and a human (or a weaker model) judges. She's done a bunch of the actual training experiments, so she'll push back on armchair stuff, which is great for the show.

I don't want the standard "so explain the proposal" first five minutes, we'll do that offline in the intro. Write me eight or nine questions that go at the actual weak points. Specifically I want at least one question each on:

- the judge problem. The whole thing leans on a weaker judge reliably picking the better argument, but there's plenty of psych literature showing people fall for confident nonsense. How does she know debate doesn't just reward whoever's better at exploiting judge biases, and what actually gets tested there vs what gets assumed.
- equilibria vs incentives. In self-play training you're not rewarding truth, you're rewarding winning. If both debaters learn that some strategically-useful lie is hard to rebut, why wouldn't they both just settle into a lying equilibrium where they take turns winning on it? I want a follow-up ready for whatever she says about honest equilibria being an attractor or whatever.
- whether debate can catch the thing she cares about. Like, ok, a debate surfaces flaws in a plan, but the debaters are instances of the same model that produced the plan -- the classic worry that they share blind spots. Is there any evidence debaters find mistakes their base model couldn't have found alone, or is it just redistribution of capability.

Also, if you have room, one question on what she thinks the debate framing does to models at deployment time, since we're clearly heading into a world where frontier models get trained to persuade. Does she lose sleep over that or is it out of scope for her.

Make the questions sound like a person asking, not a syllabus, and keep each one answerable without her needing slides -- our listeners are mostly engineers who'll click off if it gets too academic. Thanks!
