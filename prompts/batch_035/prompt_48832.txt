I teach a small seminar called "rational choice and its critics" — 16 undergrads, mix of philosophy and econ majors, 75 minutes per session. Next week is revealed preference and the meaning of utility. By then they'll know the basic Samuelson move (preferences just ARE whatever you consistently choose, utility is only a bookkeeping device for representing choices) and the standard pushback (choices can be constructed on the fly, sensitive to framing, to which options happen to be on the table, to order of presentation, and so on).

I want a game where they generate a preference ordering out of their own actual choices without being told that's what's happening, and then watch that ordering wobble or go circular once decoys and framing get layered in. Roughly three stages: (1) a clean preference elicitation disguised as something else — like a market for snacks or seminar perks, with a point scheme instead of real money, points converting to something they actually care about at the end of term; (2) a second round with decoy options and ordering effects, where the fresh choice data starts contradicting the round-one data; (3) a money-pump finale, where someone whose recorded choices imply A over B, B over C, and C over A gets walked around the cycle, paying a small point fee at each trade.

Then the debrief. I want it to land on the real question, which I can't quite get them to see from the readings: if the choices ARE the preferences — if there's nothing under the choices — then in what sense was any of tonight's data incoherrent? Calling a choice pattern irrational seems to smuggle in a standard that lives somewhere other than the choices, which is exactly what revealed preference was supposed to deny. Maybe they can escape by saying only *stable* choice patterns count as preferences and the rest is noise — but then I want the debrief to force them to say where the stability line is, since they just watched stability evaporate under trivial interventions.

Give me the full design: rules, materials, the point scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline that fits 75 minutes, and the debrief script with the specific moments of the game it should map onto. Two more things. First, tell me what predictably goes wrong on a first run — I assume a couple of econ kids will smell what's happening in stage 2 and start playing against the elicitation, and I need a way to handle that without ruining the data for everyone else or making the defectors into teachers' pets. Second, be honest about which part of the revealed-preference debate this activity just cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply in the debrief — my guess is that real-world preference data gets generated across a lifetime by a world that mostly isn't trying to pump you, but maybe you see a deeper one.
