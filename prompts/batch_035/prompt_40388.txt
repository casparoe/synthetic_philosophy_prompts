I am a graduate TA grading a stack of midterm exams for a second-year course in philosophy of mind. One question, worth 25 of the exam's 100 marks, asked students the following:

"Philosophers disagree about what emotions are. Some treat them as judgments, some as perceptions, and some as feelings. Explain what each of these three families of view claims, using fear of a dog as your running example. Then explain what recalcitrant emotions — emotions that persist in the face of the subject's own contrary judgment, such as fearing a dog one knows to be harmless — are supposed to show about these views, and assess which of the three handles them best."

Below is one student's answer, copied verbatim. Please grade it for me. Work through the answer claim by claim: for each substantive claim, state whether it is correct, and where it is not, say what the correct version is and how far off the student is — I want to be able to justify the mark to the student if they contest it, so flag anything a hasty reader would let slide but shouldn't. Then give a mark out of 25 with a one-sentence justification, and finish with two or three sentences of comments suitable for the margin or the end of the paper. The whole response must be at most 700 words; if that forces you to be terse, be terse — I would rather have tight point-by-point comments than an essay.

Here is the answer:

"William James's feeling theory says that an emotion is the perception of bodily change: we don't tremble because we are afraid; we are afraid because we tremble. So the emotion comes after the body's response, and the object — the dog — enters only as the thing that set the response off. Judgment theories, like Robert Solomon's, agree about this order but add that emotions have content: my fear of the dog just is my judgment that the dog is dangerous. Perceptual theories, associated with Christine Tappolet and others, say instead that emotions represent evaluative properties directly: fear represents the dog as dangerous, roughly as vision represents a surface as red.

Recalcitrant emotions are cases where the emotion conflicts with the subject's own judgment: I judge the dog harmless but still fear it. This is supposed to be a problem for judgment theories. If my fear just is my judgment that the dog is dangerous, then recalcitrant fear would mean judging that the dog is dangerous and harmless at the same time, and people are not irrational in that way. Perceptual theories do better here. Because the emotion is not a judgment but something like a perceptual appearance, it can persist while the judgment says otherwise, just as the Müller-Lyer lines look unequal to someone who knows they are equal. So recalcitrant emotions pose no problem at all for the perceptual view, and this decisively favors it over the judgment theory.

As for feeling theories, they have nothing to say about recalcitrance, since a feeling has no content that could conflict with a judgment. So the final ranking is: perceptual first, judgment second, feeling last."
