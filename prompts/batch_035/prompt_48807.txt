I teach a decision theory unit to a mixed group of philosophy and econ students — about 18 people, one 75-minute session — and I want them to actually feel the absent-minded driver problem instead of nodding along to it on the whiteboard. The setup they'll already have seen: a driver leaves a party at midnight and can't tell which intersection she's at, the payoffs are 0 for exiting at the first one, 4 for exiting at the second, 1 for going all the way home, and the ex ante best plan is to continue with probability 2/3 at each intersection for an expected 4/3. What I want the game to produce is the gap between what you'd commit to in advance and what looks right from inside an intersection, plus at least one participant voicing the seductive move of reasoning as if you're choosing for both nodes at once, and the group having to decide whether that move is dumb or secretly correct.

Design the game. My sticking point is the memory thing. Participants obviously remember how many intersections they've passed, and if they remember, it's not the same problem anymore — it's just an ordinary sequential decision, and the whole lesson evaporates. I toyed with handing out "amnesia cards" or shuffling people between identical-looking stations, but I suspect that just relabels the problem rather than solving it. Either fix one of those, propose something better (identical envelopes, running each driver as a committee where different people decide at each node, something I haven't thought of), or tell me honestly that it can't be done in a classroom and what the closest achievable approximation is and why it's close enough. Stakes should be real but not money — candy, points toward the participation grade, whatever works.

I also want a variant round with a randomizing device they calibrate *before* driving (pick a die-roll threshold, then have to live with it at every intersection), so they can compare committing to a threshold with deciding fresh each time and notice the pull is different. And some kind of spectator role — someone who knows which intersection it is and can shout advice — so somebody in the room has to confront that the spectator's advice is right at one node and bad as a policy.

Then the debrief: map the actual moments from the session onto the planning-optimal vs. action-optimal distinction and onto whatever the right diagnosis of the both-nodes-at-once reasoning is. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I already anticipate someone keeping tally marks on their hand, and the econ students trying to collude) and how I should handle each. And be honest about which feature of the problem no classroom version can reproduce and I'll just have to assert in the debrief.

Format: lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — which mechanic, if any, actually solves the memory problem and why — then the full design and reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate my setup back to me.
