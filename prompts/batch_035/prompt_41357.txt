I work at a charity evaluator that ranks interventions benefiting different people — deworming for children in one region, cash transfers for households in another, a lobbying effort whose beneficiaries are diffuse future people. Every ranking we publish presupposes an answer to a question our methodology section quietly avoids: how much of one person's welfare is worth how much of another's, and what makes that comparison anything more than a convention we adopted for the spreadsheet. Before I read another theory of interpersonal utility comparisons, I want the specification first: what any adequate account of them has to deliver, and for a fixed purpose — good enough to justify a ranking of social states to a donor or a health ministry, not just to settle metaphysics at a seminar.

Write the desiderata. At minimum I want the following covered, plus anything you think I'm missing:

The cases it must get right. These should include: the tinnitus-and-toothache case (is one person's year of constant tinnitus worth more or less than two people's years of intermittent toothache — and, crucially, is the answer a matter of truth or of stipulation?); the utility monster who reports astronomical gains from any resource transferred to him; the sour-grapes case, where someone's preferences have adapted to deprivation, so that comparing preference-satisfaction across people rewards the oppressed for their coping; and the swap case, where two people exchange complete life situations and the comparison should presumably come out antisymmetric.

The distinctions it must respect: between comparability of levels and comparability of differences (and why aggregation seems to need the latter); between cardinal and ordinal individual scales, and what licenses carrying the cardinality across persons rather than only within one; between welfare itself and whatever indicator we actually measure — money, health states, reported happiness — including whether the indicator's conveniences (an existing market, an existing questionnaire) are doing illicit work.

The positions it must engage rather than dodge: the ordinalist dismissal, going back to Robbins in the 1930s, that interpersonal comparisons are unscientific because no common unit exists; and the veil-style attempts to bootstrap comparability from a hypothetical impartial observer, given that the average-utility and worst-off variants of that device pull in different directions.

Then the part I most want: mark which desiderata are non-negotiable for my purpose and which are prejudices of one camp; flag any pair or subset you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable — if demanding full ordinalism with no interpersonal information makes the ranking impossible to construct, say so plainly, because that itself is a result I can take to my board. Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most work excluding the live contenders — extended preference theories, money-metric welfare, health-state utilities of the QALY sort, objective-good indices — and give a one-line verdict for each contender on which requirement it fails. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; the verdicts can be terse.
