For the past month I have been the uneasy owner of a genuine Newcomb case. An insurance broker has sent me a letter: he has forecast whether I will pay him $5,000, and he writes to tell me his forecast was that I will not pay; if he had forecast that I would pay, he would have disclosed that my house has termites, which it now turns out it does, at an extermination cost of $200,000 that my policy covers only if the broker knew in advance and never will now. His forecasting record is excellent -- across past clients he has correctly called payers payers and refusers refusers roughly nine times in ten -- and the letter invites me to prove him wrong. This is structurally Newcomb's problem with the boxes' contents held fixed, so I turn to decision theory, but I want to use it the way one uses precedent rather than the way one uses a slogan, and I need help doing that.

My department's decision theorist left for another university at the start of this term, and before leaving he emailed me a one-paragraph summary of his rulings in the cases he actually treated, so that I could carry on his advising. I paraphrase from memory, having reread the message several times: he was a resolute one-boxer in Newcomb's problem and one-boxed in the transparent-box version (where the second box is visibly full); on the blackmail variant now called XOR Blackmail, his write-up says a principled agent refuses the payment even though causal agents pay; and he held that when Death, met in Damascus, says he expects you tomorrow in Aleppo, you stay in Damascus rather than flee, since the trip is futile either way and only costs you the night's sleep. He left no note on cases with a merely fallible forecaster, and that is exactly the shape of my own case.

So my question is a two-part one, and I would like the parts kept distinct. First, the precedent-matching: what would he tell me to do about the $5,000? The natural hypotheses are that he was a consistent causal decision theorist (in which case, on the standard verdict table, he would tell me to refuse, but then his transparent-box one-boxing and his Aleppo ruling need explaining away); that he was a consistent evidential decision theorist (in which case, on the standard verdict table, he would tell me to pay, but then his refusal in the blackmail case and his Damascus ruling need explaining away); or that he was running some compromise -- evidential where the predictor is near-perfect, causal where it is not, or something subtler. Say which hypothesis best fits the four rulings I list, what each surviving hypothesis predicts about a forecaster who is right only nine times in ten, and which hypothesis is most likely on the balance of the evidence. Note also that his blackmail and Damascus rulings, on the usual telling, are the ones that run against pure evidential reasoning and the ones whose rationale most plausibly weakens as the predictor gets less reliable, and say whether that observation tilts the prediction toward paying or toward refusing in my case.

Second, and more honestly, the part that is keeping me up at night: I don't much care what my departed colleague would have said. I care what I should say, and the answer has to respect the one piece of arithmetic that no theory changes: in the broker's past runs, clients who later paid tended to be spared the $200,000 termite bill and clients who refused were not, so that the refusers have done worse on average by tens of thousands of dollars each; yet in this particular instance, where his forecast is already fixed and in my hands, paying the $5,000 loses me money outright, since the termites are mine either way. Give me your own recommendation, with the reasoning stated plainly: whether the right move turns on the broker's reliability, and if so, at roughly what level of reliability, if any, the answer flips. I would rather not have an answer that leans on the claim that I can influence a forecast that has already been made; if that is nevertheless where the strongest argument leads, say so and defend it, rather than smuggling the conclusion in through the phrase "already fixed."

Please write your answer in American English.
