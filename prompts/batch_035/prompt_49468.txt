I am a junior at a public high school and the chair of our Student Council's Philanthropy Subcommittee. Each semester we disburse roughly $4,000 raised through bake sales, a dance, and a change drive, and we also decide which local organizations may table at our spring service fair. Until now, decisions have been made by whoever argues loudest, and our faculty advisor — who has to sign off on whatever procedure we adopt — has asked us to replace that with something principled.

The problem is what "principled" should mean for us. In our philosophy club, a senior gave a presentation on a problem for consequentialism that I have not been able to stop thinking about: the idea that every action has consequences that ripple outward indefinitely, so that the total good or bad an act produces may be permanently beyond our ability to know — not merely difficult to calculate, but the kind of thing where two reasonable people tracing the same ripple can disagree about the sign of the outcome depending on where they stop counting. The examples were the unsettling kind: a famine-relief donation saves lives, but the saved lives then consume resources, emit carbon, and raise children who go on to do further things, good and bad, without end. If that is right, then even our advisor's sensible-sounding rule — do the most good you can verify — smuggles in an arbitrary stopping point, because nothing in the underlying theory says where the ripples stop mattering.

I do not want the subcommittee to react to this by becoming paralyzed or cynical, but I also do not want us to pretend the problem away. So my request is this: turn the worry into a decision procedure that a committee of fourteen- to eighteen-year-olds can actually run in a forty-five-minute meeting, twice a semester, with no philosophy training. Specifically, I need an ordered list of questions to ask about each proposal; what counts as a sufficient answer to each question; where the procedure returns a no, or sends the proposal back for more information; and where it must hand the decision back to us because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. Please mark those judgment points explicitly rather than hiding them inside the procedure, since we will be accused of rigging it otherwise. I would also like the procedure to say something honest about how far a member may legitimately press the cluelessness worry before it becomes a blanket objection to any decision whatsoever — one of our members now argues that every proposal is unknowable and therefore the money should just be split evenly among whatever anyone proposes, and I need a principled answer to him rather than a ruling from the chair.

Please then run the procedure on two proposals we actually face: first, a $2,500 donation to the local food bank's weekend backpack program, which quietly feeds students at our own school; and second, a $2,500 donation to an international deworming charity that another member researched, whose pitch is that the same money helps vastly more children, just far away and in ways we will never observe. Include at least one point at which the procedure yields a result I will not like — I am on record supporting the backpack program — and say plainly where you expect the procedure to misfire or to be gamed by whoever wants a particular outcome. Please do not give us a reading list or citations; I need a document the committee can print, fold, and use.
