Settle a debate for me. My friend says clicking "I Agree" on a terms-of-service page you never read doesn't actually bind you, and I want to argue this Hume-style.

Here's my understanding of Hume on promises, tell me if I'm off: he thought promise-keeping is an artificial virtue — there's no natural sentiment that makes a mere resolution binding by itself. What makes it binding is a social convention: certain form of words, used in the right circumstances, signals a resolution and exposes you to penalty if you break it, and we approve of the practice because it's massively useful to society (no coordination, no trust, no economy without it).

So now suppose Hume got transported to today and had to judge clickwrap agreements: 40 pages of legalese nobody reads, where you signal agreement with a single click, and where half the clauses would be thrown out in court anyway. On his own theory, what would he say? Two things I keep going back and forth on:

1. Would he say the click is a real case of the convention doing its work — after all, the convention only needs a recognized signal, not actual reading, and it's still socially useful for software to be installable in ten seconds — or would he say the convention has become too degenerate to generate an obligation, since nobody even pretends the words mean anything?

2. And what would he say about the fact that the penalty side has basically withered away — you can't really be sued for breaking an EULA in most cases? If the convention is supposed to work via usefulness plus penalty, and the penalty is gone, does the moral force go with it?

Basically I want you to reconstruct his reasoning from the convention theory itself, not just give me a verdict. Also flag anywhere I'm misreading him.
