Write a dialogue between two evolutionary theorists: one who holds that the gene is the fundamental unit of selection — the only entity with the combination of fidelity, longevity, and quantity of effect that selection requires, with everything above the gene being a vehicle whose fate can, in principle, be fully accounted for at the gene level — and one who holds that selection acts at multiple hierarchical levels, and that which level dominates in a given case is an empirical question about the relative strength of within-group versus between-group selection, not something settled in advance by definition.

At some point fairly early on, introduce the following hypothetical case, constructed for this dialogue, and have both characters work through it seriously:

A clonal marine invertebrate forms colonies of genetically identical zooids that remain physically connected and share a circulatory system. Occasionally, a zooid lineage acquires a somatic mutation that causes its members to stop releasing digested nutrients into the shared circulation and instead shunt resources into their own gonads. Because the colony reproduces partly by budding, a defecting lineage can spread within a colony and can be carried into new colonies in budded fragments. Colonies in which defecting lineages have spread leave fewer and smaller colony-level offspring.

The two should disagree about whether the spread of the defecting lineage within a colony is best described as selection at the lineage level running against selection at the colony level, and about whether the colony-level process is genuine selection or merely the gene-level view with different bookkeeping. The multilevel theorist should press the claim that colony-level selection here is a causal process whose strength can in principle be measured — for instance by how the variance in total reproductive output partitions between differences among colonies and differences among lineages within colonies — while the gene's-eye theorist should press back that any such partition can be re-expressed without remainder at the level of genes, and should explain why she takes that re-expression to be more than notational, for instance by invoking what she thinks makes something a replicator. Neither character should be a strawman: each should concede what the other is right about, and the gene's-eye theorist in particular should not be written as denying that within-colony competition exists.

Two further requirements:

- The dialogue should have real back-and-forth: each character should respond to the other's actual arguments rather than deliver rehearsed monologues.
- The dialogue may end unresolved, but if it does, the residual disagreement should be located precisely — ideally by making contact with the distinction between a unit of replication and a unit of adaptation, or with the claim that multilevel and gene-level accounting are formally intertranslatable, so that the reader can see exactly what would have to be true for one side to win.

Finally, a request about honesty: if you think the hypothetical as I've described it is ill-constructed — for example, if you think it quietly collapses into an already-standard case, since somatic mutation plus clonal inheritance is very close to how we ordinarily think about cancer-like defection, or if you think I have stacked the setup so that one side has no reasonable reply — say so plainly rather than writing around the difficulty. And append a brief note, three or four sentences, after the dialogue stating where, if anywhere, you took liberties: for instance, where you put a real advocate's position in a stronger form than its actual proponents would accept, or where the biology of my hypothetical is under-specified enough that your characters had to fill in assumptions on my behalf.
