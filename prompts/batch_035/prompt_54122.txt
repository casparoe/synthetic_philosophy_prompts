I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy, and my dissertation chapter on Strawson's Freedom and Resentment (the 1962 lecture) has reached the stage where I need to take stock of the objections literature rather than keep accumulating it. My advisor and I keep circling the same disagreement about how much of Strawson actually survives his critics — she thinks the framework is half-ruined, I think it's mostly intact but aimed at a smaller target than people assume — and rather than litigate it verbally yet again, I want a considered ranking that I can push back against.

Could you rank the following five objections from most to least damaging to Strawson's position, where damaging means that it shows the framework to be wrong or vacuous, as opposed to merely incomplete or in need of trimming at the edges? First, the is-ought objection: even granting that reactive attitudes and the participant stance are psychologically inescapable features of human social life, inescapability is not entitlement, so Strawson at best explains why we hold people responsible, never whether we are justified in doing so. Second, the revisionism objection: the participant stance is not in fact inescapable, since people can and do shed reactive attitudes wholesale — through therapeutic effort, or by adopting something like a Stoic or Buddhist posture toward others — so the premise that we cannot help ourselves is empirically false rather than a framework assumption. Third, the exemption-line objection: Strawson's distinction between excusing someone (no ill will was manifested) and exempting someone (they are not a candidate for the participant stance at all — children, the severely mentally ill, those subjected to unusual strains) does no real work, because the framework offers no principled criterion for where the line between mere excuses and full exemptions falls, and the line tracks our squeamishness more than any feature of the agent's will. Fourth, the scope objection: since the reactive attitudes track quality of will, the framework can at most ground responsibility for intentional actions and attitudes, and is silent about outcome responsibility — the drunk driver who by luck harms no one, the good Samaritan whose inaction is only contingently costless — so it covers only a fraction of what our actual practices of holding responsible are about. Fifth, the quietism objection: by treating the pessimist's question — whether the practices are justified given determinism — as somehow resting on a mistaken picture of what would justify them, Strawson changes the subject rather than answering it, and the fact that the lecture is so often read as a rebuttal of hard determinism is a misreading the text does too little to prevent.

For each objection, explain in a few sentences why it lands where it does, including whether Strawson has the resources to answer it within the lecture itself or only with later help from other philosophers. Then give me a single number — a fraction of the framework's original ambition, where 1.0 means fully intact — for how much you think survives the single most damaging objection, and defend that number against the obvious alternative. My advisor claims the is-ought objection alone costs Strawson at least half his ambition; I think that objection assumes he was trying to justify our practices rather than something subtler, which I would deny. Feel free to take sides between us, and say which of the five objections you think is most overrated by the literature as well.
