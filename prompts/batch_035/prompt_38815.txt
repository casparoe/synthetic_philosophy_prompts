I'm taking a social choice course this semester, and we just spent two weeks on Sen's impossibility result, the one people call the Paretian liberal or the liberal paradox. I actually really like the prude/lewd example he uses: two people, one copy of Lady Chatterley's Lover, and the book either goes to Lewd, goes to Prude, or gets thrown out unread. Lewd ranks Prude being made to read it above reading it himself, and reading it himself above nobody reading it. Prude ranks nobody reading it above himself reading it, and himself reading it above Lewd reading it, because for Lewd to enjoy it would be the worst outcome of all. Now if each person gets to be decisive over their own reading -- Lewd decides between Lewd-reads and nobody-reads, Prude decides between Prude-reads and nobody-reads -- the rights-respecting outcome is Lewd reads. But both people strictly prefer Prude reads to that. So respecting the two personal spheres lands you somewhere that is Pareto-dominated, and that's the theorem: no social decision rule can satisfy both minimal liberalism and the Pareto condition over all preference profiles.

Here's my problem. My flatmate thinks the theorem is much bigger than Sen says, and laid this on me over dinner. (1) In the Lewd case, respecting rights produces an outcome that both people rank below an available alternative, namely Prude reads. (2) A social rule whose effect, in a given case, is an outcome everyone subject to it prefers not to have, cannot be justified to those people by appeal to their own values. (3) Rights are supposed to be justifiable to the very people they bind -- that's supposed to be the whole point of calling them trumps. (4) The preference structure that generates the Lewd case can arise around literally any personal sphere, since anything you're entitled to decide is something others can care intensely about. (5) So every right is hostage: whenever enough preferences line up the right way, exercising it makes things Pareto-worse, and by (2) the right stops being justifiable exactly then. (6) Therefore rights are at best permissions that lapse whenever other people's preferences stack up against their exercise, and a permission like that isn't a right at all -- it's just leave we've been given, revocable without anyone being wronged. He thinks the conclusion is fine, honestly. I think it's crazy -- it would mean my religion, my reading, who I sleep with, is mine only on sufferance of a preference lottery. But I've gone through the steps and I can't point at one without losing something I want to keep: (1) is just the theorem, I don't want to deny (3) because the justification-to-the-bound idea is what makes rights morally special, and my only half-answer to (4) is to say meddlesome preferences shouldn't count, except my neighbor's distress about my purple house is also "about" my sphere and I don't think that one's meddlesome, so the line keeps sliding.

So tell me which premise to give up, or which step is doing something sneaky that I'm not seeing -- maybe something shifts between rights as claims about how outcomes get ranked and rights as control over decisions, since it occurred to me that in the story nothing actually stops Prude from reading the book of his own free will, which would get everyone to the good outcome through the rights rather than around them. Walk me through the diagnosis: why the premise I have to drop looked fine in the first place, what it costs in cases outside this one, and whether my flatmate's argument, patched, still proves something interesting.
