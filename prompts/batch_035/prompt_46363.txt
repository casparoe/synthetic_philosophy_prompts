A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about Berkeley's master argument — the one that invites you to conceive of a tree existing unperceived, observes that in conceiving it you are perceiving your own idea, and concludes that an unconceived tree cannot so much as be conceived, and so cannot be. He thinks the argument is a trick with quotation marks, a confusion between using and mentioning. I kept reaching for the distinction between the act of imagining and what is imagined: the imagining is mental, but the tree in the imagined content is represented as standing there, mind-independent, whether or not anyone walks past it. He kept replying that however I specify the content, I am the one doing the specifying. We ended exactly where we started. Write a villanelle about that standoff. The two refrains should be the two moves: one voices Berkeley's turn (however I think the tree, I am the one thinking it), the other the objector's reply (the thought of the tree is not the tree that is thought of). Let them alternate through the five tercets as they did through our evening — each time one seems to have disposed of the other, it returns verbatim, and the form should make that iterability itself the poem's subject. The two refrains should meet, unreconciled, in the closing couplet. Somewhere in the tercets, work in the feeling that the argument proves too much — by the same reasoning nothing could be past, or unnoticed — and, if you can manage it without pastiche, the thought that for Berkeley the tree was never after all unperceived, since it is held in the mind of God. Let neither refrain win; that is honestly where the argument left us. Someone who knows the act/object distinction should be able to tell which line is which and why they can never settle it, and someone who doesn't should still find it a good poem.
