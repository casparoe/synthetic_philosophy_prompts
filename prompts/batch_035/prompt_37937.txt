I teach a mid-level undergraduate seminar on philosophy of history and historical methodology — sixteen students, mostly philosophy and history majors, meeting once for eighty minutes. We've just finished a unit on the tension between contingency-centered and structure-centered explanation: the folk intuition captured by the old nursery rhyme about the battle lost for want of a horseshoe nail, set against Tolstoy-style suspicion of great-man narratives and against historians who insist that deep institutional or economic forces would have produced roughly the same outcome no matter whose horse threw a shoe. I want the students to feel, in their bones, both the pull of the small-cause intuition and the disciplined suspicion of it, before we turn to the secondary literature next week. A colleague in the math department keeps telling me the whole debate is really about amplification — about whether social systems are ever actually "poised" in the way a chaotic physical system is, or whether that's an abuse of dynamical-systems vocabulary — and I'd like the activity to put the students in a position to have that argument with each other rather than settle it for them.

What I want is a simulation, not a discussion exercise. My sketch: the class is split into four groups, each running the same stylized crisis through several decision rounds — I'd like it modeled loosely on a July-1914-style mobilization spiral, but with invented states, invented leaders, and invented treaty networks, so that nobody can litigate the actual historiography instead of engaging the structure of the situation. Each group makes decisions with limited and delayed information under a schedule that punishes hesitation. In round one, a die roll (or some other seemingly trivial mechanism) injects a micro-perturbation into exactly two of the four groups — something genuinely small, like a delayed telegram, a misprinted timetable, or a minister's sprained ankle — and the rules should be built so that this perturbation can be amplified into something large or damped into nothing depending on choices the groups themselves make. Crucially, no group is told whether it received a perturbation, and the four resulting post-histories are compared only at the end; the divergence itself should do the teaching. Please give me the full rules, the materials, the round-by-round timeline, how information flows, and the payoff or scoring scheme (which shouldn't require real money or grades).

For the debrief, I need the mapping made explicit: where the trivial event sits as a proximate cause versus the standing structural conditions that did the amplifying; the difference between a system merely being complicated and one where small differences compound because self-reinforcing mechanisms — mobilization schedules, alliance credibility, loyalty cascades — are already in place, so that the nail never acts alone. Then three honest notes. First, tell me what the activity cannot reproduce: my own worry is that the divergence it displays is manufactured by whatever ruleset you and I invent, and there is no fact of the matter about whether real history would have diverged, so the facilitator has to supply that epistemological caveat rather than letting the students conclude that the simulation proved contingency. Second, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I expect some groups will converge anyway, some will treat it as a strategy game and optimize rather than role-play, and some students will refuse the counterfactual enterprise on methodological grounds — and say which of these are genuine failures to fix and which are teachable moments to lean into. Third, in your design rationale, please separate what is settled among people who work on explanation in history (for instance, that professional historians disagree about how much weight individual decisions versus structural conditions carry) from what is genuinely contested (for instance, whether chaos-theoretic language is legitimate in historiography at all, or only a seductive metaphor), and don't present the contested views as consensus.
