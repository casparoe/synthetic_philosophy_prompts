In my reading group on the philosophy of time, one member keeps reaching for the same analogy whenever the thermodynamic arrow comes up: entropy increase is like shuffling a deck of cards. A new deck comes from the factory sorted by suit and rank; shuffle it a few times and it is scrambled, and no amount of further shuffling will ever return it to factory order — not because some force of disorder is at work, but simply because there are vastly more scrambled arrangements than sorted ones. He uses the analogy to do two distinct jobs. First, a deflationary job: the thermodynamic arrow is nothing but probability. The direction of time is not in the microscopic dynamics at all, which are (so he grants) time-symmetric; it lies entirely in the fact that macrostates have wildly unequal numbers of microstates. Second, a more aggressive job: the puzzle of the early universe's low entropy dissolves. Asking why the universe began in such a special state is, he says, like asking why a deck comes out of the box sorted — someone at the factory put it that way. If you find that answer perfectly satisfying for the cards, you have no standing complaint about the universe; and if you find it unsatisfying for the universe, you should admit the cards were never really explained either.

Please audit the analogy. I would like the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the microstate, the macrostate, the dynamics, the initial condition, and the factory — and then a careful accounting of where the mapping breaks. I am especially interested in which disanalogies matter for each of his two conclusions and which are harmless. Some candidates worth weighing: that shuffling is itself a time-directed process carried out by entropy-consuming agents, so the analogy may presuppose the very arrow it is meant to explain; that sortedness in the card case is a human-salient designation while thermodynamic macrostates are fixed by coarse-graining over positions and momenta, with the macrostate boundaries not a matter of what strikes us as neat; that for every shuffling sequence there is, in principle, a reverse sequence, so that any asymmetry must live entirely in the initial condition, and it is unclear whether the analogy can even represent that; that cards do not interact, so nothing in the analogy corresponds to collisions, mixing, or the spreading of energy through the degrees of freedom; and that the deck's state space is finite and discrete, with no conserved quantities constraining its evolution.

Then give me your verdicts. Does the deflationary conclusion about the arrow survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or does the analogy quietly smuggle in the temporal asymmetry it claims to reduce? And does the stronger conclusion about the low-entropy past survive — or does the factory, an external agent outside the system being explained, do exactly the work that makes the second argument look better than it is? If the analogy is doing more concealing than revealing at some point, say so plainly, and tell me either what a better analogy would be or what you take the failure to show about the constraints any such analogy would have to meet.
