I sit third in the speaking order on a seven-member hiring committee. Before each meeting, every member independently reads the candidate dossiers and files written notes, but the actual decision is made out loud, sequentially, with the chair calling on us in the same fixed order every time. At our last session, the first two members both endorsed the same candidate, and by the time the fourth member spoke, the vote was effectively settled. I had privately ranked that candidate in the middle of the pool: strong on paper, but with a worry about her that neither of the first two speakers mentioned, and that I only half-trust myself. By the time I was called on, I found myself saying something like "I can live with her," and the two members after me followed suit, one citing the "clear consensus." The final record reads as a unanimous recommendation, and it will be treated as one.

I have three questions, and I want them treated together rather than separately.

First, what should I actually say in that moment? If I voice my reservation straight out, I may derail a decision my colleagues are better placed than I am to make — for all I know, their enthusiasm reflects things in the file I underweighted. If I stay quiet or soften my view, my one piece of independent judgment vanishes from the record entirely, and what gets recorded as consensus was really the first speaker's assessment laundered through six rounds of deference. Is stating a softened version of my view dishonest in any sense that matters, given that my view itself has already been changed by hearing theirs? Where exactly is the line between revising my belief on legitimate evidence and misreporting what I believe?

Second, am I entitled to count my colleagues' agreement as evidence at all, given that the second speaker was himself reacting to the first? I can never fully separate how much of any later statement reflects the underlying file and how much reflects the statements before it, and I know the same is true of everyone else's view of me when I speak. Does that circularity undermine my deference, or is it still the best I can do with the information available?

Third, and most importantly for the group: the chair has floated changing our procedure so that we submit simultaneous written ballots before any discussion. I'm inclined to push for it, on the grounds that our current unanimity conveys almost nothing about the number of independent eyes that actually cleared the candidate. But some members argue that discussion improves judgment and that written ballots would just lock in first impressions. Before I take a side, I want to understand whether my own conduct in these meetings can be defended at all — whether there is some way of speaking that is both rational for me and useful for the committee — or whether the only real fix is to change the procedure so that people like me are never put in that position. If it's the latter, tell me plainly, and tell me what the fairest arrangement would be.
