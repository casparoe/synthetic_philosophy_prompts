I have no academic background in risk analysis or philosophy; I'm just someone who has been keeping a private list of my gut reactions about when caution should win, and I've started to suspect the list doesn't add up to anything coherent. I'd like to know whether there is a single principle behind my verdicts, and if not, which of them I should give up.

Here are the verdicts. Holding a new drug off the market until long-term safety testing is done: obviously right. The FDA reviewer who refused to approve thalidomide in the early 1960s while it was causing birth defects elsewhere: a hero, and proof that caution pays. Leaded gasoline and asbestos: we should have acted decades earlier than we did, so the same caution was right there, just late. Germany phasing out nuclear power after Fukushima: my gut says that was the right call, but the phase-out meant coal plants ran longer, coal pollution kills people, and I can't comfortably hold both positions at once. Golden rice, the genetically modified rice built to make vitamin A: my gut says don't rush a new crop to market, but every year of delay means children going blind, and I notice my gut doesn't register that as a cost at all, even though I know it is one. DDT: my gut says the reaction against it was an overreaction that cost lives to malaria, though I've read the actual history is more complicated, so I don't want the answer to lean on that story. And one feeling I can't justify: I regard it as far worse to approve a drug that turns out to harm people than to delay a drug that would have helped people at the same rate. The first feels like a crime and the second feels like nothing happened. I want a principle that either captures that asymmetry honestly or overrules it — not one that just inherits it silently.

My tentative rule has been: when one side of a decision is irreversible and the other can be undone, choose the side you can undo. But I've noticed something disturbing about it: which option counts as "irreversible" seems to depend on which status quo you treat as the baseline, and both sides of any real argument claim irreversibility for their own favorite catastrophe. I'd like the answer to face that directly rather than assuming the baseline is obvious.

So: is there one principle that captures all or most of these verdicts? State it precisely, tell me which verdicts it keeps and which it forces me to drop, and say what it predicts for a case I haven't listed — for instance, a proposal to release gene-drive mosquitoes to stamp out malaria, or to deploy solar geoengineering against climate change. I'd also like you to be explicit about whether harms caused by my own caution count as harms in the principle, or whether caution gets treated as a safe default that the rule never weighs. Please write your answer in American English, and don't simplify it on my account — I can follow an argument; I just can't supply the training.
