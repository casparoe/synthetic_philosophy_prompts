Last night a friend and I spent three hours arguing about abortion, and we ended in an unusual place. She defends Thomson's approach from "A Defense of Abortion" (1971); I am drawn to Marquis's future-like-ours argument from "Why Abortion Is Immoral" (1989). We noticed that in nearly every case we could think of, the two arguments point the same way: wherever Thomson's bodily-rights reasoning licenses abortion, Marquis's deprivation-based reasoning either licenses it too or is silent (unplugging the violinist is letting die, not killing), and wherever Marquis objects, the woman's burden is usually large enough that Thomson objects to forcing her to continue anyway. So we want a stress test of whether the two frameworks are genuinely rivals or merely orthogonal, and that requires a case built in the hard direction.

Please construct a thought experiment — one paragraph, realistic medical circumstances, no science-fictional machinery — in which Marquis's argument implies that an abortion is not seriously wrong while Thomson's framework implies that it is wrong. The natural suggestion, and you should engage it rather than dodge it: a pregnancy in which the fetus is known to have no future of value (say, a condition guaranteeing death within days of birth regardless of what anyone does), combined with a burden of continued gestation so small that aborting at that point looks like a refusal of what Thomson calls Minimally Decent Samaritan assistance rather than a refusal of Good Samaritan heroics. Constraints: the verdicts must fall out of the theories rather than being stipulated by the case's emotional framing; no reliance on rape, life-of-the-mother emergencies, or other devices that make permissibility trivially easy; and no smuggling in a fetal right to life, since Thomson's whole point is to grant that arguendo.

After the scenario, please do three things. First, walk through what each argument actually delivers in the case, taking care with the gap between Thomson's injustice and mere indecency: if the strict Thomsonian says the woman acts within her rights but indecently, explain whether that verdict can honestly be described as "wrong," and say how the case would need to be strengthened to close that gap — or whether the gap is unclosable in principle. Second, identify which single feature of the case is doing the work, and show what happens when you remove it (for instance, restore a normal fetal prognosis, or make the remaining gestation arduous). Third, tell me how a committed Thomsonian would attack the case, and whether the exercise supports my friend's parting shot that the two arguments never really compete because one concerns what is lost and the other concerns what is owed.
