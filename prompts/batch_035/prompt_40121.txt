I'm a volunteer commissioner on my mid-sized city's public art commission, and at next month's meeting we must choose between two finalist designs for a prominent downtown wall. One is a purely abstract composition of color fields; the other is a figurative mural depicting our city's 1930s labor strikes and carrying an explicit message about who the city belongs to. The commission is split. Some of us think art bought with public money should do work for the community — say something that changes how residents see their neighbors. The others say that once we demand a message we are buying advertising, not art, and that the commission has no business ranking which messages are worthy of a wall.

I have no academic background, but I've been turning this over on my own. I once read that in the 1920s W. E. B. Du Bois argued, against younger Black artists of his era, that for a group whose humanity is widely doubted, art is never just art: whatever one of its artists makes will be read as a statement about the group as a whole, so the artist and the patron might as well choose what it says. His opponents replied that this turns art into a poster. That exchange feels close to our disagreement, but I can't tell whether it actually settles anything for us or merely relabels it.

Please draft the short decision memo I'll circulate before the meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — one of the two designs, or a defensible alternative if you see one — give the losing commissioners reasons they could actually engage with rather than restating our mandate, present the strongest point on the side it rejects fairly, and distinguish which parts of the recommendation rest on the argument about how audiences read art and which parts are just policy judgment the commission could reasonably have made the other way.
