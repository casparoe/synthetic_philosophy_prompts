I teach an upper-level metaphysics seminar titled "Laws of Nature" and am writing the three-hour final exam. Please write one essay question on the dispute between Humeanism and anti-Humeanism about laws of nature — the Humean's claim that the laws are nothing over and above the total distribution of particular facts, cashed out via the Mill–Ramsey–Lewis best-system picture, versus the anti-Humean's insistence that laws govern or constrain the distribution of facts rather than merely summarizing it.

The question must be built around the following thought experiment, which should be stated in full within the question itself, so that students cannot get by on having memorized the standard literature: the Copyist's World — a world whose complete history of particular facts is, moment for moment and point for point, a perfect duplicate of ours, but where, by stipulation, every regularity is a colossal accident, nothing in it necessitates anything else, and had things gone otherwise in any one place, the rest of the world would have carried on exactly as it in fact did. Students should be asked whether each side can make sense of this stipulation, whether either side must deny that the stipulation is even coherent, and whether the disagreement that survives the case is substantive or merely verbal. The question should be worded so that reciting the standard positions earns little: the challenge is to say what verdict about the Copyist's World each view is compelled to reach, and what that compulsion reveals about the view.

Alongside the question, supply a model answer of roughly 700 words and a marking scheme worth 30 marks, organized around five must-hit points. These should include at least: (i) an accurate statement of the Humean supervenience thesis, including what "nothing over and above" commits the Humean to; (ii) what the Humean must say about the Copyist's World — namely that its laws are identical to ours, and why that verdict follows necessarily rather than optionally; (iii) the strongest anti-Humean use of the case as a modal datum, together with the Humean's best reply that the possibility intuition is itself theory-laden; (iv) the best-system-for-whom worry, i.e., whether simplicity and strength can be made objective rather than relative to minds like ours, and how this bears on the Copyist's World; and (v) the anti-Humean's own burden — how governing or necessitation relations are supposed to be detected or known, given that they are not themselves part of the particular facts they govern. For each must-hit point, state its mark value, distinguish an A-quality treatment from a B-quality one, and add a one-line note on the mistake students most commonly make at that point.
