I'm a PhD student in environmental history, and a running dispute in my dissertation committee has crystallized around a metaphor that I now can't get out of my head. My advisor, who works on epidemic history, likes to describe the Americas after 1492 by saying that the smallpox virus was the most effective conquistador and that men like Cortés were merely its delivery mechanism; she'll similarly call the 536 CE eruptions and Tambora in 1815 sovereigns who redrew political maps, and she treats this as the corrective to older climate-determinist history in the Montesquieu and Ellsworth Huntington line, where non-human factors were scenery and only human decisions counted as action. Another committee member, who works on institutional history, insists the traditional picture was right in structure even if wrong in emphasis: germs and volcanoes are boundary conditions, like the stage on which human actors perform — immense, constraining, and mute — and my advisor's rhetoric just swaps which side of the stage/actor line things sit on without earning the swap. My chapter has to adjudicate this, and I'd like a genuine audit of the actor metaphor rather than a verdict I could have predicted in advance.

Could you lay out the correspondence table first: in the my-advisor picture, what is supposed to play the role of the actor's ends, its means, its allies, its adversaries, and its battlefield? Then identify where the mapping genuinely breaks — I'm especially unsure whether an entity with no capacity to respond to reasons, no representation of outcomes, and no sensitivity to blame can coherently be assigned credit for what happened through it, given that the epidemics traveled only along trade routes, siege camps, forced labor systems, and quarantine regimes that humans built on purpose. Which disanalogies actually matter for her conclusion, which ones are pedantic, and does her deflationary claim — that human decisions were mostly transmission infrastructure — survive once the broken parts of the metaphor are removed, or was the conclusion being carried by the agency vocabulary the whole time? Finally, if the actor metaphor fails as she uses it but the old stage metaphor was also wrong, is there a better comparison available — something that captures differential mortality, the timing of harvests, and the location of trade routes doing enormous historical work without attributing any of it to quasi-agents — and what would the two camps have to concede if we adopted it?
