You are a professional philosopher specializing in ethics and the philosophy of risk, with working knowledge of how environmental and safety regulation is actually written.

Here is a pattern of practice I want you to analyze. In most countries, environmental and occupational safety regulation works through thresholds: an agency sets a permissible concentration or dose for each pollutant, and emissions below the threshold are lawful, full stop. No one adds up the millions of individually negligible exposures produced by thousands of sources each operating lawfully under the limit; the aggregate burden simply has no legal location. Regulators also lean on the de minimis idea: a risk small enough is treated as no risk, not as a small risk. Yet the same legal systems, in toxic-tort and class-action contexts, will happily compensate people for individually tiny harms precisely by aggregating them — fractions of a cent per person across millions of claimants. And in criminal law, distributing a prohibited total harm across many victims (e.g., each victim receiving a sub-threshold dose) does not obviously dilute culpability the way the regulatory thresholds suggest it should.

What I want to know is what this pattern presupposes about the aggregation of tiny harms. Please begin by brainstorming candidate views before committing to one. Then reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can: does the threshold structure presuppose a genuinely non-aggregative, threshold-based view of badness — some harms simply do not count, so no number of them sums to a serious one — or is it only an administrative rationale (tracking costs, transaction costs, unmanageable causation questions) that a thoroughgoing aggregative utilitarian could also endorse? Distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it is merely consistent with. Say whether the parts of the practice cohere: is there really a tension between refusing to aggregate diffuse exposures in permitting while aggregating tiny losses in tort, or do these follow from a single consistent implicit view about aggregation across persons versus across time or sources? Identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, such that several rival theories of aggregation would all license it. Finally, tell me what regulation would have to look like if the presupposed view were replaced by straightforward aggregation of the sort at stake in debates about whether a single grave harm is worse than a colossal number of trivial ones — would permitting thresholds survive in any form, and what would replace them where they would not? Please think carefully and be precise; I care more about the quality of the distinctions than about a verdict.
