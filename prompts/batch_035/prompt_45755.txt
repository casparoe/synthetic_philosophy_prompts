You are a decision theorist specializing in sequential choice, and in particular in decision problems with imperfect recall. I want you to evaluate a classic puzzle and several proposed ways of defusing it, and I want the evaluation in the form of explicit rankings rather than a discursive survey. Please think carefully and do some private brainstorming before committing to your rankings; then present the final results cleanly.

The problem, stated precisely so that we share the setup:

A driver sets out at night along a route with two identical highway exits. She cannot tell the exits apart and cannot remember whether she has already passed one — she is, in the jargon, absent-minded. At each exit she must decide whether to continue or exit, and because the exits are indistinguishable, whatever she does at one she does at both; her strategy is therefore a single number, the probability p of continuing. The payoffs: exiting at the first intersection yields 1; exiting at the second yields 4; continuing past both yields 0. This is the setup introduced by Piccione and Rubinstein in the 1990s, with the numbers as I give them here.

Part 1. Rank the following five policies from best to worst by expected payoff under the payoff structure above, and show the calculation for each (a few lines of arithmetic suffices):
(i) always exit (p = 0);
(ii) never exit (p = 1);
(iii) continue with probability 3/8 — this is the value that maximizes expected payoff when fixed in advance, which you may verify;
(iv) continue with probability 1/2;
(v) continue with probability 2/3.

Part 2. The paradox. When the driver is actually at an exit, she knows she is at an exit but not which one; she must distribute her credence between the two. Reasoning from inside that vantage point, the policy that was optimal from the planning perspective can appear suboptimal — deviating from it looks profitable — even though she has received no new information. This tension between the planning perspective and the action perspective is what gives the example its bite. Rank the following four proposed resolutions in tiers (top tier / middle tier / bottom tier) according to how much you trust them to dissolve the paradox, with one or two sentences of justification each:

Resolution A (planning priority): the ex ante perspective is the only coherent one. The driver's inability to tell the exits apart is old news, not a discovery; the action-stage calculation that finds a profitable deviation is a computational illusion, and once done correctly it vindicates the plan.

Resolution B (one choice, not two): because the driver cannot distinguish the two intersections, there are not two decisions here but one. The action-stage reasoning errs by treating the continue-probability at each exit as something that could be varied node by node; whatever is changed at one occurrence of the choice is changed at both, and the apparent incentive to deviate evaporates once this logical connection is respected.

Resolution C (fragmentation): the problem is ill-posed as a single-agent decision problem. Absent-mindedness means the driver at the wheel is, for modeling purposes, a different decision-maker from the planner, and it is no paradox that two agents' recommendations come apart. The honest fix is to reframe the problem as a game among these agents and look for its equilibria.

Resolution D (revise the decision theory): the disagreement between the two perspectives is real, and it shows that something is wrong with the standard of rationality being applied. The right response is a decision procedure that, unlike either perspective, treats the multiple occurrences of the choice as logically linked from the start — even at the cost of abandoning the intuition that the agent standing at an exit should reason as if her choice there were isolated.

Part 3. A stress test for your tiers. Consider the variant in which continuing past both exits yields a payoff of 1 rather than 0 (exiting first still yields 1, exiting second still 4). Recompute the planning-optimal continue-probability for this variant, and determine whether the planning-versus-action tension persists or disappears. Then say whether this variant shifts your ranking of the four resolutions, and if so, which resolution it most weakens and why.

Conclude by identifying the single question you would most want settled, mathematically or philosophically, in order to be confident in your top tier — and what would make you reconsider your bottom tier.
