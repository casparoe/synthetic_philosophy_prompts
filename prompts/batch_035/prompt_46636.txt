I'm a software engineer with no background in philosophy, and I've recently come across Douglas Hofstadter's idea of superrationality: roughly, that if you and others face the same one-shot decision and reason about it the same way, you should act as if everyone will decide identically, and therefore cooperate even where defecting is individually optimal. My situation at work looks like a candidate case: several peer teams each independently decide how much of a sprint to spend on shared platform work — keeping the CI pipeline green, maintaining internal libraries, updating documentation nobody gets credit for — versus shipping features their own roadmaps are judged on. Most of my colleagues have never heard of superrationality, and some of them, if they heard it, would reject the reasoning outright.

Please describe the most important implications of superrationality for how I should split my time between the shared platform work and my own team's deliverables. In particular, I'd like you to address: (1) whether the superrational argument survives when the symmetry it relies on is broken by the fact that only some of the decision-makers find the argument compelling at all; (2) whether the argument changes once the one-shot framing is loosened — we face versions of this choice every sprint, but with a rotating cast of teams and no clear repetition against the same counterpart; and (3) whether there are recognizable configurations of my situation in which the superrational consideration actually points away from the shared work, and what those look like. Please reason from the idea itself rather than treating it as settled doctrine, and flag where you think the argument is doing real work versus where it's just a dignified name for ordinary prudence.
