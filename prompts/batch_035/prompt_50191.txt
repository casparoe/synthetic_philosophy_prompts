During my working life I was a physical chemist, and I grew accustomed to the idea that one and the same system admits of several descriptions: the trajectories of its molecules, its thermodynamic state variables, the gross behavior of the apparatus that confines it. None of these struck me as the "real" one to the exclusion of the rest; temperature does not become a useful fiction merely because it is an ensemble average. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy, and I gathered that Daniel Dennett's doctrine of the three stances says something in this neighborhood. A popular book I have been reading puts the view as follows:

"Dennett distinguishes three strategies for predicting a system's behavior. The physical stance applies the laws of physics to the system's physical state. The design stance assumes the system was built, or evolved, to do something, and predicts that it will do just that, without attending to the physical details. The intentional stance goes further and treats the system as a rational agent: it attributes beliefs and desires and predicts that the system will act so as to further its desires given its beliefs. Since only the physical stance describes how things actually stand in the world, the other two are instruments of calculation — convenient fictions, like the perfectly rational consumer of economics. A system genuinely has beliefs only in the way a constellation genuinely has a shape: the pattern is there to be found, but it is a pattern we project. On this view Dennett is a behaviorist about the mind: nothing in particular is going on inside you when you believe something; belief-talk merely forecasts what you will do."

Something in this strikes me as off, but the book is by a professional philosopher and I am a newcomer to the field. Would you go through the summary and tell me which parts a careful Dennettian would accept, which are simplifications of a defensible sort, and which misrepresent the position? I am particularly unsure about two points. First, the claim that only the physical stance describes how things stand in the world: my own training suggests that a description can be objective without being microphysical, and I had formed the impression that the phrase "real patterns" was introduced precisely to secure that. Second, the behaviorist conclusion: it appears to follow from the earlier sentences only if forecasting behavior is all the intentional stance does, and I suspect that inference is where the summary does its quiet work. Finally, if parts of the summary are indeed wrong, does the book's closing conclusion — that for Dennett, mental states are no more real than the shapes we trace in constellations — survive the corrections, or does it collapse along with them?
