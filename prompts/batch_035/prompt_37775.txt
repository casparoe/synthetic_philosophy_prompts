Hey! Quick bit of context: I write for the science-and-ideas section of a general interest magazine, and I've been running a loose series where I drag dead thinkers to the present and ask what they'd make of debates they never lived to see. Next installment I'm toying with: Simone de Beauvoir versus the so-called gender-equality paradox.

Recap in case it's been a while: the "paradox" is the finding that in countries that score higher on gender equality (think the Nordic countries, Switzerland), men and women diverge MORE in their choices of fields of study and occupations — the oft-cited version is that women's share in physics and engineering programs is, counterintuitively, lower in some very egalitarian countries than in places like Algeria or Tunisia. Now, I know the finding is contested (people fight about which equality index to use, about what those indices actually measure, about country samples), so assume for the column that the empirical pattern holds, but I want a line acknowledging the dispute rather than just treating it as settled fact.

So my ask: write the column channeling what Beauvoir would plausibly say, staying honest to her actual framework — the situation/freedom distinction, her insistence that one is not born but becomes a woman, her allergy to biology-as-destiny — rather than just putting generic feminist slogans in her mouth. Structure it as follows, in this order:

1. "Where she'd start" — roughly 150 words. Set up how her core commitments make this an awkward case FOR her, not an easy win.

2. "Her probable reading" — around 350 words. What she'd most likely say. Bear in mind the tricky part: her whole apparatus is about situation, and here the allegedly least constraining situations show the biggest divergences. Would she point at residual socialization (family, advertising, childhood), at the structure of paid work itself, or somewhere else? Pick a line and defend it as authentically hers.

3. "Where she'd hesitate" — about 200 words. Be fair — where would she genuinely struggle, and where would she refuse to draw a conclusion at all?

4. "My parting shot" — under 100 words. End the column with the one-sentence version of her take, stated as sharply as she'd have stated it.

Don't have her cite any studies or numbers — she couldn't have known them, and I don't want the pastiche to break.
