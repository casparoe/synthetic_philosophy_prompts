In our reading group, someone has been arguing that the Dutch book machinery, taken seriously, convicts central cases of scientific rationality. The argument runs: (1) An agent's credences should be probabilistically coherent, because any violation of the probability calculus leaves her willing to accept a set of bets, each individually fair by her own lights, that jointly guarantee a loss. (2) The same construction extends across time: if you now plan to adopt, after some future experience, credences other than those obtained by conditionalizing your current probabilities on that experience, a bookie who knows your plan can place bets before and after and lock in a sure loss. (3) It follows that any planned departure from conditionalization is irrational — including planning to revise in the light of a theory you cannot yet formulate, or a conceptual change that re-carves your probability space so that old propositions are abandoned and new ones introduced, since each such plan is a sure-loss betting pattern in prospect. (4) Yet wholesale revision of exactly this kind — acquiring new concepts, reassigning probabilities over a richer algebra, letting a new theory reshape what you take the live options to be — is a paradigm of rational learning, and a rationality requirement that forbids it would make inquiry as we know it impossible. (5) So either the method behind (1) is unsound or (4) is false. I would like to keep both (1) and (4) if I can, but I cannot see which step should go, why that step looked acceptable in the first place, or what rejecting it costs. In particular, does rejecting the diachronic step damage the synchronic argument in (1), or can the two be cleanly separated? Please answer in American English.
