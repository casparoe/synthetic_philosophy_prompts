Taking a phil of science class right now (unit on determinism, free will, that whole cluster) and I want to write my term paper about something I noticed that I can't quite untangle. So weather services will happily give you a day-by-day forecast for about the next week, but they flatly refuse to go beyond roughly 10 days — you just can't get a day-specific forecast for 3 weeks out, and the reason always given is chaos, butterfly effect, errors in initial conditions blow up exponentially so the forecast is garbage past the horizon. Ok, fine, that part I get, it goes back to Lorenz in the 60s.

But the SAME institutions (met office, NOAA, whoever) will publish seasonal outlooks, like "this winter is likely to be warmer and wetter than normal", and they'll project what the climate will be like in 2100, sometimes with regional detail. So the people who apparently cannot tell me whether it will rain on March 4th next month can tell me about the average March in 2050?? And there's a third thing too: forecasts used to just say "it will rain tomorrow" and now they say stuff like "60% chance of rain," which came in with ensemble forecasting where you run the model a bunch of times with slightly perturbed starting conditions.

What I want to do in the paper is reconstruct what theory of predictability (and of determinism?) this whole institutional setup presupposes. Like, does the practice only make sense if you hold (a) that the atmosphere is a deterministic-ish chaotic system where initial-condition error grows exponentially and swamps you past a horizon, AND (b) that climate is a genuinely different kind of target, constrained by slowly varying stuff (ocean heat, solar, greenhouse gases) so that even when the trajectory is unpredictable the statistics are not? Or is the 10-day thing just an empirical rule of thumb about what's currently tractable — i.e. the horizon could creep outward as models and observations get better and the practice would carry on unchanged, in which case it doesn't really presuppose any deep claim about determinism at all? I've seen claims both ways and I genuinely don't know which the practice commits you to.

Also the probabilistic shift: is going from "it will rain" to "60% chance of rain" just honest error bars layered on top of a deterministic system (the probabilities being about our ignorance of the true initial state), or does the practice quietly treat the probability itself as a fact about the weather rather than about us? And do the two halves of the practice — a hard limit for weather but confident projections for climate — presuppose compatible things about determinism, or is there a tension there my instructor would want me to poke at? Basically: I want to figure out which parts of the implicit theory the practice strictly NEEDS, which parts it just fits nicely with, and where it's underdetermined such that several different views of predictability would license the same institutional behavior. Bonus if you can tell me what a weather service run on a strictly probabilist view of evidence would do differently, since we just did a unit on Bayesianism in courtrooms and I think there's a parallel but I'm not sure.
