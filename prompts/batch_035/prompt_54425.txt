I teach a 300-level cognitive science seminar called Concepts and Analogy, and next week we start the unit on fluid concepts, built around the letter-string microdomain of Hofstadter and Mitchell's Copycat tradition: problems like abc → abd, ijk → ?, where the answer seems mechanical until the domain shifts under you — until the alphabet runs out, or a letter repeats, and the concepts doing the work (rightmost, successor, sameness, first-of-a-kind) have to bend for the answer to stay elegant. My students will have read none of this literature; I want them to feel, before anything is named, that the hard part of analogy is not matching fixed descriptions but rebuilding them on the fly. Design an in-class activity for 18 students, one 75-minute session, that accomplishes this through gameplay rather than lecture — the slippage should happen to them, in the rules and the scoring, not be described to them afterwards.

What I imagine — though please improve on it — is a team game played inside the microdomain itself. Teams write or bid on transformation rules, apply them to analogy items, and the payoff scheme has to be arranged so that a rigid rule-follower racks up points on the easy items and then visibly falls apart on the slippery ones, while a genuinely elegant answer is something the other teams reward in points (no real money). Seed the item set so it spans mechanical to treacherous: for instance, the pair abc → abd :: ijk → ? against abc → abd :: xyz → ?, where the first is routine and the second forces a choice between wrapping the alphabet, inventing a cycle, or moving the operation to a different letter entirely; and something like mrrjjj, where the natural answer requires slipping quietly between rightmost letter, rightmost group, and rightmost repetition — a slippage no one will have announced in advance. Give me the full materials list, about a dozen items with answer keys (flagging honestly which answers are contested and why), the scoring scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the parts of the idea: where the concept did the slipping, what the slippage cost, and why the elegant answer felt forced on the players rather than chosen by them.

Two more requirements. First, predict what will go wrong the first time I run it — I assume some team will try to game the scoring, and someone will demand the one right answer for a contested item — and tell me how to handle each failure without collapsing back into asking-the-professor mode. Second, be frank about the one feature of the phenomenon the activity cannot reproduce: my students will be making these slippages deliberately and out loud, with time to argue about them, whereas the whole point of the fluid-concepts view is that slippage happens fast, quietly, and below the level of argument. Tell me where that gap is and how the debrief should gesture at it without pretending the game closed it.
