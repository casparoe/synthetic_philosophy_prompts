I'm in a history of economic thought seminar and we just finished the socialist calculation debate — Mises's 1920 challenge, Hayek's knowledge papers, Lange's reply that a planning board doesn't need to solve thousands of equations simultaneously but could just run a trial-and-error procedure, raising prices where shortages appear and cutting them where goods pile up, until things roughly clear. Our professor has assigned us something a bit goofy for the final project: write the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a committed Hayekian and a committed Langean market socialist — picture two members of a reading group who've been arguing about this for a solid year and finally sat down to produce the document they should have produced months ago.

Format it as a numbered section of claims both sides can sign (about what the debate actually established, about the difference between a formal proof that an equilibrium exists and any practical procedure for finding it, about what markets demonstrably do well, whatever they genuinely share), then a disagreements section where each residual dispute is phrased so its own side would accept the wording — I'm thinking the Misesian's stronger claim that calculation under socialism is impossible in principle, not just computationally hard, versus the Hayekian's softer epistemic one about dispersed, tacit knowledge that no board could collect even in principle, versus the Langean's view that the trial-and-error procedure needs no perfect solver and no omniscient statistician.
