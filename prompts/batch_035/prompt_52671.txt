My grandmother was diagnosed with Alzheimer's about six years ago, back when she was still pretty much herself. Before things got bad she sat down with a lawyer and signed one of those living will / advance directive documents. This wasn't out of the blue — her own mother spent her final decade in a nursing home, barely responsive, kept alive with a feeding tube, and grandma made it clear to all of us over the years that she would rather die than spend her last stretch like that. The directive basically says no life-prolonging interventions once she can no longer recognize her family or express her wishes.

Now here's where it gets messy. She's in a later stage now, and last month she developed swallowing problems, so the doctors asked the family whether we'd want a feeding tube if things continue downhill. On paper the answer is no. But the woman in the bed doesn't seem miserable — she lights up for her caregiver, likes watching birds out the window, smiles at my mom. And in one semi-lucid moment recently someone asked her whether she wants to keep living, and she said yes, of course, why wouldn't she.

My uncle says the document is basically sacred: the woman who signed it knew exactly what she wanted, did everyone the favor of deciding while she still could, and if we second-guess it the whole exercise was pointless. My mom isn't so sure — she looks at her mother now and thinks the person whose life is actually at stake is *this* person, the one smiling at birds, not the one who signed a piece of paper six years ago, and this person appears to want to be here. I keep flip-flopping between them and can't put my finger on which consideration should actually settle it. Whose wishes should carry more weight, and why? Is there some principled way to think about this that doesn't just collapse into whichever version of my grandmother we happen to feel more emotional about?
