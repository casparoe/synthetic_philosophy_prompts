I sit on the editorial board of a general-interest science magazine with a long-running puzzle column. Our columnist proposes to revive a contest of a kind run once, to his knowledge, in the early 1980s. The mechanics: readers may submit any number of entries, from one to an effectively unbounded maximum; after the deadline, one entry will be drawn at random and its submitter awarded a prize that shrinks in exact proportion to the total number of entries received. Every entry a reader submits lowers the expected value of every entry anyone else holds, so if readers treat it as an ordinary contest, the outcome is an arms race of maximal submissions and a prize destroyed down to cents — yet if every reader submits exactly one entry, someone wins a substantial sum, and no one could have done better for themselves by any unilateral deviation the others don't also make. The columnist wants to run the contest alongside an essay arguing that the rational move, for readers prepared to trust that the other readers are reasoning as they reason, is to submit exactly one entry — he describes the whole exercise as a live experiment in whether a million strangers can deliberate as a single agent. One board member objects that the essay rigs the experiment: urging collective restraint is not observing cooperation but manufacturing it, and if the contest nonetheless collapses into a flood of maximal entries, we will have spent our readers' goodwill teaching them that restraint is for suckers. Another worries that the follow-up write-up risks reading as a lecture about human irrationality rather than as an honest result. Draft the decision memo I will circulate before Tuesday's meeting: recommend whether to run the contest at all, and if so in what framing — with the essay, with a neutral statement of the reasoning, or with the structure left for readers to work out — giving reasons the losing side could recognize as a fair treatment of their concern, and saying what a follow-up column should report either way. The memo should state plainly how confident it is in its recommendation and single out the one consideration it is least sure about.
