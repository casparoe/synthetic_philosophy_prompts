I help run an interdisciplinary reading group on mechanistic interpretability, and a colleague from computer science left half the room convinced of a conclusion I can't accept but can't manage to refute. His argument, as I reconstruct it:

(1) A state of a system represents F only if two conditions are met: information about F is recoverable from that state, and the system uses that information in producing its F-involving behavior. Call these the information condition and the use condition.

(2) Everything interpretability research offers as evidence for representational claims bottoms out in one of two kinds of result: decoding — training an external classifier (a probe) to read F off internal activations — and intervention — ablating, patching, or amplifying internal states and observing behavior.

(3) Take decoding first. A linear probe applied to the activations of a randomly initialized network of the same architecture decodes features of the input well above chance, since random weight matrices are close to information-preserving maps; and a probe with enough capacity, trained on randomly permuted labels, will drive its training accuracy arbitrarily high, because the probe is itself a learner. So above-chance decoding is a property of the probe-and-dataset pair, obtainable from systems that have learned nothing whatsoever.

(4) A signal that is present whether or not the system has done the thing the signal is supposed to indicate is not evidence that the system did that thing. So decoding accuracy, however high, is no evidence of representation.

(5) The obvious reply is that the trained network's accuracy exceeds the random baseline and that the margin is the evidence. But the baseline's very existence shows the raw score is partly probe artifact, and a margin over an artifact is not a representation; at best it is an uninterpreted statistical excess. To treat it as representational content, you would need a theory of what the baseline subtracts, and no such theory is on offer.

(6) Now the use condition. The only evidence for use is intervention. But intervention shows causal relevance only, and causal relevance is cheap in a network whose features are superposed: ablating almost any direction of sufficient norm changes behavior somehow, and whatever change you observe can be described in terms you invent after seeing it.

(7) So no probe result and no intervention result is evidence of representation, and these two exhaust the evidence interpretability could in principle supply.

(8) Nothing in the argument turned on gradient descent, artificiality, or scale: population decoding in neuroscience and lesion or stimulation studies in brains have exactly the structure of (3) through (6).

(9) Therefore nothing — artificial or biological — has ever been evidenced to represent anything, and the interpretability program is not merely unfinished but impossible in principle.

He is serene about (9): representation, he says, was always a loanword, and the program was misdescribed from the start. I want to keep (1), which nearly everyone in the room accepted without comment, and I half-suspect the trouble is somewhere in (4) and (5) — but every time I try to say what is wrong with (4), my objection starts to prove too much in the other direction, since it would apparently discredit any test that an untrained or unprepared control subject partly passes, which is half of psychometrics. Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked fine to a room of people who work on this for a living, and what rejecting it costs — including whether my colleague can simply accept your diagnosis, concede that step, and retreat to a defensible core of the argument.
