(b) Using Zorn's lemma, prove that every nonzero commutative ring with 1 has a maximal (proper) ideal.
(c) A classmate claims that in your proof from part (b), the fact that you're taking unions of chains of ideals — totally ordered by inclusion — doesn't really do any work, since "a union of ideals is an ideal either way." And here's what the student wrote:

(a) Zorn's lemma: if a partially ordered set P has the property that every chain (totally ordered subset) in P has a least upper bound in P, then P contains a maximal element. It is equivalent to the axiom of choice.

(b) Let R be a nonzero commutative ring with 1, and let S be the set of proper ideals of R, ordered by inclusion. S is nonempty because (0) is a proper ideal (since R is nonzero, 1 ≠ 0, so (0) ≠ R). Now let C be a chain in S and let U be the union of all ideals in C. Since a union of ideals is an ideal, U is an ideal, and U is proper since no ideal in C is all of R. So U is an upper bound for C in S, and Zorn's lemma applies, so S has a maximal element M. Since S is the set of all proper ideals of R, M is a maximal ideal of R. Hence every nonzero commutative ring with 1 has a maximal ideal.

(c) The classmate is right that the union is an ideal either way — ideals are closed under addition and additive inverses, and so is their union. Where the total ordering actually matters is the properness claim: if the ideals in C weren't totally ordered, the union could pick up the identity from somewhere and end up being the whole ring. So the ordering does do work, just not the work the classmate thinks.
