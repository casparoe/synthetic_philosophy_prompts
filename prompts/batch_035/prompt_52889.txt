Last week at my school's philosophy club, two members argued about the sorites paradox and could not be reconciled. We had begun with our teacher's example of a pile of biscuits: one biscuit is not a pile, and taking a single biscuit from a pile of two hundred cannot turn a pile into a non-pile; yet iterating that step all the way down would prove that a single biscuit is a pile. Maya said this shows that vague words such as 'pile' are incoherent: the reasoning is valid and the premises seem undeniable, so the word itself must be broken, and wherever anything important turns on it we should replace it with exact numbers. Dev replied that she was moving too fast — a valid argument from premises that cannot all be true shows only that some premise is false, and the interesting question is which one; he said the culprit is the second premise, because removing a single biscuit can make a difference: there is an exact cutoff between piles and non-piles, only nobody can know where it lies. Maya said an unknowable sharp line was worse than no line at all, since there is simply no fact of the matter about which number divides piles from non-piles. Dev answered that unknown facts are everywhere — nobody knows exactly how many biscuits were eaten in Britain yesterday, yet there is a true number — and he observed that Maya's verdict would make 'pile', 'tall', 'bald' and 'heap' all broken words, when plainly they serve communication perfectly well. Maya's rejoinder was that successful communication needs only rough meanings, as with directions telling you to continue 'a bit' down the road, and that Dev owes us an account of what makes his hidden cutoff fall where it does, which he cannot give. Our teacher declined to referee. Could you go through their exchange and say who is right at each step, not merely who wins overall, and tell us what, if anything, both of them are missing? Please take definite positions and give your reasons for them rather than setting out the options and leaving us to choose, and please answer in British English.
