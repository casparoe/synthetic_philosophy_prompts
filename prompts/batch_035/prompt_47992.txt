I'm taking a seminar on Bayesian epistemology, and we recently covered van Fraassen's Reflection principle — roughly, that my current credence in a proposition, conditional on my future self at a given time assigning it probability p, should itself be p. I've started cataloguing my gut verdicts on cases to figure out whether I actually accept it, and the results are messier than I expected. Tonight I'll sleep on a stubborn logic puzzle, and I predict that tomorrow morning I'll believe my answer is correct with about 70% confidence; I feel I should give that answer 70% tonight. Tomorrow a biopsy result arrives, and I'll believe whatever the lab reports with near certainty; again, I'm happy to defer now. But tomorrow afternoon I will have checked the stove for the third time and will believe it's off with a confidence far beyond what the third check warrants, and I don't think today's me should defer to that one. Nor will I defer to the version of me at midnight who has had a couple of glasses of wine and is certain my essay draft needs no further revision. After next week's exam, I'll have forgotten which of two similar-looking inference rules is valid; my future self will be genuinely uncertain where I now know, and there too I refuse to defer, even though that future self won't be drunk or compulsive — merely forgetful. Then there's the case I can't place: next month I'll read a long technical report that I know is slanted but that also contains real new evidence, and I can predict fairly well how much more confident it will leave me. I suspect I should defer part of the way to that future self, and I can't tell whether partial deference is coherent or just a failure of nerve.

What principle, if any, captures these verdicts? Is it Reflection itself with a restriction I haven't articulated, or the thought that I should defer only where my future opinion will arise from my present one by conditionalizing on new evidence, or something about which processes of opinion change I would endorse in advance — with the compulsive checking and the wine and the failing memory failing that endorsement test for different reasons? Please state the candidate principles precisely, say which of my verdicts forces which clause, and be explicit about what the cleanest principle cannot accommodate: I'm prepared to give up a verdict or two, but I want to know exactly which ones and at what cost in complexity. I'd also like the resulting account's verdict on two cases I haven't settled. First, my future self, after hours of chess analysis, will be convinced a sacrificial plan works, though I know my assessments of sacrifices are systematically overconfident — the analysis is mostly sound but predictably skewed in a direction I can name. Second, a colleague I know can argue any side of anything will tomorrow argue me out of a view I currently hold, and I expect to find his case persuasive at the time while suspecting I could rebut it myself a week later.

One request about the spirit of the answer: if you think these verdicts don't add up to any coherent principle, or that I've misunderstood what Reflection actually requires (for instance, that some of my cases fail its antecedent conditions rather than refuting it), please say so directly rather than bending a principle to rescue me. I'd rather learn that my gut is running two incompatible rules than be handed a barrel of epicycles.
