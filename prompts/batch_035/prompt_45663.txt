I teach intro decision theory to undergrads (about 15 students, one 80-minute session) and next week is the two-envelope paradox. I'm tired of just writing the 5A/4 expected value on the board and watching them nod, so I want a game where they fall for the argument themselves before they see where it breaks. My idea: instead of me filling the envelopes, each student secretly writes down any positive dollar amount (cap it at $20, I'll pay out real small amounts or use tokens). Then I pair them up, flip a coin to pick one student's number as X, and put X in one envelope and 2X in the other. Each student gets one envelope, looks at their amount, and can pay a small fee to swap. My hunch is the reasoning "my envelope has A, the other has 2A or A/2, so switching gains me A/4 in expectation" will feel airtight to them — even though with this filling procedure always-switching obviously can't help on average. I want the debrief to take them from "the math lied to us" to "the math was never about this distribution", i.e. that the textbook argument smuggles in a prior over amounts that no real procedure for stuffing envelopes actually produces. Design the whole thing: rules, where they do the expected value calculation, what I keep track of on the board, and how to run the debrief. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time (I'm guessing someone writes $0.01, or people try to coordinate on numbers with their friend they got paired with) and be honest about what the game just cannot show — like whether the switching argument is actually fine under some genuinely weird prior, which I dimly remember being a real subtlety and not just a technicality.
