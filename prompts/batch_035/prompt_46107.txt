I've been working through Parfit's teletransporter material in a metaphysics course, and rather than accepting anybody's theory straight off, I've been keeping a list of my actual gut verdicts on cases, hoping they add up to something. Here they are. The ordinary machine on Earth scans me, destroys me, and builds a replica on Mars: I say I died, even though the replica sincerely remembers being me and everyone on Mars treats her as me. The faulty version, where the machine fails to destroy me and I live on for a few days while the replica is already walking around on Mars, before the malfunction kills me: I say I survived those few days, and my death shortly after doesn't retroactively change that. A surgeon replaces my neurons with functionally identical silicon chips one at a time over several months, with me awake throughout: obviously I survive. I'm resuscitated after twenty minutes of cardiac arrest with my memories intact: trivially survived. I come out of a long operation having formed no memories of it: still me, of course. My grandmother with advanced dementia no longer remembers most of her life: still her, though it seems something has been lost that isn't lost in the anesthesia case, and I can't say what.

Now my own additions, because the textbook cases all run on the same rails. My phone backs me up: suppose a company keeps a rolling scan of my brain with a one-week lag. I die in a crash on Tuesday; on Wednesday they print the backup, which remembers everything up through the previous Tuesday. I want to say I partially survived — survived with a week missing, the way you survive a night you don't remember — but nothing in the psychological-continuity story marks that gap, since my memories causally produce the replica's just as surely here as in the ordinary Mars machine. A variant that bothers me more: the scan is taken at thirty, stored, and printed only after my natural death at seventy-eight, so the replica wakes up being entirely me-at-thirty with no idea the intervening half-century happened. Psychological continuity is as tight as ever — tighter than in the dementia case — and yet this feels like a biography being reissued rather than me surviving. And fission, where one scan prints two replicas: that feels flatly like death, though each replica alone would apparently have been... what? I genuinely don't know whether to say each would have been me, or neither, or to stop asking the question.

My tentative thought is that what matters is an unbroken, forward-moving chain — there being someone alive who remembers being me from the inside, at every moment — rather than identity itself. That would explain the faulty machine (the chains overlap; the replica's arrival interrupts nothing) and perhaps the week-lag backup (interruption), but it does nothing for the reissued-at-seventy-eight case, whose chain is also unbroken if you count storage, and it sits awkwardly with the anesthesia and dementia verdicts. So: is there one principle — identity-based, continuity-based, Parfitian in making what matters something other than identity, or something you think better — that captures all or nearly all of these verdicts? I want a committed answer, not a survey of the options: state the principle precisely, say which of my verdicts it saves and which you would tell me to give up and why, and then take a definite stand on the cases I haven't listed — whether the replica should be charged with my crimes, whether my wife would be widowed, and whether, on your principle, I should agree to step into the machine.
