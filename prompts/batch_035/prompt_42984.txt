Last weekend a friend hosted a dinner that turned into a two-hour argument between two of her guests — a historian and a physician — about whether the world is becoming less violent. I stayed out of it, but I've been turning it over since, and I'd like a verdict on how the exchange actually went.

(1) The historian opened by claiming the long-term trend is unambiguous: the chance that a randomly chosen person dies violently — in war, homicide, or judicial execution — is far lower today than in any earlier era, and has been falling for centuries.

(2) The physician objected that this holds only under a narrow definition of violence as deliberate physical killing. If you count harms the modern world inflicts indirectly — deaths from preventable disease, dangerous working conditions, and above all the tens of billions of animals killed each year in industrial farming — the claim collapses or reverses: by raw numbers, the present may be the most violent period in history.

(3) The historian called this gerrymandering: on that definition almost every bad outcome counts as violence, the word loses its meaning, and the original claim becomes vacuous rather than refuted. A definition that counts a death from unsafe machinery as violence will also count deaths from legal products people freely use, and at that point the debate is about how much badness there is, not about violence.

(4) The physician conceded a line must be drawn somewhere but said the historian's line is equally a choice: it counts the forms of harm that weapon-wielding humans inflict directly — exactly the forms the modern world has become good at suppressing — while excluding the forms it has industrialized. Whoever controls the definition controls the conclusion, and "narrow" is not the same as "neutral."

(5) The historian then turned to denominators: even granting the narrow definition, one must choose between per capita rates and absolute numbers, and he argued per capita is the right measure when the question is how dangerous the world is for a typical person. Absolute totals answer a different question — how much suffering the world contains — which population growth can change independently of how violent any society is.

(6) The physician replied that the dead do not care about denominators: a world in which two hundred thousand people die violently each year is, in one plain sense, more violent than one in which fifty thousand did, even if its population is ten times larger, so the choice to report the flattering ratio is itself taking a side. He then went further: perhaps there is no fact of the matter at all, since "the world is becoming less violent" is not about a natural kind, and whether it is true depends on how one weights kinds of harm — a moral judgment masquerading as an empirical one.

(7) The historian closed by insisting not everything is definitional: some trends are robust under any reasonable definition — falling homicide rates, the abandonment of once-accepted practices like judicial torture — and definitions are not all equal, since one that makes "violence" track nearly every bad outcome is a worse definition than one that doesn't. Fix a sensible definition and there is a fact of the matter.

They ended where they began, and the rest of us never settled who had the better of it. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the seven steps, say whether that speaker is right, partly right, or mistaken, and why. Then say who is more right overall, and identify what, if anything, both of them missed.
