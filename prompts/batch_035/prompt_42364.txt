I teach a civics elective at a community center — twelve adults, one 90-minute evening session, mixed backgrounds, no philosophy training. Years ago I stumbled on Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain argument and it's been rattling around my head ever since: you start from whatever distribution you think is perfectly just, then a million people each voluntarily pay 25 cents to watch Chamberlain play, and you end up with him rich and the pattern broken. If the starting point was just, and every step was voluntary, what exactly became unjust? I want my group to feel that instead of just hearing me describe it.

Design an in-class activity where the participants construct this themselves rather than being told about it. My vague idea: give them tokens at the start from some distribution they've been led to endorse, then run a series of trades or performances where they can each choose whether to give up tokens for something they actually want — and let them watch the distribution they approved of dissolve without anyone doing anything wrong. But I'm open to better mechanics, especially ones where the "something they want" is real to them in the room rather than hypothetical. No real money. They should not hear Nozick's name or the word "patterned" until the debrief.

Please give me: the setup and materials, the rules, how the initial distribution gets chosen, how the voluntary transfers are made genuinely voluntary and appealing, and a debrief that maps what actually happened in the room onto the steps of the argument. I'd also like the debrief to press on the soft spots — e.g., what a participant could say if they insist the final distribution is still unfair (the "what about the people born after all the trading?" point), and how I should handle it honestly rather than talking them out of it. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and which part of the argument no classroom activity can really reproduce (I suspect it's something about scale or about people who never consented to the initial distribution, but I'd like your view).
