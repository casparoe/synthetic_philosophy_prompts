I teach metaphysics, and every couple of years I run the properties unit as a kind of confession: I stand there presenting Armstrong's sparse universals as the obviously sane middle ground between abundant set-like properties and full-on nominalism, while privately not being sure my credence is even above coin-flip territory. So let me lay this out and have you do it properly, with numbers.

The proposition, stated as precisely as I can: there exist sparse universals — genuinely recurring, mind-independent features that are literally shared by their instances — as opposed to abundant properties (which are just cheap sets of possibilia and nobody denies), and as opposed to nothing at the level of shared being at all. My credence sits around 0.6. My office-mate, a resemblance nominalist of the stubborn kind, is at maybe 0.2. She has this line she calls "matching without sharing": the claim that exact co-instantiation of primitive respects of resemblance does everything universals were hired to do, at strictly lower ontological cost. I've never had a good comeback.

Give me your own number, but I care way more about the derivation. Here's my current decomposition — five sub-questions, my tentative probability on each, and I want you to redo the whole thing, not just bless mine:

1. The One-Over-Many demand is a genuine datum and not grammar conning us: that saying "this apple and that firetruck are both red" requires something literally present in both, rather than the predicate just being true of many things. Me: 0.5, and honestly I suspect this is where all the action is.
2. Resemblance nominalism can survive the degrees problem — the one where resemblance coming in degrees either comes in shared-feature-sized chunks (which is the problem restated) or is primitive and unexplained, in which case primitiveness had better be doing legitimate work. Me: 0.35, though she thinks I'm badly underestimating how much philosophy gets to take as primitive.
3. Structural universals don't sink the ship. Armstrong's move was that methane's structure is built up by partial identity among simpler universals rather than being a set-like composite; Lewis found the set-like version fishy and I think he was right to. If those really are the only options and structural universals turn out set-like, parsimony starts to look ugly for my side. Me: 0.45.
4. The dispute isn't merely verbal. The quantifier-variantist thought is that she and I are talking with different meanings for the quantifiers and there's no substantive question left over once you see that. If that's right, my 0.6 and her 0.2 are both noise. Me: 0.7 that it's substantive, mostly becuase I can't shake the feeling that the realist's explanation of the pattern of resemblances does something her stipulation simply doesn't.
5. Science would need them. Whether a finished physics with real quantities and objective kinds more or less forces realism, or whether everything a physicist actually says gets paraphrased nominalistically without explanatory loss. Me: 0.55, and this is the term I'm least confident in.

Then combine, tell me which of the five your bottom line is most sensitive to, and what would actually move that one — is there an argument that could do it, or is this the sort of thing where only a fully worked-out case study, some specific corner of science, would budge it? And the question I'd most like an answer to: we've been circling this for a decade, my office-mate and I. Where does the real disagreement between 0.6 and 0.2 live? My guess is it's not about ontology at all but about how much explanatory weight you're allowed to hang on primitive resemblance before it's doing the work for free, but I'd genuinely like to be told I'm wrong, with reasons.
