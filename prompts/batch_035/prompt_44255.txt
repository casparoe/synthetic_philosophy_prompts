You are a professional philosopher with expertise in nineteenth-century German thought, including Nietzsche's early essays, who has also served long enough on university committees to know how to write a document that non-philosophers can act on. Before drafting, think through the case carefully — you may include a short preliminary analysis section if it helps, but the deliverable is the memo itself.

I sit on my university's Committee on Naming and Commemoration, and I need a decision memo I can circulate before Thursday's meeting. The case: Whitcombe Hall, our 1920s chemistry building, is named for E. R. Whitcombe, its principal benefactor. Whitcombe was a chemist of genuine accomplishment, but late in life he became a national officer of a eugenics society, helped draft model sterilization statutes, and quietly paid the legal costs of defending forced-sterilization programs in court. Student petitions to rename the building have run for two years; some alumni donors have threatened to withhold gifts if the name goes.

A colleague circulated a one-page handout on Nietzsche's 1874 essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life (the second of the Untimely Meditations), and it has split the committee into three factions, each claiming Nietzsche for its side:

1. The celebrants: keep the name and add a plaque honoring Whitcombe's scientific legacy. They rely on what the handout calls monumental history — the past as a gallery of great exemplars, preserved to show students that great things are possible and to inspire present striving.
2. The curators: keep the name but install a permanent, unflinching exhibit on the eugenics career. They invoke antiquarian history — the preserving, almost loving attachment to origins, including what we would rather not have inherited; they argue you cannot curate the past selectively without forfeiting its authority.
3. The removalists: rename the building now. They cite critical history — on the handout's reading of Nietzsche, the living must occasionally drag the past before a tribunal, condemn it, and partially destroy and forget it, just as a person must periodically shed what they have become in order to grow.

The removalists also wield the essay's diagnostic thesis: an excess of history — compulsive, exhaustive remembering — exhausts what the handout calls the plastic power of a living thing, its capacity to form and re-form itself out of its past; the removalists say our endless litigation of Whitcombe's memory is exactly that paralysis, and that a committee which catalogs every wound cannot act. The celebrants reply with the same thesis, turned around: a community that periodically erases its monuments to keep its conscience clean is practicing history as moral bookkeeping, which they claim is the excess Nietzsche diagnosed, not the cure for it.

I don't need you to referee Nietzsche scholarship for its own sake. The memo should: (a) recommend one concrete course of action, with a process and time frame the committee could adopt on Thursday; (b) state what the Nietzschean framework can and cannot settle here — where it tracks our real disagreement and where it merely relabels it; (c) honestly present the strongest consideration against your recommendation, in a form its supporters on the committee would recognize as theirs; and (d) say what for life could mean for a plural institution like a university, since the whole framework turns on that phrase and our handout never defines it.

Please do not quote Nietzsche; paraphrase the ideas instead, and do not include citations or reading lists — my colleagues are capable of looking things up. Aim for roughly 1,000 words, in plain language. Someone will move to adopt whatever you recommend, so make the bottom line easy to find and the reasoning hard to dismiss.
