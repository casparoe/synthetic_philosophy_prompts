For the last two years I've co-run a political philosophy discussion group whose two most stubborn members — one a natural-rights libertarian who thinks taxation of earnings is morally on a par with taking the earnings by force, the other a social democrat who thinks a democratic community setting the terms of its property regime is nothing like a mugging — have been arguing about whether taxation is theft without either budging an inch. We've decided to force a structured output instead of another free-for-all: the two of them will spend a term writing an adversarial-collaboration document, and I've been asked to draft the assignment. Here's the context that needs to go in. The libertarian holds that persons own themselves, that holdings acquired through labor and exchange without violating anything like a Lockean proviso have a moral character prior to any legal convention, and that forcibly extracting a share of what a person earns from her labor is continuous enough with forced labor that the burden of proof lies on anyone who claims otherwise. The social democrat holds that what counts as a holding in the first place is fixed by a legal order nobody opted into — the currency, the registry, the courts that make an earnings stream a stable thing rather than a hope — so there is no pre-political fact of the matter about what portion of GDP is really yours, and a tax is a decision about the framework's terms rather than a unilateral taking from outside it. Both, notably, accept that actual states waste and abuse tax revenue, that no existing citizen has literally consented, and that the slogan can't be settled by pointing at the worst policies each side dislikes.

I want the assignment to seed the cruxes rather than let them relitigate everything. In particular, the document must engage: (1) the island colony case, where a thousand settlers unanimously adopt a constitution with progressive taxation, and the question is whether taxing the discontented grandchildren — who never signed anything and have nowhere else feasible to go — is theft or something else; (2) the compulsory club, i.e., whether a libertarian should accept a fee for genuinely state-provided services (courts, defense) when the fee is compulsory but the services are things he would have bought anyway, and if so whether the slogan is really about the uses of tax revenue rather than taxation as such; (3) the resource-rent test, i.e., whether even a thoroughgoing libertarian can accept taxing the unimproved value of land and natural resources, since no one's labor created them, and if so whether that reveals the dispute to be about which tax bases are legitimate rather than about taxation per se; and (4) the deeper question of whether their fight over the word "theft" is partly verbal — whether the libertarian means a moralized sense (taking what one has no entitlement to) and the social democrat a descriptive one (nonconsensual transfer), in which case each side's slogan is true in its own sense and the real disagreement is about entitlement, not theft. The assignment should warn them that agreed claims must not be vacuous filler, and that any disagreement they list as verbal must come with a reason for thinking no substantive question hides behind it.

The deliverable format: a numbered section of claims both can sign, each substantive enough that a third party who learned nothing else would still learn something true; then a disagreements table with one row per residual disagreement, each row's phrasing vetted so the named side would accept how its own view is stated; then a cruxes section identifying the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party, with, for each crux, a concrete case, thought experiment, or argument both have agreed in advance bears on it, plus each side's written-in-advance prediction about how the case comes out, so that either party can be visibly wrong; and finally a short section separating the disagreements that are verbal from those that are substantive, with the reasons. The two of them should each be able to put their name on the finished document without feeling sandbagged, and a reader should come away knowing exactly where the two views touch and exactly where they shear apart — not whether taxation is theft, but what the actual disagreement is made of.
