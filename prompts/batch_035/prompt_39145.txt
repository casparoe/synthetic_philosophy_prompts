I'm a palliative care nurse practitioner and I chair the ethics consult team at a mid-size hospital, and a huge share of our consults are about the same thing: a patient who can't tell us what they want anymore, an advance directive or a statement in the chart from when they could, and a surrogate (usually a spouse or adult kid) pointing at the person in the bed saying "but look at her, she's happy, she said that years ago." We keep improvising. I want an actual written procedure the consult team can run in under an hour with the chart and the family in the room, based on whatever the philosophy of precedent autonomy actually supports -- the idea that the earlier, competent self gets to set direction for the later self, and the various reasons people have doubted that (the person now isn't harmed in the usual way by overriding it, current preferences can seem more real than stale documented ones, etc.). The procedure should say: which questions to ask in what order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it terminates with "follow the directive" or "follow the surrogate," and where it honestly hands off to a values judgment no procedure can make. It has to handle the hard distinctions, like whether the directive was meant to cover this exact situation vs. being read onto it, whether the person's earlier stated reasons still apply, and whether the current patient's contentment should count for anything. Then run it on three cases we actually get: (1) a directive refusing antibiotics written by a patient who now has moderate dementia and is reportedly enjoying her days on the unit, family wants the antibiotics; (2) a directive refusing feeding tubes where the directive is specific to stroke but the patient is now in a different, worse condition from a degenerative disease; (3) a young man's written refusal of treatment that his family says was made during a depressive episode he was never treated for, and there's no way to check. Pick a definite position on where the earlier self's authority ends and say so -- don't give me a menu of views. And be upfront about where the procedure will misfire, including at least one case type where following it will give the family an answer they'll hate and we'll have to live with that.
