I cover science and medicine for a mid-size outlet, and our copy desk keeps fighting about how to describe people at the edge of death — organ donors on ventilators, the phrase "brain dead," cases where a heart stops and gets restarted. Our stylebook just says don't use "brain dead" metaphorically, which dodges the actual problem. Write me a procedure an editor can run on a sentence in five minutes: what to ask about the case, when plain "dead" is safe, when it needs a qualifier and which one, and where the procedure has to stop and say this is a values call a human has to make. Ground it in the real philosophy — whole-brain death vs. permanent loss of integrated bodily functioning vs. losing the person while the body lives on — but usable by someone with zero philosophy background and a deadline. Then run it on three cases: a donor declared brain dead being wheeled to the OR with a beating heart, a donation-after-circulatory-death case where the heart is stopped for only a couple minutes before its stopped for good, and a patient in a permanent vegetative state whose family asks us not to call her dead in our story. Flag the places where the procedure gives an answer readers or families will hate, and be honest about what it cant settle.
