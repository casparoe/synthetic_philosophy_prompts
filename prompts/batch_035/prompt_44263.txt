I spent the better part of my career in epidemiology, where a cause is something you can, at least in principle, manipulate, randomise, or stratify on, and I have recently been reading my way into philosophy of social science after retiring. Weber's thesis on the Protestant ethic and the spirit of capitalism has become something of an obsession, and what strikes me is not the thesis itself but the state of the argument around it, which appears to have been going in circles for the better part of a century. One camp treats the thesis as a causal hypothesis like any other and keeps trying to kill it with data: they point out that capitalist accumulation flourished in Catholic Italy long before any Reformation, that Protestant regions differed from Catholic ones on a dozen confounders such as literacy, geography and state capacity, and that once you control for these, the residual effect of religious affiliation on economic outcomes shrinks or disappears. The other camp replies that this entire research programme misconstrues what Weber was doing: he was not estimating an average treatment effect of doctrine on GDP, but showing how a certain religious psychology made a certain constellation of dispositions — the methodical conduct of life, the anxiety over one's election, the moralisation of worldly labour — intelligible as a precondition for a form of economic conduct that would otherwise have seemed irrational or contemptible. To this camp, asking whether the coefficient survives controls is to have missed the point. There the matter sits, with each side periodically declaring victory.

I would like you to act as a philosopher of social science who knows both the Weber literature and the modern quantitative social science, and to think carefully before answering. My working hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that this is not really an empirical disagreement at all, but a dispute about what an explanation is: the quantitative camp tacitly assumes that to explain is to subsume under a covering law or estimate a cause, while the interpretive camp assumes that to explain is to make an action or disposition intelligible from within, and the two camps therefore grade the same historical facts against incommensurable standards of success. But I can see at least two rivals: perhaps it is a disguised factual question about the historical facts themselves, in which case more evidence could in principle settle it; or perhaps the two sides are answering genuinely different questions under one name, in which case the appearance of conflict is spurious and the debate should simply be split in two. Please diagnose the disagreement using what each side actually claims, tell me which of these diagnoses — or some fourth one — is best supported, and be honest about what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis: a better design, a sharper distinction between kinds of explanation, a reformulation of the question, or an argument that we should expect permanent stalemate and why.

Please answer in British English and in continuous prose only, as one would write a considered letter — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. I have no interest in a survey of the positions for its own sake; I want a diagnosis with reasons, and a straight answer at the end about whether this dispute is resolvable, and if so, what would move it.
