I facilitate a monthly philosophy café at my local library: about fifteen regulars, mostly retired teachers, engineers, and avid readers of popular science, with no formal philosophy background but strong opinions. Next month's session is fifty minutes on Wittgenstein's late thoughts about certainty, centered on the idea of "hinges": the thought that our ordinary doubts and justifications only make sense against a background of things that are not themselves doubted or justified, and that this background is not a set of beliefs we hold but something more like the framework within which believing and doubting take place. The immediate hook is G. E. Moore: several regulars have already encountered Moore's gesture of holding up his hands to prove the external world, and someone will inevitably ask whether Wittgenstein was just agreeing with him more aggressively. So I need the arc to confront Moore head-on, and the audience has to see that Wittgenstein's response to Moore is not "you proved it" but "you pointed at exactly the thing that is exempt from proof, and that's why the gesture feels both irresistible and pointless."

Please don't write the explanation itself; write me a teaching plan. As someone who knows the late Wittgenstein well, map out the order the pieces have to come in and why — in particular, what has to be established before the audience can even make sense of the claim that a certainty can fail to be knowledge without being ignorance or error, and where in the sequence the notion of hinges as rules of the game rather than propositions in the game should be introduced, given that "language game" itself will be new to many of them. Identify the single carrying example I should build everything around: I'm inclined to use the idea that the earth existed long before my birth, or that I have never been to the moon, rather than anything about hands — but tell me if a different example does the work better, and say what the chosen example must have and what it must lack. Then give me a misconception map: the predictable misreadings I should expect (that hinges are just very well-entrenched beliefs, that Wittgenstein is endorsing dogmatism or the impossibility of knowledge, that this is a reply to Descartes rather than to Moore, that "hinge certainty" means psychological certainty) and, for each, the exact point in the sequence where I should defuse it rather than after it has set. Finally, propose one closing question whose answer would show they've genuinely grasped the distinction between doubting within a framework and doubting the framework itself — not merely that they found the session interesting — and name one thing I should openly decline to cover at this level and say so explicitly. Think through the dependency structure carefully before committing to the sequence; a session like this lives or dies on whether the first ten minutes plant the right puzzle.
