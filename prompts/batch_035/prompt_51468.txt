I'm a philosophy professor and next week I'm presenting to our summer seminar, which is a strange mix: philosophy of mind grad students, two logicians, and a handful of CS students who basically grew up on Hofstadter and treat GEB like scripture. My thesis: the Gödelian material in Gödel, Escher, Bach is doing motivational work rather than argumentative work — people come away feeling like self-reference has been shown to somehow generate minds, and I want to argue the analogy can't carry that weight.

My sketch, so you know what to attack: (1) Gödel sentences are self-referential only relative to a coding scheme imposed from outside, and the self-reference lives in the eye of the interpreter, so to speak — the arithmetized sentence on its own is just a statement about numbers; (2) whatever self-modeling brains do, it doesn't remotely resemble a numbering scheme mapping expressions onto expressions; (3) the "strange loop" in Gödel's construction crosses between two formal levels (syntax and arithmetic), whereas the claim about consciousness needs a crossing between an object level and the thing doing the representing, and its not obvious that's the same kind of crossing; and (4) I'll gesture at Tarski to say that a sufficiently rich system can represent its own syntax but not its own semantic notions, which cuts against reading Gödel as "a system becoming aware of itself."

Prepare my Q&A sheet. Give me the objections this specific audience will raise, ordered by how much damage they'd do — the logicians will have one family of complaints (I suspect they'll quibble with my level-crossing point), the CS kids will have another ("you're strawmanning Hofstadter, he never claimed a proof"). For each: how they'd actually phrase it, a two-minute reply, and where the reply is soft. Include the question I'm praying nobody asks, and tell me which parts of my four points I should just quietly drop from the talk. Also tell me honestly if any of my points are just wrong as a matter of the actual mathematics.
