A junior in my philosophy club believes she has proved that no one ever has a reason to investigate anything, and although her conclusion is plainly absurd, I could not find the mistake when she presented it, because every step looked acceptable on its own.

Her example comes from her own week. On Sunday night she checked the deadbolt on her front door twice before going to bed, and she remembers doing it, so she says she knows the door was locked when she fell asleep. On Monday morning her neighbor, who is reliable and has no motive to mislead her, texted to say that at midnight the door was standing wide open. Her argument, as she wrote it out:

(1) If I know that p, then I know in advance that any future evidence against p will be misleading evidence: since I know that p is true, whatever appears to count against p must in fact be pointing away from something else.
(2) Evidence that I already know to be misleading gives me no reason to examine it; going to the door would be as pointless as going to verify that 2 + 2 = 4.
(3) So when the text arrives, I may rationally ignore it and stay where I am.
(4) Nothing in this reasoning is special to doors or to texts: for any proposition that anyone knows, any later evidence against it can be dismissed in advance.
(5) Therefore no one who knows something can ever be rationally required to re-examine it, however strong the counterevidence becomes.
(6) But if (5) were true, juries and laboratories would be ceremonies rather than inquiries, and we all think the text at least gave her a reason to look.

She concludes that (6) is the step to deny. I would rather keep (6), and I would also like to keep the claim that she really did know the door was locked on Sunday night. Which step should I reject? Explain why that step looked secure when she presented it, and set out what each available rejection costs — including hers, since I do not want to give up (6) simply because that is the comfortable option.
