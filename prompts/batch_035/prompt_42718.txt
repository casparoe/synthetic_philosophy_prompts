I'm trying to sort out what I actually believe about supererogation — acts that are praiseworthy to do but not wrong to omit — and I'd like you to act as a moral philosopher doing reflective equilibrium with me. Here are my verdicts on a set of cases. Donating a kidney to an anonymous stranger: clearly above and beyond the call; heroic, but nobody is blameworthy for keeping both kidneys. Pulling a drowning child out of a shallow pond at the cost of ruining an expensive coat: obligatory, full stop — I'd blame someone who walked past. A firefighter who, after completing the assigned rescue, goes back into the collapsing building for one more occupant: supererogatory, and I'd hate a department that required it of her. A person who gives away 40% of her income to effective charities for thirty years: praiseworthy, but my intuitions here are murkier — it feels less like an optional heroism and more like something closer to a duty that most of us fail, even though the lifetime cost surely exceeds that of a single kidney. And here is the pair I can't reconcile: I think refusing to donate a kidney to a stranger is fine, but refusing to be the sole bone marrow match for your sibling feels at least pro tanto wrong — even though the marrow case involves someone with a special relationship and the kidney case doesn't, and I can't see why the relationship should raise the bar rather than just adding a reason. I keep flip-flopping on monthly blood donation too: individually painless and minor, yet I don't think most people who never donate are doing anything wrong.
