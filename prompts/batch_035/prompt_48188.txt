I teach a small philosophy of biology elective, 16 students, 80 minute sessions, and next week is the units of selection debate. Honestly every time I teach it the discussion just collapses into "Dawkins says genes, Gould grumbles" and nobody feels why anyone cared. Design an in-class game where they run into the actual structure of the problem before I name any of it for them.

Roughly the effect I want: players get sorted into small groups, play some simple cooperate/defect-style game over a few rounds, then groups get scored *as groups* and either stay together or get dissolved and reshuffeld into new groups. The point is that they should personally find out that cheaters always come out on top inside any group, while groups full of cooperators collectively crush groups full of cheaters — so by the end, half the room is convinced selection acted on groups and the other half insists it was all just allele-level sorting, and both halves got their numbers from the same game.

Practical stuff: materials should be things I actually have in a classroom (index cards, poker chips, dice, a whiteboard). No real money, but the points need to matter somehow — e.g. standing for who gets to leave class a couple minutes early — so people actually play to win. I want at least two structural variants run back to back: one where groups stay intact across rounds, one where everyone is reshuffled every single round, so they can watch the outcome flip depending on group persistence. And one last twist where you have them recount the exact same final round as pure gene frequencies across the whole room, no groups mentioned at all, and the numbers still fully determine what happened — so the "but which unit is REALLY selected" question starts to smell like bookkeeping rather than biology.

Then a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the within-group vs between-group selection components, and onto why a gene's-eye type says the between-group part is just an artifact of averaging (the move where you average over groups and pretend that's one population). Name whatever needs naming only at the end.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — people miscounting chips, alliances forming across groups, one student who refuses to defect on principle and accidentally ruins the punchline — and how I should handle each. And be honest about which feature of the debate the game flat out cannot reproduce, e.g. that real trait groups aren't little islands of 4 people sitting at desks, or the claim that group-level "adaptations" aren't really adaptations at all, and where I just have to stop the game and lecture for two minutes.

Keep the whole design, debrief included, under 750 words. No pre-reading, nothing graded.
