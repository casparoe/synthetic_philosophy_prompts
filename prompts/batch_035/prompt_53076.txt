I recently read about a result in computer science saying that algorithmic fairness is mathematically impossible: when two groups have different base rates, an algorithm cannot at the same time be well calibrated and have equal false positive and false negative rates across the groups. If I understand it correctly, this means that any algorithm making decisions about people — whether to grant bail, to call someone for a job interview, to approve a loan — will inevitably be biased against one group or another, and that the apparent fairness of any such system is really just a matter of where we have chosen to hide the bias. That being so, I have two questions. First, is it correct to say that all machine learning used in high-stakes decisions is irredeemably discriminatory in a strict mathematical sense? Second, if the theorem shows that fairness itself is impossible, why do researchers and governments keep proposing "fair algorithms" and passing regulations demanding algorithmic audits, rather than concluding that we should simply stop using algorithms for these decisions altogether and return to human judgement? I would also like to know whether the impossibility result applies to human decision-makers too, since presumably the same statistical constraints would govern any method of prediction, whether a computer or a panel of experts — or whether there is something about human judgement that escapes the theorem.
