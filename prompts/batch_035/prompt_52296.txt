Last night a philosophy-grad-student friend of mine and I spent two hours arguing about Kavka's toxin puzzle, and it left me dissatisfied in a very specific way I want your help with. To remind you of the setup: an eccentric billionaire places before you a vial of toxin that, if you drink it, will make you painfully ill for a day without threatening your life or leaving lasting effects. He will pay you a million dollars tomorrow morning if, at midnight tonight, you sincerely intend to drink the toxin tomorrow afternoon — and you need not actually drink it; the money will already be in your bank account before the time for drinking arrives, and you are perfectly free to change your mind once it is paid. My friend and I agree on the naive statement of the paradox: the intention looks worth having tonight, but by tomorrow morning drinking buys you nothing but illness, and knowing in advance that you will rationally not drink, it somehow seems you cannot form the sincere intention at midnight that the payment requires.

Here is my problem. I am an applied game theorist by profession — my working vocabulary is decision nodes and information sets, backward induction, subgame-perfect equilibrium, commitment devices, incentive compatibility, screening, and the difference between a contract that a court can enforce and one that is merely self-enforcing. When I run the puzzle through that vocabulary, everything goes smoothly for a while: the drinking node falls off the equilibrium path once the payment is sunk, backward induction "solves" the case, and I find myself describing the midnight intention as a kind of internal commitment device — like burning my boats, only cheaper. My friend says this is precisely where the puzzle bites, and that I am smuggling in my framework's assumptions. A commitment device, in my usage, is an external mechanism that removes options or changes payoffs; nothing in the game tree has changed between noon and midnight except, supposedly, my mind, and the whole point of Kavka's case is that the intention is supposed to do its work from the inside, without any scaffold. She kept reaching for distinctions I have no words for: something about the difference between predicting that I will drink and being settled on drinking, about intention having an inertia of its own once formed, about deciding now versus merely foreseeing that I will decide later. She also dragged in the old debate from the deterrence literature about whether a rational person could ever usefully dispose herself to act irrationally later — the constrained-maximization idea — and claimed the toxin case is a counterexample even to that, since the self-binding trick fails exactly when you can see through it.

So here is what I want. Restate the toxin puzzle in my vocabulary — information sets, equilibrium paths, commitment and self-enforcement, types and screening — as faithfully as you can, flagging wherever the translation is doing work the original does not. Then give me the residue report in three parts: which elements of the puzzle map cleanly (I suspect the backward-induction reasoning does), which map only with distortion (I suspect intention-as-commitment-device is one of these — say exactly what gets distorted), and which have no expression at all in a game-theoretic description of the situation. Finally, take a side on what the residue shows: is it a defect of my vocabulary that it cannot represent whatever my friend is pointing at, or a sign that the something-more she insists on is less substantive than it looks — an artifact of how philosophers talk about minds rather than a feature of the decision problem itself? Assume I know the standard rationality results; you do not need to explain backward induction to me, only to say where it applies and where the philosophers would say it misses the point.
