I'm a graduate student working at the seam of metaphysics and philosophy of science, and I keep noticing that debates about natural kinds go nowhere because the two sides are tacitly demanding different things of the concept. One camp cares mostly about whether classifications support induction and explanation, another about whether special sciences like psychiatry or economics are thereby licensed to claim their categories are real, and a third about deflating the whole joint-carving image inherited from the Phaedrus. Before I write anything arguing about particular candidate kinds, I want the specification settled: a worked-out list of what a theory of natural kinds actually has to deliver, so that later arguments can be about whether a given theory meets the requirements rather than about which requirements to have.

So: write the desiderata. A theory of natural kinds should, presumably, get the paradigmatic cases right (chemical elements and compounds behaving as the gold standard), handle the disjunctive embarrassments (jade as two distinct minerals under one label), say something principled about the species problem, where biologists seem to work with several incompatible species concepts at once without anyone treating that as a scandal, and cope with special-science kinds like depression, markets, or ecological communities, which support some projection but are revisionary and messy in a way the chemical cases are not. It should presumably tell us the difference between a real kind and a merely useful classificatory shorthand, and between a kind's being real and its being the only good way to cut up the domain. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it; mark which are non-negotiable and which are preferences of a particular camp; and flag any pair or subset you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied -- if the list itself forces a choice, I want that made explicit rather than papered over. In specifying the requirements, assume the theory is meant to serve both the epistemologist's question (why do inductions over some classes succeed?) and the working scientist's need to classify without metaphysical apology; if these two purposes pull apart, that is itself something the list should reveal.

End with no more than one sentence each on how classical essentialism and homeostatic property cluster views fare against your list, and identify the single requirement that does the most exclusionary work. The list is the deliverable; don't turn it into a survey. Please keep the whole thing under 700 words -- numbered requirements with brief motivation, not an essay.
