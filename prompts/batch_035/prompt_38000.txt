My brother and I have been arguing for months about whether there is a real moral difference between lying to someone and telling them a strictly true thing that you know they'll take the wrong way. He says the strict-truth version is clearly not as bad, and when I couldn't explain why, I built an argument to show they're the same. It started as a way to needle him, but now it worries me, because if I follow it out it seems to clear people I think are plainly doing something wrong. Here is the argument:

(1) When deceiving someone is wrong, it is wrong because of the outcome you intend: you get them to act on a false picture of the world, and the words are just the delivery mechanism. (2) Suppose I ask whether you've ever smoked, and instead of saying "no" you say "I have never once held a cigarette," which is strictly true because you only ever smoked a pipe — and you say it knowing I'll conclude you've never smoked at all. (3) In an outright lie, I hand you the falsehood directly; in the strict-truth case, I arrange things so you pick the falsehood up yourself. But in both cases the false belief ends up in your head, and in both cases I acted intending it to end up there — the difference is only in how it traveled. (4) That looks like a difference of manner, not of kind, like the difference between pushing someone into a pool and leaving a banana peel where you know they'll certainly step. (5) So a strict-truth deception is, morally, just as bad as the lie it replaces whenever both are aimed at the same false belief.

Here is what bothers me. (6) If the responsibility shifts to you the moment you do your own concluding, then a con man who never asserts anything false — who only asks questions like "How much would you say a car like this loses each year?" and lets you talk yourself into a bad deal — has done nothing wrong, since every false claim in the transaction came out of your own mouth. (7) And someone who notices a friend operating on a false belief and simply stays quiet about it, letting the friend act on it, would also be blameless, because the belief was already there and they only declined to remove it. My brother says the argument proves too much: if it lets off the con man and the silent friend, something inside it must be broken, and I can't find where. So tell me which step to reject, why that step looked fine on the way in, and what giving it up costs — because if I abandon (1), I end up saying the choice of words matters more than the result, and if I accept (7), I'm saying silence in the face of someone's mistake is no wrong at all, and I honestly don't know which of those is harder to live with.
