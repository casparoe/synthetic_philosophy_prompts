"Bernard Williams holds that all genuine reasons for action are internal reasons. (a) Explain his distinction between internal and external reason statements, including the role of the agent's subjective motivational set and of sound deliberation. (b) Reconstruct his argument that there are no true external reason statements. (c) Explain why some philosophers find that conclusion unacceptable where morality is concerned, and assess whether their objection succeeds."

Here's the student's answer, name redacted, typos theirs:

"Internal reason statements are ones whose truth depends on the agent's subjective motivational set. An agent A has a reason to φ only if some element of that set, combined with correct deliberation, could motivate A to φ. Deliberation can do a lot of work here — correcting errors of fact, working out means and consequences, even constructing new motivations out of old ones — so an internal reason doesn't have to correspond to any desire the agent has before deliberating. External reason statements, by contrast, claim that A has a reason even though there is no sound deliberative route from anything in the agent's motivational set to φ-ing.

Williams's argument against external reasons goes like this. Suppose an external reason statement about A were true. For a statement of reason to be true it has to be able to explain why A would act on it, and that means acting on it must be connected to motivation. If the motivation can be reached by deliberation from the agent's set, the reason was internal all along. If it can't, then there is nothing that could ever move A, and a consideration that could not in principle motivate anyone is not a reason at all. Either way no external reason statement is true.

The moral worry: imagine a man thinking of leaving his wife without telling her, who genuinely cares nothing for her — nothing in his motivational set bears on keeping her informed. Williams has to say he has no reason to tell her, so whether a person has moral reasons depends on what they happen to care about, which seems to make morality hostage to desire. McDowell bites the bullet here: he agrees with Williams that external reason statements are all false, but thinks we should keep asserting them anyway because they play a useful regulative role in our practices. So the objection is that internalism makes moral criticism of the indifferent impossible, and I think that's a price most of us shouldn't pay."
