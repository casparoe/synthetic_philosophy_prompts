I run social media ads for a living, so I've gotten weirdly familiar with engagement metrics — watch time, likes, shares, comments, all the little hover and rewatch signals the platforms track. Lately something's been bugging me about how these systems get defended. Whenever someone accuses a platform of making feeds addictive or toxic, the standard company answer is basically "we just give people what they want — the algorithm only shows you stuff because you keep watching it." And on the surface that sounds fair. But I keep noticing cases where it doesnt add up. I hate-watch videos about a football team I can't stand, and the algorithm happily serves me more of them — am I "wanting" that? My girlfriend doomscrolls news that visibly makes her miserable every night, and she's the first to say she wishes she could stop. And sometimes my own signals contradict each other: I'll watch something all the way through and then be annoyed that it wasted my time.

I mentioned this to a friend who studies CS, and he said something interesting: in AI there's literally a research area about inferring what people want purely from their behavior — you watch what someone does and try to reconstruct their goals from it. He said the classic approach has to assume the person is a rational agent pursuing what they like, otherwise the whole thing falls apart — and apparently economists have an old version of this idea too, where your choices are supposed to reveal your preferences. That got me thinking: is the "we give people what they want" defense the same move? When a platform points at my clicks as proof of what I want, what is it assuming about what wanting even is? Is it assuming my behavior can't lie? Because it seems like my behavior lies all the time — or maybe "lies" isn't the right word, maybe the different signals just don't measure the same thing, like clicks and watch time and shares are pointing at different states of me.

Can you take on the role of a philosopher who works on preference and choice, and really think this one through carefully? Maybe start by brainstorming what "wanting" could even mean in this context — like wanting-as-clicking vs wanting-as-enjoying vs wanting-as-endorsing the next morning, if that distinction makes any sense. Then tell me: what view of human preference does engagement-based recommendation actually presuppose, does the "we just show people what they want" defense hold up under that view or does it need something stronger, and what would a feed look like if it were built on one of the rival views instead? Would it even be workable in practice, or would it collapse back into clicks anyway? For what it's worth, I'm inclined to think the platforms are measuring something real, just not the thing they claim to be measuring — but I might be off, so tell me if I'm framing it wrong.
