The thesis: everyone tells maths students that Zorn's lemma is "obviously true" — the picture being that you start somewhere in your poset, and whenever you're not at a maximal element you climb up, since the chain-boundedness condition gives you something above the whole chain you've built so far. Keep going and eventually you must get stuck, and getting stuck just means you've hit a maximal element. My notes argue this feeling of obviousness is basically an illusion, for two reasons.

1. The climbing picture is an induction picture, and it works fine for finite posets. For infinite posets the naive climb can genuinely never get stuck at any stage you reach: at a limit stage there's no "next rung" — the construction has no story about what to do when you've done infinitely many steps and you're still not done. My claim is that the sense of obviousness is imported wholesale from the finite case and never audited. (I do know the real proof via ordinals — the point is about the *felt* obviousness, not the truth of the theorem.)

2. The picture also smuggles in choice without flagging it: "keep going" means making selection after selection with no rule for them. So the lemma feels obvious partly because it's illustrated with exactly the principle that's up for debate. A bit like "proving" AC by saying just pick one element from each set.

Then the more philosophical bit, which is where I want pushback most: equivalence doesn't distribute warrant. Zorn's lemma, AC, well-ordering, Tychonoff's theorem etc. are all interderivable over ZF, and textbooks seem to treat this as meaning the felt obviousness of Zorn's lemma settles the whole cluster. My notes claim that's a mistake — derivability from X is not the same thing as X-ness, and the conceptual distance between the equivalents matters for how much justification actually transfers across the derivations. So: is this defensible, or am I just confusing psychological obviousness with epistemic justification? Maybe the honest move is to concede obviousness was never doing any real work anyway.

Last section is pedagogy — whether lecturers should present Zorn's lemma via the ordinal proof (where you can see exactly where choice and replacement enter, instead of the lemma being waved in as a black box before the applications) or whether that's hopeless in a first course. A lecturer friend says nobody would sit through the ordinals, which is probably fair.
