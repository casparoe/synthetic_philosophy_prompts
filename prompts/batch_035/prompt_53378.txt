I'm a PhD student in classics, finishing a dissertation on Ciceronian dialogue, and I've been asked to give the five-minute opening remarks at a geroscience workshop — a room of molecular biologists and a couple of longevity-biotech founders, plus a few philosophers of medicine — whose organizing theme is essentially the engineering of aging out of human life. The organizers wanted a humanistic opener before the keynote, and they asked me because of what I work on. My instinct is to build it around Cicero's Cato Maior de Senectute, the essay from 44 BCE in which Cato the Elder, past eighty, consoles two younger men by answering the four standard complaints against old age: that it withdraws us from active affairs, that it weakens the body, that it deprives us of pleasures, and that it stands close to death. But the more I sit with it, the more I suspect the fit is worse than it looks. Cato's project is consolatory through and through — he takes it as fixed that the body will fail and that the task is to show a life remains good inside those limits, with influence through counsel replacing action, and quieter pleasures replacing louder ones. This room's premise is precisely the denial of that limit, and Cato's serenity could read to them as the respectable face of a condition they intend to treat.

And yet I don't think the dialogue is simply the opposition, either. Cato also insists that old age must be actively resisted, that the mind kept at work, and that a cramped and anxious senility is partly the old man's own fault — there is something closer to preventive medicine in that than the caricature of serene acceptance. And there is the anecdote he tells about Sophocles: when the poet's sons tried to have him declared incompetent to manage his affairs, Sophocles answered the court by reading aloud the tragedy he was still composing — the Oedipus at Colonus — asking, in effect, whether that sounded like a man out of his mind. My tentative plan is to organize the speech around that courtroom scene, treating it as an early argument that capacity is demonstrated rather than assumed, and letting the room hear both how far they have traveled from Cato and how close his instinct was to theirs — the tension on the table rather than smoothed over.

Write the speech. Constraints: five minutes spoken, so short; it must work for a mixed audience (one or two Latin words at most, everything translated, no lecture on the dialogue's structure); it must not condescend to the scientists or imply that extending healthy life is hubris; and it must not pretend Cato would have been neutral about the workshop's premise, because the interesting version puts the friction in view. If you think the Sophocles framing misreads the dialogue, or would land as a gimmick with that audience, say so plainly and do something else rather than forcing it. Follow the speech with a brief note flagging where it takes liberties with Cicero — which readings of the essay it leans on that a classicist would contest, and where it stretches the anecdote's role in the original. End with a single one-sentence statement of the speech's central claim, as the last line.
