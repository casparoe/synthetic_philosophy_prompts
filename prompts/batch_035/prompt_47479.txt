I'm a second-year history major in a seminar on historiography and historical memory, and last week we were discussing laws against Holocaust denial in Germany. When someone asked whether such laws sit well with academic freedom, a classmate — not a denier, I'm fairly sure, just fond of contrarian aphorisms — said that of course the standard account of the Holocaust ends up state-protected, because "history is written by the victors": the Allies won, so their narrative became the official one, and dissent gets criminalized. When pressed, he added that he thought the same logic applied to what Chinese historians write about the Nanjing massacre. I have the feeling that a single sentence is doing several very different jobs at once in his argument, but I can't untangle them cleanly, and our instructor has suggested I write up the exchange to open next week's discussion.

What I'd like is a careful disambiguation of "history is written by the victors" as it was being used in that room. What are the distinct precise claims the sentence could be expressing, ordered from weakest to strongest — starting with modest observations like "victorious states leave more archives, fund more chairs, and shape the schoolbooks of the defeated," moving through stronger theses about the winners excluding or discrediting inconvenient testimony, and ending at the radical reading on which all historical narrative is an instrument of power and "mainstream history" simply means "the history the powerful settled on." For each reading, tell me whether it is plausible in general, whether it is actually true of the Holocaust in particular (where, awkwardly for the slogan, much of the strongest evidence comes from the perpetrators' own records, and where the Western victors spent the early Cold War downplaying Nazi crimes for reasons of realpolitik — a case that seems to cut against any simple victors-versus-truth story), and, most importantly, whether that reading would actually support the inference my classmate was drawing: from the slogan to "therefore the standard account deserves reduced confidence" or "therefore criminalizing denial is victors' censorship." I'd also like your view on which reading people who reach for the slogan in that kind of argument usually intend, and whether the reading they intend is the one doing the argumentative work or is quietly swapped for a stronger one mid-sentence.

Please write this as continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — as one flowing piece I could read aloud at the seminar or post to the discussion board, in roughly the register of a well-prepared student rather than a journal article.
