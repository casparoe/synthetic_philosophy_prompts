I'm a philosophy PhD student with a mostly analytic background (epistemology, some philosophy of language) and two years of pretty shaky Sanskrit, and I'm trying to pick a dissertation topic within Nyāya epistemology. My advisor is encouraging me but keeps saying "follow what's actually unresolved, not what's fashionable," and honestly I'm having trouble telling the difference in this literature. So I want a defended prioritization: which open questions in pramāṇa theory are most worth someone like me spending three years on, and why.

To make it concrete, here are the candidates I keep circling between, though I'd take others too. First, the reducibility question: the Nyāya tradition swears by śabda (testimony) as an independent pramāṇa, but it's not obvious the arguments against collapsing it into anumāṇa (inference) actually work, and this maps interestingly but imperfectly onto the contemporary reductionism vs. anti-reductionism fight about testimonial knowledge. Second, the vyāpti problem: how the universal connection between smoke and fire is supposed to be established without either enumerating all cases (obviously impossible) or quietly presupposing what needs proving -- basically whether the Nyāya treatment of tarka as a supporting method dodges or just relabels the problem of induction. Third, the taxonomy question: whether the number of pramāṇas (two for the Buddhists, six for the Mīmāṃsakas, four-ish for Nyāya depending on the period) reflects a principled carve-up of cognition or is just scholastic bookkeeping, with upamāna and anupalabdhi as the obvious test cases of sources that maybe don't earn their keep. Fourth, whether nirvikalpaka perception -- the indeterminate, non-conceptual awareness Nyāya posits underneath determinate perception -- can genuinely do the work that current philosophers of perception want "nonconceptual content" to do, or whether the resemblance is superficial.

For each one you'd prioritize, I want the reasoning, not just the label: how much it matters downstream (do other questions in the field implicitly presuppose an answer to it?), how tractable it is for someone whose Sanskrit is serviceable but not great (some of this might be locked up in Navya-Nyāya texts I'd struggle through), and whether the payoff is mainly for comparative/historical scholarship or whether it could actually feed back into live analytic debates without becoming one of those "X anticipated Y" papers everyone rolls their eyes at. Also tell me which of these you'd actively deprioritize and why -- e.g., whether the vyāpti stuff is just Hume in a different costume and therefore already done. And what would change your ranking: if my goal were a job in a strongly analytic department vs. a religion/South Asia department, does the ordering flip?
