I'm a retired geophysicist who got properly into philosophy of science after stopping work, and my reading group keeps going round and round on this one. A friend of mine says scientific discoveries are like statues already sitting inside the block of marble — the sculptor just removes what's in the way (Michelangelo supposedly thought something like this). Her point: calculus, natural selection, oxygen, whatever — if Newton and Darwin hadn't been on the scene, Leibniz and Wallace would have come out with the same things, and in fact they basically did, because the statue was already there in the marble of the times. So who gets there first is down to luck, the priority disputes are a bit embarassing, and we'd do well to stop fussing so much about credit. I have a vague unease with this but can't quite pin it down, and I'd rather be argued out of it than into it.

Can you lay out what maps onto what — what plays the role of the marble, the statue, the chisel, the sculptor choosing which block to bother with — then say where the mapping breaks down? The thing that bugs me is that a block of marble seems to contain exactly one statue, whereas Leibniz's calculus and Newton's were pretty different beasts (the notation that actually stuck around was Leibniz's, I gather), and Darwin and Wallace didn't exactly carve the same statue either. So maybe tell me which disanalogies matter for her conclusion and which ones are just noise, and then whether the stop-fussing-over-credit conclusion actually survives once those are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole way. If there's a better analogy for the situation I'd genuinely like to hear it. And please say how confident you are in the different bits as you go, and flag the single point you're least sure of — I'd take honest uncertainty over a tidy verdict any day.
