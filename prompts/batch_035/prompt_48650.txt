I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I commission the short fiction slot we run in each issue. The pieces we run take up a live conceptual question rather than illustrate a settled one, and for the coming issue the question is how we decide which animals get the benefit of the doubt about consciousness.

I'd like a story that avoids the obvious moves. In particular: no first-person interior monologue of an animal, which settles by fiat the very question at issue; no dreamy what-is-it-like-to-be musing; no dystopia in which some authority sorts species into sentient and non-sentient. The problem I want dramatized is evidential rather than metaphysical. Our grounds for attributing experience to a species are of several distinct kinds — an animal trading off pain relief against a resource it wants, self-administration of analgesics, guarding of injured kin, performance on mirror tests, the sheer architecture of the nervous system — and these different kinds of evidence run out at different places in the tree of life, so the boundary of the plausible is a ragged edge rather than a line. An octopus and a prawn sit on the same coast in our intuitions and at opposite ends of our legislation, and the story should make a reader feel why that is genuinely hard rather than tell them it is.

Ideally, then, the story follows one character — a researcher, a welfare inspector, a fisherman, a chef, your choice — being pulled toward different verdicts by different kinds of evidence, with the story itself refusing to tip the answer. Compress it as much as you can: present it as exactly five numbered scenes, each no more than two sentences, with no introduction, epilogue, or connecting narration between them. The five scenes should be the whole story.
