You are an expert in value theory and formal ethics — the sort of philosopher equally at home in a seminar on the logic of comparatives and a decision-theory workshop. I need you to audit a package of my considered views for consistency. Before writing anything, brainstorm the candidate tensions carefully: I have deliberately not sorted them for you, some pairs that look bad may be fine, and I don't want a list where everything is flagged as equally problematic.

The views:

1. Better-than is not always transitive. What convinces me are spectrum arguments: take a day of torture and fifty years of headaches, and interpolate a continuum of intermediate outcomes (headaches growing slightly longer and more intense, with slightly worse trade-offs at each step) such that each adjacent pair is too close together for the difference to be meaningful. If better-than were transitive, these negligible local differences would accumulate into the verdict that a day of torture is no worse than fifty years of headaches — an absurd conclusion. So I accept that the better-than relation sometimes fails to be transitive, at least for outcomes that vary along several dimensions at once. I should say that I find this conclusion comfortable when the endpoints are far apart, and uncomfortable when I think about mid-spectrum comparisons.

2. In those spectra, I believe the adjacent items are exactly equally good — not vaguely comparable, not "roughly equal," but precisely indifferent. So the failure is specifically in the equally-good relation: there are A, B, and C where A is equally as good as B, B is equally as good as C, and yet A is clearly better than C.

3. The at-least-as-good-as relation is, and must be, fully transitive. I can't make sense of a comparative that is supposed to structure deliberation if it fails to chain: if A is at least as good as B and B is at least as good as C, then A is at least as good as C. This strikes me as close to constitutive of what a coherent ranking is.

4. I am convinced by money-pump arguments. An agent whose preferences are intransitive can be led, trade by rational-looking trade, from A to B to C and back to A, paying at each step, ending where she started and poorer. Since being exploitable in this way is a paradigm of irrationality, I take it that a rational agent's preferences must be transitive.

5. I am a maximizing act consequentialist: an act is right if and only if no available alternative would have produced a better outcome. The point of moral deliberation, on my view, is to locate the top of the outcome ranking and choose it.

6. I accept expected utility theory as the correct account of rational choice under risk. The axioms strike me as individually compelling, and I regard the representation theorem as showing something deep about rational preference, not as a mere mathematical curiosity.

7. I hold that in the ideal limit — a fully informed, perfectly rational agent — there is no daylight between preference and value: such an agent prefers A to B if and only if A is really better than B. Ideal rational preference just is the better-than relation, viewed from the inside.

Now the audit. Go through my views and tell me which combinations are genuinely inconsistent or jointly untenable, and which merely look that way at first glance — for the ones that only look bad, explain precisely what dissolves the tension. For each genuine tension, rank it by severity, say exactly which commitment is doing the damage, and identify the cheapest fix together with what that fix would cost me elsewhere in the package. Do not assume I have a favorite sacrifice in mind; I genuinely don't know where the problem is. Conclude by telling me which single revision would clean up the most, and whether any residual package is still recognizably mine.

A few specifics I want you to be careful about: attend to how my commitments interact through the formal structure of the comparisons (e.g., what follows if some relations are defined in terms of others), be explicit about whether a fix merely relocates the problem, and check whether any of my views are jointly satisfiable only by narrowing their domains — and whether that narrowing would be principled or ad hoc. Take your time and think it through step by step before committing to a verdict.
