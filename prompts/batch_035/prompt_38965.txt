Ever since an argument with my brother about scratch tickets I've been keeping a list of cases where I have pretty firm gut verdicts about what it's rational to believe, but I can't for the life of me find the rule behind the verdicts, and I'm hoping you can help me extract one.

Here's the list. I bought a ticket in a fair lottery with a million tickets and exactly one winner. I believe my ticket will lose — I'd say it flat out, bet money on it, no hedging. My friend bought one too, and I believe his will lose. In fact, for any ticket you pick out of that lottery, I believe it'll lose. But I obviously do NOT believe every ticket loses, since one has to win. Fine so far. Second case: a historian writes a book making 500 separate factual claims, each one checked as carefully as anything in his field, and he believes each individual claim. Being honest with himself, he also believes the book contains at least one error somewhere — no book of 500 claims gets them all right, and he'd tell you that himself. I think that's a perfectly reasonable state of mind for him, not a contradiction in any bad sense. Third case: a factory foreman believes, based on the inspections, that each of the 100 widgets coming off the line today is fine, but given a 1% historical defect rate he also believes one of today's widgets is bad. Also seems fine to me. Now the one that bugs me: a gambler reasons 'I believe ticket #1 loses, and #2, and so on through #1,000,000 — so no ticket wins, so there's no prize, so this whole lottery is a scam.' Clearly irrational. Yet he did exactly what the historian and foreman did: believed each item, then accepted something that rules out the conjunction of them all.

So my question is whether there's a principle about when you're allowed to go from believing each of a bunch of things to believing them all together (or about when accepting 'one of these is false' is fine and when it isn't), that keeps all three of my verdicts — historian rational, foreman fine, gambler nuts. I tried probability at first: maybe it's fine to believe each item and also believe 'one fails' as long as each item is likely enough? But that can't be the whole story, because each lottery belief ('this ticket loses') is way more likely than any single claim in the history book, and the gambler is still the crazy one. And it can't just be about how many items there are, because the foreman only has 100 widgets and the historian 500 claims and both seem okay. If no principle can save all my verdicts, then tell me which verdict I should give up, because right now I feel like I'm just deciding who gets to be irrational based on vibes. And please also tell me what your principle says about the case I genuinely can't make up my mind on: if I sit there with the whole million-ticket lottery in my head, believing of each ticket that it loses, am I allowed to believe 'no ticket wins'? It has to be false, so it feels like no — but the historian's 'one of these is false' also has to be true-ish, and I said he was fine. Whats the difference, if any?
