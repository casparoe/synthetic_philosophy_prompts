In most contemporary welfare states, need-based income support comes with a machinery of desert attached. Unemployment claimants sign a document committing to a weekly number of job applications; they must attend scheduled appointments, participate in mandatory training or CV workshops, and accept any offer of "suitable" employment; and missing an appointment or failing to document enough searches triggers a sanction — a suspension of payment that escalates with repeated failures. Meanwhile, the same systems maintain a parallel track of assessment: a claimant judged to have limited capability for work, via a functional test of what they can do rather than how hard they are trying, is moved out of the conditionality regime entirely and onto a less conditional, often more generous benefit. And at the edges, workfare programs pay benefits in exchange for community work that would be worth far below minimum wage on the open market, while means tests treat a partner's income as available to the claimant regardless of the claimant's own conduct, and tax relief on mortgage interest flows to homeowners with no requirement that they seek work at all.

What theory of desert and responsibility does this apparatus presuppose? Specifically: does the sanction regime presuppose that poverty tracks choice — that the unemployed could, and therefore should, work — while the disability assessment presupposes the opposite, that some poverty is a matter of brute luck the state must absorb? Are these two presuppositions consistent with each other, or does the system quietly rely on a luck/choice distinction that, applied consistently, would also sanction the involuntarily idle and excuse many sanctioned claimants whose job search fails for reasons outside their control? Please reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguishing what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits well with — for instance, whether conditionality could be justified by reciprocity (a fair return for contributions) rather than desert, so that the claim of the unlucky job-seeker is no weaker than that of the disabled claimant. Finally, tell me what the apparatus would have to look like if the presupposed view were replaced: by an unconditional basic income on one hand, or by a strict reciprocity-based system on the other, and which parts of the current machinery would survive each replacement unchanged.
