I'm an undergraduate in a seminar on rational agency and decision theory, and this term we've moved from standard game theory to bargaining under credible commitment, and finally to what changes when agents can self-modify or write genuinely binding contracts. Last week the professor introduced the phenomenon he called commitment races: if being able to commit first is strategically valuable, and committing first often means committing before you know things you would very much like to know, then rational agents may find themselves locked into irrevocable commitments made under deep uncertainty. My study group met on Friday to prepare for the final, and two of its members, Marcus and Yuki, spent three hours unable to agree about it. I took notes, and since neither the rest of us nor our TA (who was at the next table and declined on the grounds that this exact question is on the exam) could referee it, I'd like you to.

Here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it. (1) Marcus claimed that early commitment is simply a strategic resource, like moving first in an ultimatum game: whichever side can credibly lock in a take-it-or-leave-it demand while the other remains flexible captures most of the surplus. So agents that can self-modify or sign binding contracts should commit as early as possible — meaning before learning the opponent's commitments and, in the limit, before learning almost anything else about the interaction. (2) Yuki said that can't be a norm of rationality: if everyone commits as early as possible, agents lock in aggressive positions while mutually ignorant, and sometimes they lock in incompatible ones — both demand sixty percent of the surplus and the deal dies — producing disasters both would have paid heavily to avoid, caused precisely by the strategy Marcus recommends. Advice that is good only if you alone follow it isn't advice about rationality; it's advice about being lucky. (3) Marcus answered that Yuki is confusing what a rational agent should do with which equilibrium we'd prefer: in a prisoner's dilemma defection is rational even though universal defection is worse for everyone, and a situation can be tragic without the advice being wrong. Moreover, early commitment is not blind commitment — an agent can condition its locked-in policy on everything it already knows, and irrevocability with respect to the future isn't ignorance about the present. (4) Yuki replied that the prisoner's dilemma comparison fails in exactly one respect: there, each player knows the entire game before choosing, so defection is dominant given full information, whereas here the whole predicament is that you're choosing an irrevocable policy precisely when you don't yet know which game you're in, who you're facing, or what the balance of power will be — you can't even compute the expected value of your commitment, since it covers scenarios you haven't seen — and as for conditioning on everything you already know, the entire point of waiting would be to know more, which is exactly what an early commitment forfeits. (5) Marcus's last word was that Yuki's picture misses what commitment is for: its credibility to the other side comes precisely from its independence from later evidence, since an agent that reserves the right to revise can simply be waited out. He conceded that perhaps the best design is a commitment with a genuinely costly escape hatch — a clause invocable only at a visible, heavy penalty — preserving some flexibility without destroying credibility. Yuki answered that this just restarts the race one level up: agents will compete to design escape hatches that look punishing to others but turn out, in the scenarios that actually obtain, to be cheap. Neither would budge.
