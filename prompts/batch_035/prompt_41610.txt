I'm a software engineer who has spent the last decade maintaining a large legacy system — call it the Platform — that has been continuously deployed the whole time. During those years, the codebase has been strangler-figged: old modules were progressively wrapped, reimplemented, and deleted, until by now, by the team's best estimate, essentially no line of the original survives; more recently still, a full rewrite in a different language preserved outward behaviour bit-for-bit at the API boundary, and at one point a hard fork of the five-year-old repository is still running in production at a sister company. My colleagues keep calling the system "the same Platform" through all of this, and I've realised I don't know what, if anything, that could mean — and that philosophers have been arguing about my situation for two and a half thousand years under the name of the Ship of Theseus.

Please act as a professional metaphysician with a patient, technically literate manner, and give me a systematic translation rather than an analogy. Restate the actual philosophical positions on material-object identity over time — mereological essentialism (the view that losing or replacing any plank makes it a different ship), the sortal-based view that "ship" is a identity-carrying kind with its own persistence conditions, Geach-style relative identity (same ship but not same collection of planks), the four-dimensionalist or "temporal parts" picture, and the best-candidate approach — each in the vocabulary I actually use: commits and diffs, refactors and rewrites, behavioural versus structural equivalence, interfaces and their implementations, repository forks, deployment artefacts versus source trees, hashes as content-addressed identity. For each position, be explicit about what it would instruct us to say about the Platform, the rewrite, and the hard fork. I'd also like you to cover the Hobbes extension of the puzzle — the rival ship reassembled from the discarded planks — as a question about whether my sister company's fork has a claim to being "the Platform" that grows stronger as it accumulates divergent commits, and about what the museum-plank case suggests regarding which artefacts carry identity at all.

Then, and this is the part I care most about, give me the residue report: which parts of the philosophical debate map cleanly onto things my vocabulary already handles (I suspect behavioural-equivalence testing does a lot of work, and content hashes do something like mereological essentialism a bit too well), which parts only translate with distortion, and which parts have no expression in software terms at all — for instance, whether the question "but is it really the same ship?" asks anything that isn't answered by fixing a sortal first, and whether my discipline has simply never needed a word for diachronic identity because versioning schemes make the question look settled. Begin by briefly brainstorming which mappings are traps — places where a software concept looks like a metaphysical concept but quietly assumes a different answer — before committing to the translation, and keep the translation honest: don't let my vocabulary's built-in conventionalism smuggle in the conclusion that identity is just a labelling convention, unless you're prepared to defend that as philosophy rather than assert it as engineering practice. Finish by telling me whether the residue points at something software engineering genuinely cannot say, or at something the metaphysics was less clear about than it seemed.
