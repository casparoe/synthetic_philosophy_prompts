In my applied ethics seminar we have just finished Peter Singer's 1972 essay on famine and affluence, the one built around the case of a child drowning in a shallow pond. My classmate Maya has been recording her own verdicts on duty-of-rescue cases all term, and next week the seminar turns to organ donation, so I want to predict where she will land on a new case before she announces it.

Her track record so far, all as verdicts of "there is a duty":

1. Passing a shallow pond in which a child is drowning, where wading in will ruin a coat costing several hundred dollars: must rescue.
2. A mailing from a reputable charity stating that two hundred dollars will save a child from a waterborne disease, where the money would otherwise pay for a concert ticket: must give.
3. The same case, except Maya learns that hundreds of thousands of other donors received the same appeal, so that no particular child's survival hinges on her gift: still must give.
4. A physician who could staff a free clinic every Sunday indefinitely, at the cost of nearly all her weekend leisure for the foreseeable future: must do it.
5. A rescuer who can save a drowning swimmer only by wading into a flooded river carrying a real but small risk of being swept away: must attempt the rescue.

The new case: Maya has been donating fifteen percent of her income for five years — well beyond any plausible fair share of the global shortfall — and a colleague in her department needs a living kidney transplant. She has just learned she is the only compatible donor in the registry; the surgery carries a small mortality risk, two months of recovery, and no lasting harm is expected.

I would like the following, please. First, a prediction of her verdict on the kidney case. Second, at least two distinct rules that each fit her entire track record equally well but disagree on the kidney case — I suspect the disagreement turns on whether the duty extends to irreversible sacrifices of body as opposed to money, time, and risk, and on whether having already exceeded a fair share discharges further duties, but tell me if other rules fit better. Third, your best guess as to which rule Maya is actually running, with your reasons. Fourth, the single further case you would put to Maya that would most cleanly separate the surviving rules — for example, something testing continued monetary giving well past her fair share, or a reversible bodily donation such as bone marrow, but choose the case you think is genuinely most diagnostic, not necessarily one of these. Finally, please state how confident you are in each of your claims and single out the point about which you are least sure — I would rather have an honest marking of the weak spots in your reconstruction than a seamless prediction.
