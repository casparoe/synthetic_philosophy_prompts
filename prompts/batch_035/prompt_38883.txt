I'm running a social epistemology seminar for third-years, 18 students, one 75-minute session, and I want to open our unit on conspiracy theories with a game rather than a lecture. The thing I want them to feel in their bones is NOT "conspiracies never happen" — obviously they do, that's the trap. It's that the reasoning pattern that leads you to a true conspiracy belief and the one that lands you in paranoid fringe territory is structurally identical from the inside, and what actually separates them (base rates, how you treat counterevidence, whether anything would count as refutation) is invisible while you're doing the reasoning. So the game should put them through perfectly sensible-looking inference steps and have them end up either vindicated or badly wrong, with the difference only becoming visible in the debrief.

My rough idea, improve it or throw it out: six editorial desks of three. Each desk gets a dossier on a suspected cover-up — some desks get real historical cases (things that turned out to be actual conspiracies), others get ones I fabricate beforehand with the same texture: an insider affidavit, some leaked-looking memos, a pattern of official denials, a couple of genuinely awkward-but-innocent facts. Each desk decides publish / don't publish / investigate further, with scoring where false positives and false negatives both cost you, but investigating also costs and the session clock is the budget. My worry: this collapses into "whoever got the fabricated dossier just loses," which teaches the wrong lesson — it makes it seem like the fabricated ones should have been detectable, when the whole point is they weren't, from the inside.

What I want from you: full rules, materials I can actually prep in one evening, the payoff scheme (no real money, but something that stings a little — classroom status is fine), a run sheet with timings, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the epistemic points — the ad hoc immunization of the theory, the asymmetry where debunking evidence gets re-read as further cover-up, the base rate point. Take an actual stance on whether my design works or whether the point gets drowned by it, and design around the failure mode I'm worried about — I'd rather you restructure the whole thing than patch it. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it live. And be honest about which part of the idea a game simply can't reproduce and I'll have to supply myself in the debrief. Don't hedge between options: pick one design and defend it.
