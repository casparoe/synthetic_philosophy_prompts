I teach a 200-level philosophy of mind course at a state school, ~22 students, one 80-minute session. Next week is the reliability of introspection, and I want them to hit the problem before we read any of the skeptics — they're arriving from a qualia unit still thinking introspection is basically the gold standard for knowing your own mind. So I need an in-class activity that dismantles that confidence from the inside, something they run on themselves rather than a demo I do at them from the front of the room.

Here's roughly what I've been imagining, but improve on it freely: a two-part thing. Part one, they make choices or solve little puzzles and immediatly write down how and why they did it — confident first-person reports. Part two, the setup quietly messes with those reports. I'm thinking of something like the choice blindness studies, where you hand someone back the option they *didn't* pick and they happily defend it as their pick, but staged so the students do it to each other in pairs. I can print materials beforehand (color printer, index cards, tape, willing to bring candy as stakes), and I have a TA who can handle the shuffle mechanics. What I don't know is how to make this actually work in a room of 22 without it collapsing into into chaos or half the class seeing through it in the first five minutes — so design the logistics so it survives that.

Then the debrief: I want to map specific moments in the activity onto the actual distinctions — process vs. post-hoc report, being wrong about why you chose vs. there being no fact of the matter about why, the difference between "introspection is sometimes unreliable" and the much stronger skeptical conclusion — without it turning into me lecturing for the last twenty minutes. Rules, materials, a timeline, whatever payoff scheme makes the reports feel real to them.

Two more things. Tell me the predictable failure modes the first time I run this — the student who spots the trick at minute two and wants to announce it, the reports that are honestly ambiguous rather than confabulated — and how to handle each. And be honest about which part of the skeptical conclusion this activity just cannot deliver no matter how cleanly it runs, the thing I'd have to argue for on the page instead of showing them in the room.
