After a career in physiology I have spent my retirement reading philosophy, and at present I am deep in Aristotle. I have also been asked to give the farewell address at the retirement dinner of my oldest scientific colleague, a developmental biologist who spent her whole career defending talk of function and purpose in organisms against colleagues who dismissed it as pre-scientific leftover. She loved invoking Aristotle as the first great biologist, and her fiercest critics loved replying that the same Aristotle defended natural slavery in the Politics — that some humans are slaves by nature, instruments with souls, to be ruled as the body is ruled by the soul. Now that I have actually read those chapters, I find I cannot give an honest speech that just changes the subject: the argument there is rooted in the very same teleological picture of nature she admired, the idea that each thing has a function and that some beings are for the sake of others. Please draft the speech. It must honor her defense of biological function-talk without laundering Aristotle, and it must say something true about what it is to take the good parts of a thinker whose framework also produced this — for an audience that is mostly biologists, plus her daughter, who teaches ancient philosophy and will notice every philosophical shortcut I take.
