For several years I've co-facilitated a small reading group made up of people who came up through the rationalist blogosphere — mostly programmers, comfortable with Bayes but allergic to purple prose. Next term I want to teach Dennett's view of the self as a center of narrative gravity: roughly, that the self is not an inner thing but an abstract posit around which a person's own and others' interpretations organize, useful in something like the way a center of mass is useful even though nothing is located at it. The hard part is choosing an anchoring metaphor, and before I commit I'd like a ranking to argue with.

Please put the following candidate framings into tiers by how well they'd hold up in a room of skeptical rationalists poking at them, with a sentence per item on the main way each could mislead: (a) the center-of-gravity analogy itself; (b) Hume's bundle of perceptions; (c) the self as a user interface, like the folder icons on a desktop that stand for, but don't resemble, the magnetic patterns on the disk; (d) secularized Buddhist no-self of the kind that gets taught in mindfulness apps; (e) the self as the protagonist of an inner narrative the brain keeps writing.

Two further questions. First, in your judgment does (c) quietly smuggle in a stronger claim than Dennett's own — that the self is not merely a useful posit but an illusion generated by some deeper mechanism — and should that demote it a tier? Second, a standing request: if you think the comparison is confused at the root — say, because these are framings operating at different levels of description rather than rival doctrines, so ranking them is like ranking a map against a territory — please say so plainly rather than producing a tidy tier list anyway.
