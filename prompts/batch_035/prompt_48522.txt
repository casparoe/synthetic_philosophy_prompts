I've been reading a popular science book about the mind, and one chapter sums up Daniel Dennett's view of the self like this: "Dennett's idea is that the self is a story the brain tells. In physics, every object has a center of gravity, a single point at which all its mass can be treated as concentrated, and in just the same way each person has a single point at which their thoughts and choices converge, which is what we call 'I'. Since stories are made up, the self is made up too: it is a fiction, and fictions do not exist. So when you feel that you are deciding something, there is really no one there doing the deciding — the brain does what it does and tells the story afterwards. Dennett's conclusion is that there is no self, and therefore no one is ever really responsible for anything they do; we keep the fiction going only because it is useful, the way we keep treating characters in a novel as if they were people."

Something about this chapter felt off to me, but I don't know Dennett's work well enough to say where. Could you go through the summary part by part and tell me which claims a careful defender of Dennett would accept, which are harmless simplifications, and which are genuine misrepresentations? In particular, please check whether the analogy to a center of gravity is being used the way Dennett uses it, and whether the leap from "the self is a fiction" to "no one is responsible for anything" is something Dennett actually commits himself to. Finally, does the book's conclusion follow from whichever parts of the summary are accurate?
