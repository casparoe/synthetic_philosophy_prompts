hey, quick background: for my metaethics course we have to submit a short "where I stand" statement that the rest of the seminar gets to tear apart next week, and I'd rather find the problems now than in front of everyone. I've written up my commitments and I have a nagging feeling at least two of them cant coexist, but I genuinely can't tell which. here's the list:

1. Moral realism: there are moral facts that don't depend on anyone's attitudes, and some moral claims are just true.
2. Naturalism: those facts are natural facts, part of the same furniture as chemistry and economics, and they supervene on the natural — no two possible worlds that are identical in every natural respect but differ morally.
3. No a priori reduction: I don't think 'good' or 'wrong' can be defined in non-moral terms, and I'll admit I feel the pull of the open-question intuition. But I think that's fine, because plenty of legitimate reductions (water = H2O) weren't a priori either — so the failure of conceptual analysis doesn't scare me off naturalism.
4. Debunking: I take the evolutionary debunking worry seriously enough that it's actually one of my main reasons for NOT being a non-naturalist — the non-naturalist seems to face an even worse coincidence problem about how our intuitions line up with the facts, right? so naturalism is supposed to be the safe harbour here.
5. Companions in guilt: I also think the same evolutionary pressures shaped our logical and epistemic intuitions, including the ones driving the debunking argument itself, so the debunking can't be that corrosive in the moral case either.
6. Method: reflective equilibrium, but weighted towards intuitions about cases — when my theory clashes with a verdict about a particular situation, the verdict usually wins.
7. A stubborn one: I think keeping a promise can be genuinely binding even when breaking it would produce better consequences, and I'm broadly sympathetic to consequentialism about everything else. this is the verdict I refuse to give up.
8. Normativity: I like the buck-passing picture — calling something good is just saying there are reasons to favour it, and the reasons are the fundamental normative facts.

I suspect 4 and 5 are somehow in tension (how can the debunking be serious enough to rule out non-naturalism but defanged by generalisation?), and maybe 2, 3 and 8 pull against each other too, but honestly I might be wrong about where the real problem is.
