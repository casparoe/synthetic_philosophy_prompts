I've been working through the classic sea battle problem from Aristotle's De Interpretatione, chapter 9, where he seems to deny that propositions about future contingent events are already true or false. My question is about how this debate relates to modern physics, because it seems to me that physics has now decisively settled things in Aristotle's favor. Quantum mechanics tells us that the future is genuinely indeterminate: even given the complete state of the universe right now, many future events (like the outcome of a particular measurement) are not determined by that state. So it looks like future-tensed propositions simply cannot have truth values until the events in question occur, and the whole apparatus philosophers have built up for resisting this conclusion — the move of saying that a proposition about tomorrow is already true, but only contingently so — is obsolete. Insisting that a sentence like "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" is true right now seems like a residue of Laplacean determinism: if nothing in the present makes it the case that the sea battle happens, in what sense could the claim be true now rather than a prediction awaiting verification? Yet contemporary philosophers mostly carry on using bivalent semantics for future contingents as though nothing happened. Please explain where exactly this line of reasoning goes wrong, and what quantum indeterminacy does and does not establish about whether future contingents have truth values.
