I spent the better part of thirty years doing ecological modeling before I retired, so I came to this fairness-in-algorithms business with a strong prior that calibration is basically the whole game: if the model says 20% risk and things happen about 20% of the time for everyone it says that to, what's left to complain about? But my gut verdicts on actual cases don't all line up with that, and I'd like to know if there's one principle behind them or if I'm just inconsistent. So here are the cases and my verdicts.

Case one: a stroke-risk model, calibrated within each sex. For the women it flags as high risk, strokes happen at exactly the advertised rate, same for the men, even though the two groups have different base rates to begin with. Verdict: fair, full stop. Feels obvious to me. Case two: the pretrial bail kind of model you read about in the paper -- calibrated for Black and white defendants, yet among the people who would NOT have reoffended anyway, the Black ones get labeled high risk noticeably more often. Verdict: unfair, and I can't shake it. And yes I'm aware these two cases have the same shape, which is embarrassing, because apparently people proved you can't have calibration and equal error rates at the same time when the base rates differ, so one of my two verdicts has to be wrong somewhere. Case three: two groups with identical base rates, model never sees group membership, but it still flags one group twice as much. Verdict: unfair, no contest. Case four: model uses zip code instead of group membership, and zip code happens to recreate the racial disparity almost exactly. Verdict: still unfair, but somehow less bad than case three, and I honestly can't say why, since mathematically it's the same model with one column renamed. And the one I keep going back and forth on: a hiring model trained on the company's past hiring decisions, where the past decisions were themselves biased. The model is perfectly calibrated against the label -- when it says this candidate is a 10% hire, candidates like that one get hired about 10% of the time -- but the label itself is crooked. Fair to the data, unfair to the world? Does that distinction even do any work, or is accuracy to a biased label just accuracy?

So that's my tentative principle, I guess -- calibration within every group is fairness -- and it clearly can't survive contact with case two, unless someone can show me why case two isn't really the counterexample it looks like. Is there a principle (or maybe a couple of candidate principles) that captures all five verdicts or most of them, stated precisely enough to apply to new cases? For each clause, tell me which case is motivating it. Then tell me what the principle says about a case I haven't listed: say a cancer-screening triage model where the true prevalence genuinely differs by group, and the only way to equalize missed-detection rates across groups is to let the model be badly miscalibrated for at least one of them. And please be upfront about which of my verdicts the winning principle flat-out cannot save -- I'd rather overturn one of my gut reactions than carry around a principle with a special epicycle bolted on for each case.
