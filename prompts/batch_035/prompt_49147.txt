I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy training, and I've settled on a position I'd describe this way: the doing/allowing distinction carries real moral weight, but only when the death or harm runs through you as the proximate intervention point in the causal chain — when flipping a switch, deploying or rolling back code, or withholding a patch is the last decision before the harm occurs. On my view, a monitoring system that alerts me and I decline to roll back a bad deploy is nearly as bad as deliberately deploying it, but being one of a thousand engineers who could have caught a bug is nearly exculpatory. Tell me what would count against this position: which cases it can't describe plausibly, which intuitions about rescue and remote harm it should be expected to capture but doesn't, and whether my "proximate intervention point" criterion just quietly collapses into consequentialism or into the very distinction it was supposed to replace. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me honestly whether I've defined the position so flexibly that nothing could refute it — and if so, whether that matters for a claim of this kind.
