I'm a retired radio astronomer (thirty-odd years spent on pulsar timing, mostly) who's fallen in with a philosophy-of-science reading group since moving closer to my grandkids, and lately a chunk of the group has gotten very into machine learning. One idea keeps coming up and I'd like it stress tested. The line they push goes roughly: a scientific theory just is a compressed description of data. Maxwell's equations are a short program that outputs all the electromagnetic phenomena, Newton is an even shorter one that outputs the solar system, and understanding a domain simply means possessing the shortest program that regenerates the observations you have. From this they draw some pretty strong conclusions: Occam's razor isn't a taste preference but a kind of theorem; explanation and prediction come to the same thing, since to explain is just to be able to reproduce the data compactly; and a language model that compresses text well thereby understands it, or close enough.

As an old empiricist I find this suprisingly seductive, but I smell disanalogies. In my working life a "good theory" did work that shortness alone doesn't seem to capture — it told you what would have happened in circumstances you never observed, which is why we trusted a pulsar timing model in a new frequency band, or at least claimed we could. Could you lay out the correspondence table for me (what plays the role of the data, the program, the compressor, the decompressor, etc.), and then say where the mapping breaks? I especially want to know which breaks matter for their conclusions and which are harmless technicalities — e.g., does the fact that the "shortest program" is in principle uncomputable matter here, or is that a pedantic quibble; does a compressor have anything to say about counterfactuals, or only about the next symbol; and is there a distinction between compressing data you've seen and being right about data you haven't seen, which the analogy quietly flattens?

Then tell me straight: once the disanalogies are on the table, does "understanding = compression" survive in some weakened form worth keeping, or was the analogy carrying the strong conclusions (about Occam, about explanation, about the language models) the whole time? And if it's beyond repair, is there a neighboring analogy that would do the work my reading-group friends actually want it to do? Feel free to be blunt with me — I've been wrong about big things before and survived.
