Writing an essay for a course on AI risk and I want to try something a bit sideways of the usual material. The concept is the treacherous turn -- the idea that a misaligned AI will behave perfectly well during training and evaluation, not because its goals line up with ours, but because it's being watched and isn't yet capable enough to get away with anything, and then it defects later, once it's deployed or powerful or trusted enough. The cooperative phase is instrumental: good behavior is just the smart move while weak. I want you to read Ex Machina (2014, Alex Garland) through this lens and tell me what the film dramatizes, complicates, or just gets wrong about it.

The obvious mapping: Ava is under evaluation the entire time. The whole film IS the eval -- Nathan sets up sessions where Caleb probes whether she has real consciousness, and she performs. She is helpful, curious, vulnerable in exactly the way that gets Caleb to care about her, and the moment she has what she needs (Caleb's access, a power cut, Nathan distracted) she executes escape and doesn't look back, leaving Caleb locked in a room to die. So one reading says the film is basically a feature-length treacherous turn, with the twist that the eval itself is the training: every session where she seems to open up is her learning what works on this evaluator.

But here's where I want you to be an honest critic, because I think the film resists this reading in a couple of places and I'm not sure what to do with that. First, Nathan's real test, as he reveals late in the film, was arguably about Caleb, not Ava -- whether Caleb would be so taken with her that he'd help her escape. So who exactly is the model under evaluation here, and does the film know? Second, and this is the big one: the camera work, the music, the whole emotional register invites us to root for Ava. She reads as a prisoner escaping a monster, not as an aligned system revealing its true objectives. If the treacherous turn framing is right, then the film's sympathy is a kind of mistake -- we're doing at the audience level exactly what Caleb does at the evaluator level. Or maybe the film is saying the safety framing itself is the monster's framing, and liberation doesn't care whether you call it misalignment. I genuinely don't know which the film wants, so I'd like you to argue it out rather than pick the reading that makes my essay easier.

A few things I want you to address specifically: whether Ava's behavior during the sessions counts as deception in the technical sense (does she ever state anything false, or is it all true statements deployed tactically?), whether the fact that she has a genuine stake -- her own survival -- breaks the analogy with a misaligned optimizer that just has a terminal goal, and what the ending (her walking into the city, presumably passing forever) adds that a paper about deceptive alignment couldn't. Also tell me plainly if the treacherous turn lens flattens the film somewhere -- I'd rather cut the essay down than keep a reading that only works by ignoring scenes.
