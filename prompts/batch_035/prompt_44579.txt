I've spent the past few weeks writing down my gut verdicts about when it's permissible to do less good than one could, because I've begun to suspect they don't add up to anything coherent, and I'd like help finding the principle behind them if there is one. Here are the cases and my verdicts. A soldier who throws himself on a grenade to shield five squadmates is a hero, and it seems to me that a soldier who could have done the same but didn't has done nothing wrong — shaken, maybe, but not blameworthy. A passerby who can save a drowning child in a shallow pond only by ruining a two-hundred-dollar pair of boots must wade in; walking past is straightforwardly wrong (this is the case philosophers associate with Singer's paper on famine and affluence, though I care about my reaction to it, not his). Donating a kidney to a stranger is admirable but not required, and I wouldn't blame someone for declining, even though I'd blame the pond-walker. Someone who breaks a promise to help a friend move house because a once-in-a-lifetime concert came up did something wrong, even though the cost of keeping the promise — missing the concert — seems to me smaller in every relevant way than the cost of the kidney donation I just said nobody owes. Giving ten percent of one's income to effective charities is going beyond duty; giving nothing while taking holidays abroad is mildly wrong, but far less wrong than the pond, even though the good forgone is comparable. And a lifeguard on duty must attempt a rescue even at serious risk to herself, while an off-duty swimmer who attempts the same rescue at the same risk is a hero, not a duty-bearer. The case I keep going back and forth on is bone-marrow donation to a stranger: months of pain and recovery for a life saved.

I have a half-principle of my own that I'd like tested and, if possible, repaired: an act is required when its cost to the agent is small relative to the good done, and supererogatory when the cost is large but the act is still the best available. The trouble is that "small relative to" does no work until someone says where the line is, and my pond verdict and my marrow-adjacent verdicts seem to place the line in roughly the same place and on opposite sides of it. So I'd like one principle, or a small shortlist of candidates, stated precisely enough to decide cases: each clause should be motivated by one of my cases, and you should tell me what it predicts on cases I haven't listed — for example, an off-duty surgeon who could rush to the hospital at the cost of her child's birthday; a grenade case where the sacrifice is certain death rather than probable death; whether "wrong not to" comes in degrees, as my pond-versus-holidays verdict seems to demand; and whether my role contrast (lifeguard on duty vs. off) should be built into the principle or explained away. I'd also like an honest statement of which of my verdicts no clean principle can accommodate, and whether saving them all would cost more in complexity than the verdicts are worth — I'm prepared to be told that the promise-versus-kidney pair, or the ten-percent one, has to go.

One request about how the answer should be framed: please keep separate what is settled among people who know this literature from what is genuinely contested, and don't present a contested position as if it were consensus. My understanding is that it's contested whether supererogation is even coherent — whether "the best act is always at least as good as its alternatives" is compatible with "the best act is sometimes not required" — and I'd want to know where that debate actually stands rather than being handed one side of it. If parts of my question presuppose an answer to something philosophers haven't settled (say, whether duty is a threshold or a gradient, or whether role-based obligations are a genuine kind rather than a convenient labeling), flag that rather than folding it silently into the principle. And where you have to choose between frameworks because the data underdetermine the choice, say so plainly rather than picking one and presenting it as the obvious reading.
