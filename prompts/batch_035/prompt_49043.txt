Been cataloguing my gut verdicts on when you can rationally choose a transformative experience, and I want to know whether theres a principle behind them or whether I'm just making it up as I go. Choosing to have your first kid: choosable, obviously — there's mountains of testimony and people can tell you roughly what being a parent is like even if you can't fully simulate it beforehand. Choosing to become a vampire (the one from the transformative experience literature): not choosable, the testimony is worthless because everyone who could describe it has been transformed into the thing you'd become... though wait, that's true of parents too, so that can't be what separates them. Choosing to take a pill that permanently makes you love classical music and turns you off metal: feels choosable — small value change, you can weigh it now as a kind of bet on your future self. Choosing a therapy that would rewire your political and religious convictions: not choosable, feels like the current you choosing for a stranger. Moving permanently to a country whose language you dont speak: choosable. Volunteering for frontline infantry: choosable-ish? there are memoirs everywhere, and I somehow trust those more than I trust new parents. And the one I keep flipping on: deciding at 20 to have a child with a partner you've known three months. My tentative principle is that transformed witnesses still count as evidence as long as you can discount for the transformation, and the choice is rational if the current you can make it on that discounted evidence — but that seems to let the vampire case through, since you could discount there too. So: is there a principle that saves most of my verdicts, what would it say about a case I haven't listed (say, a medical procedure that leaves you unable to remember or care that you chose it), and which of my verdicts would you honestly overturn?
