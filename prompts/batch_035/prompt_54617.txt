My cousin is a second-year law student and at Thanksgiving he walked me into an argument about judicial review that I can't find my way out of. His claim is that when courts strike down laws passed by elected legislatures, that's not just sometimes unwise, it's democratically illegitimate, full stop. The argument:

(1) A rule for making collective decisions is democratically legitimate only if everyone bound by a decision had a fair say in it.
(2) A "fair say" isn't just casting a vote that gets recorded and then overruled — it means that if your side loses, there remained a realistic route, no harder than the one the winners used, by which your side could later win ordinary elections and get the decision reversed.
(3) When a legislature passes a statute, the losers can reverse it later by winning the same kind of majority the winners won. Fine.
(4) When a court strikes down a statute, the only route back for the people who enacted it is a constitutional amendment — two-thirds of both houses plus three-quarters of the states, which on anything contested basically never happens.
(5) A route that never happens isn't a route at all.
(6) So judicial review denies the losers a fair say, and is illegitimate.

Here's my problem. The argument looks valid, but it seems to prove way too much. A veto override needs two-thirds while the original passage needed a bare majority — so by his standard the presidential veto is illegitimate too. The Senate gives a voter in Wyoming vastly more representation than a voter in California, and the constitution says the small states can't be stripped of equal representation without their own consent, so that route is even more of a wall than amendment — so the Senate is illegitimate too. And if the constitution's rules bind people today who never voted to ratify them, then by premise (1) the whole document is illegitimate, including the parts he'd need to justify whatever system he wants instead. When I pushed him on this he just said the amendment threshold "isn't a route, it's a wall," which felt like he was leaning on premise (5) doing all the work with no principle behind it. But I can't actually name the flaw, and honestly I suspect there isn't one — maybe the conclusion really does follow and the argument just exposes that I should be against more things than I thought.

So: which premise should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I give up (2), I lose the ability to call anything unfair except total exclusion. If I give up (5), I need some principled line between "harder" and "too hard," and I can't find one that isn't arbitrary. And is my "proves too much" reaction even right, or do the extensions to the veto and the Senate genuinely follow and I should bite those bullets? Tell me where you land, but also flag where your own diagnosis is most likely to go wrong, and what would change your verdict — like if some consideration about how judicial appointments actually work, or some cleaner way to draw the difficulty line, should flip the answer. Don't just pick a premise and declare victory; I want to know what I'd be paying for each way out.
