I'm a second-year graduate student taking a seminar on theories of truth, and in our reading group a senior member has taken to ending any discussion of the Liar paradox with the slogan 'truth is disquotation.' The exchange that bothered me: I was presenting the Liar — a sentence that says of itself that it is not true — and sketching how different substantive theories of truth might try to accommodate it, when he cut in that the whole issue is trivial: by disquotation, a sentence that says of itself that it is not true is true if and only if it is not true; that is a straight contradiction; so the sentence is degenerate and there is nothing left for a 'theory of truth' to do. The discussion ended there, and I still can't tell whether he dissolved the paradox or merely restated it.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher who works on truth and logical consequence, and think carefully before answering — start with some brainstorming about what 'truth is disquotation' could even amount to, since I suspect the slogan compresses several distinct theses into one line. Then lay those readings out as a numbered menu I can bring back to the reading group: for instance, the near-tautological equivalence holding for each particular sentence; the claim that the truth predicate's entire function is generalization, letting us say in one finite sentence what would otherwise demand an endless list; the stronger claim that truth is not a substantive property at all and there are no deeper facts about truth than the disquotational instances; and any further readings you think are genuinely in play. For each, tell me whether it is a platitude, whether it is contentious, and — this is the part I care about most — whether it licenses his move of treating the Liar as a harmless degenerate case that requires no theory. My own suspicion is that on the stronger readings the Liar is a worse problem for him than for the substantive theorist, since he has fewer resources to explain why the sentence misfires rather than being simply false, but I want your honest view, not confirmation of mine.

Finally, don't leave me with a menu and no verdict. Commit: say which reading he most likely had in mind given the work he made the slogan do, and take a definite position of your own on whether the Liar can be set aside this way. If you think some deflationists can answer it — say, by a hierarchy of languages, or by denying that the Larike sentence expresses a proposition at all — say so and give your reasons; if you think his dismissal was illegitimate and the paradox is in fact a standing embarrassment for the slogan, say that flatly. I would rather have a committed answer I can argue with in seminar than a balanced survey I can't use.
