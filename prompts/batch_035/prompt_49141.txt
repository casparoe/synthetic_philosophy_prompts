I'm a PhD student in economic history, and my thesis chapter on the Great Divergence keeps getting stuck in a way I'd like help diagnosing rather than adjudicating. The debate I mean is the one opened in earnest by Pomeranz's The Great Divergence around 2000 and its aftermath: whether the economic divergence between Britain (or northwest Europe) and China was late and contingent — turning on coal's location and New World resources, with the Yangzi delta and the European core comparably developed until roughly the eighteenth century — or early and structural, rooted in differences going back centuries in institutions, scientific culture, or the competitive state system. What I've noticed is that the argument has stopped moving even as the data has improved: the real-wage and GDP-series work of the past twenty years gets contested line by line, the two camps each have a story about why the other's numbers are built on false commensurability, and nobody appears to have changed their mind since roughly the mid-2000s.

My question is what kind of disagreement this actually is, and I have a hypothesis I want tested rather than endorsed. Philosophers of science sometimes analyse explanation as contrastive: a "why" question always carries an implicit contrast class, so "why did Britain industrialise first?" and "why did China not industrialise first?" are different questions that can license different explanations even from a shared set of facts. It strikes me that the divergence debate might be a disguised instance of this. The contingency camp seems to be answering "why did the Industrial Revolution happen where the coal was, rather than somewhere else?" while the structural camp is answering "why was England, as a whole and over centuries, on a different trajectory from China, as a whole?" — and when they argue, they fight about the unit and period of comparison (the Yangzi delta versus England; the whole of China versus the whole of Europe; divergence dated to 1700 or to 1800) precisely because each framing is what makes their own question answerable.

So: is my diagnosis right, in whole or in part, and where does it go wrong? Consider seriously the alternatives — that this is a genuine empirical dispute about dating and magnitudes that better price and wage series could in principle settle; that it is a dispute about standards of evidence, where the two camps disagree over what counts as a relevant comparison rather than over any fact; that the sides are divided over the legitimacy of different levels of explanation, proximate resource geographies versus ultimate institutional causes, and are fighting about which level "really" explains; or that the Needham-style question — why Europe and not China? — is simply ill-posed, smuggling in an assumption that China is the natural counterfactual Britain against which Britain must be explained, when no such privileged comparison exists. Note that answering this requires saying something about what each camp actually claims and how their arguments bite, not just labelling them. And be honest about what would count as progress under your diagnosis: a new dataset, a change in how the question is posed, a formal distinction between the kinds of explanations each side offers, or nothing at all and why. If part of the disagreement turns out to be merely verbal, identify which part, and try to restate the residue that is not.
