I'm a philosophy PhD student writing a dissertation on intergenerational justice, and my advisor keeps telling me my normative commitments form a package I haven't paid for. Before I defend my prospectus I want a genuine audit, not a pep talk, so here's the set. (1) I hold a person-affecting view of wrongness: roughly, an act is wrong only if it's bad for someone. This is mostly because impersonal value — total wellbeing across all people, whenever they exist — strikes me as morally spooky, and I argue against it in chapter 1. (2) I'm firmly convinced that the depletion policy, Parfit's case where we use up the resources and future people consequently live worse but clearly still worth-living lives, is wrong in the strong sense: we wrong those future people. This is basically the normative core of my dissertation and I argue it agressively in talks. (3) I also accept the nonidentity claim that depletion changes who exists, so the particular people living under depletion would simply not have existed under any alternative policy. (4) I deny that merely possible people have any moral status — you can't owe duties to people who never exist, there's no subject there to be owed anything. (5) I think harm is comparative: someone is made worse off only relative to an alternative in which they exist, and I'm fairly confident a worth-living life can't be worse for its bearer than never existing (I think wrongful-life claims are confused). (6) Separately, in the policy work I do on the side, I say that future generations have rights, and I mean it, it's not just rhetoric.

My sense is that something in this list doesn't fit together, but I honestly can't tell whether it's one big tension or several small ones, or whether some of these pairs only look like they clash. For instance, does (6) actually conflict with (4), given that the future people I'd be granting rights to are exactly the ones who wouldn't exist under alternative policies — or does that dissolve once you notice they're future-actual rather than merely possible? And can (2) survive at all if (3) and (5) are both true, or does it need impersonal value, which (1) rules out? I genuinely don't know which commitment I'd give up first, so don't assume on my behalf. Go through the pairs, tell me which tensions are real and which dissolve on inspection, rank them by severity, and for each real one say what revising the relevant committment would cost me elsewhere in the set. If some rescue is available — non-comparative rights, a wide person-affecting view that compares across different people, whatever — I want to hear it, but I also want to know what the rescue itself costs. One more thing: parts of this terrain are live disputes rather than settled results, and I know person-affecting views and the moral status of possible people are both contested. So please keep a clear line between what's settled among people who actually work on this and what's genuinely contested, and don't hand me one side's contested take dressed up as the consensus view.
