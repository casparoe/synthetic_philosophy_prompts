I serve on the board of the Merrow County Farmland Trust, a nonprofit that holds conservation easements across a midwestern agricultural county, and I need a written opinion to circulate before our board votes next month on whether to endorse the Agricultural Integrity Act, a bill now before the state legislature.

The Act responds to the following situation. Over the past six years, a venture capitalist named Deering has voluntarily purchased roughly 52,000 acres of county farmland from willing sellers, paying a premium of around thirty percent over market value, in order to convert the land into a private rewilded bison reserve. The sales were uncoerced and legal. However, many of the parcels were worked by tenant farmers holding informal, at-will leases renewable by custom for generations; when the land changed hands, those tenants were displaced with no legal claim to compensation. The Act would (1) prohibit any single buyer from acquiring more than 30,000 acres in the county going forward, and (2) require buyers currently above the cap to divest their excess holdings within five years, with the surplus offered at market price to qualifying family operators.

Our board is split along lines that map surprisingly well onto Nozick's entitlement theory. One faction argues that if the pre-Deering distribution was just and each sale was voluntary, then the resulting pattern of holdings is just no matter how concentrated it looks, and that maintaining any preferred pattern — like an assembly cap — requires continuous coercive interference with voluntary exchange, which is exactly the failure mode the Wilt Chamberlain argument is meant to expose. The other faction replies that the entitlement framework does not even apply here: the county's land titles trace to an 1830s treaty under which a displaced indigenous nation ceded the territory under duress, so the initial distribution was not just, and Nozick's own principle of rectification may license precisely the patterned intervention the first faction decries. They also press the Lockean proviso, noting that the tenants' situation is clearly worsened even though none of their legal rights was violated — which forces the question whether the proviso tracks formal legal entitlement or material position. A further wrinkle: as an alternative to endorsement, the Trust itself could launch a capital campaign to outbid Deering on the remaining parcels, steering land toward a pattern we prefer through entirely voluntary transfers.

Acting as a professional political philosopher with expertise in distributive justice and the entitlement tradition, please think carefully through the strongest considerations on each side before drafting — including whether the retroactive divestiture clause changes the analysis in a way a mere prospective cap would not, and whether the Trust's outbidding alternative is really as philosophically clean as it looks. Then draft the written opinion I will circulate. It should state a concrete recommendation (endorse, oppose, or endorse with specified conditions) and give honest, substantive treatment to the strongest argument on the losing side, not a strawman. Format it as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: the first point should state the recommendation, and the remaining four should carry the reasoning.
