I'm writing an essay on the gap between the mathematical infinite and the physical one. Cantor gave mathematics a whole hierarchy of completed infinities, and Hilbert's hotel illustrates how comfortably mathematics lives with them; but it is a further question whether anything in the actual world is like that — actually infinite in a completed sense, rather than merely unbounded, merely potentially infinite, or merely an idealization of our theories.

Give me your credence in the following proposition, stated precisely: somewhere in the actual world, some physical structure is genuinely instantiated as a completed infinity. The candidates I have in mind are the total number of concrete entities (stars, particles, events); the number of physically real points or instants, if spacetime has physically real continuum structure; and the spatial volume of the universe, if that is infinite. For reference, my own credence is about 0.6, resting mostly on spatial infinitude; I want to see whether your decomposition earns a different number, and, if so, where ours would disagree.

Please structure your answer as follows, in this order:

1. Bottom line — one sentence giving the number.
2. Decomposition — roughly 250 words. The sub-claims the credence rests on, each with a probability and one line of justification. I'd expect at least: whether a physically completed infinity is even coherent, given worries in the vicinity of Hilbert's hotel and supertasks; whether the universe is spatially infinite; whether spacetime points or continuum structure are physically real rather than convenient fictions of our models, which a relationalist would deny; and whether, even granting infinite space, the number of discrete concrete entities is infinite — an infinite universe does not by itself guarantee infinitely many things. You may add further terms if you justify them, but note that whether mathematical finitism, if true, would undercut the question at all is itself a sub-claim you'd need to defend, not assume.
3. Combination — about 100 words on how the terms compose into the final credence, flagging any correlations that make naive multiplication wrong.
4. The load-bearing term — one short paragraph on which sub-claim your number is most sensitive to.
5. What would move it — one short paragraph on the observation or argument that would most shift your credence, and roughly by how much.

Two constraints. First, don't let the decomposition collapse into a single term doing all the work: if, for instance, your credence in the coherence of completed infinity is effectively 0 or 1, say so explicitly and explain what weight the remaining terms can still carry. Second, do not treat the fact that our best physics is formulated in the continuum as settling physical instantiation — the question is whether the world is like that, not whether our models are.
