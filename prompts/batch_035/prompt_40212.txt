Quick bit of decision theory I've been reading about: Good's theorem says a Bayesian agent facing a decision on her own can never be made worse off by a costless observation — ex ante she can plan to do whatever she would've done anyway if the news is bad, so free info can't hurt and generally helps. The famous caveat is that it fails when there are other players involved (peeking at your opponent's hole cards in poker is a disaster once they see your face). OK so here's my ask: build me a case where an agent gets a perfectly reliable, private, costless piece of information, there's no second party whose behaviour depends on her having looked, she's fully rational, and she knows in advance she's going to recieve it — and yet she'd genuinely pay money not to be told. Constraints: realistic setting (a job, a hospital, a relationship, whatever — no demons, no perfect predictors), and the verdict has to fall out of the setup itself, not be stipulated by saying "and this ruins her life". After the scenario, show the expected-utility bookkeeping, tell me exactly which hidden assumption of the theorem your case breaks, what happens if you restore that assumption (say, by letting the agent bind her future self in advance), and then take an actual stance: does the case show Good's theorem is false, or just that its picture of the "agent" is narrower than it first looks? Defend the stance, dont end on a shrug.
