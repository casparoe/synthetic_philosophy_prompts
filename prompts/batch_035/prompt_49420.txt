For a seminar paper on population axiology I've been asked to state my welfare axiology and then defend it, and before I write the defense I want an audit of whether the thing I'm defending even hangs together. Here is the package. (1) I'm a hedonist about well-being: pleasure and suffering are the only things with final value or disvalue, and everything else matters only instrumentally. (2) I hold that suffering above a certain severity threshold has lexical priority: no quantity of pleasure, however large and however distributed, can outweigh a single instance of suffering at or above that threshold. (3) Below the threshold, I'm an ordinary aggregative utilitarian: mild suffering trades off against pleasure at some finite exchange rate, and numbers count in the usual way. (4) I want the threshold set fairly low — covering serious but ordinary suffering such as severe migraines, grief, or a badly broken bone, not just torture — because I'm convinced that no amount of bliss spread across other people could compensate one person for suffering of that kind. (5) At the same time, I firmly believe that many actual human lives, including lives containing repeated bouts of above-threshold suffering, are clearly worth living and better for the person than never existing, and that humanity's existence has been better than its never having occurred. (6) Two case verdicts I'm fairly confident about: (a) it can be rational, and not a tragic mistake, for someone to undergo above-threshold suffering for the sake of their own later, merely modest pleasures — say, an athlete choosing an agonizing endurance event for the sake of the experience and the memory; and (b) if we can either prevent one stranger's above-threshold suffering or instead relieve a very large number of people of just-below-threshold suffering, we should prevent the stranger's suffering, however large the second group is.

What I'd like from you is a pairwise consistency check of these six commitments. Tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look to be; for each genuine tension, say how severe it is, what the cheapest fix would be, and what that fix costs elsewhere in the package; and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. For orientation on where I suspect trouble but can't locate it: my advisor says (5) cannot survive (2) and (4) taken together, though she didn't say why; a friend in my reading group says verdict (6a) shows I'm smuggling in values about autonomy and self-authorship that (1) forbids; and someone else warned me that (4) combined with (3) commits me to something like the sadistic conclusion in population ethics — that I should sometimes prefer creating fewer people whose lives are quite bad over more people whose lives are good but contain some above-threshold suffering. I genuinely cannot tell which of these is the real problem, whether several of them are, or whether some are spurious, so please rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally serious, and be explicit about which of my two case verdicts would have to go if it comes to that.
