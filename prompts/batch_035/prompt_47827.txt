I'm a science journalist who covers mind and brain, and over years of writing about affective neuroscience I've become fluent in its working vocabulary: Berridge's distinction between "wanting" (dopaminergic incentive salience) and "liking" (the hedonic hotspots in the nucleus accumbens shell and ventral pallidum), the two-dimensional model of core affect as valence plus arousal, opponent-process dynamics, hedonic set points. Philosophers, I gather, are stuck on something they call the heterogeneity problem for hedonism: the pleasure of tasting chocolate, of finally understanding a proof, of relief when a fear passes, and of an orgasm seem to share no single felt quality, so what makes them all pleasures — and what makes each of them good for the person having it? Phenomenal accounts answer that there is a common felt character after all; attitudinal accounts say a pleasure is just a pro-attitude toward a state of affairs, with no common feeling required; desire-satisfaction views relocate the unity elsewhere again. Restate this debate in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can: treat the phenomenal account as a claim about a single "liking" signal, the attitudinal account as a claim about an evaluative state more akin to "wanting," and so on — and don't quietly assume the neuroscientific categories settle anything the philosophers left open. Then give me the residue report: which parts of each position translate cleanly, which have to be distorted to fit, and which cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all. The two questions I most want answered: is the heterogeneity problem simply the empirical finding that "liking" is itself fractionated across hotspots and modality, such that a sufficiently good neuroscience would dissolve it; or does the philosophers' further question — what all pleasures have in common that makes them good for the one who has them — have no counterpart in affective neuroscience at all? And if it has no counterpart, is that a gap in my vocabulary, or a sign the philosophical question was never well posed to begin with?
