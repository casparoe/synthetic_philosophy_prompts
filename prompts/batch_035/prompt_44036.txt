I teach philosophy of biology, and this week a stack of midterm essays exposed me: my marginal comments on the function questions don't line up case to case, and I can't write a grading rubric until I know what my actual view is. So here are my verdicts, and I want to know what principle, if anything, captures them.

The heart pumps blood and that's its function -- trivial, both the etiological and the causal-role people get that one. A screwdriver someone uses only as a paperweight: no function, just a use -- the object's function doesn't change with how it happens to be used. A malformed heart in an embryo that never manages to pump anything: I still want to say its function is to pump blood, which I take it pushes me toward selected-effect accounts, since the poor thing has no current causal contribution worth naming. But then the trouble starts. Feathers were originally selected for insulation and only later co-opted for flight, and I honestly feel flight is the function of a modern bird's feathers now, not insulation -- history shouldn't get the last word there. Worse: my colleague down the hall engineers a plastic-digesting enzyme for her start-up, and I want to say that enzyme has the function of digesting plastic the day it's synthesized, before any selection history exists at all. Meanwhile a tumor that happens to secrete cortisol in a patient who'd otherwise have adrenal insufficiency: no function, full stop, no matter how usefull it is to the patient. And vestigial eyes in cave fish: I waver -- on a good day I say they have no function and that's exactly why we call them vestigial, other days I catch myself wanting to say they function as fat storage or whatever they've drifted into.

Is there one account that gets all of these, or close to it? I suspect the design case and the malformed heart pull in opposite directions no matter what, but I'd like to be shown otherwise. Tell me which verdicts a coherent principle would have to overturn, and what the resulting principle predicts for a case I haven't listed -- say, a pig heart valve transplanted into a human, doing its old job in a body where nothing like it ever evolved for that role. One more thing, since this has to actually work as a rubric: can a TA apply your principle to a student essay without having to know the selection history of whatever organ the student writes about, or does the account demand homework the grader can't do? That practical point matters for whether I can even use the thing.

Answer in whatever form is usefull, but please end with one fixed line, exactly this format, so I can paste it at the top of the rubric:

Principle: <one sentence stating the account>
