I am a novelist several years into a book set in the final decades of the Raj, and I have a structural problem that research cannot solve by addition. My story turns on two families whose fates are entangled by empire: mill owners in Dundee whose fortune rides on jute shipped from Bengal, and their counterparts in Calcutta, a family of lawyers drifting toward the nationalist movement. In preparation I read Niall Ferguson's Empire: How Britain Made the Modern World (2003) and Shashi Tharoor's Inglorious Empire: What the British Did to India (2017) more or less back to back, and what struck me is how rarely the two books disagree about what happened — famine mortality, railway mileage, tariffs on Indian textiles, the spread of English, the collapse of India's share of world manufacturing — while disagreeing utterly about what it all adds up to. Ferguson keeps a kind of ledger, entering the empire's crimes on one side and its legacies of capital, connectivity, and institutional order on the other, and finds the exercise at least defensible. Tharoor keeps what he insists is not a ledger at all: some entries cannot be priced against others, and the entries that can be priced were extracted rather than given. My two families are going to have exactly this argument over dinner in 1930, and I refuse to let either of them be a fool or a straw man.

So I would like you to draft, as it were, the joint statement these two camps would produce after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Please give me: (1) a numbered section of claims about the Raj that both sides could sign their names to, stated precisely enough that neither could later complain about the wording; (2) a section of disagreements — a table, if you like — in which each disagreement is phrased twice, once as the Ferguson camp would accept it and once as the Tharoor camp would; and (3) the cruxes: the subquestions such that settling one would actually move a side. I suspect one crux is the counterfactual — what would the subcontinent's economic and political trajectory have been without British rule, over the same two centuries — and another is whether the balance-sheet framing itself is coherent, or whether it smuggles in the conclusion by treating incommensurable goods as commensurable; but there may be others neither book states openly. For each crux, name the kind of evidence or argument both camps would accept as bearing on it, and record in advance what each side predicts that evidence would show. Finally, if part of their quarrel turns out to be verbal — if, for instance, they are using words like "benefit" or "responsibility" in different senses rather than disagreeing about facts — please say which part and why, since a disagreement about words will play very differently at my dinner table than a disagreement about the world. What I need, in the end, is an argument between two people who are each right about something.
