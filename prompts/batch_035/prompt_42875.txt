hey, so I'm taking a political philosophy elective this semester on migration ethics and we just had the seminar where everyone talked past each other for two hours. the prof kept pushing one analogy on the restrictionist side and it's been bugging me all week. the analogy is basically: a country is like a family home. you lock your front door at night, you decide who sleeps in your guest room, and nobody thinks that makes you a bigot. so why should a state be any different about its border? one guy in my seminar (econ background) doubled down: citizens are like co-owners of the house, the border is the front door, unauthorized immigrants are people climbing in through the window, and visa quotas are just the homeowners deciding how many guests they can afford to host. the counter that got yelled was that borders aren't doors because the analogy only has one house but there are ~195 of them in reality, and if every house locks you out you're locked out of everywhere, which is not a thing a single door does to a person walking down a street.

I want you to act as a professional political philosopher for this one. think carefully before answering, maybe start with some actual brainstorming about what the analogy is even supposed to establish — a right to exclude at all? total discretion over who? over how many? discretion over the criteria? — because I suspect those come apart. then give me a straight audit: a correspondence table of what maps onto what (house, door, lock, owners, family members, guests, trespassers, the street outside), then the disanalogies, and please separate the ones that actually matter for what the analogy is being used to argue for from the ones that don't (like, idk, houses have a fixed number of bedrooms and countries don't — cute but probably irrelevant).

some things I want you to poke at specifically: (1) a door excludes you from one space among many, but a border excludes you from every job, road, and public space on that side of it, and the exclusion is only non-total because other "houses" let you in — does the analogy hide the fact that a system of doors isn't a door? (2) a house is plausibly owned, but is a country owned by its citizens in anything like that sense, and does the analogy just smuggle in a property claim over territory that needs its own argument? (3) the family in the house gets to be arbitrary about guests — you can turn someone away from your dinner party for any reason or no reason — is that the standard we want at border level, and if not, does the analogy import it anyway? (4) does the analogy do better at justifying some things restrictionists want (control over welfare-state membership, voting rolls) than others (excluding people from territory and labor markets), i.e., do the front door and the membership list come apart at some point?

then tell me straight: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion the seminar guy wanted — that exclusion at the border needs no more justification than a lock on your door — survive, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? and if the analogy is broken, is there a fix, like a condo building, a club, or a housing co-op analogy, that would do the job better — or does every version of "the state is a piece of property with a door" fail for the same underlying reason? bonus points if you can say whether the analogy cuts both ways against the guy who used it: homeowners can't lock the door on a spouse or a kid, there are people house-logic itself says you can't just throw out, and there's a difference between not inviting someone in and bolting the door on someone who's already in the living room. where would those cuts land on actual border policy?
