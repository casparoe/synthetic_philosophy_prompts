I'm a PhD student working on social epistemology, and I keep circling a question about the testimony literature that I can't quite settle on my own. The reductionism/anti-reductionism debate — Hume's demand that testimony be backed by inductive evidence of the speaker's reliability, versus the Reidian view that trust is a default that stands until defeated — looked, after Coady's 1992 book, like the central question of the epistemology of testimony. Thirty years later it hasn't been resolved so much as abandoned in place: the anti-reductionists never answered the reductionist's charge of gullibility to the reductionists' satisfaction, the reductionists never answered Coady's point that the "global check" reductionism demands — non-testimonial verification of the general reliability of testimony as a source — is not just unavailable but incoherent, since the background knowledge you would check against is itself soaked in testimony. Fricker's retreat to local reductionism, where the hearer needs positive reason only in defeater-flavored cases, was supposed to split the difference, but each side read it as a concession by the other, and then the field simply drifted off to testimonial injustice, expertise, peer disagreement, and the epistemology of deference, where the original question is presupposed rather than answered.

My working hypothesis is that the two camps were never actually disagreeing about one thing. The reductionist seems to be making a structural claim about the justificatory standing of individual beliefs — nothing testimonial counts as justification unless the hearer possesses collateral grounds — while the anti-reductionist is making a claim about entitlement in a normative-practice sense: given that a community's epistemic life only functions with default trust, hearers are entitled to rely on it without grounds. If that's right, the debate might be partly verbal, but not trivially so: it would be two answers to different questions (what justifies this belief now vs. what policies a rational community can license) that share a vocabulary, and the interesting work would be showing exactly where the equivocation on "justification" does the work. But I can also see the case that it's a genuine internalist/externalist dispute in disguise — reductionism presupposing access to grounds, anti-reductionism content with entitlements the subject can't articulate — in which case it's as substantive as that dispute and as unlikely to resolve. And there's a third reading I find harder to dismiss: that the disagreement is really about what epistemology is for, with the reductionist treating anti-gullibility as a design constraint on a belief-forming system and the anti-reductionist treating the epistemology of the isolated individual as a fiction that the whole question illegitimately imports.

I'd like a diagnosis, not a survey — I know the positions. Tell me which of these readings (or some other) best fits what the principals actually argued: does Coady's incoherence objection really refute reductionism, or only a strawman global version that no serious reductionist held? Is Fricker's local reductionism a stable halfway house or an unstable position that collapses into one of the poles once you press on how hearers are supposed to detect defeaters without doing exactly the checking the reductionist wanted? And given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress: a distinction, a reformulated question, a formal model, results from the psychology of source monitoring — or whether the drift of the literature away from the question was itself the correct verdict, and if so, what exactly the verdict was.
