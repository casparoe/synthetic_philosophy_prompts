You are a professional philosopher of biology with a strong interest in how institutions quietly take sides on contested metaphysical questions. Please think carefully and systematically before answering: start by brainstorming the candidate commitments each practice below could be resting on, list them explicitly, and only then argue for which ones are genuinely presupposed versus merely consistent with the practice.

I've read rationalist blogs and forums for a long time, and one recurring lesson I've absorbed is that you can learn a lot about an undefined concept by watching what institutions do with it rather than what they say about it. So here is my question. Humanity has no agreed definition of life, yet several major institutions operate as though the life/non-life boundary were perfectly serviceable, and they seem to service it in different, possibly incompatible, ways:

1. Planetary protection. Space agencies sterilize spacecraft destined for Mars and other bodies under international guidelines, and plans for a Mars sample-return mission call for handling the returned material in a Biosafety Level 4-style containment facility — the same class of facility reserved for the most dangerous Earthly pathogens. All of this happens without anyone adopting a definition of life; the policy simply assumes that "contamination" by Earth organisms is a well-defined risk, and that a returned sample is presumptively dangerous until shown otherwise.

2. Virology. Viruses are excluded from the standard tree of life — no metabolism, no independent replication, and their dedicated taxonomy is kept separate from cellular organisms — and yet research on them is funded, taught, and regulated as a life science, and few virologists lose sleep over the classification.

3. Synthetic biology. Work on organisms with minimal, artificially constructed genomes gets described as "creating life," and dual-use biosecurity policy treats engineered self-replicating entities as categorically concerning — while remaining largely silent on engineered non-replicating systems.

What theory of the life/non-life boundary, if any, does this patchwork presuppose? Does planetary protection strictly require that life is a natural kind with sharp edges — that any organism, spore, or virus is unambiguously "alive" and hence a contamination risk — or would a deflationary, family-resemblance view of life license exactly the same sterilization and quarantine practices? Is the BSL-4-style quarantine of a Martian sample coherent, given that it must be designed before anyone knows whether the sample contains anything that paradigm-based definitions of life would even classify as an organism — what would the facility actually do with a self-replicating non-metabolizing structure, or an informational molecule that spreads without Darwinian evolution? And is the patchwork internally consistent, or does each institution presuppose a different boundary drawn for its own purposes? Please distinguish what these practices strictly require from what they merely sit comfortably with, identify where the practices are philosophically underdetermined — such that a NASA-workshop-style working definition like "a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution" would change nothing operationally — and say what would concretely have to change in the practices if we replaced the implicit theory with an explicitly graded, no-sharp-boundary view of life.
