My grandfather turns eighty-five next month, and I've somehow ended up giving the main speech at his birthday dinner. He was a classics don for over forty years and the one thing he can't abide — he will literally get up and leave the room — is the modern liturgy of ageing well: "eighty-five is the new sixty," "young at heart," staying curious as if curiosity were an anti-ageing cream. He thinks all of it is a refusal to actually defend old age on its own terms, and he's said as much, at other people's parties, after a sherry. The book he's read more times than any other is Cicero's essay on old age, the one where Cato explains to the two young men why the four usual accusations against old age don't hold, and I'd like the speech to be built on that skeleton — the four charges, answered one by one through my grandfather's actual life (he still referees papers, he's on his third guide dog after going half-blind, he never wanted the pleasures he's supposedly been deprived of) rather than through quotation or name-dropping.

The tricky bits. The audience is mixed: maybe a dozen former colleagues who have all taught the essay and will notice if I get the structure wrong or put words in Cicero's mouth, and then the family, who mostly want to laugh and cry a bit. And there's one honest problem, which is that Cato leans fairly hard on the immortality of the soul at the very end, when he takes up the fear of death — and my grandfather is a cheerful agnostic who has told me more than once that he expects nothing, and would find a pious ending to his own birthday speech a betrayal. Cicero, as I recall, is careful enough to leave himself an exit there (something about death either being a dreamless sleep, which isn't bad, or a passage to something better) and I want that honest fork, not a hedge. Also — and this is the part I keep failing at — my grandfather is genuinely difficult. Grumpy, competitive at crosswords, holds grudges about a departmental meeting in 1979. Cicero is big on old age only deserving respect when the character deserves it, and faults getting worse with age unless you fight them, so the speech can't sand him off into a saint either. It has to make the case for him while admitting the case against him, if that makes sense. Aim for something I can deliver in about six or seven minutes, written so that the classicists catch the architecture and my mum catches the man.
