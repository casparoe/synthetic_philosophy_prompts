I'm presenting my paper at a departmental workshop in a few weeks and I need to get my Q&A prep in order. Quick setup: the paper defends a take on the demandingness objection to consequentialism that I think is genuinely underexplored, and the room is going to be hostile in a very specific way.

My thesis: the demandingness objection is a real problem, but everyone runs it wrong. The standard move is intuition-mongering -- picture a life with no hobbies, no deep projects, every spare dollar and hour given away, and then ask the consequentialist to bite that bullet. I argue this is weak, because bullet-biting is exactly what good consequentialists are trained to do. Instead I claim demandingness should be run as a structural complaint about agent-neutral optimization: once you grant agent-neutral reasons, the optimizer has to keep consuming the agent's life so long as marginal cost to her is lower than marginal benefit to others, and there is no principled stopping point *inside the theory*. So the complaint isn't that consequentialism demands too much -- it's that consequentialism can't even state what "too much" would be. I call this the no-floor problem. The intuition about the poor utilitarian with no violin lessons is downstream of this, not the objection itself.

The audience: mostly consequentialists, of a pretty hardline stripe. Two of them work on global poverty and think demandingness objections are status quo bias with footnotes -- they'll want to know why capping our obligations isn't just a way of making ourselves feel better about the drowning-child cases. One person there has published on fair-shares/cooperative views of demandingness, where duties get capped at what would be enough if everyone did their part, and she'll almost certainly say the no-floor problem is just her view rediscovered badly. Then there's a couple of Kantians who will be irritated that I'm conceding so much to consequentialism -- for them the real problem is alienation and integrity, not quantity of demand, so they'll probably say my whole framing mistakes which objection it is. And one senior person who works on supererogation and thinks the no-floor problem collapses into the old news that consequentialism has no room for the supererogatory.

So: give me the prep sheet. Maybe eight-ish objections, in rough order of how much damage they'd actually do, each phrased the way that specific person would phrase it (not the generic textbook version), with a reply I can actually deliver in two minutes, and a note on where each reply is soft -- I want to know where I'm exposed, not just be reassured. Flag what I should concede up front versus what I have to hold the line on. And tell me what the question I'm really hoping nobody asks is, and whats the best I can do if it comes anyway. The workshop is 90 minutes total with a long Q&A, so weight things toward what will actually come up in that setting rather than every conceivable objection.
