I've been thinking about betting and rationality on my own for a couple of years, mostly after watching people argue about it online, and I've settled on a position I'd like stress-tested: rational decision-making under risk is fundamentally about the growth rate of your bankroll over a sequence of decisions, not about the expectation of any single outcome. What convinced me is the pattern where betting everything on a favorable bet repeatedly drives you to zero almost surely, even though each individual bet is "rational" by expected value — while betting the fraction that maximizes the expected logarithm of wealth compounds fastest and, in the long run, beats any other fraction with near certainty. It struck me that when a famous crypto executive reportedly endorsed repeatedly taking double-or-nothing bets at slightly better than even odds, on the grounds that the expected value is positive and going broke a few times doesn't matter much, that wasn't just recklessness — it was a conceptual error about what a *sequence* of decisions is. The unit of analysis is the trajectory, not the bet. I've also seen essays arguing that time averages should matter more to a real decision-maker than ensemble averages, and that framing matches how I think about it.

Please take on the role of a decision theorist and give me an honest map of where this "geometric" or growth-rate view of rationality is exposed. Concretely: what arguments, cases, or observations would count against it? I already have a few worries I can't resolve myself. First, that maximizing expected log wealth just *is* expected utility theory with a logarithmic utility function, so my "position" may be a relabeling rather than a rival theory — I'd like you to address whether there is any substantive dispute left, or only a disagreement about which language to use. Second, there seem to be genuinely one-off decisions with no repetition, no rebetting, and maybe even no future in which the outcome compounds, and there it's unclear what a growth rate even means. Third, I've gathered that someone with outside income, or the ability to raise fresh capital after a loss, arguably should bet above the optimal-for-isolated-wealth fraction — which would make the rule depend on contingent facts about the agent's circumstances rather than on rationality as such, and I don't know whether that's a bug or just a boundary condition.

Think carefully before answering, and begin by spelling out the position's commitments as precisely as you can — I suspect my own formulation equivocates somewhere between a claim about what agents should do and a claim about what utility they should be assumed to have. Then rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which of them a committed defender of the view would concede versus dismiss as a misstatement of it, and be willing to conclude that the view is well insulated and to say whether that insulation is a problem for a view of this kind. I'd personally find a decisive case involving an agent who is explicitly indifferent between different lifetime wealth trajectories the most persuasive kind of objection, if one exists, so give that special attention.
