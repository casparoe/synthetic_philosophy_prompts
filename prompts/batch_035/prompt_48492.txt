I've been around the rationalist blogosphere since the original dust-specks arguments, and I've mostly stayed loyal to the "shut up and multiply" side: when the stakes are large enough, expected value should be allowed to dominate, and the feeling that a conclusion is absurd is not evidence. But a housemate of mine, who has read everything ever written about moral uncertainty, has walked me into an argument about insect and invertebrate sentience whose conclusion I can neither accept nor refute, and I want a real diagnosis rather than the usual mugging-adjacent hand-waving. Here it is, numbered roughly as she presented it.

(1) For any animal with a nervous system, our evidence cannot put the probability of sentience at exactly zero, and every proposed principled cutoff fails somewhere we already know about: the deuterostome/protostome divide, neuron-count thresholds, the presence of nociceptors, centralized versus distributed brains.

(2) Under total consequentialism with linear aggregation, the expected moral weight of an action's effects on a population of animals equals the probability of sentience, times the number of individuals, times the moral weight of one sentient individual.

(3) As you move down the ladder from cows to chickens to fish to insects to nematodes, total individual counts rise by many orders of magnitude while any reasonable credence in sentience falls by far fewer orders of magnitude. Farmed land vertebrates number in the tens of billions, fish caught or killed annually for consumption in the low trillions, and soil nematodes alone outnumber farmed vertebrates by many orders of magnitude more. So even if my credence that a single nematode is sentient is five orders of magnitude lower than my credence for a chicken, the nematodes dominate the sum.

(4) The same domination applies to the effects of ordinary actions: walking across a lawn, mowing one, driving, spraying, wading in a pond. The number of potentially affected beings with nonzero sentience probability explodes as bodies get smaller, and by (3) their combined expectation swamps the visible animals.

(5) So if expected moral value is what we should be maximizing, the dominant moral consideration in nearly every decision I make -- including where to walk and whether to keep the mosquito out of the bedroom -- is the fate of creatures whose sentience I'd price at something like one in a hundred thousand. Not one consideration among many; the dominant one.

(6) Therefore either total consequentialism with linear aggregation is false, or expected-value pooling is the wrong way to aggregate over moral uncertainty, or I am morally required to reorganize my life around mites and nematodes.

To be clear, she accepts (6): she thinks we should in fact reorganize, at minimum our charitable giving. I don't want to accept that, but I also don't want to respond the way people did to Pascal's mugging, by discounting small probabilities on vibes while calling it decision theory -- if I do that, I want to know exactly what I'm paying for the discount and to whom it gets paid. One thought I haven't been able to make precise: my credence in total consequentialism itself is nowhere near one, so maybe linearly pooling expected value across a moral hypothesis I only partly believe is doing illicit work -- but every way I try to formalize that complaint comes out sounding like motivated arithmetic.

Please go through the argument step by step. Tell me which premise or inference to reject, why that premise looked fine when she stated it, and what rejecting it costs -- including whether the cost is giving up something I want to keep elsewhere, such as "shut up and multiply" in cases where the stakes are visible and countable. If you think the argument is basically sound and the conclusion is simply true, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a flaw. And tell me what would change your verdict: which empirical finding, which formal result about aggregation, or which moral consideration would flip you to a different diagnosis, and where your own reasoning here is most likely to go wrong.
