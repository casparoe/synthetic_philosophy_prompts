For about a year now, my office mate — a graduate student working on contractualist ethics — and I have been arguing about whether it makes sense to add up costs and benefits across different people. I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy training, and I hold the utilitarian end of the argument: if I can either prevent one stranger's death or prevent five strangers from each losing the use of a leg, with the same effort either way, it seems obvious that five serious losses outweigh one greater loss only after comparing them, and that this comparison somehow involves counting people. She presses what she calls the separateness of persons objection: there is no single life that contains those five leg injuries, so the comparison is never a comparison within a life, and summing across lives is, in her words, like transferring funds between bank accounts that were never opened. My stock reply is that if sums across persons were always illegitimate, elections would be irrational, since counting heads is all a vote is. Her stock reply is that voting is a convention for making a collective decision, not a measurement of how much good one outcome contains. We have finally conceded that we are going in circles, and we've agreed to write the dispute up as an adversarial-collaboration joint statement. Because she claims I'm the only one of us forced to write specs other people can implement, the drafting job fell to me.

Draft that document for us. It should open with a numbered section of claims both of us could sign our names to — about the cases we've been fighting over, about what each side takes itself to be denying, about what the dispute is not about (it is not about selfishness versus altruism, and we both accept that persons are distinct and that the interests of the worse-off count). Then a disagreements table with a row per issue, each phrased twice, once so that she would accept the wording of her view and once so that I would accept the wording of mine. The cases we keep circling back to, which you should treat as the shared evidence base: (a) the equal-claims rescue, where I can help either one person or five, all strangers, all needing the same indivisible help, each of whom would receive the same benefit; (b) the severity trade, where a large harm to one is weighed against many tiny harms to others, and where she insists that no number of headaches, however large, adds up to a death, while I insist that at some scale the arithmetic has to bite; and (c) the spread-risk case, where a policy imposes a small probability of death on each of a very large number of people rather than a certain smaller harm on one person, and we disagree about whether the expected-value calculation is doing moral work or merely pretending to. For each crux you identify — we suspect, but are not sure, that one is whether "worse than" comparisons only make sense within a single life, and another is whether the personal standpoint (what may I do?) comes apart from the legislative standpoint (what should our policy be?) — record a case or argument both of us would agree bears on it, along with each side's prediction about what it would show, written down before the case is examined.

Two closing requirements. First, we want your honest judgment about which of our disagreements are substantive and which are verbal — she has begun to suspect that part of what we call "aggregation" is a disagreement about the word "good" rather than about the world, and I want to know if she's right. Second, the agreed-claims section must not be vacuous: if the only claims we can jointly sign are platitudes, say so plainly, because in that case the real deliverable is an exact statement of why a utilitarian and a contractualist cannot share a spec sheet at all, and we would rather learn that than paper over it. Keep the document formal; we intend to circulate it to her seminar.
