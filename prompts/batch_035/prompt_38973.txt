I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm at work on a long essay about whether humanity's choices this century should be dominated by the possibility of astronomical future suffering — what researchers in that corner of the field call s-risks, meaning scenarios where things go so badly wrong that suffering occurs on a cosmic scale, possibly involving vast numbers of artificial minds. Before I commit to a thesis in print, I want to know whether my own convictions survive contact with each other. Here is where I actually stand:

(1) Suffering has a moral priority that happiness does not mirror. Preventing one case of severe torment matters more, to my mind, than bringing into existence several additional happy lives. I would not make that trade, and I don't think I'm confused about it.

(2) Consciousness does not depend on the substrate. A sufficiently sophisticated artificial system — or an uploaded copy of a brain — could suffer for real, not metaphorically, and it would count morally for exactly as much as a biological being in the same state.

(3) Human extinction would be the worst single outcome for our species. I have written before that even a grim future is preferable to no future at all, and I have refused to romanticize extinction as any kind of merciful release.

(4) Because the scale of potential future suffering is astronomical, even low-probability s-risks should dominate our moral attention — ahead of, for instance, the suffering of animals in industrial farming today, which is vast but bounded.

(5) At the same time, I have long been suspicious of arguments of the form "the stakes are astronomical and the probability is small, therefore act as if it were certain" — I have publicly mocked Pascal's-wager-style reasoning when it appears in debates about asteroids or climate engineering, and I do not want to exempt my own favorite cause from that discipline.

I can see that (4) and (5) rub against each other, but I no longer trust myself to see the rest of the picture clearly. Please audit the set as a whole rather than lecture me on the field. Structure your reply in sections, in this order and at roughly these lengths:

- "Playing my position back" (~150 words): restate my five commitments as you understand them, so I can check you have me right before you judge me.
- "The genuine tensions" (~450 words): the pairs that really pull against each other, ranked from most to least severe, with what each one would have to give.
- "The tension that isn't" (~150 words): whichever pair looks like a problem but dissolves on inspection, and exactly why it dissolves.
- "The cheapest revision" (~200 words): the single change that would clean up the most, and what it would cost me given what I've already published.
- "What this means for the essay" (~150 words): given all of the above, the line I can honestly take without hedging it into meaninglessness.
