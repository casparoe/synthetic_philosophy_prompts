I serve on the safety committee of a regional hospital network. Two years ago we approved an AI system that helps clinicians prioritize emergency-department patients. Last month it systematically deprioritized a class of patients whose presentations it had effectively never seen in its training data, and one of them died after waiting too long. Our legal team says no one is liable: the vendor can show the system met its certified accuracy targets, the clinicians reasonably deferred to it as our policy required, and our committee relied on the vendor's validation studies, which had used a patient mix that made this failure mode nearly impossible to anticipate. My colleagues are relieved, but I'm uneasy. When every person in the causal chain behaved reasonably given what they knew and could have known, and the patient is still dead, is it right to conclude that no one is at fault — that what happened was just bad luck, like a lightning strike? Or does the harm reveal something wrong with how we carve up responsibility in the first place? And if it does, what follows for us concretely: should the committee accept some form of responsibility even though none of us individually did anything careless, and what would that acceptance actually amount to?
