Ok, here's my situation. I'm 71, retired entomologist — spent most of my career on how bark beetles smell their way to stressed trees, which is alot more interesting than it sounds. Since retiring I've gotten heavily into political philosophy, mostly through my daughter who did her degree in it, and that plus being the panel's "numbers guy" somehow got me onto the advisory body for our mid-sized city's guaranteed-income pilot (nine of us, we meet monthly, real but modest money — enough for a few hundred households, not a revolution).

The panel deadlocks on the same three or four fights every meeting, and I've started to suspect they're philosophy fights wearing a policy costume. One member says anything with a household income cap "isn't basic income, it's just welfare with extra steps" and should be rejected on principle. I'll confess the annual-lump-sum payment idea is mine — people can plan around a big payment better than twelve small ones, or so I argued — but I'm no longer sure I believe it. Another member wants payments to taper off sharply as people earn more and calls that "encouraging work," while a third says it's really a hidden eighty percent tax on the poor and I suspect they're both half right. And a fourth keeps insisting that when you aim a program narrowly at the poor, the program tends to become politically poor itself, so the poor can end up with less than under a broader scheme — half the panel thinks she made that up, and nobody can settle it. Above all that, we've never actually agreed whether our goal is relieving poverty or something bigger about people being free not to have to ask permission.

So what I want from you is a decision procedure the panel can run on any proposal that reaches us: the questions to ask, in order, that a proposal has to survive. For each question, indicate what kind of proposal dies there, and at least one of the five should be explicitly flagged as a values question that no amount of my number-crunching can settle — because if everything looks technical we'll keep pretending it is. Don't sand anything down to keep the peace, including on my lump-sum idea; and don't bother with citations or who-said-it-first, I don't care, I only care whether it holds up.

Format: exactly five numbered items, one per question, each a sentence or two, no introduction and nothing after item five. I get maybe ten minutes of table time per meeting and I want to print this on one page and slide it across.
