I've been reading LessWrong and the EA Forum for something like a decade now, and I've followed the longtermism debates from the sidelines: the "shut up and multiply" arguments, the pushback about tiny probabilities of astronomically large payoffs, Pascal's mugging, bounded versus unbounded utility, and the recurring worry people call fanaticism — the view that essentially all expected value lives in the far future, so near-term causes are noise by comparison. I have my own half-formed opinions, but recently something happened in a comment thread that I can't get out of my head, and I'd like to see it worked through properly instead of traded as one-liners.

The claim was this: if you take Bostrom's simulation argument seriously, you should *dampen* your fanaticism about the far future. The rough idea, as best I can reconstruct it, has two prongs. First, if we are in an ancestor simulation, then a technologically mature civilization already exists and has (from its own standpoint) already determined or recorded how our history plays out, including whatever we do or fail to do about existential risk — so the open-ended astronomical future that motivates the fanatic is, on that branch, not open at all; it's a completed artifact viewed from outside, and whatever value our actions produce inside the simulation is dwarfed by the value in the simulators' world that we have no leverage over. Second, even on the branch where we're not simulated, the simulation argument's own premise — that mature civilizations would run enormous numbers of ancestor simulations containing moral patients — suggests that simulated minds overwhelmingly outnumber unsimulated ones across the universe, which changes the population ethics of "shaping the far future" in ways the fanatic's expected value calculations never bothered to model. The commenter concluded that the fanatic's EV arithmetic double-counts the value of our light cone.

The fanatics in the thread were not persuaded, and honestly some of their replies seemed strong: that within the simulation branch our actions still affect astronomically many simulated lives, so x-risk work still dominates; that if we're *not* simulated the ordinary case stands, so at most this is a reweighting across branches, not an abandonment of the logic; and that a world where mature civilizations run countless value-rich simulations is a world with *more* total value at stake, not less. But the dampeners had replies to those too, and the thread degraded before anything got resolved.

So: write me a dialogue between two characters who each deserve to win. One is a fanatical longtermist — not a strawman, but the sophisticated version: she knows the Pascal's mugging literature, has settled views on bounded utility and on which decision theory to use, and concedes exactly as much as a careful EV maximizer should. The other takes the simulation argument seriously and thinks doing so genuinely dampens fanaticism — also not a strawman: he can distinguish the trilemma's disjuncts, he knows the difference between his argument being about causal leverage versus about population ethics, and he doesn't overreach. I'd like the dialogue to work through at least the following, with each side responding to the other's actual strongest points rather than talking past each other:

1. Whether the "completed artifact from the simulators' standpoint" prong is doing any real work — whether a future being determined-or-recorded from outside is decision-theoretically relevant to agents inside the simulation, or whether that's a confusion between indexical and objective facts that the fanatic is entitled to dismiss.

2. Whether the population-ethics prong cuts the way the dampener wants — including whether the existence of many simulated minds reduces our expected leverage on total value, increases it, or is orthogonal, and whether the fanatical longtermist can simply absorb it.

3. A point nobody in the thread made but that I suspect matters: whether the dampening argument is really about simulations at all, or about something more general — that our causal leverage might be screened off by value we can't affect — in which case the simulation argument is a vivid illustration rather than the load-bearing premise. If that's right, the two characters should notice it.

4. Whether the credence to assign to "we are in an ancestor simulation" behaves like ordinary empirical uncertainty for the purposes of this decision, and whether the dampener's argument goes through under both self-sampling and self-indication style reasoning or only under one of them — and, if only one, what that says about how much weight the argument can bear.

Please give both characters at least one point on which they genuinely concede something to the other, and end the dialogue with them locating the actual crux between them, even if they don't resolve it. I'm less interested in a verdict than in seeing where the arguments really bind and where they're rhetorical.
