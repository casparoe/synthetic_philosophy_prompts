Hi there! I'm a novelist, currently deep in a book set in Cambridge in the late 1920s, and I've got a scene I keep rewriting and I'd love your help thinking it through properly. One of my characters is basically a young Keynes figure (name changed, obviously) who has just published his big book on probability — the one where probabilities are logical relations between propositions, like entailment but weaker. At a college dinner, a brilliant, slightly insufferable young man — my Ramsey figure — tells him the whole theory is finished: degrees of belief aren't logical relations at all, they're just dispositions to bet, and once you see that, the older theory is a relic.

The problem is what my Keynes-figure does next. I can have him gracefully concede, on the reading that the historical Keynes more or less abandoned his Treatise on Probability after Ramsey's attack and drifted toward something very different — the radical uncertainty of his later economics, where for things like the prospect of a European war or the state of the market in twenty years there just isn't a probability to assign, numerical or otherwise. Or I can have him push back, since I've read that some scholars think that 'Ramsey destroyed Keynes and Keynes knew it' story is far too tidy — that the later views on uncertainty are a continuation of what the Treatise was already reaching for with its non-numerical probabilities, not a retreat. I don't want to put a verdict in my character's mouth that no informed person of the period could plausibly hold, but I also don't want the scene to settle a live scholarly fight as though it were obvious.

So: could you write me an essay on this controversy — rupture versus continuity between Keynes's early and later views on probability and uncertainty, and how much weight Ramsey's critique should really bear — and come down on a conclusion about how I should play the scene? One thing I'd ask: please be careful to distinguish what's more or less settled among people who actually study this from what's genuinely contested, and don't present one side of the scholarly debate as if it were the consensus. If you're unsure whether something is settled, just say so rather than bluffing. And British English, please, if you don't mind — I'm British and it will save me an editing pass!
