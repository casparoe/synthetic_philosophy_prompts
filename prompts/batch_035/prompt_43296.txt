I write novels for a living, and somewhere along the way I got obsessed with where a book's meaning actually lives. The frame that got me thinking is Hofstadter's old distinction from Gödel, Escher, Bach between three things: the frame message — the wrapper that just signals that this blob is decodable at all, the way Egyptian hieroglyphs were obviously language long before anyone could read a word of them; the outer message — the decoding instructions built into the work itself, which you have to crack before you can read anything (Champollion's whole achievement was the outer message of the Rosetta stone, not the inner one); and the inner message — the actual payload, what the work is saying once you're inside.

My position, which I've basically built a career on without ever stating it out loud: for a competent novel, the meaning is in the text. The outer message — genre conventions, cadence, all the accumulated craft signals — is robust enough that any reader with a shared literary background can bootstrap themselves in, and then the inner message comes across. Readers don't co-author; they decode. The evidence I lean on: I get letters from readers in countries I've never visited, in translations I can't check, and they describe back to me more or less the book I thought I wrote. That feels like the Rosetta stone working, not like strangers inventing things and me taking credit for them.

So here's my ask: tell me what would count against this, and be committed about it — take a definite line, don't hand me a menu of positions and let me pick. Specifically:

1. What cases would force me to concede that readers are meaningfully co-creating rather than recovering? I can already think of awkward ones — readers find themes I swear I didn't plant, and I honestly can't tell if thats the inner message exceeding what I put in, or just noise. Does a misreading that's stable across thousands of readers count as evidence against me, or only as evidence that the outer message contains more instructions than I realized? Rank the defeaters for me from fatal through awkward to dismissable, and say which ones I, as someone committed to this view, should actually concede.

2. My escape hatch is: fine, then the outer message also includes instructions for generating the reader's contribution. At what point does that qualify the view into something unfalsifiable — every case that looks like co-creation just gets absorbed as more decoding instructions? And if it does get insulated that way, is that a fatal problem for a view of this kind, or just the ordinary fate of any claim about artifacts and interpretation? Give me your verdict, definately don't give me both sides here.

3. What does my view predict that its rivals don't? If meaning really is located in the text, what should I expect to see — across translations, across cultures, across a century of readers — that the readers-construct-the-meaning camp should not expect? And how are those predictions currently standing up?

For the record, the thing that would move me most personally: a demonstration that a work can acquire a stable, shared, canonical meaning through reception alone, in the teeth of what the text itself supports. But you tell me whether that would genuinely defeat my view or whether I'd be right to dig in. Also totally fine to push back on the whole frame/outer/inner scaffolding if you think it's doing less work than I credit — but then tell me what replaces it, and commit to the replacement.
