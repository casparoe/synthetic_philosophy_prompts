My mom was diagnosed with early-stage Alzheimer's back in March, and we just had one of those awful family meetings about the paperwork — power of attorney, living will, the whole thing. She's still sharp enough to be making these calls for real, and she wants her wishes in writing now while she can. I assumed this was straightforward legal stuff, but it's opened a genuine philosophical can of worms for me: if the person the directive eventually applies to is a diminished, in some ways different self, why should the earlier self get to decide for the later one? I've been testing my gut on a bunch of cases, and I'd like to know whether there's a principle underneath my verdicts, because right now they look inconsistent and my brother and I got nowhere arguing about it.

Here's where I land. My grandmother (mom's mother) signed a directive while lucid refusing a feeding tube in late-stage dementia, and when she reached that stage I felt strongly that the directive should settle it — I still think that, even though by then she wasn't visibly miserable and the doctors said a tube would probably have kept her comfortable another year or so. A friend's father refused antibiotics by directive and, now deep in dementia, begs anyone who'll listen not to be allowed to die — and I still think the directive wins, though it makes me sick to say so. But then there's my uncle: as an able-bodied 25-year-old he wrote something like "if I'm ever paralyzed, don't keep me around," and now, forty years on, he's a paraplegic who says his life is genuinely good — and I definately don't think that young man gets to veto anything. Same form of document, opposite verdict, and I can't for the life of me say why. Also: I think my mom saying "don't ever keep me alive like that" over the dinner table for years would bind about as hard as anything signed, but I don't think a directive someone scribbles on a napkin in a morbid moment at 25 should count — which suggests the formality of it matters less than something else, but I can't tell what.

My best attempt at a principle so far is roughly: the earlier self has authority when the later self can no longer have wishes of her own that are worth counting — so the demented self's pleadings don't count, but the paraplegic's clearly do. But that feels cruel, and it seems to depend on a claim about personal identity (that the demented later self is somehow less of a continuation of the original person) that I'm not sure I actually believe. So, is there a principle, or a small set of candidates, that captures my verdicts — and where it can't, which verdicts would you overturn and why? And what does the resulting principle predict for the case we're actually facing: my mom, still lucid, insisting on a directive that will bind the confused, possibly frightened person she's going to become — someone who, like my friend's father, may well say she wants to live? Should I feel as ok about supporting that as I currently do?
