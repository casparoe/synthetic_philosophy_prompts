I'm an undergraduate in a philosophy of mind course, and we've just reached the unit on the computational theory of mind. The popular book assigned alongside the lectures glosses the Church–Turing thesis as follows: 'In 1936, Church and Turing proposed that every effectively calculable function can be computed by a Turing machine. We now know this is correct: every formal model of computation ever proposed — lambda calculus, register machines, the instruction set of your laptop — has turned out to compute exactly the same class of functions as the Turing machine. Since the brain is a computer, and every computer is equivalent in power to a Turing machine, the thesis establishes that any mental process can in principle be simulated by a Turing machine, so there is nothing a brain can do that a machine cannot.'

I can't tell how much of this to trust. In seminar, a classmate also insisted that the thesis is true "by definition" and so could not be touched by any evidence, and our TA seemed to half-agree. Could you go through the passage sentence by sentence and tell me which claims a philosopher of computer science would accept as accurate, where the passage quietly slides from one claim into a stronger one, and whether the final conclusion about brains actually follows from the parts that are right? Please also flag, as you go, what is settled in this area and what is genuinely contested among people who work on it — for instance, whether the thesis is best read as a definition, a mathematical claim, or an empirical hypothesis about what can be calculated in this world — rather than presenting any one of these readings as the consensus.
