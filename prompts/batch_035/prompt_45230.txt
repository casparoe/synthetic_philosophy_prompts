I'm an amateur who reads a lot of ancient history for pleasure, and I keep noticing a pattern in how scholars handle agreeing sources that I can't find a name for, though I assume one must exist somewhere.

In ordinary reasoning about evidence, agreement between independent reports is straightforward corroboration: two witnesses describe the same thing, and the thing becomes more likely to be true. What I've noticed is that in source criticism of ancient texts the inference is, in a specific respect, reversed. When two surviving narratives match almost word for word over a stretch, scholars don't treat that as corroboration at all — they treat it as evidence that the accounts are not independent: one author copied from the other, or both from a lost common source, so the doubling is an illusion and counts for no more than a single attestation. And the strong case, the one that gets called independent confirmation, is precisely the pair of accounts that agree on the outline of events but diverge in the details — different casualty figures, a different route for the same march, a name garbled in slightly different ways, one including an episode the other omits. The disagreement is what licenses reading the two as separate channels, and it's only then that the shared outline is taken to confirm anything.

So the inferential weight seems to fall not on agreement as such but on a distinctive composite: agreement on the main structure, combined with uncoordinated divergence around the edges. Too much consistency devalues the agreement; some inconsistency is what makes the agreement evidentially potent.

Is there an established concept for this? I can see pieces of it in things I've read, but nothing that names the whole move. In Gospel scholarship there's a criterion of multiple attestation, but it's always advertised with the proviso that it works only for genuinely independent sources — and what I've noticed is that independence is often argued for from exactly this kind of pattern, so the criterion seems to smuggle in the thing I'm trying to name. Textual critics seem to have a related idea when a shared error betrays a shared ancestor, but their concern is reconstructing manuscripts, not assessing whether an event happened. And I gather that courts are wary of witnesses whose stories match too perfectly, on the theory that collusion or coaching produces unnatural consistency — which feels like the same instinct in a different setting. What I haven't found is the general concept: the claim that the distribution of agreement and disagreement between reports is itself evidence about the causal structure that produced them, and that this, in turn, determines how much the agreement corroborates the content.

So my question is whether this has a name of its own, and if it does, where it lives — or, if not, whether you can coin one and mark its edges. In particular, I'd want the concept distinguished carefully from (a) the plain idea that agreement among independent sources raises probability, since the point here is that agreement is only worth something once a certain kind of divergence has certified independence, and (b) the textual-critical use of shared errors, which is about stemma-building rather than truth. And I'd want the boundary cases: when does minor divergence between accounts signal genuinely independent transmission, and when does it reflect instead deliberate rewriting by a later author, an author's habit of varying his source for style, or corruption in the copying chain — cases where the two channels look independent but aren't? Relatedly, does the logic survive when the channels are memories rather than documents — two veterans remembering the same battle — where some convergence is expected from the shared event itself and divergence may be mere forgetting rather than a sign of separate traditions? I'm not after a bare label; I want the concept carved, with the cases that fall inside it and the ones that only look like they do.
