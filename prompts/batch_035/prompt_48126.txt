This one's been keeping me up at night. I'm not a philosophy person, I drive a delivery van, but a while back I read about a weird condition and now I can't stop chewing on it.

The condition: some people, because of certain brain damage, still feel pain in every sense that matters for noticing it — they can tell you where it is, how strong it is, whether it's sharp or dull or burning -- but it doesn't bother them at all. They don't flinch, they don't want it gone, some of them even seem amused while a doctor is deliberately hurting them. I believe it's called pain asymbolia. Here's the argument I built out of it, and I think it proves way too much:

(1) Those people are in genuine pain in the full sensory sense of the word.
(2) If pain is bad in itself -- bad just by being felt, not because of anything it drags along with it -- then their pain is bad for them.
(3) But by thier own account nothing is going wrong for them: no fear, no misery, no wishing it would stop. Sure, the body might be getting damaged, but set that aside, I'm only asking about the feeling itself.
(4) So pain is not bad in itself. The badness must live in the hating-it part, the suffering, not in the raw feel.
(5) And the same has to go for the rest of us: whenever pain is bad for us, it's only bad because we mind it. The feeling itself is neutral, like a fire alarm -- it only matters as a signal.
(6) So a pill that keeps the feeling but switches off the minding would cost us nothing.
(7) Going further: if every future person were wired that way from birth, pain would basically be retired as a bad thing, and nothing of value would be lost.
(8) And by the same logic, hunger, nausea, even grief -- none of it is bad in itself either, only the disliking is.

I can actually live with (6). It's (7) I can't stomach -- the idea that a world absolutely soaked in intense pain that nobody minds is no worse, pain-wise, than a world with none of it. And (8) feels like it proves too much, with grief especially.

So tell me: which step is the weak one, and what does rejecting it cost me? I'm half suspicious the trick is buried in (1) -- maybe what they feel just isn't the same thing we feel, and the word pain is quietly doing two different jobs. If that's it, say so, but then explain how anyone could ever tell the difference. And if you think the right move is rejecting (3) instead, be honest about the price: that would mean something can be bad for you while leaving no trace of badness from the inside, which also sounds pretty strange. I'd rather keep the idea that pain itself is bad, but I want to know what I'm paying either way.
