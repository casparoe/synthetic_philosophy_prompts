Like many people who've spent too long on rationalist forums, I treat my own case verdicts as data, and the dataset I've been assembling is about when a consideration counts as a reason for an attitude — for believing, wanting, admiring — as opposed to a reason to pretend, or a bribe aimed at the attitude. The old blog maxim that beliefs should pay rent in anticipated experience was my first stab at a principle, but my verdicts won't all fit under it. Here they are. A bookmaker offers me a thousand dollars to believe that the number of stars in the Milky Way is even; I have no evidence either way, and the payout depends only on my believing. Verdict: that is not a reason for the belief — at most a reason to want to have it, nothing that could make the belief warranted. Before a difficult performance, if I believe I will play well, I relax, and I play well: the belief helps make itself true, and here the benefit does seem to count in favor of the belief itself, though I can't say how this differs in kind from the bookmaker except through the causal route. My mentor is dying, and it would ease his last weeks if I believed his life's work had mattered; the evidence is thin, and my belief changes nothing about whether it did. Verdict: no reason for the belief — I can tell him it mattered, and act as if it did, but the benefit to him is the wrong kind of consideration to make my believing rational. My students' scores are poor, and if I believe they are lazy I will grade harshly, producing the low scores that confirm the laziness; I think I have a reason not to form that belief, one that feels moral, or maybe prudential about the feedback loop, but in any case not evidence about their character. My friend's paintings do nothing for me, but if I admired them he would keep painting; I have a reason to praise them, and no reason at all to admire them — wanting to have an attitude is not a reason to have it. And the one I keep going back and forth on: I suspect my boss dislikes me, my believing it makes me defensive around him, and my defensiveness makes him dislike me, so the belief, once adopted, manufactures its own confirming evidence; I lean toward saying the self-fulfilling loop is itself a reason against the belief, but I can't explain why the loop helps in the performance case and hurts here. I have a tentative principle: a consideration counts in favor of an attitude only if it bears on whether the attitude fits its object — for belief, on whether the belief is true, through some route other than the belief's own effects; for admiration, on whether the object did anything admirable. Test it against my verdicts, repair it or replace it, and then commit: state the final principle in a sentence or two, tell me plainly which of my verdicts it overturns and why that one rather than the principle, and say what it predicts about a case I haven't listed — for instance, whether a parent has a reason to believe in a child's talent when the child's developing that talent depends on the parent's visible confidence. Don't leave me with a menu of candidate principles; pick the one you would defend and defend it.
