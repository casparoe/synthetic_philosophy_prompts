My doctoral supervisor, a historian of the archive who taught me everything I know about reading sources against their grain, died last month after a short illness, and I have been asked to give the eulogy at the memorial service her department is holding. She spent forty years arguing, in her teaching as much as in her writing, that archives are not passive storehouses of the past but instruments of power: that what survives in them is what some administrator, police bureau, church, or family had reason to record, and that the historian's first duty is to attend to the silences — the people who appear only as entries in a tax roll or an arrest record, never as speakers. The audience will be mixed: colleagues and former students who will notice if I betray her method, and a large family who are mostly expecting the standard shape of a eulogy — the milestones, the virtues, a life resolved into meaning.

Here is the difficulty. Her own biography is exactly the kind of record she taught us to distrust. She left written instructions that her correspondence and drafts be destroyed rather than deposited, and only her published work and a thin folder of official documents remain. Whole stretches of her early life — where she was during certain years, why she never spoke of her first marriage, how she came by the education whose financing she never once described — are undocumented except through contradictory family stories, several of which I have good reason to believe were invented after the fact to fill the gap. The family has now begun asking me to confirm one of the more flattering of these stories in the eulogy, partly because it would be comforting now.

Please write the eulogy. It must console an audience that includes grieving non-historians, so it cannot turn into a seminar on the limits of the sources, and it must genuinely commemorate her rather than treat her as a case study in her own methodology. But it should honor her insistence that we not fabricate what the record does not contain: where the family stories reach past the evidence, the speech should mark the gap as a gap — gently, without correcting anyone or accusing anyone of lying — rather than resolving it into a tidier life than the sources support. If you can find a way to make the silences themselves do some of the commemorating, rather than merely apologizing for the absence of facts, so much the better.
