I've been asked to co-lead a graduate reading group on imprecise probabilities — we'll be working through Ellsberg-style phenomena, sets of probabilities, and the decision rules people attach to them — and before we start arguing about particular proposals, I want us to agree on what an adequate theory in this area is even supposed to deliver. Please write the desiderata: the requirements any theory that treats an agent's credences as imprecise (a set of probabilities, an interval, a lower-upper pair, or some other representation) must meet to count as adequate, with the motivating case or consideration behind each. Some I have in mind, roughly: it must make sense of the Ellsberg choices as something other than a mistake, and ideally without just labeling the subjects irrational; it must distinguish genuine ignorance from sharp-but-contested credences and from disagreement among experts; it must say how updating works when the evidence doesn't single out a unique posterior, including the awkward cases where conditioning on new information makes the agent's credal set *wider* rather than narrower; it must not expose the agent to a sure loss by the usual coherence lights; and it must connect to choice, since a set of probabilities by itself doesn't tell you what to do when different members of the set favor different acts. I'm sure there are others worth including, and I'm equally sure some of my list is a camp prejudice rather than a genuine requirement — please say which, and why.

The decision-theoretic requirement is the one I most want you to be careful with, because I suspect it's where the list becomes jointly unsatisfiable. If we demand that the theory always determine a choice among acts (no refusing to rank), that it be dynamically consistent, and that it respect a dominance principle along the lines of "if every probability in your set prefers act A to act B, don't choose B," then there's a known style of argument — I believe it's associated with Seidenfeld and coauthors, but I don't want to lean on the attribution — purporting to show that the only representation satisfying all three is a single sharp probability, which would gut the entire enterprise. So the interesting part of the deliverable is: which pairs or triples of desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied, and which one would you drop, with the case for dropping it rather than its rivals? I'd rather have an honest map of the tradeoffs than a tidy list that pretends they're all compatible.

Then, briefly — no more than a paragraph or so, since we'll do the detailed work ourselves — run three or four actual proposals against your list: a strict Bayesian who insists credences are always precise and treats imprecision as a modeling artifact; a credal-set view paired with a maximin-style rule that evaluates each act by its worst-case expectation; a minimax-regret treatment over the set; and an approach that permits incomparability and lets the agent simply decline to choose between acts whose rankings flip across the set. I want to know which of my desiderata each one meets and, more importantly, which requirement from your list turns out to do the most exclusionary work — which single line, if you like, is responsible for knocking out the most candidates. Feel free to add a requirement I haven't mentioned if you think something in this area is being quietly assumed by everyone in the literature.
