My friend and I rewatched WALL-E over the weekend and it turned into a two-hour argument about whether the movie is actually a collapse story. Context: we'd both recently gone down separate rabbit holes -- she'd been reading about the late Bronze Age and how a lot of archaeologists now describe the "systems collapse" around 1200 BC as more of a regional reshuffling where most people just kept living their lives, and I'd been stuck on the Maya, where the classic line is that the civilization "collapsed" even though millions of Maya are obviously still around today and it was mostly the kings and the stone-carving that stopped. So we're both primed to be suspicious of the word collapse. And WALL-E, we realized, is a weird test case: Earth is trashed and abandoned, sure, but humanity doesn't die out -- it leaves, and the Axiom society runs stably for 700 years. Everyone is fed, safe, disease-free, no war. By any demographic or functional measure that's not a failed society, it's a wildly successful one that has simply transformed into something the camera treats as pathetic. Is that a collapse, or is it exactly what the revisionists would predict -- a society that didn't fail so much as change into a form we're aesthetically horrified by?

She's arguing the film is a straight collapse narrative (the humans on the Axiom are what a collapsed people look like -- they've lost the capacity to walk, to build, to even notice anything is wrong, and the ending with the plant in the boot is the recolonization/return arc that every collapse story needs). I'm arguing the film cheats: it gets to have its cake and eat it by depicting a post-catastrophe society that is objectively thriving and then just filming it in a way that makes you recoil, which is basically smuggling an aesthetic judgment in where a conceptual one should be. Also I keep thinking the ending undercuts her reading, because if humanity can just stand up and restart civilization from one seedling, how collapsed could they ever have been?
