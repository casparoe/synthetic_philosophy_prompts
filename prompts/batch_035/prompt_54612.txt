I'm a retired population ecologist (spent most of my career on mark-recapture studies of lake fish and small mammals before getting into philosophy a few years back) and I've talked myself into a corner in an online argument I now can't finish. The context: I suggested on a wild-animal-welfare forum that it's at least an open empirical question whether habitat destruction reduces total suffering, since if most wild animals live lives of net negative welfare, then more habitat just means more aggregate suffering. Someone shut the thread down with "the balance of nature" — as in, ecosystems self-regulate, populations are held in check by their own dynamics, so the picture of an ever-growing pile of misery out there doesn't make sense, and anyway interference always ends up worse than leaving things alone. That was more or less the end of the discussion, and I don't think it deserved to be.

Here's what's bugging me. During my working life "the balance of nature" was already getting a bit embarrassing in the field — we'd moved toward nonequilibrium thinking, density dependence, source-sink dynamics, disturbance regimes — so I know first hand the slogan isn't a single claim. But I can't lay out cleanly what it could mean. Can you break "the balance of nature" into the distinct precise theses it might stand for? I'd expect something like: populations are regulated around an equilibrium by density-dependent factors; communities resist invasion and return after perturbation; suffering can't be systematically negative because everything is counterbalanced; and some almost normative reading where noninterference is always better than interference — plus whatever other readings you can see that I haven't spotted. For each one, tell me whether it's a platitude, a substantive claim that's defensible, or just false, and — this is the part I actually need — whether it supports what my opponent used it for, namely that habitat loss can't be reducing suffering and that leaving nature alone is always the welfare-maximizing move.

I have a suspicion that some readings are true but do no work for his argument, and that the one reading that would support him is false, but I'd like this as a list I can post back to the thread rather than my half-formed gripe. And please flag any readings that cut against ME too — e.g. if fluctuating, non-stationary population dynamics make the "more habitat = more animals = more suffering" arithmetic less simple than I was implying, I want to know that before I double down. I care about getting this right more than about winning the thread.
