I've been a longtime reader of the rationalist blogosphere, where bargaining problems surface constantly — Aumann agreement arguments, fairness heuristics like split-the-difference, endless disputes about how much deference is owed in negotiations between agents with different information — and at some point I worked through the axiomatic bargaining literature as a hobby. What keeps nagging at me is how static the standoff between Nash's bargaining solution and the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution has been for roughly half a century. Everyone agrees on the setup: two agents, a convex feasible set of utility pairs, a disagreement point, and the requirements of Pareto optimality, symmetry, and invariance to positive affine transformations of utility. Nash adds independence of irrelevant alternatives — expanding the feasible set shouldn't change the choice if the chosen agreement was already available — which yields the maximal product of utility gains. Kalai and Smorodinsky replied in the mid-1970s by dropping IIA and imposing individual monotonicity instead: if one agent's opportunities improve across the board while the other's stay fixed, the first agent's share of the gains shouldn't shrink. Their solution sits where the normalized gains from the disagreement point toward each agent's best attainable payoff stand in the same ratio, which makes the utopian point — each agent's maximum feasible utility — normatively load-bearing rather than a mere artifact of normalization. Every axiom on both sides sounds either obviously right or obviously wrong depending on whom you ask, and as far as I can tell the debate hasn't moved in decades.

I don't want another rundown of the axioms; I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. My working hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis: the two camps aren't really disagreeing about which axioms are true but about what a solution concept is for. Nash's IIA looks compelling if you think the solution should pick out something intrinsic to the pair of preference orderings — the agreement rational agents would converge to regardless of the surrounding menu — while Kalai–Smorodinsky's monotonicity looks compelling if you think the solution is an arbitration standard sensitive to what each side forgoes, so that a player's best available option creates a legitimate claim even when that option isn't chosen. In other words, maybe one side is answering "what would rational bargainers agree to?" and the other "what should a fair arbitrator impose?", and the phrase "bargaining solution" is doing double duty while the two groups talk past each other. Is that right? Or is this a genuinely substantive disagreement that could in principle be settled — by a theorem, by experimental evidence about how people actually divide surpluses, by seeing which solution generalizes more gracefully to many agents, nonconvex problems, or applied domains like algorithmic fair division and federated optimization? Or is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed, e.g., that the disagreement point is underdetermined between a description of breakdown and a strategic threat selection? If it is verbal or question-divergent, tell me what reformulation would actually dissolve the standoff rather than merely relabeling it.

Two epistemic requests. First, wherever your diagnosis leans on how a given camp motivates its axioms, flag the places where you're inferring motivation from the formal results rather than reporting stated reasons, since I know the secondary literature flattens these positions. Second, tell me explicitly what would change your diagnosis — which argument, result, or piece of evidence would flip you from "different questions under one name" to "substantive and merely hard," or in the other direction — and identify the step in your own reasoning where you'd bet you're most likely to have gone wrong. Please write your answer in American English.
