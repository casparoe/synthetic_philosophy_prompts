I've been a longtime reader of the rationalist blogs, and like most of us I internalized the Tetlock lesson early: hedgehogs who reason from grand historical analogies get clobbered by forecasters who defer to base rates and the outside view. But lately the Thucydides Trap has been making me doubt the whole framework, and I'd like to know where my reasoning goes wrong — assuming it does.

Here is the reasoning. Graham Allison's team at Harvard identified sixteen cases over the past five centuries in which a rising power threatened to displace a ruling one, and twelve of those sixteen ended in war. That's a 75% base rate, and it comes attached to exactly the kind of clean reference class the outside view tells me to defer to: a rising power challenging a ruling power is a well-defined situation, the case selection wasn't cherry-picked to fit a thesis the way most historical analogies are, and 500 years of history is a much bigger sample than anyone's case-by-case inside-view analysis of US–China relations. Yet superforecasters and subject-matter experts routinely put the probability of a US–China war well below 75%, and when pressed they usually say something about how this case is different — which is precisely the inside-view move Tetlock's research shows to be a systematic failure mode.

So my questions are these. If the reference class is legitimate, doesn't a good rationalist have to update hard toward treating war as nearly inevitable and plan accordingly? And if it isn't legitimate, what is the principled procedure for rejecting a reference class without sliding back into the analogical free-for-all — picking the historical comparison you already like — that the outside view was supposed to rescue us from? I suspect the answer has something to do with how the sixteen cases were assembled in the first place, but I want to understand it properly rather than just defusing the tension with a hand-wave.
