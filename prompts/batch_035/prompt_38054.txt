My roommate and a friend of his got into a long argument after dinner last week and I couldn't settle it. Background: we all know my cousin, who says every night he'll work on his dissertaion chapter "tomorrow morning," and every morning by 9:30 he's on his phone feeling awfull about it. That's what started it.

His friend (psych masters) said the whole thing is just hyperbolic discounting: the value of a reward drops steeply with delay, so whenever the phone and the chapter are both close in time, the small-sooner pleasure of scrolling beats the larger-later value of the chapter; and the preference reverses when both are far away, which is why he sincerely plans at night. On that view, the friend said, there's no extra fact called "weakness of will" at all — just one person's preferences flipping around as time passes, plus feeling bad about it afterward. Calling it a "weak will" is like calling a discount curve a character flaw.

My roommate (philosophy undergrad) said that can't be the whole story, because you can be akratic with no time element at all: someone who, at the very moment of choosing, judges that going for a walk is better on the whole, but stays on the couch anyway. If the discounting story needs a delay between the plan and the act to generate the reversal, it has literally nothing to say about that case — and that case seems like the purest form of acting against your better judgment there is.

The friend answered that the couch case just shows the "judgment" was never his real preference — talk is cheap, and if you look at what he does, couch beats walk, so where's the conflict? The roommate replied that this makes the whole position unfalsifiable: whenever someone seems to act against their better judgment, you just define their true preference as whatever they actually did, so akrasia is ruled out by fiat instead of being discovered. And, he added, people clearly distinguish being *unable* to resist (something compulsion-like) from not bothering to resist, and the discounting story flattens that difference completely.

The friend's last word was that the distinction can be rebuilt inside the framework anyway: compulsion is when the urge wins even at very short delays and high cost, weakness is when it wins at moderate ones — a matter of degree, not of kind. And he said the roommate's "pure case" is never actually observed in the wild: track the couch guy second by second and you'll find he wanted to stay put the entire time; the "better judgment" is a story he tells afterward. The roommate muttered something about how people have been fighting over this since the Greeks — as I remember it, Socrates basically denied anyone knowingly acts against their better judgment, so the friend is in old company — and then they both looked at me and I had nothing.

Can you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right at each point, not just overall? And is there something both of them are missing that would dissolve part of the fight?
