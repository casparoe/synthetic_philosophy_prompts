I've basically bought the inefficacy objection, and I want someone to stress-test it. My version goes like this: my individual emissions almost certainly don't cause any extra harm. The chance that my one flight, or my one burger, is the one that tips some threshold that wouldn't otherwise have been tipped is miniscule, and for a lot of consumption (clothes stitched in a sweatshop, say) there's no threshold at all — nothing for my purchase to be the straw that breaks. So individual consumption choices aren't wrong, and the people who scold me about flying have confused themselves about moral maths. Note I do accept duties to vote, donate and push for policy change — climate change is a massive injustice, it's just not my lunch order's fault. I also think the voting analogy actually supports me: the reason a single vote can matter in a close election is precisely that elections have thresholds, and consumer markets mostly don't.

Tell me what would count against this. Not just the standard 'drops of water' stuff I can recite myself — I want the actual defeater map, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Which assumptions could fail, and what would the world have to look like for each: the threshold structure of consumption effects, the expected-value arithmetic (does 'tiny probability times huge harm' do real work here or is it a trick), the assumption that harm is the only morally relevant currency, the framing that says the only alternatives to 'individually wrong' are 'individually fine'. Where do the defenders of the view themselves concede ground — the people who hold something like my position on individual emissions — and what do they refuse to give up even at the cost of sounding callous? Conversely, which of my commitments should a critic target first, because if it went the rest would follow? And be honest about whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability: if every apparent counterexample gets absorbed by 'that's really a political act' or 'that's supererogatory', is that a problem for a view of this kind, or just what you'd expect from a structurally collective-harm case? The thing that would move me most, I suspect, is something showing my expected-harm reasoning is actually broken, rather than intuition-pumping about complicity — but tell me if you think that's the wrong thing to be moved by.
