I'm a philosophy PhD student writing a chapter on choice without preference — the Buridan's ass problem — and I've come to suspect that the standard examples are quietly rigged in the other direction. In the classic setup the two haystacks are exactly equidistant and otherwise identical, so every rival method of resolving the tie does equally well, and the debate never gets any traction: coin-flippers, arbitrary pickers, and defenders of "deliberation must terminate in a reason" all look fine, and the only live question is whether the donkey starves. What I need for my argument is a case rigged the other way. Please take the role of a decision theorist who specializes in rational choice under indifference; think carefully and do a little private brainstorming about candidate settings before committing to a design, but present only the finished product.

Construct a thought experiment meeting these specifications. First, the agent faces a genuine, robust tie at the object level: no amount of further deliberation, calculation, or consultation could uncover a consideration that favors one option over the other — if your case leaves room for someone to say "just look harder," it fails. Second, the setting is realistic and mundane — a workplace, hospital, or civic context; no supernatural predictors, no perfect demons, no one starving to death. Third — and this is the heart of it — the tie-breaking methods themselves have downstream consequences that differ: randomizing, arbitrarily "just picking one," following a standing policy such as "when tied, take the leftmost," and consulting a second-order preference about how one chooses must not all come out equally rational, because the choice of method is visible to, or shapes the expectations of, other parties in the scenario. Fourth, the case must not smuggle in a hidden object-level difference that secretly breaks the tie in favor of one option; that would be cheating, since my chapter is precisely about what to do when the tie is real. After the scenario itself, which should be no more than one paragraph, demonstrate that the design works: state what each of the four tie-breaking approaches recommends in the case and why they diverge in advisability; identify exactly which structural feature of the scenario is doing the work; and give me the strongest thing a defender of one losing approach — say, the committed randomizer — could say to argue the case is unfair to their view, so I can preempt it in a footnote. Keep the entire response under 600 words; a tight design with a crisp walkthrough is worth far more to me than an exhaustive survey of the literature.
