I am a graduate teaching assistant for an introductory social epistemology course, and I have been given responsibility for grading the midterm. Question 4, reproduced below, is one I wrote myself: the case study is fictional, but I modeled it on something I went through two winters ago, when I spent several months trying to get clinicians to take seriously a cluster of symptoms for which their charts had no settled name, and I know from that experience how much turns on getting these classifications exactly right rather than roughly right. That is why I want this answer graded with precision rather than kindness.

By way of context for the standard: the course has worked through Miranda Fricker's Epistemic Injustice: Power and the Ethics of Knowing (2007) in full, including the chapters on testimonial injustice, hermeneutical injustice, and the virtue of testimonial justice. Students were told they would be credited for correct classifications, for correct justifications, and for recognizing when an epistemic harm falls short of being an epistemic injustice.

**Question 4 (20 points).** Dolores Ibarra has worked seasonal harvests in Kestrel County for eleven years. Beginning in her sixth season, she experiences weeks-long episodes of dizziness, hand numbness, and gaps in short-term memory following each spraying cycle. Four episodes from her history are at issue:

(a) At the clinic, Dr. Whitfield records her account but weights it downward, noting in the chart that "this population tends to inflate symptoms for compensation claims." The note is about seasonal workers as a group; his discounting of her report tracks the note.

(b) On one visit, her account of symptom timing is discounted further because it conflicts with a blood assay that was later found to have been miscalibrated by the laboratory. Whitfield had no reason to distrust the assay at the time, and nothing about his treatment of her on this visit had anything to do with her identity.

(c) During the first three seasons of symptoms, no term for the cluster exists anywhere — not in occupational medicine, not among the growers, not among the workers. Dolores keeps a private notebook tracking the episodes, privately suspects the sprays are responsible, and tries twice to raise the pattern with the clinic; both times the conversation goes nowhere, and she cannot get the pattern treated as a pattern in any forum that counts.

(d) In year ten, the state agricultural extension service, working mainly from growers' insurance data, publishes the term "harvest fog" for the cluster. Some clinics adopt it; Dolores's clinic does not. Her reports continue to be discounted, and the chart note from (a) is still being made.

**Task:** For each of (a)–(d), state whether it constitutes an epistemic injustice in Fricker's sense (specifying testimonial or hermeneutical), an epistemic harm that falls short of injustice, or neither, and justify each classification. For anything that falls short of injustice, state what would have to be different for it to count as an injustice.

---

**Student's answer:**

"(a) This is a clear case of testimonial injustice. On Fricker's account, a testimonial injustice occurs when a hearer deflates a speaker's credibility because of prejudice connected to the speaker's social identity. Whitfield's chart note is precisely an identity prejudice about seasonal workers, and his discounting of Dolores's report tracks it, so she suffers an identity-prejudicial credibility deficit in her capacity as a knower. Fricker's own central example is Tom Robinson in To Kill a Mockingbird, and this case has the same structure. 5/5.

(b) This is also testimonial injustice, though of a systemic kind. Dolores said something true and was disbelieved. It is true that Whitfield was not prejudiced against her on this visit — the discounting came from the miscalibrated assay rather than from his attitudes — but Fricker's point is that testimonial injustice is a matter of credibility deficits, and the assay produced one. An institutional instrument that systematically underdelivers credibility to speakers like Dolores functions as a structure that does the same work prejudice does, so the injustice is present even though the prejudice is absent. The fact that Whitfield had no reason to distrust the assay at the time shows the system, not the individual, is at fault. 4/5.

(c) This is at most borderline hermeneutical injustice, and I would say it is not one. Hermeneutical injustice on Fricker's account is when a gap in the shared conceptual resources leaves someone unable to understand their own experience — her paradigm is Carmita Wood and the period before 'sexual harassment' existed as a concept, when what was happening to women at work could not be made sense of. But Dolores understood her experience perfectly well: she kept a notebook, spotted the pattern, and worked out that the sprays were responsible. What she lacked was a way of getting others to believe her, and that is a testimonial problem, not a hermeneutical one. The years before 'harvest fog' was coined therefore show collective epistemic bad luck — the science simply had not caught up — but not an injustice done to Dolores. A lacuna becomes an injustice only when someone who possesses the concept deliberately withholds it, and nothing like that happened here. 2/5.

(d) Once the extension service published 'harvest fog,' the hermeneutical lacuna closed: the interpretive resources now exist, so any remaining failure is not hermeneutical. The clinic's failure to adopt the term is ordinary institutional sluggishness — regrettable, but hermeneutical injustice requires the resources to be missing, and they no longer are. What does continue is testimonial injustice: the chart note from (a) is still being made, and Dolores's reports are still being deflated on identity-prejudicial grounds. So (d) is testimonial injustice, of the same kind as (a). 4/5.

A note on remedy. Since testimonial injustice consists in a deflated credibility score, Fricker's corrective is for the hearer to recalibrate by deliberately weighting upward the testimony of groups that have historically been disbelieved — a compensatory credibility excess that corrects for past discounting. So in this case the virtue of testimonial justice would require Whitfield to give Dolores's reports more credibility than the evidence before him warrants, in light of the history of disbelief her group has suffered."
