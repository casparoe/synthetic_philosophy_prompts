So I've gotten roped into the grant committee at the small family foundation my aunt runs (long story, don't ask), and this cycle our big fight is between funding cataract surgeries for a couple thousand people versus a deworming program that relieves mild but real misery for something like a million kids. My cousin says this is literally the torture vs. dust specks debate at lower stakes, and honestly he's kind of right, which got me reading old threads on it and realizing that debate has been going in circles for like 20 years now and nobody ever moves. It's the old LessWrong chestnut but underneath that it's just the ancient aggregation question, and everyone's positions are fossilized.

Here's roughly where the sides have dug in, as far as I can tell: the straight aggregators say you just sum, badness is additive across people, and refusing to admit that some huge number of dust specks is worse than one torture is basically a math error dressed up as a moral insight. The anti-aggregators say there's something deranged about trading off any quantity of specks against fifty years of torture, and they offer either thresholds (below some line, harms don't aggregate to something worse than harms above it) or what one guy in our group calls "locality" — the idea that badness lives inside each person's life and doesn't cross persons, so a million instances of one person's itchy foot aren't the kind of thing that can add up to anyone's torture at all. The threshold people can never say where the threshold is or why it's there, which the aggregators treat as a confession that it's ad hoc. And the locality people have to swallow that no number of deaths is worse than one death if the deaths are spread thin enough across persons, which they mostly don't seem to notice or don't care about. Meanwhile the psychology people keep pointing out that humans are scope-insensitive (the famous birds-in-the-oil-slick experiments) so maybe ALL of our intuitions here, on both sides, are just noise from a brain that wasn't built for large numbers, which conveniently lets everyone dismiss whichever intuitions they don't like.

So what I actually want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict on the surgery vs. deworming question and not a survey of the positions. What kind of disagreement IS this? Is it verbal, like the two sides meaning different things by "worse"? Is it that they're secretly answering different questions — maybe one side is describing an axiology (what makes an outcome bad) and the other is insisting on a decision procedure or a set of constraints on what you may do to one person for the sake of others? Is there a hidden formal question doing the work, like whether value is separable across persons, such that if you granted separability the aggregation would follow and if you denied it you'd get locality? Or is it bedrock intuitions all the way down, where nobody can argue for their starting point and the "argument" is just intuition ping-pong? My own half-baked hypothesis, feel free to shoot it down, is that the thought experiment is ill-posed in a specific way: the numbers involved (the 3^^^3 versions especially) are so far outside the regime our moral concepts were built for that the intuitions being polled are basically undefined inputs, and the sides disagree about how to extrapolate, which is a disagreement about extrapolation rules rather than about harm at all. But I'm not sure that doesn't prove too much, since it'd also cover the million-vs-thousands version we're actually facing at the foundation, where the numbers are ordinary and the fight persists anyway.

Whatever diagnosis you land on, tell me what would even count as progress. A distinction, a formal result, a better-designed thought experiment, some psychology finding, a change of question? Or is this thing just permanent, and if so why — and given all that, how should a committee of relatives who've been arguing for three weeks about it think about actually making our grant decision?
