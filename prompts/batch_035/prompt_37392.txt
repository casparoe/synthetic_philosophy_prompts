Before I take a side in the fights breaking out at our local historical society, I want the spec for what a theory would even have to deliver. The situation: our museum covers a region with a real communal story about itself -- how the town was founded, who "really" settled the valley, a beloved account of a mill strike that the archival record doesn't support in several particulars -- and a new director wants to "correct the record" in the exhibits while the old-timers are furious. Rather than wing it, I want the desiderata: what any adequate theory of when collective memory should defer to the historical record (and when it shouldn't) has to get right. The cases it must handle, the distinctions it can't collapse, the things it can't contradict -- with the reasoning behind each requirement, and a flag on any pair of requirements you suspect can't be jointly satisfied, because that's the part I most want to see.

Some cases and complications I'd want on the list, though add your own: memory that later turned out to be right when the record was wrong or thin -- Indigenous oral histories in Australia describing coastlines from thousands of years ago that match what we now know about sea-level change, or residential school survivors' accounts that outpaced the official archive for decades; memory that's wrong in detail but carrying content the archive never bothered to record (the archive itself being written by the people with the filing cabinets, i.e., the winners and the tax collectors); a founding myth that's archeologically false but functions as the actual glue holding the community together; state "memory laws" where the supposed record is itself curated by whoever holds power; and the distinction between flat factual error (wrong date, wrong number of dead) and selective emphasis, which might not be an error at all.

Distinctions I'd especially not want collapsed: epistemic deference (what we say happened) vs. symbolic or practical function (what the community does with the story); a memory being false vs. a memory being told for different purposes than a chronicle; the difference between correcting a community's memory and correcting its keepers -- whether deference is owed to the record or to the people harmed by the error. And it has to be usable by people like me, not just by theorists: it should tell a museum board or a school district something actionable about an exhibit label, not just pronounce from 30,000 feet.

So: give me the requirements with the case behind each, mark which are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's prejudice (is "the record always wins on matters of fact" a requirement or a slogan?), and identify the pair or set of desiderata that no theory can satisfy at once. If you think the whole framing presupposes something contestable -- like that there's a clean line between "the memory" and "the record" -- say so early, but still give me the list under the framing. Then, briefly, how three rough stances do against it: archive supremacy (the record always wins on facts), memory primacy (the story is identity-constituting and not answerable to archives), and whatever reconciliation view you find least bad. Keep the weight on the criteria, I'll do the scorecard myself at the next board meeting. No need for citations or reading lists, I want the reasoning.
