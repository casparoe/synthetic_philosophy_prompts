A friend and I spent most of last weekend arguing about the original/derived intentionality dispute between Searle and Dennett, and we've agreed to try writing up where we actually stand rather than continuing to talk past each other. My position is closer to Searle's: I think the symbols in a running program mean nothing in themselves — the "understanding" belongs to whoever interprets the outputs, the way the words on a page mean something only because writers and readers confer meaning on them — and that this is a fact about the systems, not about our habits of description. My friend is closer to Dennett: she presses Dennett's two-bitser example and his evolutionary argument, claiming that if we refuse to call a vending machine's coin-acceptance a genuine belief about value only because a designer built it, then by the same reasoning we should refuse to call our own states genuine beliefs, since evolution and culture "designed" us too; on her view the intrinsic/derived line marks where we stop tracing the causal history, not a difference in kind, and Searle's insistence that brains produce meaning "the way stomachs produce digestion" is an appeal to a miracle rather than an explanation. We've noticed that at least some of our disagreement may be verbal — perhaps about the word "intrinsic" itself — but we suspect not all of it is.

Please draft the joint statement our adversarial collaboration should produce, written so that each of us would accept the wording attributed to our own side. Structure it as follows: first, a numbered section of claims we both accept (we can already agree on things like what the Chinese Room is meant to establish and what it isn't, that the two-bitser shows at minimum that some apparent intentional states are artifacts of transposition between interpretive contexts, that a system's causal history and a system's current capacities are distinguishable questions, and that no currently available behavioral test discriminates between our views); second, a disagreements table stating each residual disagreement in phrasing each of us would sign; third, the cruxes — we think at least one concerns whether the underdetermination of formal description by physical fact (the observation that one and the same physical system can be mapped onto different computational descriptions, so which symbols it is manipulating seems to depend on an interpreter) applies to brains as much as to artifacts, and another concerns whether "causal powers of the brain" is an explanatory posit or a placeholder for an explanation we don't yet have; and finally, for each crux, the argument, case, or hypothetical discovery we both agree would bear on it, with each of our predictions recorded in advance — for instance, what each of us would say if neuroscientists found a principled, non-arbitrary way to individuate a brain's internal states that had no analog for transistors or marks on paper, or conversely if every attempt at such individuation turned out to be as gerrymandered for brains as for artifacts.

A few ground rules for the draft: don't attribute to either of us anything we wouldn't actually sign — no slanted phrasings like calling my view "mysterianism" or hers "behaviorism"; flag explicitly any part of our dispute you conclude is verbal rather than substantive, and say which part; and don't resolve the dispute in either direction by fiat — the deliverable is a document both parties could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact. If a disagreement is genuinely open, say so. Please write the whole thing in American English.
