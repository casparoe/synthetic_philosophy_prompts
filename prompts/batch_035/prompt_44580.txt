I would like a careful explanation of why "Death in Damascus" (the case introduced by Gibbard and Harper in their 1978 work on counterfactuals and expected utility) has been thought to reveal a defect in causal decision theory that Newcomb's problem alone does not. To make sure we are working from the same set-up, let me state the case as I have in mind: an agent in Damascus knows that Death will find him tomorrow, and that Death will be waiting in whichever city — Damascus or Aleppo — a trustworthy predictor foresaw the agent would be in. Wherever he flees, the fact that he settles on fleeing there is itself evidence that the predictor foresaw it, and hence that Death awaits him there.

Please explain three things in particular, at a level suitable for someone who already understands Newcomb's problem but has never worked through this harder case.

First, the instability itself. Spell out exactly how the causal decision theorist's reasoning in this case differs from her reasoning in Newcomb's problem, and why the difference matters: why does settling tentatively on one option here rationally undermine the very credences that made it choosable, so that deliberation oscillates without terminating, whereas in Newcomb's problem the causal decision theorist reaches a stable (if contested) verdict? I would like a gut-level explanation of the mechanism, not just a formal statement of it.

Second, the diagnosis. Is this instability a genuine flaw in causal decision theory, or is it an artifact of treating one's own future act as exogenous news during deliberation — something a more careful theory of deliberation could defuse? And what exactly does the evidential decision theorist gain here, given that she seems to end up indifferent between the two cities (since conditional on each, she dies)? Please be explicit about where you are reporting a standard position and where you are reconstructing or extending the argument yourself.

Third, a variant I have been chewing over and would like your view on. Suppose that, before deliberation begins, the agent can swallow a pill that induces a dreamless sleep, from which he will wake in whichever city a fair coin toss — conducted tomorrow morning by strangers, after the predictor's prediction is already fixed — selects. Does the availability of such an irrevocable randomization device dissolve the puzzle? If a predictor can foresee everything except quantum dice, then the agent seems to buy himself a genuine fifty-percent chance of survival, and neither causal nor evidential reasoning seems to stand in the way. But I can see two worries I would like you to assess: whether the value of randomizing shows something substantive about the causal-versus-evidential dispute, or instead merely that the original case was rigged with an infallible predictor; and whether the randomization merely pushes the problem back a level, since a predictor who foresees the pill might arrange things so that even the coin toss's city is covered. If the pill does help only when randomization is truly unpredictable, what does that suggest about what the Death in Damascus case is really testing — the decision theories, or the assumptions we built into the predictor?
