I am a high school student working through an introduction to algorithmic information theory on my own, and I have constructed an argument that I cannot defeat, though I am confident something in it is wrong. It concerns whether Solomonoff induction can justify Occam's razor.

(1) Occam's razor, made precise, says: among hypotheses that fit the data equally well, give more credence to the one with the shorter description.
(2) But simplicity is relative to a language: what has a short description in one descriptive system can have a long one in another, and for any particular hypothesis one can always design a language in which that hypothesis is the simplest of all. So, by choosing a language, any prior ranking of hypotheses by simplicity can be reversed.
(3) Therefore a prior that ranks hypotheses by simplicity is arbitrary: no such ranking is any better justified than any other.
(4) Solomonoff induction is supposed to answer this. It fixes a universal Turing machine U and assigns each computable hypothesis a prior weight that decays exponentially with the length of the shortest program for U that produces it.
(5) Its defenders say the choice of U does not matter, because a theorem — the invariance theorem — guarantees that switching to a different universal machine changes every description length only by an additive constant.
(6) That constant, however, has no fixed size: for any finite set of hypotheses and any desired ranking of them, one can build a machine that is still universal and that realizes that ranking.
(7) So the Solomonoff prior is arbitrary in exactly the sense (3) describes — it depends on which U you pick, and different choices can give different or even reversed rankings — and (5) is no defense, since the additive constant that bounds the differences can be far larger than the differences the razor is supposed to detect.
(8) Hence Solomonoff induction does not justify Occam's razor, and the razor's justification, if it exists at all, cannot appeal to simplicity.

Each step looked fine to me when I wrote it down, but (8) seems to prove far too much, and I cannot locate the false move. Please tell me which premise I should reject and what rejecting it costs; if several premises are candidates, compare their prices. I would most like to keep (1) and (5), since (5) is the only thing standing between my argument and the conclusion, but I cannot see how anything in (7) could be false without (5) being false too. Please end your reply with a final line of the form \answer{N}, where N is the premise you would reject, or \answer{sound} if you think the argument stands as written.
