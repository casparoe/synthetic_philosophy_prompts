I'm in a bioethics reading group and we've been going in circles for months on when someone actually dies. Same facts on the table for everyone: the old Harvard criteria, the 1981 uniform statute that most states adopted, whole-brain death as the legal standard, the higher-brain people who insist a permanently vegetative patient should count as dead, and the donation-after-circulatory-death practice where they take organs after just a few minutes without a pulse even though hearts occasionally restart on their own. Everyone knows all this and nobody has budged an inch. So what kind of disagreement is this, actually?

I have a pet theory that it's not a biological question at all: "death" is doing double duty, where one side means the death of the organism and the other means the death of the person, and a permanently vegetative patient could be a living organism and a dead person at the same time. But one member of the group swears the whole fight is really a disguised policy battle over organ transplantation — people are scared that loosening the rules makes doctors into killers, so the concept of death gets quietly shaped around what we want to permit, and the biology is downstream. And a third guy says it's just bedrock intuitions about what a person is, and no argument ever moves those.

Diagnose it using what each side actually claims — include stuff like the critics who keep bringing up brain-dead pregnant women kept on support long enough to deliver healthy babies, since that's supposed to wreck the "the body just falls apart without the brain" story that the whole-brain camp leans on. Then tell me which of our three theories, if any, is right, or whether its something else entirely. And be honest about whether anything could count as progress here — a conceptual distinction, some new empirical finding, a change in the question — or whether we're just going to keep circling forever. Please answer in American English.
