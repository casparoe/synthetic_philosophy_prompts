I'm on the committee that writes our PhD program's metaphysics comprehensive exam, and I've been assigned the question on grounding and fundamentality. In the last cycle the question was effectively answerable by reciting the formal profile of grounding — irreflexivity, asymmetry, transitivity, hyperintensionality — plus a ritual nod to priority monism, and we ended up with thirty answers that were indistinguishable from each other. This year I want a question built around the actual load-bearing argument in that literature: whether the thought that everything is grounded forces there to be a fundamental level, and, if it does, where exactly the force comes from. The crux I want to test is the gap between two claims the candidates tend to run together — that every fact has a ground, and that every grounding chain terminates in something ungrounded. Those come apart if an infinite descent of grounds is coherent, so the question has to make candidates isolate which premise rules out the infinite descent: the formal features of grounding themselves, or some substantive extra assumption about explanation that a metaphysical infinitist could simply refuse. Candidates sit the exam in a single three-hour sitting with a choice of questions; this one should be designed for ninety minutes of their time.

Please produce three things. First, the exam question itself, one paragraph, addressed directly to the candidate, and worded so that it cannot be answered by listing the formal features of grounding — the question should make the student deploy them. Second, a model answer of 700–800 words. The full-credit answer must contain at least: a statement of the regress argument explicit enough that its premises can be separately accepted or rejected; a distinction between vicious and benign regresses that actually does argumentative work rather than being recited as a label; serious engagement with whether an infinite totality of grounded facts leaves an explanatory residue — i.e., whether "every fact in the chain is grounded" and "the chain as a whole is explained" can both be true; and the infinitist's strongest reply, presented in its best form rather than as a strawman. Third, a marking scheme out of 25, organized around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth, a note on the most common way candidates miss each one, and an explicit statement of what separates a B answer from an A answer — my expectation is that a B answer correctly recites the machinery but never identifies which premise bears the weight, and I'd like the scheme to say so in almost those terms. One further stipulation: a candidate who denies the question is well-posed — for instance, by attacking the assumption that grounding chains are the kind of structure that can be reasoned about set-theoretically, or by challenging the borrowing of regress intuitions from epistemology into ontology — should be able to earn full marks if the challenge is executed well; please build that allowance into the scheme rather than treating it as a dodge. Please write all three components in American English.
