I write teaching materials for a third-year philosophy of physics course whose students have already completed a general relativity module. Next term we cover the hole argument, and I want a decision-tree handout that sorts students — and, frankly, me — into a position on the ontology of spacetime, not by inviting them to pick a label but by walking them from commitments they already hold to whichever leaf fits those commitments.

Please take the role of a philosopher of physics who has taught this material for years: begin by setting out, in a paragraph or two of private reasoning before the tree itself, the candidate diagnostic questions and which pairs of positions each one would actually separate, then build the tree from the survivors. Every reader enters at the same place: they accept that physicists are right to call general relativity deterministic, in the sense that the initial-value formulation is well-posed, and they also find themselves, when looking at a manifold diagram, thinking of the points as genuinely there — as distinct from the metric and matter fields placed upon them. The tension between those two commitments is where the tree begins.

Each internal node should be a question a student could answer without knowing where it leads. The distinctions I want on the tree, in whatever order makes the cleanest instrument:

1. whether two models related by an active diffeomorphism — one that leaves everything outside some hole-shaped region untouched but rearranges the field values inside it — describe two possible histories or one and the same history;
2. whether the hole argument differs in kind from ordinary gauge freedom in electromagnetism, or is only rhetorically more alarming: in electromagnetism the redundancy involves quantities we were already prepared to call unobservable, whereas the hole seems to threaten determinism about everything at once, so a student's reaction to this comparison is diagnostic;
3. whether bare manifold points have identity conditions of their own, or whether whatever individuates an event comes from the metric and matter it carries;
4. whether, if forced to choose, the student would rather pay for realism about the points with indeterminism, keep determinism by giving up haecceitism, or dissolve the question by denying that point-sized events are the right relata for an ontology in the first place.

Aim for five or six internal nodes and at most seven leaves. Leaves should carry the familiar names where the literature supplies them — naive substantivalism, Leibnizian relationism, sophisticated substantivalism, structuralist treatments of spacetime — and an explicit unnamed label where a live combination of answers has no occupant, with one sentence diagnosing whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored. Flag, rather than paper over, any pair of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate.

Because students will use the handout without me in the room, close with a short epistemic note: state how confident you are in the tree overall, and single out the one node you are least sure genuinely discriminates between the leaves it is meant to split, together with your reasons for that doubt.
