I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm building an interactive element for a long feature on whether the language you speak shapes how you think. The problem with the Sapir-Whorf question is that readers arrive with labels rather than views: they've heard the strong claim was "debunked," or that it was vindicated by the experiment in which Russian speakers, whose language has separate basic terms for light and dark blue, discriminated those blues a shade faster than English speakers, and they sort themselves into camps accordingly. So what I want from you is not a survey of the debate but a diagnostic instrument: a decision tree titled, in effect, "which position on linguistic relativity do you actually hold?", where every branch node is phrased as a question a reader could answer without knowing any position's name, and every leaf is a named position arrived at by what the reader says about cases.

I'd like the tree to be built around three distinctions that I suspect do the real sorting, and I want you to use them even if you would have chosen others — though tell me if you conclude one of them does no work. First, the online/offline line: does an effect count only if it shows up when language is not in use (silent memory tasks, nonverbal perception, categorization with no speech being planned), or do effects tied to preparing to speak count in their own right? Second, the degree/kind line: can every language in principle express any thought, with some merely doing it more awkwardly, or are some contents genuinely unavailable to speakers of some languages? Third, the permanence line: are the effects fixed traits of a bilingual's cognition, or only active while the relevant language is being used? The starting case should be the Russian-blues one, and at least one node should involve grammatical gender — say, the finding that when asked to personify a bridge, Spanish speakers, whose word for it is masculine, reach for stereotypically masculine traits while German speakers, with a feminine noun, go the other way.

The leaves must include at least: the strong relativist view that some thoughts are unthinkable in some languages; a universalist view that concedes only "thinking for speaking" packaging effects; a moderate neo-Whorfian view on which language nudges attention and memory offline but never renders anything unthinkable; and a deflationary view on which every reported effect is an artifact of language being online. Add further leaves if the logical space demands them (a view on which effects are permanent in monolinguals but switchable in bilinguals seems like it might need a home), but construct the space of node-answer combinations first and locate the positions in it afterward. For any combination no named position occupies, say so and give a one-sentence diagnosis of whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; and if there are two leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate, flag that rather than papering over it. My readers will scroll past anything long, so keep the entire deliverable, tree and commentary included, under 500 words.
