got into an argument on a rationality forum about the Allais paradox, and the other guy ended it with an argument that seems to prove way too much, and I can't find the hole in it. Setup in case you need it: in the first choice, A is $1M guaranteed, and B is a 10% chance at $5M / 89% chance at $1M / 1% chance at nothing — most people (including me, tbh) take A there. Second choice: same two gambles but the shared 89% branch becomes nothing instead of $1M, so A' is an 11% chance at $1M / 89% nothing, and B' is a 10% chance at $5M / 90% nothing — and the same people now take B' over A'. The independence axiom says your ranking should carry over between the two choices, so this is a straight violation. His argument:

(1) The violation only exists because the outcomes are described coarsely. Winning $1M for sure, feeling safe, is a genuinely different outcome than winning $1M out of a gamble you sweated through.
(2) If you slice outcomes that finely — the anxiety, the relief, the story you tell yourself afterwards — the Allais preferences can be made consistent with expected utility after all.
(3) More generally: for ANY pattern of choices whatsoever, if you're allowed to define the outcome of an act partly by which other options sat next to it on the menu, you can always redescribe things so the chooser comes out EU-consistent.
(4) So with rich enough outcomes, the independence axiom never actually forbids anything.
(5) But a normative theory that can't forbid any behavior isn't a theory, it's just bookkeeping.
(6) So either the Allais choosers are irrational after all, or expected utility theory is empty — and he says this means the paradox was never evidence against EU in the first place, which was his whole point.

I feel like the trick is somewhere between (2) and (3) — the descriptions in (2) are about the act itself (how risky it was), but (3) sneaks in descriptions that depend on what you didn't pick, and those seem like a different thing. But I can't nail it down, and he just keeps saying its only a description. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? For what it's worth I'd like to keep (2) — the safety thing seems real to me.
