So here's my situation. I write a science-and-ideas column, and a big chunk of my beat is the predictions industry — AI forecasters telling us the exact year everything changes, before that Fukuyama's end of history, before that the Marxist stages of history. Every generation seems to produce somebody claiming history has a direction you can read off and extrapolate, like it was physics.

For the next piece I've been reading Popper's attack on what he called historicism — the doctrine that there are laws of history and that predicting society's future in broad strokes is therefore a science. His core arguments, if I'm understanding him right: real prediction needs laws plus initial conditions; any prediction of future knowledge would have to already contain that knowledge, so the growth of knowledge is the one thing we can't forecast in advance; and treating whole societies as a single unit you can extrapolate is a category mistake, since what happens next depends on what people will learn.

And it hit me that Isaac Asimov basically ran the experiment at almost the same moment Popper was working this stuff out. Hari Seldon's psychohistory in the Foundation trilogy is supposed to be exactly the thing Popper says is impossible: a genuine science of history that predicts the fall of the Galactic Empire and charts the next thousand years. And the funny part is that Asimov's own plot keeps vindicating Popper. The Seldon Plan works right up until the Mule — a single unpredictable individual — wrecks it, and then the story has to quietly bring in a hidden Second Foundation to keep the Plan on track, which looks alot like the historicist's old trick of explaining every surprise as part of the forecast after the fact.

So here's what I want from you: read the original Foundation trilogy (Foundation, Foundation and Empire, Second Foundation — don't lean on the later books, most readers don't know them) through Popper's critique. Where exactly does psychohistory fail by Popper's lights — the unpredictability of new knowledge, the role of exceptional individuals, the holistic treatment of society, or something else? Is the Second Foundation a fix for psychohistory or an admission that it never worked? And is there a serious reading on which Asimov knew all this and the trilogy is secretly an argument AGAINST laws of history rather than a fantasy of them — the Mule being the point, not a plot device?

Also be honest with me about where the analogy breaks down. Psychohistory is about a whole galaxy of people; maybe at that scale the math genuinely is different, and Asimov was writing pulp adventure on a deadline, not a treatise on methodology. If the reading only works by sanding off things that are actually in the books, say so — I need something I can put in the piece, not "wow, science fiction predicts the future."
