In a reading group on normative decision theory, our presenter kept closing off discussion of alternatives to expected utility theory with the line that any agent who violates the axioms can be turned into a money pump: someone who isn't an expected utility maximizer is, in his words, a walking profit opportunity, and since no one wants to be a money pump, rationality requires maximizing expected utility. I found myself unable to say precisely where this argument is strong and where it smuggles things in, and I'd like a careful map of what the slogan is actually asserting.

Please lay out the distinct precise theses the claim could be expressing, ordered roughly from weakest to strongest, with a verdict on each. I'd particularly like you to distinguish at least the following possibilities, and note any others you think the claim is equivocating between: (1) the purely formal observation that certain structures of preference, such as cycles, guarantee the existence of a sequence of individually acceptable trades whose net effect is a loss; (2) the stronger claim that such a sequence can actually be executed against the agent by some exploiter, which seems to require assumptions about the agent's willingness to accept repeated offers, the bookie's knowledge of the agent's preferences, transaction costs, and whether the agent can refuse or revise mid-sequence; (3) the claim that mere vulnerability to a one-time small loss is irrational, rather than just a cost of, say, having incomplete preferences that get completed over time; (4) a representation-theorem reading, on which the point is not about exploiters at all but about which preference structures can even be represented as maximizing some function; and (5) the sweeping conclusion that anything that isn't literally expected utility maximization, including designed alternatives meant to capture observed behavior like probability weighting or rank-dependence, is thereby incoherent.

For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false, and — most importantly — say which of them, if any, actually licenses the move my presenter made, namely going from money-pump vulnerability to the normative conclusion that a rational agent must maximize expected utility. If that inference requires bridging assumptions that are themselves contestable, please identify them explicitly. Present the readings as a numbered list I can bring back to the group, each with a one-line verdict, followed by a short section on which reading the presenter most plausibly needs and whether it holds up.
