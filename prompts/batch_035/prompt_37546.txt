so i'm a retired physical chemist -- 35 years in spectroscopy before that means anything to you -- and since retiring i've fallen hard for philosophy of mind, reading my way through the stuff i ignored my whole working life. latest was von neumann's The Computer and the Brain, the little book he never finished: he was supposed to give the Silliman lectures at yale, got too sick to deliver them, and the manuscript came out in 1958 after he died. everyone said it was the origin of the whole "brain is a computer" idea, so i had to read it.

here's my situation. my grandson works at one of these AI companies, and whenever we talk he leans on the analogy his whole field implicitly runs on: the brain is just a computer, only made of meat. slower parts, sure, but same deal -- signals, codes, memory, computation. therefore (he says) human-level AI is just an engineering problem, and the mind is nothing over and above computation. he's not being smug about it, he genuinely believes it, and honestly i half believe it myself after a lifetime of watching physicalist explanations win.

but the funny thing is, from what i remember, von neumann spent much of that book noting how badly the mapping fits. the brain's components are enormously slower than the computers of his day, but there are vastly more of them; the brain does arithmetic (he says) with far less precision than any computer tolerates -- he talks about short words and shallow arithmetical chains; and near the end he speculates that the brain's "language" is probably statistical in character, nothing like our symbol-by-symbol codes, maybe closer to something out of fourier analysis (which was my bread and butter, so that bit stuck with me). so as far as i can tell, von neumann was saying the brain is like a computer in some respects and unlike it in exactly the respects that might matter -- and then he died before finishing the argument.

can you give me an honest audit of the analogy as my grandson uses it? i'd like: the correspondence table -- what plays the role of the processor, the memory, the code, the clock if any; then where the mapping breaks, seperating the disanalogies that actually matter for his conclusion (AGI is just engineering) from the ones that don't; and then whether his conclusion survives once the breaks are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole time. and if there's a better analogy to reach for -- von neumann seemed to hint at one himself -- tell me what it is and what it gets right that the meat-computer picture gets wrong. keep in mind i'm a scientist, not a philosopher: i can handle math but not jargon. and please correct me where i've missremembered the book.
