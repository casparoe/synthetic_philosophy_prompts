I'm a novelist, and I'm stuck in the middle of an argument that broke out between two friends of mine over dinner, and since I genuinely can't tell who's right, I'm hoping you can referee it move by move.

Context: my current novel has a secondary character, an old historian, who keeps saying "history is written by the victors," and I'd been feeling smug about how worldly that line was. My friend Marta, who actually teaches history, rolled her eyes at it, and my editor Dev pushed back on her, and off they went.

(1) Marta said the line is mostly false as a description of who actually writes. The vivid, emotionally intense accounts tend to come from the losers, she said -- the defeated write memoirs and laments because they have time and grievance, while the victors are busy governing. She brought up the Lost Cause: the Confederacy lost the war and then won the memory war for decades, with former Confederate generals and their sympathizers shaping how the South taught its own history to schoolchildren. Exiles and losers, she said, are history's most prolific writers. So my line, in her view, is a lazy truism that a novelist should be embarrassed by.

(2) Dev answered that the losers write, sure, but the victors decide what gets an audience -- what's canonized, taught, reprinted, optioned. The Lost Cause didn't triumph because losers were persuasive on the merits, he said; it triumphed because it served the needs of the people who held power after Reconstruction was abandoned. Losing voices survive, he argued, precisely to the extent they're useful or harmless to whoever's holding the levers, and get quietly buried otherwise. So the thesis is right, just at the level of transmission and curriculum rather than authorship.

(3) Marta came back hard: then "victors" is doing sneaky double duty. If every account that survives counts as having served the victors, the claim becomes unfalsifiable -- whatever survived, survived because the powerful allowed it. That's not a discovery about history, she said, it's a tautology dressed up as one, and no historian could ever, even in principle, count against it. A thesis that can't lose isn't a thesis.

(4) Dev replied that a tautology that keeps you honest is worth more than a falsifiable claim that keeps you flattered. And here he turned it on me, which stung: novelists, he said, run the opposite bias. The losers' account is more vivid and narratively satisfying, so we believe it more readily -- and "history is written by the victors" makes the underdog story feel like courage by default. He told me my historian character is a lazy device not because the line is false but because it licenses me to skip the actual work of figuring out who transmitted what and why, and just hand the moral high ground to whoever's bleeding on the page.

Neither of them would budge after that, and I honestly couldn't referee it, though I noticed both of them were arguing about my book more than about history by the end.

Can you go through their exchange and rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with brief justification? Then tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing -- I suspect there's something about the fact that "the victors" isn't one group across time (the people who won later fights get to re-rank the earlier winners), and whether that helps either of them I can't tell. And since I need to actually decide how to revise the character, please end with a final line of the form

Verdict: [short phrase naming who's more right overall and about which specific points]
