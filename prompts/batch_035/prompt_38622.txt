I am preparing for ordination examinations, and the process includes a written doctrinal statement defended orally before a board drawn from several traditions. I have drafted my sections on providence and free will, and preliminary feedback has split the examiners: one tells me my position is Molinism in denial, another that it quietly collapses into soft determinism. I believe both are mistaken, but before the defense I want a genuine consistency audit of the position rather than reassurance. Here it is, as drafted:

(1) In any free choice, I could have done otherwise, and nothing up to and including the moment of choice settles which way I will go; a free act is not the output of prior sufficient conditions.

(2) God has infallible, specific foreknowledge of every future free act. God knew, centuries beforehand, the particular acts by which Judas would betray and Peter would deny, and God knows what I will say tomorrow. This knowledge is not inferred from determining causes, since on my view there are none.

(3) I am not a Molinist. I accept the grounding objection: prior to a free decision, there is no fact about what a free creature would do in circumstances it never actually faces, because nothing determines the outcome and the creature never decides there; hence there is nothing for God to know in such cases.

(4) God's providence is meticulous. The circumstances of every life — when and where one is born, which nation's history one is swept into, whom one meets — belong to a single plan, and God secures the outcomes of that plan by placing creatures in circumstances, never by coercing a will.

(5) God is not the author of sin. Responsibility for evil acts rests with the creatures who perform them; God's role in arranging the surrounding circumstances neither excuses the creature nor impugns God.

(6) Foreknowledge is compatible with freedom, since God's knowing that I will do a thing no more causes it than my knowing a fire will burn causes the burning.

The examiners focused their fire on whether (1) and (2) can coexist, and I take (6) to answer that charge; if there is a real problem in my draft, I assume it lies there or in the pairing of (4) with (5). What I want from you: test all six commitments against one another, rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than flagging everything equally, and show me which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection. For each real tension, say what would have to give and what the revision costs elsewhere in the package. Where a standard escape route exists — appeals to God's timelessness, to the non-causal character of knowledge, to the distinction between God's permitting and God's securing — tell me whether it actually works for commitments I have made or only for a package I have not signed. Then tell me which single revision would repair the most damage. Please do not give me a historical survey of who held what; treat the position as six theses and test them on their own terms.
