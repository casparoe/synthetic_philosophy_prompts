I just got out of a three-hour argument with my housemate about Langdon Winner's old line that artifacts have politics, and I want to stress-test my side of it, becuase I won on volume but I'm not at all sure I won on the merits.

Here's roughly where we ended up. We both signed off on the weak version: designers can bake their politics into a thing — the famous case being the Moses overpass story, which I know is shaky as history, but the general point stands, structure can do quietly what a law does loudly. Fine, not what we fought about. What we actually fought about was the strong version, which is the one I hold: that some artifacts are political in their own right, not because anyone put the politics there, but because the technical setup is only workable under one narrow kind of social arrangement — the textbook case being that you can't have backyard-scale nuclear power, so the technology drags a centralized, security-heavy apparatus along with it whether the designers were saints or not. My line was: with a technology like that, the politics lives in the physics and the scale, not in anyone's intentions, so there's a real sense in which the artifact itself has a politics. Her line was that this smuggles a whole sociology in under the name of engineering — she says the "needs" are always needs relative to some cost threshold and some tolerance for risk and some legal regime, and if you shift those, the same machine lives happily under a very different arrangement, so what looks like politics-in-the-artifact is just politics from somewhere else showing through. Her counterexample was the internet: built by an establishment and repurposed into something they hated. My comeback was that "repurposing" is doing a ton of unexamined work in her argument — a lot of what gets called repurposing is just the artifact working exactly as designed, for a group the designer never had in mind, which is a different thing from the structure being genuinely open. We went around on that last bit for a good hour without resolving it.

So: what would count against the strong version? I don't want the boring "people sometimes use things in unexpected ways" objection — I already concede that, everyone does. I want the stuff that should actually move me. Concretely, I'd like: (1) what the strong thesis predicts that her view doesn't, such that we could in principle go and look — it seems to predict, for instance, that for genuinely structure-political artifacts, repurposing at scale never happens without first defeating or dismantling the structure, and I want to know whether that's even testable and whether anything in history looks like a clean counterexample; (2) which of the assumptions could fail without the thesis collapsing back into the weak version, and which failures would collapse it; (3) which objections she could raise that I'd have to concede actually count against me, versus which I could reasonably wave off. And be honest about whether the thesis has quietly been qualified into something unfalsifiable — I've noticed that whenever an example goes the wrong way I say "oh, that one doesn't count, the structure isn't really doing the work there," and I want to know if that's me protecting the thesis or whether the thesis genuinely has a domain and the boundary-tracking is legitimate. Also, if you think the distinction I drew at the end — open structure vs. artifact working as designed for unintended users — does real work, say so, since that was the part I was least sure of even as I was saying it. Rank the defeasers from fatal to merely awkward, and at the very end tell me which side of the argument you'd honestly rather be on.
