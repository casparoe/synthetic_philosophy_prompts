I cover science and ideas for a mid-size paper, and for the last few years my beat has been the replication crisis, so I've gotten knee-deep in the world of pre-registered studies and registered reports. There's something about the whole apparatus that naggs at me, and I think it's the philosophy underneath it.

Here's the setup. Journals increasingly demand that researchers file their analysis plan before seeing the data. If you write down beforehand that you predict the effect in the left tail, then finding it there counts as strong evidence; if you notice the same effect in the same data after poking around, it counts as basically nothing, or at least as something needing a whole new confirmatory study. Some journals even accept papers based on the proposed design before a single subject is run — the paper is guaranteed publication whatever the result turns out to be.

On its face this is pretty strange. The data are the data. The photons don't care when the researcher first thought of the hypothesis. Two labs could run the identical experiment, one with the hypothesis in hand on Tuesday and one improvising on Thursday, and the profession treats their identical results as carrying different evidential weight. So the practice seems to presuppose that the epistemic value of a result depends partly on the researcher's intentions and timing — which sounds like exactly the kind of psychologism that science is supposed to have stamped out of itself, the whole reason we have blinding and standardized instruments.

And yet when I ask the methodologists about this, basically none of them cop to being Popperians. They talk about the garden of forking paths, researcher degrees of freedom, perverse incentives — it's all framed as preventing self-deception, not as any grand theory of falsification. So my questions: what view of evidence or scientific rationality does this machinery actually presuppose? Is it falsificationism smuggled back in through the side door, with the pre-registered prediction playing the role of the risky conjecture that could have embarrassed you? Or is it compatible with a Bayesian story on which the timing of the hypothesis genuinely changes what the data tell you? Or is it something more deflationary — an anti-fraud and anti-bullshit mechanism with no real epistemology underneath at all? And is the profession being inconsistent when it celebrates serendipitous discoveries — penicillin, the cosmic microwave background, the pill that was supposed to treat angina — in its op-ed pages while its methods sections quietly classify that kind of finding as second-class evidence? I'd also like to know where the practice is genuinely underdetermined, meaning which rival philosophies of science could each sign off on it without discomfort, since I suspect the answer isn't a single hidden theory.
