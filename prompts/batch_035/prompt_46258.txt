I've come to hold the view that a rational agent should evaluate acts by their risk-weighted expected utility rather than their expected utility. On this view, roughly: order an act's possible outcomes from worst to best, and weight each outcome's utility not by its probability alone but by a transform, applied to the probability of doing at least that well, that reflects the agent's attitude toward risk; a risk-averse agent weights the downside more heavily, so a fifty-fifty gamble between nothing and a large prize can rationally be valued below its expected value, even below the smaller amount some sure option pays. This is essentially the theory Lara Buchak defended in her 2013 book Risk and Rationality, and what draws me to it is that it lets me say that an ordinary person who takes the sure thing, and even one who pays a steep premium for certainty, is fully rational rather than merely excused.

Tell me what would count against this position. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward: which patterns of observed choice, which constructed cases, which formal results about decision theories of this general shape, and which pressures from neighboring questions about what it takes for a theory to explain behavior rather than merely re-describe it. For each defeater, say whether a committed defender of the view would concede that it counts against the theory or would dismiss it, and on what grounds. I am especially interested in the accommodation worry: since the risk function is a free parameter, and since the utilities assigned to outcomes are themselves up for grabs, perhaps any consistent set of preferences can be retro-fitted to the theory, so that it never rules anything out. If that is right, has the position been insulated into unfalsifiability, and is that a defect in a theory of this kind? Relatedly, tell me what the view genuinely predicts that expected utility theory does not, so that I can see where its exposure is real. Please answer in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
