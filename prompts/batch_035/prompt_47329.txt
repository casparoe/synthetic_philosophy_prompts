Each semester, the finance committee of my high school's student council has to divide about $4,000 of discretionary funds among competing proposals from clubs and event organizers. The rubric we inherited scores every proposal out of a hundred — points for expected attendance, points for educational value, points for serving underrepresented students — but we all privately admit the numbers are invented and the weightings arbitrary, because we are comparing things like a one-night cultural festival, a robotics team's registration fees, and a tutoring program for struggling freshmen that don't share a common measure. From some reading, I understand that philosophers sometimes call such values incommensurable, and that some of them argue two options can be "on a par" — neither better nor worse nor simply equal — so that choosing between them is not a matter of getting the comparison right but of committing to one. I would like a decision procedure my committee can actually use in place of the fake rubric: the questions to ask about a proposal, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure should stop with a no; and where it should hand the decision to a person or a vote because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. The procedure should tell us what to do when two proposals seem on a par rather than forcing an artificial tiebreak. Please then run it on three proposals — the robotics team's trip to a regional competition, a cultural festival expected to draw half the school, and a peer-tutoring program for students failing courses — including at least one case where the procedure yields an answer I will not like, since my own club's proposal is among them. Finally, be honest about what the procedure cannot do.
