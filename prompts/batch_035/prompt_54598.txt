I'm a retired chemist — thirty years in process safety and environmental risk assessment at a big plant — and since retiring I've gotten deep into philosophy, first philosophy of science and lately decision theory, which is where I've run into the Allais paradox and the independence axiom and gotten thoroughly obsessed. Something that I never thought to question during my working life now looks philosophically loaded to me. When the regulator we dealt with (and its equivalents in most countries) does cost-benefit analysis, it's strict expected value all the way down: a 1-in-100,000 annual mortality risk to workers or neighbors gets multiplied by a value per statistical life, summed over the exposed population, and compared against the certain cost of the safety retrofit, and the retrofit lives or dies on the arithmetic. That looks like it presupposes expected utility theory, and specifically the independence axiom — that how you value a gamble can't change when a common outcome is tacked onto both alternatives. Fine so far. But the very same agencies, for a certain class of hazards — catastrophic plant failure, a pesticide with a suspected long-latency effect, anything irreversible — operate on something like a precautionary principle, where even a tiny, badly-quantified probability of a very bad outcome can veto the project no matter how favorable the expected-value calculation is. I was discussing this with an old friend from my actuarial consulting days and she said the two-track setup is just politics, but I don't think that's the whole story.

Here's what's bugging me specifically. That pair of institutional preferences looks structurally like the Allais pattern itself: strict expected value in the middle of the distribution, but a near-certainty demand for security once you get to the tail — the certainty effect reappearing at the level of institutions rather than individuals. And Allais thought that pattern shows the independence axiom is false as a norm of rationality, not that people are just being silly. So my question is: what theory of rational choice, if any, does this two-track regulatory practice actually presuppose? Is it flatly inconsistent — the institutional version of choosing one way in Allais' first gamble and the other way in his second, which violates the very axiom its cost-benefit half presupposes? Or can the combination be reconstructed coherently, e.g. as ordinary risk aversion in outcomes rather than a violation of independence, or via a genuine distinction between risk and what I understand is called Knightian uncertainty (the tiny catastrophic probabilities being precisely the ones nobody can actually quantify), or via some thought about consent — that imposing uncertain risks on third parties without their agreement is a different kind of wrongdoing than failing to install a safety valve? I'd like a real reconstruction that seperates what the practice strictly requires philosophically from what it merely fits comfortably, and that tells me where the practice is underdetermined — where several rival theories of rational choice would each license both tracks without embarrassment.

Could you start with a prose reconstruction of the implicit theory (or theories), and then, so I can file it with my notes, finish with a table having exactly four columns: the element of the practice being analyzed (e.g. statistical-life arithmetic, precautionary vetoes), what it presupposes about rational choice or value, which rival views it quietly rules out, and whether that element is actually underdetermined so that several theories would permit it. If your honest conclusion is that the practice is just philosophically incoherent and only survives because nobody holds both halves in view at once, I'd rather hear that than a rescue attempt — but if there's a defensible reconstruction, I want to see exactly which moves it needs and what it costs.
