so i work on post-training at an AI lab and something about our whole pipeline keeps bugging me and nobody answers it cleanly. the core recipe, roughly: we show crowdworkers pairs of model responses, they pick which is better, we train a reward model on those pairwise picks, and we optimize the policy against the reward model. then on top of that we do safety training where the model is taught to refuse certain kinds of requests outright regardless of what the user wants, and the whole thing gets sold internally and externally as just learning human preferences, the model serves the users values, etc.

what theory of whose values count does this practice actually presuppose? when i try to spell it out, the pieces fight. the preferences being aggregated are the annotators, not the users; the annotators are steered by guideline docs the lab writes, which encode substantive views (about politeness, about what counts as harmful, about what helpful even means) that nobody elected; conflicts between users get handled how exactly -- if a solid majority of users would love the model happily writing one-sided persuasive essays for their side, and the rest hate it, we just refuse everyone, which looks like it presupposes something other than aggregation. and the pairwise-averaging thing quietly assumes there is a fact of the matter about the aggregated preference, even tho preference aggregation over three or more options can be cyclic (condorcet cycles, arrow-style stuff), so the aggregate may not even exist as a coherent object.

so: is there a coherent implicit theory here? preference utilitarianism with everyone thrown in the pool? per-user relativism, each user gets served their own values, which the safety layer then flatly violates? something like liberal neutrality where the AI takes no stance on contested conceptions of the good, except the guidelines obviously do take stances? or is the honest answer that the training pipeline itself only strictly presupposes something much weaker -- a single scalar reward that some weighted mix of humans rates higher -- and is compatible with a whole bunch of aggregation theories, with the real value commitments living in the guideline doc rather than in rlhf at all?

reconstruct the implicit theory as best you can, tell me which parts of the practice strictly need it versus which parts merely fit nicely with it, flag where the practice is philosophically underdetermined so several theories would license the same behavior, and then say what would have to change in the pipeline if we swapped the presupposed view for, say, a rawlsian overlapping-consensus design or a fiduciary/trustee model (model as a professional who serves your interests rather than your stated wants, like a doctor who talks you out of the treatment you asked for). i genuinely don't know whether the deep issue is the aggregation step or the override step, so don't just assume its one or the other.

end with a final line in the form \answer{...} containing a short label for whatever view you think the practice as a whole most closely presupposes (e.g. preference utilitarianism, per-user relativism, incoherent mix of relativism and lab paternalism, too underdetermined to say), followed by a one-clause reason if you can fit it in.
