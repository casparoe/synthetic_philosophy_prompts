hey, so I'm a phd student in chinese philosophy and I'm the TA stuck writing the section-exam essay question for our intro asian philosophy course — students get ~50 minutes. I'm so tired of grading thirty bluebooks that say "Mencius thought humans are innately good, Xunzi thought innately evil, and both agreed education matters." Write me one essay question on the Mencius–Xunzi debate that can't be passed by reciting that line, along with a model answer of about 600 words and a marking scheme out of 20 marks.

The question should push students toward the thing I actually care about: a chunk of the dispute is arguably verbal. Xunzi defines xing roughly as whatever you have prior to any deliberate shaping, while Mencius seems to treat it more like a developmental capacity that grows when fed and withers when starved — so "human nature is good" and "human nature is bad" may not even share a truth value across the two. I want the question set up so that noticing this is table stakes, and the marks come from what happens after.

For the model answer, it has to hit at least these: (i) how Mencius's Ox Mountain move lets him absorb Xunzi's evidence from everyday selfishness — a hill stripped bare by axes and grazing shows nothing about what grew there originally, so observed badness is compatible with intact sprouts — and whether Xunzi has any reply to that which isn't question-begging given his own definition; (ii) the circularity worry on Xunzi's side: if everyone's wood starts crooked and only gets straightened by ritual and standards that human artifice supplies, who built the first steaming-frame? Can his habituation story get going without at least some spontaneous pro-social tendencies, and does granting that much concede the real point to Mencius; (iii) say where a genuinely substantive disagreement survives once the verbal part is set aside — my own candidate is whether spontaneity can ever be a *source* of moral value or only ever raw material, but they can argue otherwise.

The marking scheme should name the specific moves that separate an A from a B, and flag the two mistakes I keep seeing: reading Xunzi's "bad" as something like original sin (a moralized reading the text doesn't really support), and treating Mencius's sprouts as full-blown virtues rather than germinations that cultivation finishes. Keep the question itself short enough that a student can read it twice in the exam without panicking.
