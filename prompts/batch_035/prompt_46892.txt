I have been following the metaontology debate for several years now — the exchange, running roughly from the Metametaphysics volume onward, between the deflationists and the substantivists. On the deflationist side: Hirsch's quantifier variance, together with his principle of interpretive charity, on which the disputants in fights over mereological composition or the existence of tables are each speaking truly in their own languages, so the fight is verbal; and Thomasson's easy ontology, on which the existence of tables and holes is settled by inferences so trivial that no serious disputant could contest them. On the substantivist side: Sider, for whom the question whether our quantifier carves at the joints keeps ontological questions substantive; Fine, who holds that the Quinean question is shallow and the genuine one must be framed in terms of what is real rather than what exists; and Chalmers, who is deflationist about existence claims while insisting that questions about structure survive. My sense is that the exchange has settled into a restatement cycle: each camp has its canonical moves, everyone knows them all, and nobody has budged in over a decade.

What motivates my question is an oddity I have not seen squarely addressed. As far as I can tell, the two methodologies issue the same verdict on nearly every first-order sentence anyone cares about: both sides accept that there are tables and prime numbers, and even Hirsch grants that van Inwagen speaks truly under his own meaning of the quantifier. The whole disagreement concerns the status of the residual disagreement — whether the first-order truce masks a substantive question or dissolves it. I want a diagnosis of that second-order dispute, not another restatement of the first-order positions.

My hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis: the meta-dispute is itself a verbal dispute, a standoff between two explicata of the word 'substantive'. Read as 'answerable prior to any choice of conceptual scheme', the notion makes the deflationist win by definition; read as 'answerable in the vocabulary of the best theory', it makes the substantivist win by stipulation. But the hypothesis carries an embarrassment I cannot resolve on my own. If it is right, the deflationist's charge against the first-order ontologist appears to be verbal in her own sense, and it is not clear whether she should count that as a cost. Meanwhile the substantivist can concede that the meta-dispute is verbal while insisting that a further, non-verbal question survives — which quantifier our inquiries should use — though whether that question has a correct answer (Sider: yes, whichever carves at the joints; Hirsch: no fact of the matter) looks like a first-order dispute about the objectivity of structure, relocated one level up rather than resolved.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, really — verbal, normative in the sense of being about which vocabulary deserves a place in inquiry, or a substantive first-order disagreement in disguise? Please ground the diagnosis in what the participants actually say rather than in generic taxonomies of disputes, and address the regress or relocation worry sketched above. Then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a worked case in which the two methodologies genuinely diverge on a first-order verdict, a precisification of joint-carving that makes it meet the deflationist's own standard, or an argument that no progress should be expected because the question, as posed, is ill-posed and the dispute should simply be retired.
