I'm writing my term paper for a graduate seminar on consequentialism, and I've ended up defending a position I've started calling lexical threshold negative utilitarianism: suffering above a certain severity threshold gets lexical priority over everything else — no quantity of wellbeing, and no quantity of sub-threshold discomfort, can compensate for or trade off against even a single instance of threshold-level suffering — while below the threshold, pleasures and pains commensurate on an ordinary cardinal scale and aggregate across persons in the usual way. What pulled me toward it was an asymmetry I can't shake: the certainty of extreme suffering for one person seems to demand a kind of justification that no number of modest gains for others can supply, and the analogues of Parfit's cases comparing lifetimes of mild contentment with a single day of agony seem to force exactly this two-level structure rather than a single scale with pain weighted heavily. But I've noticed that my seminar cohort splits in a way that suggests the position is more exposed than I thought: the ones who accept commensurability say my threshold is an ad hoc compromise, and the ones who reject commensurability say I've given away the store by aggregating below the threshold at all.

I should be honest that my version carries two qualifications I added to blunt familiar objections, and I don't know whether they stabilize the view or just complicate it. First, the threshold is vague — a sorites-style range rather than a sharp line — which I hoped would let me dodge the pinprick argument (that any view granting lexical priority to extreme suffering must grant it to mild suffering too, since there's no non-arbitrary cutoff). Second, the lexical priority applies only to deliberately inflicted extreme suffering, not to natural extreme suffering, which I hoped would block the objection that the view licenses refusing to create lives of bliss containing one instance of extreme natural pain, or even destroying a world of happy people painlessly to preclude future agony. There's also a question I've been chewing on lately that may be more serious than either: under risk, does the whole structure collapse? If I must choose between a certainty of moderate suffering for many and a small probability of threshold-level suffering for one, it looks like expected-value reasoning reintroduces exactly the commensurability the view was built to deny — unless I forbid aggregating across probabilities, which then seems to paralyze ordinary decision-making.

So here's what I'm asking for: a map of what would actually count against this position, not just the standard litany. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal through serious to merely awkward — including specific cases it cannot plausibly describe, theoretical results it conflicts with (for instance, anything it implies about the representability of its value ordering in a social welfare function, or about what it can say about very low probabilities of catastrophe), and consequences of the view that a reasonable person simply cannot accept. Tell me which of these a committed defender would concede as counting against the position and which they would dismiss, and on what principled grounds. And give me an honest assessment of the endgame: with my vague threshold and the deliberate-infliction restriction in place, does the view risk anything at all, or have I qualified it into something unfalsifiable — and if the latter, is that a fatal problem for a view of this kind, or just what moral theories look like when they take vague terms seriously?
