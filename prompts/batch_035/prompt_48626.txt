I'm the standards editor at a mid-sized general-interest science magazine, and my fact-checkers keep getting into arguments they aren't equipped to referee. Our op-ed and freelance desk regularly runs pieces that lean on heritability numbers — twin-study estimates, sometimes SNP-based figures from genome-wide studies — to support conclusions about education policy, weight, mental health, or workplace behavior. The writers rarely misquote the number; what they do is slide from the number to a conclusion it doesn't license ("it's 60% genetic, so the school program can't matter"; "highly heritable, so trying to change it is futile"). My checkers have genetics and statistics training at roughly the college-course level, can read an abstract, but cannot reanalyze data or adjudicate a contested literature, and they have perhaps fifteen minutes per piece. I want a decision procedure they can actually run on a paragraph that cites a heritability figure: an ordered list of checks, each stated as a question about the piece, with what counts as a pass, what counts as a fail that requires revision, and where the check can't be completed from the text alone and has to be handed back to me. The checks should cover the ones I half-remember from my own reading — that heritability is a property of a population in an environment, so it moves when environments get more or less uniform; that a within-population figure says nothing about differences between populations; that heritable doesn't mean unchangeable; that twin and molecular estimates come apart, with the "missing heritability" gap cutting both ways. But the procedure also has to be able to pass a piece: if someone uses a heritability figure correctly — say, to argue that a trait's heritability in one setting doesn't preclude big environmental effects elsewhere — the procedure should say so, not just function as a veto machine. And it should mark, honestly, which steps are genuine checks with a determinate answer versus judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and at least one point where the remaining question is a value question ("should we intervene?") that no fact-check can settle.

Then run the procedure on three paragraphs from our recent slush pile, which I'll paraphrase: (a) an op-ed citing a twin study reporting heritability around 0.6 for children's reading ability in a nationally representative sample, concluding that literacy gaps between schools largely reflect ability rather than teaching, so reading-intervention funding is being wasted; (b) a wellness piece citing a heritability estimate around 0.7 for body-mass index, concluding that since the trait is mostly genetic, urging individual dietary discipline is blame-adjacent nonsense and the piece's own advice should pivot to medical treatment; (c) a piece I personally liked — an argument for universal early dyslexia screening that leans heavily on reading disability being heritable — where I suspect the procedure will flag the use made of the number anyway, and I want it applied straight regardless of whose ox is gored. For (c), I'd especially like to see whether the procedure distinguishes "hereditary, so screen for it" (often fine) from "heritable, so fixed" (never fine), or whether that distinction turns out to be a judgment call the procedure has to punt on.

Please also include a short section on where the procedure will misfire — the cases where the ordered checks give a confident verdict that's actually wrong, or where two reasonable checkers applying the same step would diverge. And throughout, tell me how confident you are in each part of what you claim, and single out the one point you're least sure of — if any check in your list is really your own reconstruction of the reasoning rather than something practitioners would recognize as standard, say so plainly rather than presenting it as settled method.
