I'm a senior backend engineer at a mid-sized company with no philosophy background, and lately I've noticed that a lot of the friction in my working life has the same underlying shape: two teams can each either invest a sprint in the shared foundation — the flaky integration suite, the on-call runbooks, the migration tooling nobody owns — or free-ride and race ahead on features, and whoever free-rides looks better at review time even though everyone ends up worse off when both do it. There's no referee and no way to sign binding agreements about any of this. A friend pointed me at Hofstadter's idea of superrationality: roughly, that when two agents face a symmetric choice and will reason their way to the same conclusion, each should treat their own choice as if made for both — cooperate because your defection "predicts" theirs, even though it doesn't cause it. The idea appeals to me because it seems to make cooperation rational without communication or enforcement, but I can equally see it becoming a sophisticated way of talking yourself into being the sucker while the other team optimizes for something else entirely.

What I want is not the philosophy but a decision procedure I can actually run when one of these recurring conflicts comes up — something that tells me, step by step, whether superrational cooperation genuinely applies or whether I should just defect, or whether the case is really an ordinary repeated-game situation where I should cooperate for reputational reasons and the superrationality framing adds nothing. The procedure needs to force the checks I'd otherwise skip: whether the situation is truly symmetric or only looks symmetric from my side; whether the counterpart is plausibly running a decision procedure similar enough to mine for the "we'll reason identically" premise to hold, versus pursuing an unrelated objective; whether our choices are correlated in the way the argument requires or causally independent; whether repetition, visibility, or the ability to publicly precommit changes the verdict; and what to do when the symmetry is real but the stakes are wildly unequal. It should say what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where it halts with a clear "don't apply this here," and it should be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as checks.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (a) my team and a peer team each deciding, independently and without coordination, whether to write candid postmortems that admit our own mistakes or defensive ones that shift blame; (b) me deciding whether to spend a weekend improving a shared library when the other team that depends on it has a consistent history of free-riding; and (c) a one-time dependency dispute with a team that is being disbanded at the end of the month. At least one of the three should come out with an answer I won't like. Present the procedure as exactly five numbered steps, each a sentence or two, followed by the three worked cases, each resolved through the five steps in no more than three sentences, with no introduction or conclusion anywhere.
