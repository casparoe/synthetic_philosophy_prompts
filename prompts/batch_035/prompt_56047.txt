got a request that might take a bit of thinking. I'm teaching a grad seminar next term on language and social reality, and I want to open it not with the usual survey but with a spec sheet: the list of requirements any adequate theory of speech acts / performative utterances has to satisfy. my students tend to treat Austin's felicity conditions as settled fact, and I'd rather they see them as one theory's answer to a set of demands we argue about first.

heres the twist, and the reason I don't want the standard list: I want the requirements drawn up for the world we actually live in, where a huge share of utterances that look performative come out of systems that aren't human. when a chatbot says it's sorry, when a system message says your booking is confirmed, when an automated email terminates your account — does a theory built for human speakers standing in social conventions even apply? I don't want you to answer that, I want you to write the requirements such that the answer falls out of them. e.g. does the theory have to take a stand on who or what can be a speaker, or can it stay neutral? must the act's success be checkable from outside (some third party can tell it happened)? must the speaker be capable of the intentional state the act conventionally expresses? those are just examples off the top of my head — come up with the real list, don't just recycle mine. and please include the stuff Austin famously set aside as etiolated or non-serious — stage actors, quoted speech, reciting, talking to no one — because a chatbot arguably sits in exactly that bucket, which makes those cases the main event rather than a footnote.

structure I'm after: each requirement with the case or consideration behind it; then mark which are non-negotiable and which are camp preferences (is sincerity really a felicity condition or just a norm of use? is uptake by the audience necessary, or just typical? does there need to be a pre-existing convention at all, or can conventions be created on the fly, as with new online speech acts like verifying or banning someone?); then flag any pair you suspect can't be jointly satisfied — my hunch is that "the act succeeds purely by convention, in a way that's transparent to participants" and "artificial speakers' utterances count as performing acts" pull against each other, but I could be wrong. if some subset of the requirements turns out jointly unsatisfiable, say so straight out — that's the part I care most about, since then the list itself is the finding, and my students get to watch a desiderata set do philosophical work.

please don't score the classic theories (Austin-style conventionalism, Searle-style intention-based accounts, anything else you think belongs) against the list — we'll do that in seminar ourselves. no citations, no bibliography, no reading list; I've got all that. aim for maybe 1200-1500 words, and write it in American English — my program is in the US and I keep slipping into British spellings out of habit. thanks, this ones a lifesaver if it works.
