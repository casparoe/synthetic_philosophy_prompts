In debates in my city over removing a colonial-era statue from the town square, one side keeps insisting that pulling down statues is like tearing pages out of a history book: whatever the man did, the monument is part of the public record, and deleting the shameful parts of the record is censorship by installment. The other side replies that a statue is not a record but an honor — closer to a trophy on a shelf than a page in a book — and that taking a trophy down erases nothing, since the history remains in archives and classrooms regardless of what stands in the square. Each side is using its analogy as an argument, not a decoration. Could you audit both? Lay out the correspondence table for each — what plays the role of the book, the page, the reader, the tearing in the first; of the trophy, the shelf, the awarding body, the removal in the second — then say where each mapping breaks, what each analogy quietly smuggles in (for instance, about whether commemoration is describing or praising, about who the presumed reader or spectator is, and about whether a record can honor at the same time as it records), and whether either side's conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether both analogies were carrying conclusions the underlying facts cannot. Please also indicate which consideration would be most likely to flip your verdict about which analogy (if either) holds up, and where in your reasoning you think the analysis is most vulnerable.
