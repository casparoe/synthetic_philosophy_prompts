I edit the newsletter for a local history society, and with all the arguing in our town about the founder statue (and a couple of street names that turned out to honor some real winners), I want to run an interactive piece: a "what do you actually believe about commemorations?" quiz, delivered as a decision tree, so readers get sorted to a named position by how they answer concrete cases.

Take the role of a professional philosopher specializing in public ethics and the politics of memory. Before writing the tree, brainstorm internally: the entire value here is in the diagnostic questions, so think hard about where the real positions actually come apart. Build the tree from the logical space, not from a list of position names — first work out the branch questions and what combinations of answers they permit, then locate the actual positions in that space, and note any unoccupied combinations (is nobody there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored?). Also flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really separate instead of pretending it can.

Each branch question should be a concrete case someone can answer without knowing which position it leads them to. No question can just ask whether they believe in erasing history or whether they honor the person or the values — those are the labels, not the diagnosis. Please make sure the tree covers at least these kinds of cases (reshape as needed, these are meant as seeds):

- a case where the person's main achievement is inseparable from the offense — say, a general celebrated primarily for victories in a war fought to defend slavery
- a case where the offense and the achievement are entirely seperate — a statue of a scientist or poet whose awful private views or conduct had nothing to do with what they're honored for
- a case where the honoree publicly repudiated the offending views late in life — do we honor the whole person or the person at their best?
- a case where the commemorative meaning has drifted: a street named after someone nobody living remembers, or a statue that over generations became a beloved landmark for reasons unrelated to who's depicted
- a case about who decides: the current residents, the descendants of the harmed group, a city council, a referendum
- a case about what happens after a removal: museum with full explanation, storage with nothing, empty plinth, or a replacement commemorating the harmed group?

Leaves should include at least: keep-as-is retentionism, on-site contextualization (plaques and the like), the counter-memorial move (keep the object but subvert or shadow it with new art), relocation to a museum, removal with nothing taking its place, and removal with a replacement. If there are other positions in the logical space, include them; if one of my seeds turns out not to split any positions that actually matter, say so rather than padding the tree.

Aim for around 8 branch questions, each with two or three answer options, plus a short intro paragraph for the newsletter. The audience is thoughtful general readers with zero philosophy background, so no jargon in the questions themselves — the position names can appear at the leaves with a one-sentence gloss on each, including its most awkward commitment (the thing its defenders have to swallow).

One more thing: in the intro, make the point that a lot of people who call themselves against removal answer the concrete cases like contextualists, and plenty of self-described removalists balk at the museum option once they see whats actually involved — the quiz exists to catch that kind of mismatch. Dont resolve the mismatch for the reader, just expose it.
