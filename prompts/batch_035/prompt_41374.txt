I'm a software engineer by training and have no formal background in philosophy, but I've read enough popular economics and decision-making material to be dangerous. Once a quarter I run a 60-minute "critical thinking" session for my team — 12 engineers, a mix of senior and junior, every one of whom has personally worked on a project that they privately knew should have been killed months before it finally was. The next session is on sunk costs, and I want to do better than the standard treatment.

Here is my complaint about the standard treatment: it presents sunk cost honoring as a straightforward bias — the failure mode everyone memorizes from Psych 101 and from every project post-mortem ever written. But the harder and more interesting question is whether honoring sunk costs is always irrational, and the answer is not obviously yes. A few cases keep nagging at me. First, completion sometimes has informational value: finishing a nearly done project can teach you whether the underlying bet was sound in a way that abandoning it at 80 percent never can, and that knowledge is worth something for your next bet even if this one is already lost. Second, there are agency and reputation effects: a team known for abandoning things at the first sign of trouble may never be trusted with long-horizon work again, and that trust is worth real money. Third, costs are not always fully sunk — some are partially recoverable, and quitting itself can carry switching costs — in which case the textbook principle quietly stops applying. I've also read that biologists have found nonhuman animals apparently honoring sunk costs (the so-called Concorde fallacy), which suggests either that the bias runs very deep or that it is not always a bias at all.

Design an in-class activity — a game with rules and payoffs, not a lecture — for this session. My requirements:

1. In the first phase, participants should feel the pull of escalation of commitment: the rules and payoffs should be arranged so that many of them, behaving rationally in the moment, pour more resources into a losing course of action, and only see the pattern when the round ends. The failure should emerge from what they do, not from anything I say up front.

2. Then run a second phase, or a variant round with changed rules, in which honoring the sunk cost is actually the payoff-maximizing choice — and the debrief should force them to articulate exactly why the usual "just ignore sunk costs" slogan fails in that round. I want them to leave able to distinguish genuine sunk cost bias from situations where past investment legitimately changes the decision, not just able to recite that one of them is a fallacy.

3. Materials should be things I can get in an office: index cards, a whiteboard, a timer, and a points ledger or play money. Payoffs should be points, never real money. Give me a minute-by-minute timeline for the full 60 minutes including debrief.

4. The debrief should map specific moments from the game onto the three nagging cases above — informational value of completion, reputation and agency effects, and partially recoverable costs — in plain language I can deliver credibly as a non-philosopher. It should end with a short bridge to our actual work: how a kill decision on a real project differs from what the game captured.

5. Tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — engineers gaming the system for points, someone announcing the punchline in the first five minutes, senior people dominating the discussion — and how to handle each failure mode.

6. Finally, be frank about which feature of the philosophical question no points-based game can reproduce, and which part I will simply have to supply myself in the debrief.
