I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for a long time — long enough to have watched the decision-theory wars cycle through several rounds. Every few months the same family of arguments resurfaces: Hofstadter-style superrationality, evidential decision theory's treatment of your own choice as information about correlated agents, the reasoning behind one-boxing, and the grandest version of all, the idea usually called evidential cooperation in large worlds. As I understand the core of it: if the world is extremely large — a multiverse, a cosmos vast enough to repeat things, whatever — then somewhere there are agents whose decision procedures are relevantly similar to yours, such that your choice is evidence about what they choose. You can't signal them, and they can't affect you or you them; no causal channel exists or ever will. But if you cooperate in ways that cost you little locally, then conditional on your cooperating, it's more likely that your correlated counterparts are cooperating too, and the aggregate value of that can swamp the local sacrifice — or so the argument goes. I've watched people treat this as the most important idea in practical ethics and others wave it off as decision-theoretic sympathetic magic. Both camps seem to me to be having a real disagreement about something genuinely hard.

For a writing project, I'd like a satirical piece aimed at this idea — not at the people who hold it, and not at rationalist culture generally, but at the structure of the idea itself.

The form: a membership agreement plus FAQ, around 800–1000 words in total, for a fictional organization (call it "The Coalition" or invent a name you think is funnier) that brokers multiverse-wide cooperation agreements for its members. Deadpan bureaucratic register — think mutual-aid society, credit union, or trade association, swollen far beyond any sensible scale. Numbered clauses, then a short FAQ section.

The humor has to come from getting the idea right, so that someone who actually believes in evidential cooperation would find the piece fair rather than a strawman. Clauses and FAQ entries might cover: the full-disclosure statement that the Coalition can never verify that counterparties exist, verify that they cooperated, or contact them in any way; a tiered system for classifying how similar another agent's decision procedure must be before your choice counts as evidence about theirs; the consequences of local defection (may your counterparts find out? they can't — so what, exactly, changes?); what happens to a member's standing if they convert to causal decision theory mid-term and are no longer correlated with anyone; the marketing of one's infinitesimally small share of total coalition influence as an anti-free-rider feature; dues, payable in a currency deliverable only through correlated decisions; and how the Coalition answers the accusation that it is merely a roomful of people unilaterally cooperating and hoping. Exaggerate what is really there. Do not give the Coalition causal powers, telepathy, messages, or enforcement mechanisms of any kind — the joke must be that there aren't any, and never could be, and the bureaucracy carries on regardless. The strongest version of the view should emerge looking like itself run through a compliance department, not looking stupid through misstatement.

Keep the irony legible but unwinking: no narrator elbowing the reader. A specialist should be able to reconstruct the actual argument from the satire alone.

Finally, append an author's note of roughly 150–250 words, clearly separated from the piece, that sorts things into two buckets. First: which features of the piece merely reflect commitments that any serious version of evidential cooperation genuinely has — the absence of causal contact, evidence rather than enforcement doing the work, the individual's negligible share of total influence. Second: where the piece leans on premises that are genuinely contested among people who know the literature — whether correlations of the needed strength between distant agents are at all plausible, whether evidential reasoning of this kind is the right way to act at all, whether the world is large in the relevant way, and how much of the payoff story depends on exotic cosmology or metaphysics. Where the piece takes a deliberate liberty for comic effect, say so plainly. In the note and the piece alike, do not present any contested commitment as settled consensus within this corner of decision theory — the disputes between evidential, causal, and functional decision theorists are live, and the note should not quietly resolve them in either direction.
