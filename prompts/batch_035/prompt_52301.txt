I'm prepping a handout for our reading group on pragmatic encroachment (we just finished Hawthorne and Stanley, before that DeRose), and one thing I keep noticing is that people conflate epistemic contextualism with subject-sensitive invariantism / pragmatic encroachment, treating them as two names for the same view. They're not. Could you give me a worked example that makes the divergence vivid, step by step?

My idea for the shape of it: build a case where the *attributor's* situation is relaxed but the *subject's* situation is high-stakes — something like a third-person, maybe even past-tense ascription, where the speaker casually says someone "knew" the bridge was safe, while the person crossing the bridge at the time had their life depending on it. Then walk through, with the same fixed facts, what the contextualist says (the truth of the ascription shifts with the attributor's conversational context, so the casual speaker speaks truly) versus what the subject-sensitive invariantist says (the meaning of "knows" stays fixed, but the practical stakes of the *subject* change whether the ascription is true, so the casual speaker speaks falsely). Please spell out the verdicts of each view on the exact same sentence, uttered in the exact same context, so the difference can't be waved away as "well, different situations, different answers."

A couple of requests for the write-up. First, when you lay out each view's verdict, please mark clearly which parts are just standard, settled textbook material and which parts are genuinely contested among people who actually work on this — for instance whether contextualism can really handle the intuition that the high-stakes subject and the low-stakes speaker are disagreeing with each other, or how SSI deals with cases where the subject's stakes and the attributor's stakes pull apart. I don't want the handout presenting a contested line as if it were the consensus view. Second, dialogue is welcome if it makes the case easier to follow. Third, write everything in British English (so "characterise" and "colour", not the -ize/-or spellings).

Don't worry if the example ends up a bit contrived — that's fine for a handout — but the mechanics of the two verdicts need to be exactly right.
