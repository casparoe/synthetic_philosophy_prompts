As a PhD student working on eighteenth-century moral philosophy and self-culture, I'm trying to write a conference paper on how Franklin's "Project of Moral Perfection" would map onto contemporary self-optimization culture, and I'd like your help thinking through the speculative core of the argument.

To set the stage: Franklin's scheme, as recounted in the Autobiography, involved thirteen virtues he defined for himself, a little book ruled with columns for the days of the week, and a regimen of focusing on one virtue at a time while marking his daily failures with black specks. Notably, he abandoned the project in its strict form (he says Order gave him the most trouble), and he was candid that the practice served him socially — the appearance of diligence and punctuality brought him credit and trust in business. He was also quite sensitive to the sin of pride, famously reasoning that even if he ever conquered it, he'd probably be proud of his humility.

Now suppose Franklin could observe three modern artifacts:

1. A habit-tracking app with streaks, badges, and a social feed where users publicly display their adherence metrics to friends and strangers;
2. A corporate wellness program that pays employees cash bonuses for meeting step counts and sleep targets monitored through employer-issued devices;
3. An AI life-coach trained on the user's journal entries that offers personalized, dialectically tailored exhortations toward the user's own stated goals.

For each of the three, how do you think Franklin would have reacted, given what we know of his temperament and stated views? In particular, I'm interested in whether his blend of instrumentalism about virtue, commercial pragmatism, and wariness about ostentatious piety cuts differently in each case: does the social-feed app fall to his pride concern, the wellness program to his dislike of imposed moral authority, or does his love of mutual-improvement institutions like the Junto make him more sympathetic to communal enforcement than we might expect? And do you think he would regard the AI coach as merely an automated version of his own little book, or as something that changes the character of the enterprise because the reflective judgment is outsourced rather than exercised? I'd also welcome your view on whether his self-confessed failure with the virtue of Order should make us think he'd be more or less sympathetic to the streak-preserving gamification that modern apps use to prevent exactly the kind of lapse-driven abandonment he experienced.
