Next month I'm speaking at an interdisciplinary workshop on operationalizing animal welfare criteria across species. My thesis is a graded view of moral status: status comes in degrees; the degree should be fixed by a brain-based quantity, roughly the number of neurons in the forebrain, taken as a proxy for the capacity for valenced experience; and species membership as such carries no independent weight. My argument, in sketch: behavioral criteria systematically under-report experience in animals that lack the means to demonstrate it, so we need a structural proxy; of the available structural measures, forebrain neuron count does better than absolute brain size (which is dominated by body size) and better than encephalization-style ratios; and since the capacities underlying sentience come in degrees, all-or-nothing categories drawn at species boundaries get the ordering wrong even where they get the verdict right.

The audience is about forty people, and I need to know what they, specifically, will throw at me. The bulk are animal welfare scientists: their working method is to infer sentience from behavioral and physiological evidence — trade-off and self-medication paradigms, analgesic self-administration, threat-response and stress-indicator work — and they will suspect, in their vocabulary, that my metric measures cleverness rather than suffering. Two of the attendees are comparative neuroanatomists who know the allometry and neuron-counting literature cold; they will not let anything sloppy stand about brain mass versus neuron number, or about what the metric does with animals whose brains are large but sparse versus small but densely packed. There is also a legal scholar whose job is to turn whatever criterion the workshop endorses into administrable regulation, and who thinks in terms of bright lines, categories, and enforcement.

Please prepare my Q&A sheet. List the objections this audience will actually raise — phrased as these people would phrase them, not as a philosophy seminar would — ordered by how much damage each would do to the thesis. For each, give me a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft, distinguishing what I should concede up front from what I must hold. Cover at least the ones I anticipate: the charge that neuron count tracks cognition, not affect; the large-brain/sparse-neuron problem that the metric seems to rank some very large animals below some very small ones; the confounding of any brain measure with body size; the demand from the regulation side for thresholds that a scalar criterion refuses to supply; and what the metric implies about recent behavioral work suggesting pain-like states in animals with very small brains. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, with the reply I would need if it comes anyway. Finally, tell me which single reply has to land, in the form I give it, for the thesis to survive the session at all.
