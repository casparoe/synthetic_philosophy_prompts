Last night a friend of mine who works in economic theory and I had a long and ultimately unresolved argument about the centipede game, and I would like a considered comparison of the two positions we ended up defending, since neither of us was persuaded by the other and I suspect the disagreement is not really about game theory at all but about what kind of idealization common knowledge of rationality is.

To set the stage: in the centipede game, two players alternately choose between taking a slightly larger share of a growing pot immediately or passing, letting the pot grow further; the largest payoffs sit near the end. My friend defended the classical result that if rationality is common knowledge, backward induction dictates that the very first player should take at once. I pressed the familiar objection that this reasoning appears self-undermining: a player who actually arrives at a late node confronts a history in which someone passed at an earlier node when, by the theory's own lights, they should have taken. That history seems to refute the very common knowledge the argument needs, and it is obscure what a rational player is entitled to infer about their opponent once the hypothesis of rationality has been observably violated.

Our argument then crystallized into a comparison between two ideas, and this is what I want from you. First, the orthodox idea: common knowledge of rationality — infinitely iterated mutual knowledge — is precisely what licenses the inferences about unreached counterfactual nodes, and the right response to my objection is that these inferences are made from the ex ante standpoint, before any history refutes anything; the paradox allegedly confuses what is believed on the path with what is believed at hypothetical off-path nodes reached only by a deviation that itself falsifies the premises. Second, the dissolving idea my friend introduced as his fallback: common knowledge is an idealization, and the psychologically and logically robust notion is approximate common knowledge — it is common belief with some high probability p strictly less than one that each player is rational. He illustrated with a coordination scenario in which two parties exchange messages that each have a small probability of being lost, so that each knows the relevant fact with probability arbitrarily close to one, yet it is never common knowledge; and he claimed that an arbitrarily small probability of irrationality, iterated through the long chain of reasoning the centipede game demands, unravels the backward induction conclusion entirely, making passing for a while uniquely rational.

Please compare these two ideas properly. Specifically: (1) Does the ex ante defence of common knowledge actually answer the self-undermining objection, or does it merely relocate the problem into the players' off-path counterfactual reasoning, where it becomes unclear what a player should believe about an opponent who has just deviated? (2) Is the approximate-common-belief view a genuine rival to the orthodox one, or a refinement of it — that is, does it deny that common knowledge of rationality implies immediate taking, or only that finite, bounded agents ever have the common knowledge the classical theorem requires? (3) Does the approximation view dissolve the paradox or merely trade an infinite hierarchy of idealized beliefs for an infinite hierarchy of almost-beliefs, given that each level of nesting compounds the small probability of error? (4) Which of the two ideas better explains the robust experimental finding that human players typically pass for a while before taking, and which better explains why play approaches the backward induction prediction as the game is repeated or the stakes rise? I would like you to take a position on which idea, if either, gets the epistemology of iterated strategic reasoning right, and to say explicitly what each view would have to concede if it turned out to be the wrong one.
