I work in trust and safety at a company that deploys large language models, and something about our day-to-day practice puzzles me. Our content policies work almost entirely by enumeration: a handbook lists dozens of prohibited categories, each with definitions, borderline cases and worked examples, and the list grows every month as new failure modes surface. Whenever we try to replace the enumeration with a single general clause — 'refuse genuinely harmful requests' — the result is unusable, blocking either everything or nothing. Yet human moderators seem to apply a general notion of harm without any such list, and cannot say how. I'm told this connects to something philosophers of artificial intelligence call the frame problem, and perhaps to the closed-world assumption in database design. What view of relevance — of what counts as bearing on a decision — does our enumerative practice presuppose? Does it presuppose that the features of a situation relevant to moderation are finite, stable and knowable in advance, and is that consistent with our team's official insistence that the policy can never be complete and must be perpetually patched? Is the practice underdetermined, in the sense that several theories of relevance would license it equally well, or does it commit us to one? And does the stubborn failure of general clauses suggest that the frame problem remains unsolved for architectures like ours, or that it was never really a problem about enumeration in the first place? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say where it is coherent and where it strains, and tell me what would have to change in our practice if we took the alternative view of relevance seriously. Please answer in British English.
