So I cover science and ideas for a mid-size outlet, and I've been kicking around a piece about why everyone — neuroscientists, sleep researchers, my own editor — just assumes dreams are experiences that happen while you sleep. That got me reading the old Malcolm/Dennett spat: Malcolm's late-50s book arguing dreams are not experiences at all (the "dream" is really something that gets going in the telling, on waking), and Dennett's 70s response with his composition-and-editing model, where the dream is drafted at the moment you wake up, working backwards from whatever stimulus jolted you — the story gets written after the fact and then misremembered as having been lived through. Fine. But now I rewatched Inception and I can't stop seeing this debate all over it, and I want a proper reading of the film through it, because I think I'm onto something but I'm not a philosopher and I don't trust my own armchair work here.

Here's what I mean. The film's whole machinery — the team designing dreams in advance, the architect, the dream running on its own clock with time dilating the deeper you go — seems to flatly assume Malcolm lost: these are experiences, unfolding in real (dilated) time, with a history and an internal consistency you can navigate. But then there's Mal, Cobb's dead wife — and yes I noticed the name, Mal/Malcolm, probably a coincidence but it's a funny one — who is literally a confabulated construction, a story Cobb keeps telling himself inside the dream, which feels very Dennett: the narrative composed and re-composed rather than experienced. And the ending, the spinning top we never see fall: if you can't tell from inside whether you're dreaming, what exactly was the "experience" of watching that last scene? Was it had, or was it composed in the instant the credits rolled?

So: give me a reading of Inception through the Malcolm-vs-Dennett debate that's fair to both the film and the philosophy. Where does the film need dreams to be experiences (the time-dilation conceit, the kicks, the shared-dream tech), where does it smuggle in the post-hoc composition idea without admitting it, and does the dream-sharing technology make the whole debate moot — since these dreams are designed and occupied by multiple dreamers — or does it actually sharpen it? Be honest about where the film resists the reading; I don't want a clever essay that turns the movie into an illustration of the paper. And tell me, if you can, whether Nolan dramatizes something neither Malcolm nor Dennett quite said — my editor will want a kicker and I'd rather it be true than cute.
