I'm the director of a small regional museum. Sixty years ago we bought what everyone in town still calls "our Vermeer," and it's been the heart of the collection ever since -- schoolkids do assignments on it, the local paper prints it on every anniversary. Last year a conservator working on a crack found something under the paint, and long story short, it's a forgery. A good one, painted in the 1930s, well before we bought it, so nobody currently at the museum is implicated and the only people defrauded are long dead. We've decided not to quietly take it down and shuffle it into storage: it's staying on the same wall with a new label telling the whole story, and we're holding an evening event to sort of reopen it.

Please write my remarks for that evening, about five minutes. The room will be mixed: trustees who are furious we're airing this at all, a couple of major donors, docents who've spent decades telling visitors about the Vermeer and feel personally conned, and a lot of ordinary people who love the painting and feel weirdly betrayed even though they can't say why. I want to lean on the fact that literally nothing in the painting changed overnight -- every visible brushstroke is what it was a year ago -- while taking seriously that looking at it now feels different, and that something really did break. The docents keep saying visitors "see it differently now," and I think that's the crux I want to explore rather than paper over: what exactly are they seeing that they weren't before, given that it's the same surface? Don't resolve it neatly for the room; give them something they can actually think with. And please write it in American English.
