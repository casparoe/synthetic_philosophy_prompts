I'm a PhD student writing a dissertation chapter on doxastic control and irrationality, and a friend from the cognitive science program has an argument she thinks settles the self-deception question in the most deflationary way possible — that it's not just rare or hard but impossible — and then, alarmingly, proves more than she wants. The setup is familiar: the spouse who believes her partner faithful despite the hotel receipts, the smoker who believes this particular cigarette doesn't really count, the advisor who believes his student's dissertation is 'nearly there.' Her argument runs: (1) To deceive someone is to act with the intention of producing in them a belief that one takes to be false. (2) An intention guides action by its content, so one cannot intend to produce in someone a false belief that p without oneself believing that not-p — the falsity of the target belief has to be represented in the intention somewhere. (3) In self-deception, deceiver and deceived are the same agent. (4) So the self-deceiver believes that not-p, qua deceiver, and believes that p, qua deceived. (5) A single mind cannot hold p and not-p at once without collapsing the very distinction between deception and confusion that the intentional description was supposed to preserve. (6) Therefore self-deception is impossible, and the cases we call by that name are really just wishful thinking, motivationally biased updating, or ordinary error with good lighting.

So far this is the classic static paradox, and I could live with it — the deflationary accounts that drop the intentionality requirement and model self-deception as biased evidence management seem to handle stage A. But she then pushes the argument further, and this is the part I can't shake off: (7) if, per (2), one cannot intend to produce a belief whose content one doesn't already take some doxastic stance toward, then not only can no one deceive themselves, no one can intentionally acquire a belief at all — you can't read a paper intending to come to believe its result, can't run the experiment intending to learn how it comes out, can't even check the manuscript intending to settle what a passage says, since in each case you aim at a belief whose content outruns anything you already believe. (8) So belief formation is never intentional, and 'doxastic voluntarism' dies not at the level of willpower but at the level of the possibility of aiming at belief in the first place. That conclusion seems plainly too strong — I sat down this morning intending to find out what a certain footnote claims and now believe something I didn't before, and the intention did guide the reading — so I'm confident there's a slide between stage A and stage B, probably something about the difference between intending to believe that p regardless of how things are and intending to believe whatever is true about q. But I can't pin down the slide precisely, and, more troubling, I can't tell whether exposing it rescues stage A or merely hides the fact that stage A trades on the same ambiguity somewhere earlier.

Here is what I'd like from an answer. First, a diagnosis of each stage separately: which step should be rejected, why that step looked fine (I want to know what made it seductive, not just that it's false), and what rejecting it costs — because some of these premises I'd rather keep. I want to keep the intentionality of ordinary deception of others; I want to keep the thought that the self-deceived spouse is doing something rather than merely suffering something, which is presumably why we blame her in a way we don't blame the merely mistaken; and I'd like to avoid picturing the mind as literally divided into deceived and deceiver halves, since that seems to multiply agents to save a phenomenon. Second, a pricing of the standard escape routes: the deflationary route that keeps stage A by making self-deception unintentional and, arguably, refines it into mere motivated bias; the divided-mind or partitioning route, whatever it costs in unity of agency; and any position that bites the bullet on both stages. Third, and most importantly, whether my instinct is right that stages A and B fail together, or whether they fail independently for different reasons — and if the latter, what that asymmetry tells us about the difference between aiming at a belief's content and aiming at a belief's truth. I'm presenting a version of this at our rationality seminar in two weeks and would rather not present a friend's reductio that I myself can't repair.
