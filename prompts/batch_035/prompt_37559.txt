So for the last couple of years I've been the sortition guy in my friend group and in my local politics circle. It started with jury duty, weirdly — I served on a civil case and came away thinking the random twelve of us did a better, more honest job than any committee of career politicians would have, and I've been going down the rabbit hole ever since. I help out with a local citizens' assembly thing on housing policy, and now I've been asked to write up my actual position for a blog post / mini-manifesto, basically the case for going all the way to lottocracy. Before I publish it I want a consistency check, because my friend Sarah says my views don't hang together and I want to know if she's right (she won't tell me where the problem is, she says I should find it myself, which is annoying).

Here's the package. (a) I think a randomly selected chamber should replace the elected legislature for real — not just consultative assemblies that hand recommendations to politicians, but binding law made by sortition. (b) Selection should be stratified so the chamber is a genuine microcosm of the population — gender, age band, income, education, region — and I'd also set guaranteed floors for groups that historically get underselected even if they respond to invitations at lower rates, like young people and renters, even though I know this means some citizens have a somewhat higher chance of being picked than others. (c) The whole legitimacy of the thing comes from the fact that it's ordinary people — it's literally us in miniature, which is why I think the jury analogy is the strongest card sortition has. (d) I also think deliberation is the point: once members hear from experts and talk it through, they land on positions the general public would reject in a referendum — like the carbon tax our local group ended up recommending, which would get demolished at the ballot box — and I consider that a feature, not a bug. (e) Members serve one fixed term with no recall and no possibility of re-selection, because the moment people can campaign for another term you've reinvented the incentive structure that corrupts electoral politics. (f) Deep down I believe political equality just is equal probability of any citizen holding office — that's what makes a lottery fair where an election isn't. And (g) lately I've been getting more skeptical of courts striking down the chamber's laws — if the assembly is us in miniature, unelected judges with lifetime tenure vetoing it feels like it's the wrong way around, legitimacy-wise.

Go through these for me. Which pairs are actually in tension, which ones only look like it, and how bad is each clash? For each genuine tension, tell me what the cheapest fix would be and what that fix costs me elsewhere. And at the end, tell me which single revision would clean up the most if I bit the bullet on it. I honestly don't know where the weakest plank is — Sarah says it's obvious once you see it, which I find hard to believe, so prove her wrong or prove me wrong, one of the two.
