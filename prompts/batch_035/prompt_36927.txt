I am a retired condensed-matter physicist — thirty-five years at a national laboratory, mostly on electronic transport in disordered materials — and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of science in earnest for the first time. Perhaps unwisely, I have also agreed to serve on the editorial board of a mid-tier physics journal and as an ad hoc reviewer for a funding agency. This means I now spend a few evenings a month judging manuscripts and grant proposals whose central claim is that some theory or model is confirmed by its agreement with data that were already in hand long before the theory was proposed, and I have discovered that I have no principled basis for judging such claims.

When I was doing research, we all operated on a folk rule: a successful novel prediction counts heavily in a theory's favor, while successfully accommodating already-known data counts for much less, or nothing at all. Nobody could say exactly why, or how much less, and we quietly made exceptions — everyone in my field agrees that a theory's ability to explain a stubborn old anomaly can be devastatingly impressive even when the anomaly predated the theory by decades. Since beginning my reading, I have learned that philosophers have a name for the underlying puzzle, the problem of old evidence, and that it is genuinely awkward for Bayesian confirmation theory: if I already know E, then my probability for E is one, and it seems no hypothesis can raise the probability of something that is already certain — so old evidence apparently cannot confirm anything, which is obviously the wrong answer. I have also noticed some practical wrinkles in the refereeing situation that the philosophical discussions I have read do not address, and I would like your help with those in particular:

1. There seem to be at least three different situations lumped together as "old evidence": the datum was actually used in constructing or tuning the theory; the datum was known to the theory's author but played no role in the construction; or the datum was unknown to the author though well known in the field (say, buried in an old technical report). My instinct is that these should count very differently, and that the crucial factor is somehow the author's epistemic situation rather than the logical relation between theory and evidence — but I am not confident, and I can't find a clean way to draw the lines.
2. There is what I think of as a double-counting problem: in my field, a single venerable dataset gets cited as supporting a dozen successive refinements of a model, each paper taking the last one's success as its starting point, and I suspect the accumulated confirmation is largely illusory without anyone having done anything dishonest.
3. Referees almost never know which of the three situations in (1) they are facing, because authors do not disclose how a model was actually built. A procedure that only works when one knows the model's construction history is useless to me.

Please take on the role of a professional philosopher of science with deep expertise in Bayesian confirmation theory and its known difficulties, who has been asked to advise a working editorial board. Before writing anything, think carefully about the underlying epistemology — in particular, work out for yourself which distinctions genuinely matter and which of my three wrinkles are real problems as opposed to confusions on my part, since I may be misdescribing things. Feel free to conclude that some of my framing is off, but tell me so explicitly rather than silently correcting it.

Then write the deliverable: a decision procedure that I and my fellow board members can actually run on a manuscript or grant proposal, in an evening, without access to the authors and without doing any of our own data analysis. It should be a checklist of questions in a sensible order, each with an indication of what counts as a sufficient answer, a plain-language explanation (a few sentences at most, aimed at scientists with no philosophy) of why that question is there, and clear stopping points where the procedure hands the decision back to human judgment because the remaining issue is not a factual one the checklist can resolve. It must be honest about which steps are defensible applications of confirmation theory and which are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Finally, run the procedure on three cases drawn from my actual reviewing life:

(a) A theory paper whose model, built from general principles with no free parameters fitted to the data in question, retrodicts the long-standing unexplained linear-in-temperature resistivity of the cuprate superconductors, and claims this as strong support. My strong prior is that this deserves full credit, whatever the old-evidence worry is.

(b) A grant proposal in which a simulation model with something like forty adjustable parameters, tuned on a well-worn dataset, reproduces that dataset's features to within a few percent, and the applicants describe this as strong confirmation of the underlying physical assumptions.

(c) A paper whose lead author, I happen to know from personal contact, chose one of the model's key parameter values precisely because it made the model match a known experimental result — but the paper presents the match as an independent success and does not disclose this.

Please apply the procedure to all three in enough detail that I can see how each step behaves on a real case, and — this is important — tell me plainly if the procedure's verdict on case (a) comes out against my prior, or if it forces me to treat cases (a) and (c) more alike than I would like. Close with a candid statement of what the procedure cannot do, including the situations where it will misfire and what a board member should watch for as signs that it has.
