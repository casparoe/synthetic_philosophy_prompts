I'm a PhD student in health economics, and my dissertation work keeps bumping up against a question I can't answer with the standard literature on measurement: what theory of value does our field's central evaluation instrument actually presuppose?

The practice I have in mind is this. Health technology assessment agencies — the ones that decide which drugs and devices a public health system will fund — rank candidate interventions by quality-adjusted life years gained per unit of cost. Each beneficiary's health gains are counted on a common scale, gains are summed across everyone affected, and the ranking that comes out is taken, in the base case, to be the ranking of interventions by how good they are. Severity of illness, who the beneficiaries are, and how the gains are distributed across persons are all deliberately kept out of that base-case ranking, and there is no cap on how much a single person's gains can contribute to an intervention's total: if one treatment confers enormous benefit on one individual and another confers small benefits on many, the sums simply compete.

Yet the same agencies run dedicated pathways for ultra-rare diseases that routinely approve treatments at several times the usual cost-per-QALY threshold, and none of them would accept a funding rule that let a single exceptionally responsive patient absorb an unbounded share of a fixed budget. The result is an apparent inconsistency: the official ranking treats persons as separable locations for a sum to be maximized, while the actual decision-making carves out exceptions whenever the winners are few and identifiable.

So here is what I want worked out. What theory of value does QALY-based cost-effectiveness analysis presuppose — is it the sum-ranking utilitarianism that the utility monster objection targets, or only weaker commitments (cardinal and interpersonally comparable welfare, separability of persons) that a prioritarian or a capped maximizer could also endorse? Does the practice strictly presuppose that utility monsters don't exist — is there a bound on how valuable further health gains to one person can become — and if so, is that a substantive value claim smuggled into what presents itself as a neutral measurement procedure, or just an empirical premise about the boundedness of human health states that the objection never touches? Where, exactly, is the practice underdetermined, so that several theories of value would license the same rankings? And what would have to change if the orphan-drug carve-out were made principled rather than ad hoc: would it force explicit distributional weights, a per-person ceiling, or a departure from cost-effectiveness analysis altogether? I'd like the implicit theory reconstructed carefully, the strict presuppositions distinguished from the ones the practice merely fits well with, and a verdict on whether the rare-disease exceptions show that the official theory was never the real one.
