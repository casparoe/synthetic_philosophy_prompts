Write a dialogue between an intellectualist, in the tradition of Stanley and Williamson, who holds that knowing how to do something is a species of knowing that — specifically, propositional knowledge of a way of doing it — and an anti-intellectualist in the Rylean tradition, who holds that knowledge-how is an irreducible practical competence. Their conversation should turn on a hard case: a concert pianist who, after a severe bout of stage fright, freezes completely in performance, yet still teaches masterclasses, demonstrates technique at half tempo, and can articulate in precise propositional detail how to shape every phrase in the repertoire. Does she still know how to play the piano? The intellectualist should press the point that her retained propositional knowledge suffices for knowledge-how and that the stage fright amounts only to a loss of ability; the anti-intellectualist should resist, arguing that this conflates knowing how with merely knowing that, and should probe whether the pianist differs relevantly from someone who never had the skill at all. Where it helps, they may draw on the familiar phenomenon that explicit instruction tends to help novices while degrading the fluency of experts. Both characters should be equally competent, and each should respond directly to the other's strongest points rather than talk past one another.
