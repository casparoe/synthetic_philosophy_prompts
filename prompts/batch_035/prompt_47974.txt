Please explain the distinction between transient and permanent underdetermination of theory by evidence, as it figures in Kyle Stanford's treatment of the problem, and why only the permanent variety is generally taken to pose a serious threat to scientific realism. In doing so, please address how the distinction interacts with the Duhem–Quine thesis: does the holist claim that any theory can be preserved by adjusting auxiliary hypotheses actually entail that underdetermination is permanent, or does it merely show that underdetermination is often transient yet stubbornly long-lived in practice? I'm also interested in what the transient/permanent line itself is supposed to contrast — resolvability in principle by some possible evidence versus resolvability by evidence we can actually obtain — and whether that contrast can be drawn non-circularly. Throughout, please clearly mark what is settled among philosophers of science versus what remains genuinely contested, and in particular do not present as consensus the question of whether any real scientific case — e.g., a persisting empirically equivalent rival in physics — actually instantiates permanent underdetermination rather than merely illustrating it in principle.
