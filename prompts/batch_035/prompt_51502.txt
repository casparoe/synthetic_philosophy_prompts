I have been keeping a ledger of my reactions to contested markets, and I would like help extracting whatever principle, if any, is behind it. My verdicts so far. Paid plasma donation: fine. A tightly regulated system of compensating living kidney donors: fine, and I increasingly think prohibiting it is the cruelty. Buying concert tickets from a scalper at triple face value: fine. Paying a line-standing service to hold my place in the queue for Supreme Court oral arguments: fine. Selling my vote in an election: not fine. A corporation quietly taking out life insurance on thousands of its rank-and-file employees and naming itself the beneficiary: not fine, and viscerally so. Yet viatical settlements as they worked in the 1990s — investors buying the life-insurance policies of people dying of AIDS at a deep discount, keeping the premiums paid, and collecting at death — strike me as fine: the policyholder consented, got liquidity when it mattered, and the investor profiting from a death doesn't bother me there. I cannot say whether the difference is just consent, or the fact that the dying person shares in the benefit, or whether I am fooling myself. A hardware store tripling the price of generators the night after a hurricane: not fine. A wealthy country paying a poor one to accept hazardous waste: not fine. But carbon offsets — paying strangers to plant trees so that my flights come out carbon-neutral — feel fine to me, and I confess they have exactly the shape of the waste case: paying the less affluent to absorb the residue of my consumption. Well-compensated egg donation: fine. Paying a birth mother for her baby beyond medical expenses, even with enthusiastic consent on both sides: not fine, and I have never been able to make this comfortable with the kidney and egg verdicts. I have also heard it claimed that when places started paying for blood, the unpaid supply largely collapsed, which suggests my plasma verdict may rest on an empirical mistake I have not checked.

My tentative rule is something like: an exchange is repugnant when trading in it erodes either the good being traded or the institution surrounding it — vote selling corrodes democracy, baby selling corrodes something about parenthood, the corporate insurance corrodes the employment relation. Everything else, on this rule, is mere squeamishness, and my squeamishness should not be legislated. But I cannot see why, on my own rule, compensated egg donation does not corrode motherhood, or why the line-standing market does not corrode whatever the queue was supposed to do, or why gouging counts as erosion rather than rationing. And the rule seems to leave the grief market untouched, which is the case I keep circling: firms, in several countries, will supply professional mourners to attend your funeral or, presumably, your wedding — paid strangers performing sorrow, or joy, in your name. My gut rotates on it daily.

So here is what I am asking for. Give me the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated as precisely as you can, with each clause tied to the verdict that motivates it — the more so if different clauses pull in opposite directions on the same case. Tell me what your best candidate says about the professional mourners, about the case I flagged as resting on an uncheckable empirical claim, and about two or three further cases I have not considered but that you think would stress-test it: I would suggest paying a stranger to write my apologies for me, universities openly selling a fixed number of admission slots, and a market in handing in found wallets for a bounty. Finally, be honest about which of my verdicts the best principle cannot accommodate, and argue for which one should be surrendered rather than which clause should be bolted on to rescue it. I care more about being told that two of my verdicts are jointly unstable than about having all of them flattered.
