The exam question was:

"Describe Newcomb's problem. Then explain the dominance argument for taking both boxes and the expected-utility argument for taking only the opaque box. Explain why the two arguments conflict. Finally, state which of causal decision theory and evidential decision theory comes down on which side, and why."

Here is the student's answer, as written:

"Newcomb's problem: There is a predictor that is right about what you will decide, say 99% of the time. You are shown two boxes. Box A is transparent and visibly contains $1,000. Box B is opaque and contains either $1,000,000 or nothing. The predictor has already made its prediction and either put $1,000,000 in box B or left it empty, before you choose. You can take both boxes or only box B.

The dominance argument for two-boxing: whether B has the million is already settled and nothing you do now changes it. If B is full, taking both gets you $1,001,000 rather than $1,000,000. If B is empty, taking both gets you $1,000 rather than $0. Either way, taking both is better by $1,000, so taking both dominates.

The expected utility argument for one-boxing: if you two-box, the predictor probably predicted that, so B is probably empty, and your expected take is about $10,000. If you one-box, B is probably full, and your expected take is about $990,000. One-boxing wins by a mile.

Why they conflict: the dominance argument treats what is in box B as independent of your choice, while the expected-utility argument treats your choice as strong evidence about what is in box B. So the real question is whether that correlation matters for what it is rational to choose.

Causal decision theory says to one-box, because on CDT you do what causes the best outcome, and your decision is part of what caused the predictor to put the million in -- the predictor saw your choice coming, so in effect choosing one way caused the million to be there. Evidential decision theory also says to one-box, but for a different reason: on EDT, one-boxing causes the million to be in the box. So both theories agree here, and the two-boxers are really just people who refuse to accept any kind of backward causation. Nozick, who came up with the problem, agreed that one-boxing is obviously right.

Anyway, since the amount in the boxes is already fixed and doesn't depend on what you do now, at bottom the two options are equally rational and the dispute between them is mostly verbal."
