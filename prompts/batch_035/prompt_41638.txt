Been thinking about who should actually get a vote and my gut verdicts feel embarrassingly inconsistent, so help me figure out if there's any principle behind them. Here's where I land on cases: kids, no. Tourists passing through for a week, obviously no. Someone who's lived here 20 years on a work permit, pays taxes, kids in the local school: yes, I genuinely think it's wrong that they can't vote. My cousin who emigrated 25 years ago, never visits, barely reads the news from here but kept her citizenship: technically gets a vote and I guess that's fine, though it feels a bit off. Prisoners: I'm actually fine with them voting. Future generations who will live with the climate consequences of what we decide now: they should somehow count, even though they obviously can't cast ballots. People in the neighboring country whose economy gets hammered by our trade policy: no, they don't get a vote here, that's not even close. And it bugs me that 70-year-olds vote on 50-year infrastructure bonds that will barely touch them while 16-year-olds, who will live with the result, get nothing.

I toyed with "everyone subject to the laws gets a say," but tourists are subject to the laws while they're here and that clearly doesn't get them a vote, so that's dead on arrival. Is there one principle that captures all or nearly all of my verdicts — some theory about affected interests, or being subject to coercion, or having a stake in the ongoing project, or something I haven't thought of — and if not, which of my verdicts do I have to give up? I suspect the permanent resident and the affected foreigner are the pair that can't both survive. And tell me what the winning principle says about the case I keep flip-flopping on: should 16 and 17 year olds get to vote in a local referendum on something that'll reshape the town for the next 40 years?
