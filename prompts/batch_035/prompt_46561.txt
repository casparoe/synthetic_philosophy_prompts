I write novels, and I have come to suspect that promise-breaking is the sharpest instrument I own for moving a reader's sympathies: the same act — a character not doing what she said she would — sometimes costs her the reader's affection and sometimes costs her nothing, and I would like to know what principle governs the difference. I have verdicts on a set of cases, drawn from scenes I have written or watched readers respond to, and I want to know what, if anything, they add up to.

A woman promises her dying sister never to sell the family orchard; thirty years later, ruined, she sells it, and readers forgive her. A man promises, at gunpoint, to stay away from a town, and no reader thinks the promise binds him at all. A widow keeps a promise to her dead husband never to open the box of letters he left her, and when she opens it readers treat her as having betrayed him — even though he is dead, cannot be harmed, and will never know. By contrast, a character who solemnly vows to himself never to drink again and then drinks is pitied as weak, not condemned as a promise-breaker; the vow seems to bind no one. A mother makes her daughter promise never to put her in a home; keeping the promise grinds the daughter's life away, and readers feel the wrong lies with the mother for asking, not the daughter for complying or for chafing. And readers weigh the gravity of the asking: a promise made casually and forgotten is a flaw of memory; the same words after a formal undertaking are a stain.

Is there a single account of why promises bind — reliance, the practice, the promisee's autonomy, something else — that delivers all of these verdicts on reader sympathy? In particular, I want to know whether the widow and the self-vow case, which look structurally alike, can both be saved, or which has to go. And since I invent these scenes rather than find them, tell me whether the readers' sympathies track the real ground of promissory obligation or only a narrative convention about what keeping one's word signals about character — because if the two can come apart, that is exactly the gap a novelist like me works in. As a test, tell me what your account predicts for a case I have not listed: a character explicitly released from a promise by its living beneficiary, who keeps it anyway at real cost. Do readers admire her, or find her rigid, and should the philosopher agree with them?
