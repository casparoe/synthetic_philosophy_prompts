I am preparing a unit on causation by omission for my graduate seminar in metaphysics, and I want a decision-tree handout that sorts my students by their reactions to cases rather than by the labels they happen to have heard. My students know the counterfactual and causal-process literature on causation generally, but almost none of the literature specifically about absences, so the tree must work on someone who has never encountered terms like "quasi-causal" or "omissive overdetermination."

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: begin with the questions and the combinations of answers they generate, and only afterwards locate the actual positions within that space. I will fix the three questions that generate the space, since they are what I want the handout organized around:

(Q1) Are absences ever relata of the causal relation at all, or is causation strictly a relation among positive events?
(Q2) If absences can be relata, is what counts as the relevant absence fixed constitutively by normative facts — duties, expectations, salience — or fixed independently of them?
(Q3) If absences are relata, do they enter the very same causal relation as positive events do, or a distinct, derived relation?

The diagnostic nodes must be phrased as concrete cases a student can answer without knowing which leaf each answer feeds; no node may simply ask whether omissions are causes. The cases I want used, at minimum: (i) a hired gardener who never watered the plants, which then died, together with the variant in which the person who never watered them was the Queen of England, who was never hired and was nowhere near the garden — this case separates the student who says the Queen's non-watering is a cause no less than the gardener's, the student who says the hiring is what makes the gardener's omission the one that causes, and the student who says neither absence is a cause; (ii) a drowning child whom each of two lifeguards, acting alone, could have rescued, neither of whom acted — the omissive overdetermination case; (iii) a case of prevention by absence, such as a smoke alarm that never sounded and so never woke the sleeper. You may add cases if you need them, but these three must do real work in the tree.

The leaves: at least one representative of each of the following, named where the literature has a name and marked "unnamed" where it does not. (a) The counterfactualist who relativizes "the cause" to a salient comparison class and cheerfully takes the relata to include events that never occurred — the stance of Lewis's paper on absences in Causation and Counterfactuals, though the tree must not presuppose having read it; (b) the view that the identification of omissions is constitutively norm-dependent, so that causation by omission inherits the normativity — one horn of the dilemma pressed in McGrath's paper on causation by omission; (c) the view that the causal relation is abundant and cheap: nearly every absence of the right type in the neighborhood is a cause, and singling out "the" cause is a pragmatic matter of salience rather than a further fact; (d) the quasi-causal view, associated with Dowe, on which omissions do not cause but stand in a derived, contrast-generated relation to the outcome; (e) the contrastive or quaternary view, associated with Schaffer, on which the gardener case is analyzed as a contrast between what happened and a selected alternative rather than as an absence; (f) the commissive denialist, who holds that only positive events cause and recommends paraphrasing the ordinary omissive judgments away. You may add further live leaves, but these six must appear.

Finally, the handout should display rather than hide the dilemma that organizes this literature: for any view that answers Q2 with independence from norms, either omissions are not causes at all, or else countless absurd absences — the Queen's, every dead gardener's, the non-watering of anyone who has ever lived — are causes of the plants' death. State plainly, somewhere on the handout, which horn each leaf takes.

Please deliver the response in the following sections, in this order:

1. The logical space (about 150 words): the three questions and why the three cases pull them apart.
2. The tree (about half a page): nodes phrased as answerable questions; leaves labeled with position names or "unnamed."
3. Leaf glosses (about two sentences per leaf): the position, the author or tradition it corresponds to where one exists, and its single most awkward commitment.
4. Unoccupied combinations (about 150 words): which answer-combinations have no tenants, with a one-line diagnosis of each emptiness — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
5. Separation failures (about 100 words): any two leaves the tree cannot pull apart, and what further question would be needed to separate them.
6. The dilemma, displayed (about 100 words): which horn each leaf takes, stated so that a student can see it before choosing a path.
