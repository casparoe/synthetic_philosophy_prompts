I'm a philosopher of science working in the New Mechanism tradition — mechanisms as entities and activities organized so as to produce a phenomenon, componential analysis of mechanisms, the mutual manipulability test for when a part counts as a component rather than a mere correlate, interventionist accounts of constitutive relevance. I keep encountering machine learning researchers who speak of "mechanistic anomaly detection": roughly, checking whether a neural network is running the same mechanism on a new input that it ran on the inputs where its behavior was validated, where sameness is probed by ablating or patching components and seeing whether the output is preserved, and where they worry that the same mechanism can be realized by quite different parameter values, so that sameness cannot simply be read off the parts. Restate their debate over what counts as the same mechanism in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can: treat their components as entities, their ablations and patches as interventions, and their sameness claims as claims about which activities are being executed and how they are organized. Then give me the residue report: which parts translate cleanly, which come over only distorted, and which have no expression at all in the mechanist vocabulary — in particular, whether their notion of a mechanism holding on some inputs and not others, or holding only up to some tolerance, has any counterpart in mutual manipulability, and whether the relativity of "anomalous" to a reference distribution is something my framework simply lacks the resources to say. At the end, commit to a verdict: does the residue show that the mechanist framework needs genuine extension to capture what the researchers are tracking, or that their use of "mechanism" is loose enough that we should stop treating it as the same notion as ours? Don't just lay out the two options and leave the choice to me.
