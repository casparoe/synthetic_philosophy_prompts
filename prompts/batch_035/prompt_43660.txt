I am a novelist working on a long essay about a strange discovery I made while plotting a speculative novel, and I would be grateful for a critical assessment of the argument I intend to make.

The novel imagines a near future in which, though everyone currently alive lives out a normal lifespan, no children are conceived from a certain date onward — humanity simply winds down, and the last generation grows old together. While sketching it, I noticed that my characters' projects stopped making sense to them: an oncologist loses her appetite for her research, a couple abandons their plans to plant an orchard, a composer can no longer bring himself to finish a commission. It struck me that none of these reactions were about anyone's own death. Everyone gets a full life; no one dies early. What collapses is the assumption that there will be people after us — what the philosopher Samuel Scheffler calls the collective afterlife.

The essay I want to write makes two claims. First, that a great deal of what we find valuable in our own lives is conditioned, in a way we rarely notice, on our believing that other people will outlive us — not our loved ones in particular, but humanity in general. Cancer research, reforestation, the writing of novels meant to be read by strangers: all of these seem to presuppose an audience or a beneficiary that extends beyond our own cohort, and would lose their point for most of us even if we ourselves were guaranteed our threescore years and ten. Second, and more contentiously, that this dependence runs deeper than our fear of dying. My thought is that if forced to choose between (a) dying at the normal time with humanity continuing afterward, and (b) living somewhat longer but in a world where the apocalypse follows hard on my own death — with everyone else alive today still getting their full span — many people's deepest attachments would be better served by (a). If that is right, then the badness of human extinction is not exhausted by the deaths it involves; something distinctively future-directed in our present values is destroyed by it, and that something matters to us as much as, or more than, our own survival.

What I would like from you: an evaluation of whether the argument, as I have sketched it, is philosophically sound — in particular whether it is legitimate to move from the fact that our projects would deflate under an infertility scenario to the conclusion that our present attachments are actually structured around future people, rather than merely around the belief in them. I would also welcome comments on whether the second claim conflates the intensity of our caring with its object, and on whether the oncologist example is as decisive as I take it to be, since one might hold that her research is valuable now regardless of what anyone does with it later. Finally, please tell me whether the essay, as planned, would be interesting to a reader who knows no philosophy, and where it is most likely to lose them.
