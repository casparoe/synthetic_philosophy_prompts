I've been a Wikipedia editor for a few years now, and I'm also doing an MA with a focus on Indian epistemology, and something clicked recently that I can't stop thinking about: Wikipedia's sourcing rules are basically a pramāṇa theory in disguise, and a pretty radical one. The rules are that article content has to come from published reliable sources — the verifiability policy flatly says "Even if you are sure something is true, it must have been published in a reliable source before you can add it" — and the no-original-research policy excludes "any analysis or synthesis of published material that reaches or implies a conclusion not stated by the sources." So editors can't add what they perceived firsthand, can't add what they can prove by their own reasoning, and can't even combine two well-sourced claims into an obvious third one. In Nyāya terms, śabda is the only pramāṇa the encyclopedia admits: the editor's own pratyakṣa and anumāna are banned as sources of content, which is a weirder position than anything the classical schools took.

What I'd like you to do is reconstruct the implicit epistemology of this practice, using the pramāṇa framework as the lens, and tell me whether it's coherent. Here are the places where I keep snagging. Nyāya says testimony is a basic pramāṇa that yields true cognition when it comes from an āpta — someone who actually knows the truth and sincerely intends to convey it. But Wikipedia's "reliable source" is defined by process, not truth: the policy says to base articles on sources "with a reputation for fact-checking and accuracy," and the encyclopedia is perfectly happy carrying a claim that turns out to be false, as long as it was well-sourced. So is the implicit view actually the reductionist one, the line the Buddhists pushed, where testimony is just inference from marks of reliability (in this case institutional ones, like peer review and editorial oversight)? Or is the reliance on "reputation" — something the community itself establishes through discussion, which is to say through yet more testimony — an admission that the reduction can't close the regress, which is basically the later Nyāya argument for treating śabda as basic? And then there's the disagreement problem: the policy says that when reliable sources disagree, editors should present what the various sources say, giving each side its due weight, and walk away without adjudicating. But on the classical picture, genuine pramāṇas can't yield contradictory results — if two testimonies conflict, at most one came from a genuine āpta. Wikipedia instead institutionalizes the conflict and resolves it by majority weight, which looks like a majoritarian epistemology that no classical school would have accepted.

So, three things. First, reconstruct what the practice strictly presupposes about testimony and knowledge, as opposed to what it merely fits well with — I want to know where the practice is underdetermined, such that several theories of śabda would license it, and where it actually rules rivals out. Second, say whether the resulting position is coherent, especially the combination of "only testimony counts" with "testimony doesn't have to be true" — is that a stable position, a novel fourth school, or a mess? Third, tell me what would actually have to change in the rules if you rebuilt them on (a) the strict Nyāya view, (b) the Buddhist-style reductionist view, and (c) the Cārvāka view that testimony is no pramāṇa at all — which edits would be allowed, which policies would have to go, and would the encyclopedia still work?
