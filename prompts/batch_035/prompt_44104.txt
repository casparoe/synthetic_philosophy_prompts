I direct the policy review unit of the immigration directorate of a small, wealthy democracy. Our government accepts that freedom of movement carries real moral weight and does not want to defend border controls as a bare entitlement, but it also rejects open borders and intends to keep a selective admissions system. The minister has asked my unit for a decision procedure that front-line officers can actually use to determine whether a given exclusion — a general rule, a category bar, or an individual refusal — is justified. What we need is not a treatise but something operational, and that is where the philosophical debate has to be compressed without being betrayed.

The procedure should be grounded in the actual structure of the open-borders debate: the associational defense of the state's right to exclude, on which the state is a cooperative venture that may control membership but only within limits — its duties to refugees, its duty not to worsen the position of outsiders, the claims of people already present and the special claims of long-term residents; the open-borders position, on which birthplace is morally arbitrary in something like the way feudal birthright privilege was, and movement counts as a basic liberty that presumptively may not be restricted; and the intermediate positions that would permit exclusion only under sufficiency or legitimacy conditions. Your task is to turn this into something a generalist civil servant with no philosophy training can run on a written case file in about an hour.

Please provide the following:

1. An ordered set of questions the officer asks about any proposed exclusion, with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and the points at which the procedure terminates — either in a verdict that the exclusion is permissible or in a verdict that it is not.

2. An explicit allocation of the burden of proof. Our government concedes that movement matters morally, so the procedure should say who must show what, and to what standard, before exclusion goes through.

3. The procedure's known failure modes: where it will tend to rubber-stamp whatever the government of the day prefers, where it smuggles in contested philosophical assumptions as if they were neutral administrative tests, and where the compression from theory to checklist loses something the theory itself says is essential.

4. Worked applications to three cases: (a) an annual cap on low-skilled work visas justified as protection of domestic wages; (b) refusal of entry to the spouse of a long-term resident on income-maintenance grounds; (c) an asylum claim by someone fleeing economic collapse rather than targeted persecution. Include at least one case in which the procedure yields a verdict you expect the minister will dislike, and explain why it yields that verdict anyway.

5. A closing statement of what the procedure cannot do: the questions it deliberately brackets — including the prior question of whether selective borders are legitimate at all — and what would have to change, philosophically or factually, for its outputs to come out differently.

Two practical constraints: wherever a step requires evidence, as with whether a visa cap actually protects wages, specify what kind of evidence would suffice and what the officer should do when it is unavailable; and mark clearly the steps at which the question stops being one the procedure can settle and must be escalated to the minister as a decision of political values.
