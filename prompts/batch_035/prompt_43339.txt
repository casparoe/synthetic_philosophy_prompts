I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums since the Sequences era, and one idea from that world I've never seen made practical is moral trade — Toby Ord's observation that agents with deeply incompatible moral views can sometimes make each other strictly better off by trading concessions rather than splitting the difference, the way nations with opposed interests still manage to sign arms-control treaties. The situation I now find myself in is exactly the one that idea was written for, and I want it operationalized.

I sit on the board of a mid-sized grantmaking foundation. Our seven trustees disagree at the level of values, not facts, and no amount of shared evidence will reconcile us: two are total utilitarians who care almost exclusively about the far future; one holds a strict Kantian line and objects to funding anything that treats persons merely as means; two prioritize near-term human welfare; and one is an animal welfarist who regards our human-focused portfolio as a slow-motion moral catastrophe. Currently we compromise — every decision is watered down to what everyone can tolerate, and I suspect we are leaving enormous value on the floor, because a compromise is not the same thing as a trade. A trade would let the utilitarians get their existential-risk grants funded fully, in exchange for something the animal welfarist values more than the cost to us — instead of everything being funded at 60% intensity.

Please write a procedure the board can actually run, twice a year at our grant-allocation meetings, under these constraints: no trustee has philosophy training beyond me, each trustee holds a veto on grants touching their core concern, the whole thing has to fit into one afternoon, and the board's charter requires that our decisions be defensible to external auditors, so the procedure has to produce a rationale, not just an outcome. The procedure should cover, in order: how to identify which of our disagreements are tradable and which are constitutive (a Kantian who thinks trading away a side-constraint is itself impermissible cannot be a counterparty, and the procedure needs to discover that early rather than waste an afternoon); how to elicit from each trustee something like an intensity ordering over outcomes without demanding they fake a common currency; how to structure the trades themselves — bundling grants across rounds, side payments in budget share, commitments about future cycles, and a sunset clause so no trustee is locked into a bad deal as their views shift; and where the procedure should give up on trade and fall back to what Bostrom called the moral parliament — negotiated proportional influence over a whole portfolio, rather than deals.

It also has to handle the obvious failure modes: trustees strategically exaggerating intensity to extract better terms; trades that impose costs on parties not at the table (grantees, animals, future people) and whether third-party harm should block a deal or just count as one weight in it; and the degenerate case where the procedure silently collapses into ordinary logrolling and everyone calls it philosophy.

Then run the procedure on three live cases from our docket. (1) The utilitarians want to shift thirty percent of the budget toward existential-risk research; the near-term humanists want to launch a maternal-health program; both sides say the other's priority can wait. (2) The Kantian objects to a proposed grant to an effective charity that relies on emotional advertising he regards as manipulative, while every other trustee ranks it first. (3) The animal welfarist offers to vote with the utilitarians on the X-risk portfolio, in exchange for phasing out a line of grants to a research institute that conducts animal testing the other trustees consider scientifically valuable. In at least one of these, I expect the procedure to reach a conclusion I won't like — I'm one of the utilitarians — and I want it run honestly anyway.

Finally, be explicit about the procedure's honest limits: which steps are genuine moral philosophy in disguise, which are just mechanics, where it will systematically misfire (for instance, if trustees' stated intensities are unreliable), and where it cannot settle the prior question some of our views may raise — whether trading away a moral conviction is itself something a view can permit at all, or whether it is the kind of thing that, once you treat it as currency, you no longer hold.
