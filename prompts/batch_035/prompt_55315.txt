I'm a retired chemist who's gotten into philosophy and decision theory since I hung up the lab coat, and my grandson (ML grad student) walked me into an argument about Aumann's agreement theorem that I can't find my way out of. (1) Aumann proved that two rational agents who start from the same prior, and whose posterior probabilities for some claim are common knowledge between them, cannot disagree — they must end up assigning the same probability. (2) If I tell you my probability and you tell me yours, then those probabilities are common knowledge. (3) So after one exchange of estimates we must agree — and if not after one, then surely after a few rounds of "ok, here's my updated number, now what's yours." (4) So any disagreement that persists after the parties have actually talked it through — climate scientists who've read each other's papers for twenty years, two doctors who still disagree after a consult — has to be irrationality or dishonesty. (5) That seems crazy: half the good papers in my old field were written by people who knew each other's views inside out and still disagreed, and most of them were neither stupid nor lying. So either there's a hole somewhere in (1)-(3), or a lot more disagreement is irrational than I'm comfortable admitting. My grandson says the conclusion just is the theorem and rational people literally cannot agree to disagree, that's just the math. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? I'd much rather keep (5) than bite the bullet on (4), but I want to know exactly which step I'm rejecting and what it costs me. And please tell me how confident you actually are in the diagnosis and which part you'd be least sure of — I'd like to know where to push back on you.
