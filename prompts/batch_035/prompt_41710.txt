I'm preparing a graduate seminar unit on what makes pain bad for the one who feels it, and before we read any theories I want the specification those theories will be graded against — the requirements themselves, not a survey. Write the desiderata: what any adequate account of pain's badness has to deliver, which cases it must get right, which distinctions it must respect, and which empirical results it cannot contradict.

The anchor phenomenon is pain asymbolia. Subjects with asymbolia report genuine pain — they localize it, rate its intensity accurately, and show normal withdrawal reflexes — yet they are indifferent to it: they do not avoid the stimulus, do not mind the pain, and sometimes joke about it. The syndrome is described after cingulate lesions and in some psychiatric reports; related is the morphine-style dissociation, where patients say the pain is still present in full intensity but no longer bothers them. A theory of pain's badness owes us a principled verdict on whether such pain is bad, and if so in what sense.

The cases I want on the list, at minimum:

1. Asymbolic and dissociated pain, as above — and a variant I care about: the reflective asymbolic patient who, after considering the matter, judges that her pains are bad for her and requests analgesia she admits she never feels any need for. Does badness answer to felt aversion, to reflective endorsement, or to neither?
2. Unremembered and past pain — a painful episode in a patient who dies during it, or whose memory of it is later erased. It seems bad that it happened; explain what makes it bad if no attitude toward it survives.
3. Welcomed pain — athletic exhaustion, rites of passage, deliberately sought pain in masochistic contexts. Say whether endorsement suspends the badness or whether such pain is bad in a way that is merely overridden.
4. Beings with only part of the apparatus. Whatever the theory says about asymbolic pain in a competent adult, it presumably says the same about sensory pain without affect in a fish or a decorticate patient. Make the commitment explicit and say whether you consider it a cost.

The distinctions the theory must not collapse include at least: pain versus suffering; sensory intensity versus badness; the sensory-discriminative component versus the affective-motivational one (these demonstrably come apart, so no theory may treat the pain matrix as a unity); and being bad versus being disvalued. The results it must be consistent with include the double dissociations above and whatever else you think belongs.

Mark each desideratum as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp, with the case or consideration behind each. Then flag any subset you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable — I have in mind especially the tension between holding that badness supervenes on the phenomenal character of the pain alone, and holding that asymbolic pain is not bad or is less bad. If both cannot be kept, say which should go and what accepting the loss costs (dropping supervenience makes pain's badness attitude-dependent and puts pressure on hedonist accounts of welfare; keeping it means a pain nobody minds can be as bad as agony). Finally, tell me which single requirement on your list does the most to exclude otherwise popular theories. A brief scorecard for evaluativism, imperativism, and a simple hedonism of painful feeling is welcome but secondary — keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases. A table is fine.
