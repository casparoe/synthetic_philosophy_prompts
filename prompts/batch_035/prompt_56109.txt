I teach an upper-level seminar that sits between philosophy of mind and philosophy of biology, and next week I have a 90-minute session with 14 students on the idea that a "why" question can have a true, substantive answer even when nobody ever intended, planned, believed, or represented anything — the kind of rationale that explains and predicts a trait, an arrangement, or a custom without any reasoner behind it. My worry is that this idea is nearly inert when presented on the page: students nod at it, file it under "evolution," and move on. I want an in-class activity where they run into the phenomenon themselves, with the labels withheld until the very end, so I'm asking you to design the session rather than summarize the literature for me.

Here is the skeleton I've been imagining, which you should feel free to revise, tighten, or replace wholesale if you see something better. Split the class: about half are "makers" and half are "readers," swapping roles if time allows. Each maker draws a card telling them either to craft an artifact deliberately — a doodle, a short four-note hummed phrase, an arrangement of paperclips and sticky notes on a desk, with a function they must keep secret — or to generate an artifact blindly, using a supplied randomizing procedure (dice, a shuffled deck, drawing Scrabble tiles from a bag) with no purpose permitted. The artifacts are then displayed anonymously, and the readers' job is to do what naturalists and archaeologists do: assign each item a function, defend their reading to the group, and vote on which rationales they'd stake a prediction on. The reveal then shows which items had a designer and which were blind, and the interesting residue is the items the readers explained best that nobody made for any reason at all.

Please give me the full runnable session: rules and materials, a minute-by-minute timeline for 90 minutes, a scoring scheme that doesn't involve real money (I've used candy or bonus points before), and instructions for both phases that are complete enough that a TA could run it cold. Then the debrief: map specific moments in the activity onto the philosophical distinction, and give me the moves for handling the two objections I expect sharp students to raise — that the readers' convincing rationales for blind items are just confabulation with no grip on truth, and, conversely, that my randomization procedure can't literally guarantee zero reasoners anywhere in the causal chain, since a human shuffled the deck. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I expect, for instance, that some "blind" makers will cheat by sneaking in a pattern, or that readers will refuse to play along for hummed phrases) and how I should handle each. Finally, be honest about what no classroom version of this can reproduce: in the real cases I care about, the rationale is validated by the world over long stretches of time rather than by a room of peers, and I suspect that staging the validation with human judges quietly smuggles reasoners back into the picture at exactly the point where I need them absent — so tell me which part of that gap I must fill by talking, and frame the debrief so it doesn't undo what the game just accomplished.
