The debate over knowing how and knowing that seems to me to have stalled, and I'd like a diagnosis of it rather than a recap. On one side there is Ryle, who in The Concept of Mind (1949) treated knowing how to do something — swimming, joking, being polite — as a distinct capacity, not a body of propositions, on pain of an infinite regress of acts of contemplating propositions before any act. On the other side there are the intellectualists, revived by Stanley and Williamson in 2001, who argue that ascriptions of knowing-how have the same logical form as ascriptions of knowing-that: to know how to swim is to know an answer to the question of how one swims. Twenty years of exchanges later, the positions look frozen. The intellectualists keep offering semantic arguments about embedded questions, and the anti-intellectualists keep offering the same counterexamples — the person who has read every manual on cycling but cannot stay upright on a bicycle, or the expert athlete who cannot articulate what she does — and each side treats the other's contribution as beside the point. Is this a substantive disagreement about the nature of skill, a verbal one about the logical form of a class of sentences, or two research programs answering different questions (semantics of ascriptions versus metaphysics of abilities) under a single heading? My working hypothesis is that the counterexamples turn on a difference between what we are disposed to say about a person and what the ascription sentence strictly entails, so that both sides are right about their own question and wrong to think the other is competing. Please assess that hypothesis, say what kind of disagreement this really is, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress here — a new type of case, a distinction, a result about the relationship between the two readings — or whether we should expect none, and why. Please reply in American English.
