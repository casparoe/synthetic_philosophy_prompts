In my philosophy elective we discussed the brain-in-a-vat scenario, and then our teacher mentioned that Hilary Putnam, in his 1981 book Reason, Truth and History, argued that a brain in a vat could never truthfully think or say that it is a brain in a vat, because its words would refer only to things in its simulated environment. Here is what confuses me: if the sentence is necessarily false whenever a brain in a vat utters it, and we can all see that we truthfully say things all the time, doesn't it follow that none of us is a brain in a vat? And if Putnam proved that, why do philosophers still treat skepticism about the external world as a live issue rather than a solved one? It seems to me that a tricky point about word meanings has been mistaken for a proof about what the world is like, but I can't quite pin down where the mistake is. Please explain where my reasoning goes wrong, and along the way, clearly separate what is settled from what remains genuinely contested among philosophers — for instance, about how strong Putnam's argument is and what it actually establishes — so that I don't mistake one side of a debate for the consensus view.
