The argument in my reading group about Arendt on political lying has been going in circles for months and I want your help figuring out what kind of disagreement we're actually having, because I'm starting to think it's not about facts at all.

Here's the stall. On one side we have the Arendt groupies (I'm one of them, sort of). We read "Truth and Politics" and "Lying in Politics" (the Pentagon Papers stuff) as making a structural claim: the traditional liar only swaps out facts here and there and leaves the world of common sense mostly intact, so the old lie and the old truth still share the same real world; that shared world is what makes it possible to argue and to check people. But modern organized lying, when it becomes image-making, shifts the fight -- now it's not lie versus truth but one internally consistent pseudo-world versus another, each sealed off, and the old standards of comparison don't apply anymore, because a whole fabricated reality can hang together and be self-consistent. The disturbing thing for us isn't that politicians lie, it's that lying at scale dissolves the shared world that politics needs to be politics at all, and that people end up unable to recognize that freedom is at stake. Plus the point that truth is powerless in politics by itself and has to be defended by liars of a kind, the intellectuals, if nobody stands between the citizens and the spin machines. When we put it that way, the other side just shrugs.

The other side (two political scientists and one skeptical historian) says: this is just grand theory dressed up as diagnosis. Politicians and governments have always curated reality; the machinery of PR and propaganda is continuous with older spin, only with better tools, so there's no sharp line where the "traditional liar" becomes an "image-maker" -- it's a difference of degree, and drawing a conceptual line there is doing no real work. They also think the people-who-can't-recognize-freedom-at-stake observation is unfalsifiable romanticism, and that Arendt's world of appearances talk, whatever its merits for understanding action, doesn't actually generate any criteria for telling normal democratic bullshitting (which a functioning democracy both tolerates and survives) from world-destroying lying. So: what would even count as evidence that organized lying had crossed some threshold? If we can't say, they say, the view explains everything after the fact and nothing in advance.

Both sides know the texts well, nobody's misreading anybody as far as I can tell, and it's deadlocked anyway.

My hypothesis, offered as just a hypothesis: it's really a disagreement about what politics fundamentally is -- we're presupposing that politics is a fragile achievement that exists only where a shared factual world is maintained, so world-dissolving lies are a threat to politics as such, and the skeptics are presupposing that politics is basically institutional machinery for managing disagreement, which doesn't need deep factual consensus, just workable channels and rough accountability. On that reading we're not disagreeing about lying at all; we're disagreeing about a background conception, and each side's "evidence" never touches the other. But I'm honestly not sure, because the skeptics do seem to be making a methodological point too (no criteria, no thresholds), which is a different kind of complaint than just having a different conception of politics.

So what I want from you: (1) a diagnosis of the disagreement -- is it verbal, is it competing background conceptions, is it a hidden empirical question about historical continuity that neither side is equipped to settle, or some mix, and say which parts are which; (2) support the diagnosis with what each side actually claims (including the parts I sketched above), not with a survey of Arendt exegesis in general; and (3) given that diagnosis, what would count as progress here -- a distinction someone could draw, a kind of case study or comparison that could be specified, a reformulation of the question -- or an argument that no progress should be expected because the question itself is ill-posed. If you think my hypothesis is wrong or only partly right, say so directly, don't paper over it.
