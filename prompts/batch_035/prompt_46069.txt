A friend of mine spent last term working through Moore's Principia Ethica and now hands down verdicts on hypotheticals like a judge. So far she has ruled: that a canyon of extraordinary beauty on a lifeless planet, seen by no mind ever, has no intrinsic value at all; that a collector moved to genuine ecstasy by a mediocre daub he sincerely takes for a lost masterpiece is in a state of no positive value, perhaps negative; that the mutual affection of two friends, each tracking the other's real virtues of wit, courage, and kindness, is as valuable as anything gets — and that its value exceeds the sum of the values of its parts taken separately, a point on which she is emphatic; and that a tender online friendship with someone who turns out to be a crew of scammers impersonating a person who never existed is worthless, maybe worse than nothing. She has not yet ruled on our group's standing dispute. The case: someone stands before a genuinely sublime painting, moved exactly as an ideal viewer would be, while falsely believing it a skillful forgery; and, as a variant, two real friends each admiring the other's real virtues, whose friendship originated in a mistake long since corrected — each initially took the other for someone else. I can see at least two rules that fit her track record equally well: on one, intrinsic value belongs to the organic whole of a genuinely valuable object plus fitting appreciation, and the subject's surrounding beliefs are irrelevant so long as the object is real and the appreciation fits; on the other, the whole whose value is in question includes those beliefs, so that delusion anywhere in it contaminates it. Given her verdicts so far, what should she say about the painting and about the friendship? Which rule is she most likely running, are there other rules that fit the record as well, and what further case would best tell the candidates apart?
