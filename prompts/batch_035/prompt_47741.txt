I'm a second-year graduate student applying for a one-year dissertation fellowship, and I need help turning a half-formed idea into a two-page research proposal. The idea concerns the discursive dilemma in judgment aggregation. In the textbook version, a panel votes on two premises and on a conclusion entailed by the conjunction of the premises; proposition-by-proposition majority voting can make the group accept each premise by majority while rejecting the conclusion by majority, so the collective judgment set is inconsistent. The standard responses are familiar: aggregate the conclusion directly instead of the premises, drop one of the aggregation requirements (universality, anonymity, systematicity), or restrict the agenda of propositions on which the group rules. My suspicion is that this whole dialectic is shaped by an unstated assumption that I rarely see explicitly defended: that the group's output must be a binary acceptance or rejection of each proposition. My half-formed thought is that the paradox's sting might change — I genuinely don't know whether it would dissolve, relocate, or get worse — if individuals instead submitted graded confidences on the premises and the group needed a single graded confidence on the conclusion. In that setting, the choice between pooling the premise confidences and letting logic carry them to the conclusion, versus pooling confidences on the conclusion directly, looks like it might be a graded analogue of the premise-based versus conclusion-based choice. But I can't tell whether the inconsistency that drives the original dilemma even has a counterpart when nothing has to be flat-out accepted or rejected.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher working at the intersection of social choice theory and formal epistemology, the kind of person who has refereed many fellowship proposals and knows what makes a question tractable rather than merely open. Begin with a short brainstorming section before drafting, and in particular help me decide which of several candidate formulations of the research question is the most determinate and answerable one, since that choice will make or break the proposal. The proposal itself should include: a precise statement of the question; an explanation of why the existing positions leave it open; the method, broken down roughly by quarter, saying what I would actually do (axiomatizing the graded case, working through small examples, checking which of the classic impossibility results transfer and which don't); what a positive result and a negative result would each look like; and an honest statement of what would count as the project failing — including the possibility that the graded version turns out to be a notational variant of the binary one, which would be a finding, but perhaps not one a fellowship committee would reward. Do not include a bibliography or citations; I will fill those in myself, so refer to positions and arguments in plain descriptive terms.

Two further requests. First, write everything in American English. Second, close with a brief paragraph stating how confident you are in the main claims you make on my behalf — especially the claim that the graded question really is open rather than already settled somewhere I haven't looked — and single out the single point you are least sure of, so I know exactly what to verify before I submit.
