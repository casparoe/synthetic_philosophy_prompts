I write alternate history novels — three published, shopping a fourth — and something about my own genre has been bugging me while I plot the new book. The whole apparatus runs on what we call the "point of divergence": one clean, datable change (Lee's Special Order 191 actually reaches McClellan, or Burgoyne wins at Saratoga the way Robert Sobel has it in For Want of a Nail), and then the book just traces consequences downstream from that. Reviewers treat a well-chosen divergence as a mark of craft, and readers accept it without blinking. But sitting with my outline last week I realized I have no idea what theory of history I'm implicitly signing up for every time I do this, and the genre's own conventions seem to point in opposite directions.

So heres the question: what does the practice of building a novel around a point of divergence actually presuppose about how history works? On its face it seems to commit you to contingency — that small events can cascade into huge ones, that individuals and accidents matter. But half the classics do the opposite: the divergence lands and then history bends back, the same kind of wars happen a few years late, roughly the same ideologies win, and nobody blinks at that either. There's reader-side stuff too: you can avert the Holocaust in chapter one and that's fine, but if the butterfly effect gets too sprawling people complain about "alien space bats," and if you keep too much the same people call the book lazy. Some changes feel like cheating, some retentions feel cheap, and I couldnt tell you which is which or why.

Reconstruct for me the implicit philosophy of history the genre is working with — contingency vs. structural forces, great men vs. deep causes, whatever the real options are. Then tell me whether the craft rules actually all presuppose the same thing, or whether they pull against each other, and where the whole practice is underdetermined enough that several theories of history would license the same novel. And the practical bit: if I plotted book four from an honestly structuralist view — divergence changes the flavor of events but not their broad shape — what would I have to do differently in terms of plot structure, character, and the promises chapter one makes to the reader? Keep it at the level of arguments, no reading lists or citations, I know the shelves.
