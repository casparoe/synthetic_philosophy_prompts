I'm a first-time graduate TA grading midterms for a large introductory course on philosophy of religion, and I've run into an answer I keep going back and forth on. The exam question, written by the course instructor, read as follows: "We have distinguished two families of cosmological argument: the kalam argument, defended in its contemporary form by William Lane Craig, and contingency versions such as Leibniz's. (a) State the premise or premises distinctive of each family, that is, what each argument needs that the other does not. (b) Explain why the distinction matters: describe at least one objection that one family faces in a way the other does not. (c) Explain one serious objection to each argument and assess how serious it is." This particular student's answer, transcribed in full, was: "The kalam argument's distinctive premise is that whatever begins to exist has a cause, combined with the claim that the universe began to exist. Since space and time themselves began with the universe, the cause must be outside space and time, immensely powerful, and plausibly personal. Its need for a temporal beginning is also its greatest strength: Craig defends it on two philosophical grounds, that an actual infinite cannot exist and that an infinite series of past events could not have been formed by successive addition, and Big Bang cosmology provides empirical confirmation. The contingency argument doesn't need the universe to have begun. Aquinas and Leibniz both defended versions of it, resting on the principle of sufficient reason: Leibniz asked why there is something rather than nothing, and the answer must be a sufficient reason for the existence of everything. Everything in the universe is contingent, that is, could have failed to exist, so the universe as a whole is contingent, and its sufficient reason must therefore lie in a necessary being, one that exists by causing itself, what Aquinas called a causa sui. This is where Aquinas's Third Way fits in: it is best read as a kalam-style argument, since it holds that every contingent thing needs a cause, that an infinite chain of causes is impossible, and hence that the series of contingent things must have started at some first point with a necessary cause. As for why the distinction matters: if the universe turned out to be eternal, the kalam argument would collapse while the contingency argument would be untouched. Conversely, if the principle of sufficient reason is rejected, the contingency argument fails, while the kalam can still stand on its causal premise, which Craig notes is constantly confirmed in experience and is only doubted, for philosophical reasons, when applied to the universe. Hume's objection that the causal principle is not analytic, since we can coherently conceive of something coming into being without a cause, is therefore a serious worry for kalam. The classic objection to contingency is Russell's in his radio debate with Copleston: it is a mistake to treat the universe as a whole as one more thing needing an explanation, since explanations must come to an end somewhere. I think this is the more serious objection, because it attacks the very idea of a sufficient reason for the totality, whereas Hume's point only shows the causal premise isn't a necessary truth, and Craig can reply that we are still entitled to it as a well-confirmed empirical principle. Neither objection is fatal: for contingency, one can reply that whatever could have failed to exist is the kind of thing whose existence calls for explanation, so the demand applies even if explanations normally come to an end."
