Last night a friend and I argued until well past midnight about the Buddhist doctrine of no-self, and the whole argument turned on one analogy that I can't stop chewing on, so I want a careful audit of what it can actually carry.

Some background on the argument. I had claimed that anatta — the claim that there is no persistent self, only a stream of psycho-physical events — is really just what a Western reductionist about persons says, once you strip off the monastic packaging. My friend disagreed for a reason I hadn't encountered before: he said the standard Buddhist move isn't a reductionist argument at all but an analogy, and analogies are not arguments. His case: in the Questions of King Milinda, when the king presses the monk Nagasena on who, exactly, he is if no self can be found among his parts, Nagasena answers with the king's own chariot. The chariot is analyzed — axle, wheels, frame, yoke — and no chariot turns out to be found among the parts, or apart from them, or as the pile of parts when the chariot is disassembled; the word is just a designation for those parts arranged and functioning in a certain way. And so, the monk says, it is with Nagasena. My friend's point was that this is the move people actually use in practice: not premises marshaled toward a conclusion, but a single comparison carrying the entire doctrine. If you accept the chariot, you are meant to accept the conclusion about yourself, and the analogy does all the work.

Where it got interesting is that we disagreed about what work it does. Going in, I thought the analogy licenses more than the doctrine can support. Everything the chariot shows is that a composite object is not some further thing over and above its parts arranged properly. But a fairly conservative metaphysician of persons could grant exactly that about the body, the brain, even the whole aggregate bundle, and still insist that no-self claims something stronger: that the stream of experience is not even conventionally unified, that the felt unity of a point of view is an illusion rather than a real (if derived) feature. The chariot is merely not further; the Buddhist self is supposed to be not even that. My friend thought the analogy fails in the opposite direction: a chariot is a designed artifact, built for a purpose from without, its identity conditions fixed by the maker's intent, whereas whatever unity a person has is generated from within, by memory, intention, and causal continuity of experience — precisely the machinery the chariot lacks. On his view, every disanalogy between chariots and persons cuts against the conclusion the analogy was recruited to support. And he had a third move I also want evaluated: he speculated that the analogy was never intended as a proof at all, but as a teaching device pitched at a king — something to shift attention from who is the sufferer to what is going on in there — and that auditing it as an argument is a category error, though he couldn't say whether this rescues the doctrine or just concedes that it has no argument.

So: acting as a professional philosopher with real expertise in both personal identity and Buddhist philosophy, and thinking carefully rather than reaching for the standard textbook treatment, please audit the chariot analogy. To be concrete:

1. Give me the correspondence table. What maps onto what between chariot and person — parts, arrangement and function, the word for the chariot versus the name Nagasena, the builder, the user, the persistence of a chariot through repair — and, just as important, the items the doctrine needs in the person-side of the mapping that the chariot side quietly leaves unmapped.

2. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended use from the ones that don't. Which differences between chariots and persons — designed versus undesigned, no phenomenology versus felt unity, a spatial composite versus a temporal stream, no rebirth versus rebirth — actually threaten the inference to no-self, and which are harmless?

3. Tell me what the analogy smuggles in. In particular, does the phrase mere designation carry assumptions about conventional versus ultimate truth that are doing real work, and is the conclusion the analogy is used to license — the self is no more than a useful label on a stream — actually different from the reductionist position I originally claimed the doctrine was equivalent to? That, after all, is what we started arguing about.

4. Give me two verdicts, kept distinct. First: does the no-self conclusion survive with the analogy discarded, on some other footing you should spell out without quietly reintroducing the comparison? Second: is my friend right that the chariot was a redirect rather than a proof, and if so, does that reading strengthen or weaken the case that the doctrine is just reductionism in robes?

5. If there is a better analogy — one that carries at least as much of the doctrine with fewer disanalogies — propose it and show the work: what it fixes, what it still cannot carry. The obvious candidates (a river, a flame passed from candle to candle, a song played twice) each get you something; I want to know which gets furthest and why, including whether it is telling that the tradition itself seems to prefer the flame for rebirth and the chariot for the person, rather than one analogy for both.

Please brainstorm before committing to a verdict, be willing to say the disagreement between my friend and me is partly verbal if it is, and do not pad the answer with a survey of Buddhist doctrine — we both know the basics; the object here is the analogy and what it can and cannot carry.
