I teach a second-year philosophy of mind course, and next week I have a fifty-minute section on epiphenomenalism that I want to build toward the paradox of phenomenal judgment — the worry that if phenomenal properties make no causal difference, then our beliefs and assertions about phenomenal properties can't have been caused by the properties they are about, so the epiphenomenalist seems saddled with a suspicious coincidence in which all our phenomenal judgments track qualia they never interact with. I want students to leave genuinely puzzled by this rather than merely aware that it exists. Don't explain the view to me; plan how I should explain it. Tell me what has to come first — e.g., whether students need causal closure of the physical and a distinction between property dualism and interactionist dualism on the table before the zombies enter, or whether the zombie thought experiment should do that work itself — and flag where the sequence goes wrong if I get the order backwards. Recommend the one example that should carry the whole session (a scientist calmly publishing papers about her own qualia? a world running in parallel with an inverted-qualia duplicate?), say what it must have and lack, and argue for or against zombies as the carrier given how quickly students treat them as refuted rather than understood. Map the misconceptions I should expect — that epiphenomenalism is just a fancy name for any dualism; that causal closure straightforwardly implies physicalism; that the paradox is a one-line knockout argument, missing the epiphenomenalist's available replies via common causes or harmony-style explanations of why judgments and qualia co-vary — and where exactly in the sequence to head each one off. Identify what I should openly refuse to explain at this level (the physics behind causal closure? the details of psychophysical laws?) and how to mark it as deferred rather than dodged. Finally, give me one question I can pose at the end whose answer would show me whether students have understood the paradox itself and not just learned to restate it — something along the lines of distinguishing, in their own words, a world containing epiphenomenal qualia from a world containing none, as judged from the inside.
