He's active in his neighborhood association and fought a rezoning that would have allowed mid-rise apartments near our transit station; I keep insisting the shortage is a supply problem and that opposing new homes is free-riding on scarcity. We've recently realized we mostly argue past each other: he's talking about who has standing to decide what happens to a place people have invested decades in, and I'm talking about what happens to prices when supply is choked off. Rather than keep circling, we want to try an adversarial collaboration, and I'd like you to draft the joint statement for us to work from.

Please write the document a committed YIMBY (broad claims of newcomers and renters to live where opportunity is, skepticism of incumbent veto) and a committed NIMBY (neighborhood self-determination, the moral weight of residents' long-term investment in a place, preservation of community character) could both put their names to after a good-faith exchange. It should contain: (1) a numbered section of claims both sides accept — about the facts of the housing market, about what zoning actually does, and about whose interests are at stake; (2) a disagreements table in which each disagreement is stated in wording its own side would sign off on — I suspect one genuine dispute is whether longtime residents have a moral claim on the character of their neighborhood, and another whether opposing development can be justified even if it does worsen affordability, but tell me honestly if some of our fight is really about what the word "community" means rather than a substantive question; (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of us; and (4) for each crux, a concrete case, argument, or policy experiment we'd both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Throughout, please keep separate what is genuinely settled among people who study housing from what is still contested, and flag rather than paper over any point where the draft risks presenting one side's contested empirical assumption as agreed fact.
