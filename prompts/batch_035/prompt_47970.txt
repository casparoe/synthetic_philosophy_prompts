I run a small philosophy reading group at the public library here — mixed bag of regulars: two retired engineers, a woman who did a philosophy MA decades ago, a dentist who argues about everything. Next session is on Buridan's ass, the donkey stood between two identical haystacks that starves because it has no reason to go to one rather than the other. I don't want you to explain the puzzle to me — I know it. I want a teaching plan for the eighty minutes I've got.

A few things you need to work around. I heard somewhere that the ass never actually appears in Buridan's own surviving writings — treat that as true for planning purposes, and it's part of what I want the group chewing on anyway. One of my regulars, the dentist, is already convinced the puzzle is trivially solved by coin-flipping, so the plan has to take that seriously at exactly the right moment instead of waving it off (randomization: genuine solution, or the abdication of agency dressed up as one?). And I want the session to end somewhere bigger than the donkey — my own idea, feel free to push back on it, is that the puzzle is really about whether "acting for no reason" is even coherent, and that the interesting divide is between people who think a tie has to be broken by something and people who think you can just... start walking.

So the deliverable, in this order with rough lengths:

1. "Order of ideas" — the sequence the pieces have to come in and what depends on what, i.e. which idea has to be in the room before the next one can land (~250 words)
2. "The carrying case" — the one concrete example the whole session should be built around, and an honest word on why not the starving donkey itself if that's the right call; I'm tempted by a hiring committee deadlocked between two genuinely tied finalists but I won't be offended if you've got better (~150 words)
3. "Misconception map" — the misconceptions they'll bring or form (I'm guessing "this is secretly a free will thing" and "the donkey dies because it's too dumb to just pick"), and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off, not just named (~300 words)
4. "The coin-flip moment" — exactly when and how to handle the dentist's objection so it becomes the session's best five minutes instead of its worst twenty (~100 words)
5. "The check" — one question I can ask at the end that would tell me whether they've understood the puzzle rather than just been amused by it (~50 words)
6. "What I'm cutting" — what to openly refuse to get into at this level, and the one sentence to say so it doesn't feel like a dodge (~50 words)

Keep it practical, I'm the one standing in front of these people on a tuesday night.
