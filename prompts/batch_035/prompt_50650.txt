First year econ PhD student here, still recovering from the micro core. Last week in our applied field seminar someone presented a demand study where coffee consumption jumped right after a big ad campaign, and when a third year asked whether that meant tastes had changed, the presenter just shrugged and said de gustibus non est disputandum, and the whole room nodded like that settled it. It's been bugging me since, because I can't tell what claim the shrug was actually making, and as far as I can tell revealed preference theory lives or dies on which one it is. Can you lay out the distinct precise theses hiding in that one Latin phrase? The candidates I can half see: (1) the boring truism that arguing about tastes is pointless, pure rhetoric; (2) the methodological move that economics takes preferences as exogenous, so explaining preference change is somebody else's department; (3) the epistemic claim behind revealed preference — that choices are the only real evidence of preferences there is — which would make 'tastes changed' and 'prices, income, or beliefs changed' observationally equivalent in a lot of cases, and then utility is just whatever choice maximizes, which smells circular (preferences are revealed by choices that reveal preferences?) and I want to know whether that circularity is fatal or harmless, and whether there's any actual empirical content hiding in it; (4) the substantive empirical thesis from Stigler and Becker's 1977 paper that tastes neither change capriciously nor differ much between people, so all apparent shifts should get re-explained with prices, income, and accumulated consumption capital — which seems like it might be false or at least not the innocous thing the shrug needs, especially since Becker himself later wrote a whole book about accounting for tastes; and maybe (5) something normative about preferences being immune from criticism. For each reading tell me whether it's a platitude, substantive and defensible, or just false, and crucially whether it actually licenses dismissing the question about the ad campaign. Feel free to add readings I've missed. My own sense is the room was quietly sliding between the methodological and epistemic versions, and only one of them earns the shrug, but I can't pin down which.
