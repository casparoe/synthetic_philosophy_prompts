I'm putting together a decision tree for an intro ethics tutorial on theories of punishment, and I want it to sort students by what they actually believe about cases rather than by which label they like the sound of. The tree should start from a question everyone can answer, something like "the state deliberately harms a person who has been convicted of a crime — when, if ever, is that OK?", and then branch through the standard fault lines. I want the branch nodes to be answerable cold, so no node can just be "are you a retributivist?". The key ones I have in mind: does the fact that someone deserves it give the state a reason to punish all by itself, or does punishment need some further point beyond inflicting deserved harm; can punishment of the guilty be justified by good consequences alone (think of the case where framing and executing an innocent person would stop a riot — does your answer there generalize to punishing guilty people, or not); does the amount of punishment have to track desert at all, or can it be set entirely by whatever level best deters; and what to do about the person whose crime is discovered fifty years later, when they're now a completely different person leading a blameless life — does the old desert still bite?

Leaves should include at least: pure retributivism where desert fixes both whether and how much to punish; negative retributivism (desert only sets a ceiling and forbids punishing the innocent, everything inside the ceiling is up to consequences); pure consequentialism about sentencing; expressivist or communicative views where punishment is a kind of moral dialogue with the offender; and the abolitionist option. If some leaf is a combination nobody actually defends, say so and take a stab at why (incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored). Also please flag any pair of positions the tree genuinely can't pull apart — I suspect negative retributivism with consequentialist sentencing and threshold views end up in the same place on every concrete case, and if so I'd rather know that than have the tree pretend otherwise.

Context if it helps: the students are first-years who've read a bit of Kant and Bentham but nothing else, so nothing at the nodes should assume they've heard of any living philosopher. Keep it to around ten leaves max. Also apologies if this reads hastily — typing it up between tutorials, and my coffee hasn't kicked in yet.
