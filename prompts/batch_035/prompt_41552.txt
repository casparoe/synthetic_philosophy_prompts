I'm a novelist, and my next book follows a man who becomes a perpetrator of mass violence — not at the center of the apparatus, but in the ranks, someone who pulls a trigger when asked and goes home afterward. So I've been reading the Browning–Goldhagen quarrel as background research, and it has become the thing itself rather than the background. Browning, in his study of Reserve Police Battalion 101, reads the same men Goldhagen reads: middle-aged German policemen, given the chance at their first massacre to step out without penalty, most of whom did not step out. Browning explains them through conformity, deference to authority, career anxiety, the drift of routine, and the gradual erosion of inhibition; Goldhagen explains the same material through a specifically German eliminationist antisemitism that made the killing something they wanted to do, and he argues that Browning's conformity account absurdly portrays them as reluctant. After the fury of the 1990s, the debate seems to have simply died rather than resolved — each side's followers still cite the verdicts, and no one has budged. For my purposes as a writer this matters acutely, because these two readings give me two different novels: in one my protagonist is carried by the situation and thinks little; in the other he is carried by belief and knows what he is for. What I want from you is not a verdict on who was right but a diagnosis. Is this a genuine empirical disagreement where the evidence underdetermines the explanation, and if so, what evidence could have decided it? Is it a methodological disagreement in disguise — a dispute about whether collective action is to be explained by culture or by the psychology of individuals in situations — where each side is answering a different question under one heading? Or is the disagreement really about the moral and representational stakes, about what it would mean for a whole nation to indict or to acquit, so that the historians' evidence was never going to move anyone? Offer your diagnosis with reasons drawn from what each side actually claims, say what, if anything, would have counted as progress given that diagnosis, and — since I'll be leaning on this in constructing a character — please be scrupulous about distinguishing what is settled among historians of the perpetrators from what remains genuinely contested, and don't hand me a contested reading as if it were the consensus. If you think a novelist can responsibly proceed despite the impasse, tell me what a stance that respected both readings would look like.
