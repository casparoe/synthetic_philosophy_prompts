The debate over the Meno problem — why knowledge is more valuable than merely true belief — has, as far as I can tell, been running in place since the 1980s, and I would like a diagnosis of the impasse rather than a survey of the positions. As I understand it, the dispute now has three fixed camps. First, the process reliabilists hold that the extra value is a matter of practical payoff: beliefs formed by reliable methods are more useful across the range of situations one might face, so knowledge matters even when a particular true belief would serve just as well, because we cannot tell in advance which truths we will need. Second, their critics press the swamping objection: once a belief is true, the method that produced it contributes no additional value, just as a reliable thermometer adds nothing once one already knows the temperature; the critics take this to show that either knowledge has no value beyond true belief — which they treat as a reductio — or that value must come from something other than reliable process. Third, the virtue epistemologists respond by relocating the value: knowledge is an achievement, a success attributable to the believer's cognitive ability, and achievements have final value, prized for their own sake rather than for their products, as a fine performance is. Each camp seems to regard the others' replies as simply missing the point, and the literature has settled into mutual restatement.

My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not really about knowledge at all but about value: the virtue camp cares about things under the aspect of achievement, the swamping critics tacitly assume that value must be instrumental to some antecedently fixed good (getting things right), and the reliabilist reply in effect shifts from the value of the state of knowing to the value of possessing reliable methods. On that reading, the parties are answering different questions under one heading, and the original Meno question conflates several distinct ones — the value of true belief, the value of reliable belief-forming processes, and the value of the state of knowledge itself.

Is this diagnosis right, or is there a genuine first-order question here — whether knowledge has final value, over and above its components — that could in principle be settled by argument or counterexample? If my hypothesis is on the right track, what would count as progress: a terminological distinction separating the questions, an argument that one of the underlying axiologies is incoherent, or an admission that the residue is bedrock disagreement about what sorts of things can be valuable for their own sake? I would like the diagnosis supported by what each camp actually claims, and a candid verdict on whether this debate is substantive, verbal, or a case of ships passing in the night — along with an honest assessment of whether anything should make us expect it to end.
