I'm writing the final for a graduate seminar on deontological ethics, and I want one essay question on the paradox of deontology: the objection that it is irrational to refuse to violate a constraint when violating it once would prevent several further violations of that very same constraint. The standard seminar treatment runs through the two familiar responses and stalls there. On one side is the agent-relative reply: constraints are not to be evaluated by how much constraint-violation the world contains, since treating violations as an aggregate quantity to be minimized is already to capitulate to the consequentialist's way of counting. On the other side are threshold replies, which concede that some large enough number of prevented violations would license one violation, and then face the familiar charge that the thresholds they introduce are arbitrary or grotesquely discontinuous. I want a question that gets past this stalled debate rather than asking students to adjudicate it.

Here is the angle I want the question built around. Nearly all discussion sets the constraint against multiple violations of the same constraint: kill one to prevent five killings. But there is a structurally distinct and rarely discussed version — the question of ordering among constraints. If killing and torturing are both constrained, may I kill one person to prevent the torture of five? A nonconsequentialist owes an account of the structure among constraints: a lexical ordering, a lexical ordering with room for trade at some distance, or a comparative structure that somehow does not reduce to summing. Each option seems either to reintroduce aggregation one level up, which revives the paradox in a new place, or to leave the agent with no guidance wherever constraints conflict. My suspicion, which the question should let students explore rather than presuppose, is that the paradox is not really about numbers at all but about the logical form of constraints: are they strings of agent-relative reasons, which can interact with and weigh against each other, or defeasible agent-relative prohibitions, which either hold or fail to hold with no interaction between them? The same-constraint and cross-constraint versions of the paradox force that underlying question in different ways, and a good exam question would make that pressure visible.

Please produce three things.

First, the essay question itself: two or three paragraphs, written so that a student cannot pass by summarizing the seminar material. It should require the student to take a position on the logical-form issue and defend it against the alternative I find most tempting, namely that constraints are interacting agent-relative reasons with comparative strength.

Second, a model answer of roughly 600 words. It should commit to some position on the logical-form question and then show concretely how that position handles both the same-constraint version (kill one to prevent five killings) and the cross-constraint version (kill one to prevent five tortures), including at least one move a student would be unlikely to produce by summarizing the readings.

Third, a marking scheme built around five must-hit points worth 20 marks total, with a one-line note on how students typically miss each point, plus a closing statement of what separates an A answer from a B answer in terms of named argumentative moves rather than polish or prose quality.

Two constraints on the deliverable: the question and model answer should contain no quotations and no citations — refer to positions and arguments by description rather than by author, title, or year, since I want the exam to test understanding rather than recall of the reading list. And assume the students have completed a full seminar covering agent-relative restrictions, threshold views, and the clean-hands problem, so the question can presuppose those labels without re-explaining them, but should not presuppose any particular syllabus beyond that.
