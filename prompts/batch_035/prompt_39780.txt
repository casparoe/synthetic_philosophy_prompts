I sit on the board of a small education nonprofit, and at our last budget meeting a trustee argued, invoking Garrett Hardin's lifeboat ethics, that we should stop funding remedial tutoring and concentrate our entire endowment on our strongest existing programs. Her picture was this: our resources are a lifeboat with a strict carrying capacity; the unmet need around us is the swimmers in the water; every person we pull aboard drains the boat and brings everyone closer to sinking; so the apparently hard-hearted refusal is really the humanitarian option. Another trustee shot back that we are not in a lifeboat at all but on a spaceship — one shared craft, with the implication that capacity and generosity are not in tension the way the first picture claims. I have to write the memo that determines what, if anything, our funding policy can learn from this exchange, and I have come to suspect that neither of them has audited the analogy she was actually leaning on. So please do that audit for me. Lay out the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the boat, the carrying capacity, the swimmers, the water, the safety margin Hardin says a prudent captain keeps. Then the disanalogies, and for each one, whether it matters for our decision or is mere friction — for instance, our swimmers are inside the same educational system that built the boat rather than strangers in an open sea; the boat's capacity is partly a function of how we rig it rather than a fixed hull; taking someone aboard here may increase rather than displace capacity. Finally, tell me whether Hardin's conclusion — that the humane course is to stop taking more aboard — survives once the breaks are on the table, whether the spaceship rejoinder actually refutes the lifeboat or merely swaps in an analogy with its own unexamined imports, and what a better analogy for a nonprofit's budget dilemma would look like if there is one.
