I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background, and a coworker and I have been arguing at lunch about mind uploading — specifically the gradual replacement version where your neurons get swapped out one at a time for functionally identical silicon units while you stay awake and talking. We've managed to assemble a pile of arguments from blog posts and podcast episodes, and I want to get your honest scoring of them rather than another round of us talking past each other.

Please rate each of the following arguments on a scale from 0.0 to 1.0, where the score is how much credence a reasonable person should put in that argument as a reason to believe a gradually replaced mind would be the same conscious person as the original. The arguments:

1. Behavioral continuity: at every step of the replacement process, the system's outputs, self-reports, and introspective claims are unchanged, so there is no fact of the matter left over that could differ — if the person keeps sincerely saying they feel fine, they feel fine.
2. The prosthetic-neuron parable: if replacing a single neuron with a chip that reproduces its input-output behavior changes nothing, then by induction a million such replacements change nothing, since each step is as harmless as the first.
3. Natural turnover: the atoms making up your neurons are already being replaced on the timescale of months to years, and memories are being quietly rewritten and pruned every night in sleep, so "the original biological brain" is a fiction — gradual replacement by silicon is just a faster version of what biology already does.
4. My coworker's hybrid protocol: before each artificial neuron takes over, run it in parallel with the biological one in lockstep for some period, and only cut the biological one over once the two agree on outputs; he claims this adds a "causal handoff" that makes identity transfer unambiguous, and I can't tell if it's doing real work or is just theater.
5. Substrate independence as an engineering fact: computation is an abstract pattern, and the same pattern realized in different media is the same computation, so mind, being computation, transfers.

And two counterarguments, which I'd like rated the same way (here, high score = strong reason to believe gradual replacement would NOT preserve the person):

6. The fading qualia worry: if each replacement is 99.99% faithful rather than perfectly faithful, consciousness might degrade by an imperceptible sliver at each step, and after a hundred billion steps you could end up with a system that insists it is conscious and reports rich experience while there is nobody home — the testimony gives you no protection.
7. The empty-then-filled worry: perhaps a perfect silicon duplicate would be a new, distinct consciousness from the moment of full replacement regardless of how smooth the transition was, and the gradualness only hides the discontinuity the way morphine hides surgery rather than removing it.

Where I want you to be especially careful is the interaction between 2 and 6 — the induction and the fading worry seem to be mirror images of each other, and I suspect only one of them can be sound, but I can't articulate why. Also tell me whether my coworker's hybrid protocol (4) deserves a higher score than plain replacement (1), since that's the specific bet we've been arguing about.

Begin your response with the ranked list of scores, highest to lowest, as the first thing in your answer, then give your reasoning argument by argument after it — no preamble, no restating the question.
