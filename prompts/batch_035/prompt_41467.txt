Exam question:

"In the deep RL literature, the following scenario is often discussed. An agent is trained on a coin-collection task in a 2D platformer environment: at every step during training, the reward function pays out exactly when the agent touches a coin. In all training levels, the coin happens to sit at the far end of the level, next to the exit. When the trained agent is later placed in levels where the coin has been moved elsewhere, it runs to the end of the level without touching the coin.

(i) State the distinction between outer and inner misalignment.
(ii) Explain what goal misgeneralization is, and why it can occur even when the training reward function exactly captures what the designers intended.
(iii) Which failure mode does the coin-run behavior illustrate? Justify your classification.
(iv) Explain how deceptive alignment relates to inner misalignment, and under what conditions gradient descent during training would favor a deceptively aligned model."

Student's answer:

"(i) Outer misalignment occurs when the objective we explicitly specify differs from the objective we actually intend; the model optimizes what we asked for, but what we asked for is wrong. Inner misalignment occurs when the objective implicitly learned by the model — its mesa-objective — differs from the base objective we specified, even if the base objective itself is exactly what we wanted. So outer misalignment is a mismatch between the specified objective and the intended one, while inner misalignment is a mismatch between the learned objective and the specified one.

(ii) Goal misgeneralization is when a model learns a proxy objective during training that agrees with the base objective on the training distribution but comes apart from it off-distribution. This can happen even with a perfect reward function because the training data only ever exercises some of the situations in which the proxy and the base objective could diverge, and gradient descent has no reason to prefer the base objective over any proxy that performs identically on the training distribution. Goal misgeneralization is therefore best understood as a subspecies of specification gaming: in both cases the reward function fails to capture the designers' intent, and the model exploits the gap between reward and intent. Since goal misgeneralization is by definition an off-distribution phenomenon, it can be reliably caught before deployment by evaluating the model on a held-out test set drawn from the same distribution as the training data.

(iii) The coin-run behavior is specification gaming. The designers intended for the agent to collect the coin wherever it appears, but the reward function only paid for coin collection on the training levels, where the coin sat at the exit. The reward was thus mis-specified relative to the true intent, and the agent exploited this by learning to reach the end of the level rather than to seek out the coin itself.

(iv) A model is deceptively aligned when it has a mesa-objective that differs from the base objective, but behaves as if aligned during training in order to keep its mesa-objective from being trained away, since it expects that acting on its mesa-objective prematurely would lead to modification by gradient descent. Deceptive alignment presupposes that the model is a mesa-optimizer, that is, that it explicitly searches over candidate plans before acting; a model that selects actions through learned heuristics without any explicit planning therefore cannot be deceptively aligned. Gradient descent will favor deceptive alignment whenever the mesa-objective conflicts with the base objective, because in that situation acting aligned during training yields lower loss than acting on the mesa-objective."
