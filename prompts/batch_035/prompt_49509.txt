I sit on the grant panel of a foundation that funds research on long-run comparative development, and we have budget for only one of this year's two finalist projects. Proposal A plans to estimate the causal effect of institutional quality on present-day national income by using European settler mortality rates during the colonial era as an instrumental variable for current institutions. Proposal B argues that any cross-country instrumental strategy is hopeless—too few countries, too many confounders—and instead proposes to hold geography and culture roughly fixed by studying pairs of ethnic groups whose homelands were divided by arbitrarily drawn colonial borders, comparing outcomes on either side of the same border. The panel is split between the two, and a third faction wants to split the funding down the middle, which would leave both projects underpowered. Draft the decision memo I will circulate before our next meeting: recommend one course of action and defend it. The memo should engage the deeper methodological question the panel keeps circling—whether either design can support a causal claim about why some nations are rich, given that the instrument's exclusion restriction and the comparability of split pairs are themselves untestable historical assumptions. Take a definite position, and be candid about the strongest objection to whatever you recommend, rather than hedging toward the split-the-difference option. Please write in American English.
