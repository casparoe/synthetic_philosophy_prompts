So here's my situation. I'm a philosopher, mostly decision theory-adjacent, and I've got a slot at an interdisciplinary workshop on rationality where the audience will be basically behavioral economists — the kind of people who teach the sunk cost fallacy to undergrads as a certified textbook error — plus a couple of rational-choice modelers and maybe one other philosopher in the room. I'm going to defend a claim I've been chewing on for a while: that honoring sunk costs is not always irrational, and that the interesting cases are not the cute ones everyone already concedes (reputation, credibility, not looking like a quitter in future negotiations). The interesting cases, I want to argue, are evidential ones.

My line is roughly this: when I look at how much time, money, or effort my past self put into some project, that's data about how a person with (then-current) full information and no need to justify anything to me valued the thing. Treating my own history as evidence about the project's worth can be structurally similar to trusting a stranger's betting behavior over their words — sunk costs as signals that are expensive to fake, coming from my own record rather than from anyone's mouth. On top of that I want to claim that alot of the escalation-of-commitment findings in the psych literature don't touch my thesis, because those experiments mostly manipulate whether the subject personally made the earlier decision — which isn't the evidential channel I'm pointing at — though I have to tread carefully there, since I believe some of the later designs did vary information about the earlier choice, and I haven't sorted out which is which.

Anyway, prepare me for the Q&A. What I want from you is a prep sheet, not a defense of my view. Give me the objections this crowd will actually raise — phrased the way a behavioral economist would put them, not the way a philosopher would — ordered by how much damage they'd do to the paper. For each one: a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and a note on where the reply is soft, so I know when to hedge instead of pushing. Also tell me what I should just concede up front in the talk itself, because fighting everything will make me look desperate.

Things I can already see coming, at least vaguely: (1) "this is just Bayesian conditioning on your own past choices, nothing new" — which is annoying because it's half right and I don't want to fight it the wrong way; (2) "your past self had the same biases you do, so it's not independent evidence — you're double-counting noise"; (3) the modelers asking whether my view makes any prediction that differs observably from standard theory, and I confess I don't have a crisp answer to that one, so help me figure out what to say that isn't a shrug.

I have a feeling it's something about self-deception — that the cases where people lean on sunk costs as "evidence" are exactly the cases where the earlier investment was itself driven by wishful forecasting, so my rational reconstruction is doing all the work in theory-world and none in the messy cases people actually escalate in.
