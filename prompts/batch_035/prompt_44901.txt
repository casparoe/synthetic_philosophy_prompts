I'm a software engineer by training and temperament, with no background in philosophy beyond a few scattered readings, and I've gotten hung up on a puzzle I first encountered through Daniel Dennett's discussion of the color phi phenomenon. As I understand it, in experiments by Kolers and von Grünau in the mid-1970s, subjects are shown two stationary spots at different positions and in different colors, flashed in quick succession, and they reliably report seeing a single spot moving smoothly between the positions while changing color somewhere in the middle of its journey — a report that seems to describe a color change occurring before the second spot was ever shown. Dennett apparently offers two candidate stories: an Orwellian one, in which the subject first experienced spot-one-here, then spot-two-there, and the memory record was rewritten afterward, so the falsified record is all that remains; and a Stalinesque one, in which the brain held everything in a buffer and only delivered a fully composed experience after the second flash, staging the event complete with the illusory change. He then argues, if I've followed him, that there is no further fact of the matter that distinguishes these stories. That last step is where my views get tangled, and I'd like an audit of whether they hang together. Here is what I believe:

(1) For any episode of experience, there is a fact of the matter about its temporal structure — which contents were experienced, in what order, and roughly when, down to the scale of tens of milliseconds. Consciousness has a timeline, even where we have no instrument fine-grained enough to measure it.

(2) The brain is a latency-bound pipeline, and I have no trouble with this: sensory signals arrive late, processing stages buffer, and higher stages do constructive filling-in, interpolating and predicting rather than recording verbatim. That is simply how I'd expect any system under latency constraints to be engineered, and I accept that much of experience is assembled rather than passively received.

(3) In the color phi case, the subject's report is good evidence about what was experienced at the time. If the subject says the spot changed color midway through its motion, then that change was part of the experience as it occurred; I resist treating the report as a confabulation laid over some other, prior experience that no longer exists.

(4) I reject the Cartesian theater — no single place or moment where it all comes together for consciousness — but I also reject Dennett's further conclusion that there is no fact of the matter about the timing of experience. As an engineer, the Orwellian and Stalinesque stories describe different states of the system: rewriting a log after the write has been committed, versus deferring the write until the record can be composed, have different observable consequences in any system I've worked on. I can't see how a difference that real could evaporate into empirical indistinguishability.

(5) If you asked a subject about the episode a minute later, they would give the same report with the same confidence, and this holds for every delayed probe I can think of. I take that stability as further support for (3): an unhesitating, invariant report tracks the original experience, not merely a narrative that has since settled.

(6) Separately, I believe everyday perception is broadly veridical — the constructed product tracks the world — and I don't regard this as in tension with (2), because a well-engineered interpolation is usually correct about the world; good read-ahead caching rarely misleads.

Please go through these six commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly, which ones only look like they are, and what the cheapest fix for each real tension would cost. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally damning. Two things I'd especially like from you: first, whether my analogy in (4) between the brain and the systems I actually work on is doing legitimate work or smuggling something in — with rewriting history versus writing late, I keep reaching for the thought that the log-rewrite must leave artifacts, but I can't actually say what artifact I'd expect to find or what experiment would expose it; and second, whether the pair of commitments I'd most hate to give up, (1) and (3), can jointly survive my acceptance of (2), or whether one of them has to bend and which one is the cheaper bend.
