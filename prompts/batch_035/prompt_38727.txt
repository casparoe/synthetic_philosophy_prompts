I'm the sole archivist at a mid-sized county records office, and every month I make appraisal decisions about what to keep permanently, what to retain for a fixed term, and what to destroy — with no written framework. I've been persuaded by a line of critique that says archives are instruments of power: what an institution like mine keeps shapes what future historians and litigants can even ask, the silences in the holdings are as consequential as the holdings themselves, and offices like mine have historically preserved the paper of the powerful — deeds, minutes, permits — while the lives of tenants, migrants, and the undocumented leave little trace, partly because nobody generated records about them and partly because nobody kept the records that were generated. I'd like you to turn that critique into a decision procedure I can actually run on a collection in an afternoon: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a "destroy" or a "retain permanently," and where it hands the decision to someone else — the county counsel, a community group, a public hearing — because the question is one of values rather than of professional judgment. Then run it on three cases: (1) sixty boxes of housing-code enforcement files from the 1970s through the 1990s, mostly inspection and condemnation records for poor neighborhoods; (2) a donated shoebox of photographs and meeting notes from a tenant-rights organization that folded in 2008, material my office has no legal mandate to keep; and (3) the routine correspondence of the county commissioners, the kind of thing my office has always kept without thinking. Include one case where the procedure gives an answer I won't like, and be explicit about what the procedure cannot do — in particular, whether any procedure applied at the point of appraisal can address the critique that the silences were produced upstream of me, before any box ever reaches my desk.
