I run a film night at my local community centre and we're doing Braveheart next month, followed by a discussion about Scottish independance and whether national identity is ancient or manufactured. I've been reading around the invention of tradition stuff (Hobsbawm's edited book, Benedict Anderson on imagined communities) and it struck me that Braveheart might be a perfect double case: a film about a medieval national hero that's itself stuffed with invented traditions. Gibson's Wallace wears a belted plaid that's centuries out of period, fights in woad, and the whole plot turns on primae noctis, which historians basically treat as a myth. And then there's the statue they put up at the Wallace Monument in the 90s modelled on Mel Gibson's face, which people mocked and vandalised until it was eventualy carted off. So the film isn't just depicting tradition, it's manufacturing new ones.

I want to write a short intro for the discussion, and I'd love a reading of Braveheart through this lens. A few things I'm wrestling with: does the film work as a clean illustration of Hobsbawm and Anderson, or does it complicate them? Scotland is an awkward case for the "nations are modern" story -- it was a kingdom with real medieval institutions, and the Declaration of Arbroath (1320) sounds pretty national centuries before print capitalism. So when Anderson says nations only really exist once you have mass vernacular print, is Wallace's Scotland an imagined community or a counterexample? And where does the film resist the reading -- is there anything in it doing the work of a genuinely old identity rather than a 19th-century costume drama, and is the "Braveheart effect" on modern Scottish nationalism (people kept quoting it around the 2014 referendum) invention all the way down or not? Be honest about where the theory strains rather than forcing everything to fit.

Please write it in British English, and keep it accessible -- half the group haven't read any theory, so explain what you need as you go.
