I've been trying to work out what I actually think about when it's rational to flat-out believe something, versus just assigning it a probability and leaving it at that, and I figured the way to do it is to just go with my gut on a bunch of cases and see if any principle comes out the other end. Here are my verdicts:

- A million-ticket lottery, exactly one winner, and I'm holding one ticket. I believe my ticket will lose. Feels completely rational, no hesitation.
- Same lottery: I'll also believe that ticket #7 will lose, and ticket #8, and any other ticket you point to. But I do NOT believe that no ticket will win — I know one of them wins. So apparently I'll believe each of a million statements while refusing to believe their conjunction, and that's just where I've landed. I can't tell if that's fine or a problem.
- I'm flying tomorrow. Odds of a crash are something like 1 in 11 million. I believe my flight lands safely, and I'd even say I know it. Zero discomfort.
- My dissertation is done, and I believe every single sentence in it. I also believe, about as firmly as anything, that somewhere in those 160 pages there's at least one error. Both of those feel rational to me at the same time.
- Forecast says 90% chance of rain tomorrow. I believe it'll rain. If the forecast said 60%, I would not say I believe it — I'd say "probably." Somewhere between those two numbers the flat-out belief switches on, and I have no idea where.
- My dad's surgery has a 97% survival rate, and beforehand I believe he'll be fine. But it nags at me that if a hundred families all formed this belief, three of them would be believing something false, and nobody can say which three. I don't retract the belief, it just feels different from the plane one, and I can't explain why.

My tentative principle was just "believe p when the probability of p is high enough," with the threshold somewhere in the high 90s. But the lottery and the dissertation seem to wreck it, or at least I can't see how they don't: each ticket is like 99.9999% likely to lose, so on my own principle I should believe the no-winner conjunction, which I refuse to do. And in the dissertation I believe all the sentences and also believe their conjunction is false, which looks like believing p, and q, and not-(p-and-q), which I assume no threshold view can allow — or can it?

So what's the actual principle here, if there is one? Is there anything that keeps the lottery beliefs, the dissertation beliefs, and the flight beliefs all rational without forcing me into contradictions? If nothing does that, then which of my verdicts would you overturn, and why those rather than the others? I'd also like to know what your principle says about two cases I haven't made up my mind on: (a) a juror who's 99% convinced the defendant is guilty — can she flat-out believe he did it, knowing that some small fraction of people convicted on evidence like this are innocent? and (b) my friends' marriage — the divorce rate is maybe 40%, I know nothing specific about them that points either way, so can I believe it'll last, or am I stuck at "probably"? Please be honest about which of my six verdicts your principle can't save.
