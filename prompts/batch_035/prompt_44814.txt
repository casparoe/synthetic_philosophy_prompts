So I'm in an ethics of war seminar this semester (we started with Walzer and then moved through a bunch of the revisionist literature arguing against him) and for the last section meeting of the term, everyone has to circulate a short position memo before a class debate on the moral equality of combatants. I finally wrote mine down over the weekend and honestly the views felt fine as I was writing them, but now that I reread the whole thing I can't tell if its consistant, and I'd rather find out now than have the grad student running our section tear it apart in front of everyone. So I want someone to audit the set before I send it around.

Here's roughly where I stand: (1) I come down on the revisionist side about the moral equality of combatants — I don't think a just combatant and an unjust combatant are moral equals, and a soldier fighting an unjust war fights wrongly even if he never breaks a single rule of jus in bello. (2) At the same time, when I picture the actual nineteen-year-old on the unjust side who got conscripted under threat of prison, sincerely believes his country is in the right, and has no realistic way to find out otherwise, I genuinely can't see how he does anything wrong by fighting. (3) I'm pretty firmly a liability theorist about killing in war: the only thing that can make someone a legitimate target is being responsible, in whatever the right sense turns out to be, for an unjust threat — you can't just be killed because you happen to be useful to the enemy's war effort. (4) I also think that in practice soldiers on both sides may be targeted as long as they're combatants, and I definitely would not want the rules of war to depend on some tribunal figuring out mid-war which side's cause is just — the symmetric rules we have now are the right ones. (5) I think that in a democracy, the citizens who knowingly and enthusiastically vote for and cheer on a war of aggression are often more responsible for the resulting unjust threat than the poor conscript from (2) is. And (6) there's a case verdict I feel really confident about: the munitions-plant worker who takes the job for good pay is a civilian and may not be deliberately targeted, while the conscript may be — that targeting line seems basically right to me.

Go through the six commitments and tell me which pairs are actually in tension and how badly, which ones only look like they conflict (I suspect at least one is like this but I honestly can't tell which), and for each real tension what the cheapest fix would be and what it costs. Rank the tensions by severity instead of listing them all as equally bad, and be specific about which commitment has to give in each case. Don't pull punches to spare my feelings — I'd rather know exactly where I'm unstable now so I can figure out what my memo should actually claim.
