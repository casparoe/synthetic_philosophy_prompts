I'm preparing to write a paper on self-deception, but before I commit to defending or attacking any particular account, I want to be clear about what I should be demanding of one. Too much of the literature consists of people proposing a theory and then testing it against their two favorite cases, and I suspect the interesting disagreements are really disagreements about which requirements are genuine and which are parochial. So I'd like you to write the full specification.

Act as a professional philosopher of mind who has watched this debate for decades and has no horse in it. Think carefully, and before committing to the list, brainstorm what different camps have implicitly treated as non-negotiable: the intentionality theorists, the deflationists, the divided-mind people — and ask of each of their demands whether it tracks the phenomenon or just their theory of it. Then give me the desiderata.

Specifically, what must an adequate theory of self-deception deliver?

- The extension: which cases it must count in (the spouse who "doesn't notice" the obvious, the terminally ill patient, the academic convinced her paper was rejected for political reasons) and which it must count out (lying to another person, ordinary wishful thinking, self-serving memory, akratic belief, or simply being wrong about oneself for unmotivated reasons). For each case, say what hangs on getting it right.
- The puzzles: the static puzzle (how can one and the same mind intend to get itself to believe p's falsity without that intention defeating itself) and the dynamic puzzle (how the belief is actually acquired — why doesn't the motivated person see through the process?). A theory must solve these, explain them away, or explain why they never really arise — say which you'd accept.
- The distinctions it cannot collapse: self-deception versus other-deception, believing p while also believing not-p versus ambivalence versus mere credence-shifts, the hot process a theorist of intention describes versus the cold biased evidence-gathering the psychology literature describes.
- The motivational constraint: why some desires and fears generate self-deception while equally strong ones don't — a theory that makes every motivated belief self-deception explains too much.
- The empirical constraint: it can't contradict what's actually known about confirmation bias, selective evidence-gathering, and confabulation, but it also can't be so absorbent that "self-deception" just becomes a label for all of it.
- The normative question: whether a theory owes us an account of when the self-deceived person is responsible or blameworthy for the belief, or whether that's somebody else's job.

Mark each desideratum as non-negotiable or as a camp prejudice. And most importantly — this is the part I most want to see — flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. For instance, if we demand that self-deception be intentional in a full-blooded way and also that it not require an absurd doxastic architecture, is anything left standing? If some subset is jointly unsatisfiable, say so plainly; that would itself be a result worth writing down.

Then, briefly, run three contenders against the list: a deflationary view on which self-deception is just motivationally biased evidence-gathering (Alfred Mele's sort of picture), Davidson's divided-mind account, and a view on which the self-deceiver deliberately avoids registering what he is up to rather than believing its negation. Keep the scorecard short — the thinking is in the criteria, not the scoring. A table is fine if you want one.
