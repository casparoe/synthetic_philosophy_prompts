My cousin and I have been arguing for months about whether ordinary consumption choices matter morally. His position, which he takes to be simple common sense, rests on one analogy that he applies to everything: my driving, my steak dinners, and my vote are each like a single drop of water added to the ocean. No one can detect the rise in sea level from one drop; the tide is what it is whether or not I add mine. So, he concludes, my individual choices make no difference to climate change, to factory farming, or to election outcomes, and whatever responsibility exists belongs to governments and corporations — the parties actually pouring the buckets.

I want to push back, but the analogy feels sturdy to me, and the standard comeback I've tried — asking what would happen if everyone thought this way — seems to beg the question against him rather than answer him. So here is what I would like, laid out in order.

First, render the analogy as an explicit table of correspondences: what plays the role of the ocean, the drop, the sea level, the tide, and the buckets he says only governments and corporations hold. Second, tell me where the mapping breaks. Working through this on my own, I've come to suspect the three cases I listed are not alike: my vote is one input into a rule that declares a winner at a threshold, my steak is one input into a demand signal that presumably moves production in batches rather than continuously, and my driving feeds a quantity that looks genuinely continuous. If the target cases come apart in this way, then perhaps the ocean picture fits some of them and not others, and I would like to know which, and why.

Third, and this is what I care about most: identify what the ocean picture quietly imports. It seems to assume that the only difference worth caring about is a detectable difference in the actual outcome, and that if my drop does not raise the level by itself, then nothing I do raises it at all. But I have been wondering whether a drop can matter by slightly increasing the chance that the level crosses a lip — a nearly full glass overflows because of the last several drops, not because of any one of them, yet taking away any of those drops would have kept the table dry. Is there a defensible version of this thought, or is it just the same fallacy in a costume? I can see that being one of several last drops is not the same as being the last drop, and neither is the same as being undetectable, but I cannot sort out which of those relationships carries moral weight.

Finally, tell me whether his conclusion — that individuals' high-volume consumption choices are morally weightless, or at least not wrong — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole time. And if the ocean is simply the wrong picture, what would a better analogy be? I have half-invented one of my own, involving a hundred people each adding a single drop of acid to a tank with a fish in it, where no single drop has a detectable effect but the fish ends up dead regardless of which drop you remove, and I would like to know whether a case of that shape is doing better work than the ocean or just making me feel better about my steak.
