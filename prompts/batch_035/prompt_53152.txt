I'm designing a seminar unit on the norms of assertion, and I want the standards of adequacy settled before we turn to any of the contenders — the knowledge rule, the truth rule, the justified-belief rule — so that we judge the views against a fixed list rather than reverse-engineering the list from a view we already like. Please write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of the norm (or norms) of assertion has to deliver.

The purpose the theory has to serve: making sense of our practice of evaluating assertions — what we challenge, what we demand retraction of, what we let pass without comment, and who we blame — rather than predicting when people assert.

Include, with the case or consideration behind each, at least the following, plus any others you think the data require:

1. Moore-paradoxical assertions — saying "It's snowing, but I don't believe it is" is not false and not a lie, yet strikes hearers as defective in a distinctive way. An adequate theory should explain the defect rather than stipulate it.
2. Lottery propositions — telling a ticket holder "Your ticket will lose" seems improper to many people even when the probability is overwhelming and the speaker believes it. The theory should say what, if anything, goes wrong here, and whether the same diagnosis extends to flatly asserting future contingents like "The coin will land heads."
3. Selfless assertion — a physician who asserts to a panicking patient a diagnosis she does not herself believe, purely for its calming effect. Must the theory count this as a violation, and is counting it a violation a cost or a credit?
4. The distinction between the norm governing the act and the norms governing the speaker — an assertion can be proper by the rule yet blameworthy, or improper yet excusable. The desiderata should not collapse these.
5. Hedged and qualified assertions — "I'd guess it's under ten," "It's probably fine." Are these assertions made under a weaker norm, assertions of something weaker, or something else? The theory should have some account, even if the account is that ordinary hedging is loose talk.
6. The standing practice of answering challenges with evidence: when challenged, asserters offer grounds, not merely sincerity, which suggests the norm advertises more than the speaker's state of mind. Say what this observation does and doesn't establish.

Then do three things with the list: mark each requirement as non-negotiable or negotiable, saying why; flag any subset of the requirements you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable — this is the part I most want, since if the data pull in incompatible directions, that is itself the seminar's headline result; and identify which single requirement excludes the most candidates, since that one will structure our later discussions. Do not score the actual theories against the list; we will do that in class, and I want the criteria uncontaminated by premature verdicts.

End with two sentences of epistemic bookkeeping: your overall confidence in the list as it stands, and the one requirement you are least sure deserves its place, with a word about what would change your mind.

Hard limit: 500 words for the entire response, including the bookkeeping. That is tight, so spend the words on the motivating cases, not on stage-setting.
