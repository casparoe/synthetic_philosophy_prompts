After a career in condensed-matter physics, I came to social choice theory late and loved it precisely because it proceeds by proof rather than rhetoric. I read Amartya Sen's 1970 result — that no collective choice rule can satisfy both the Pareto principle and a minimal condition of liberalism, where each person is decisive over at least one pair of social states confined to their private sphere, as in his prude-and-reader example involving Lady Chatterley's Lover — and assumed that by now the field would have settled what the theorem actually shows. Instead, some fifty years on, the interpretive debate seems barely to have moved. One camp holds that the result indicts the Pareto principle: if individuals hold preferences over other people's private affairs, it is counting those meddlesome preferences in the aggregation that produces the contradiction, so the theorem is really an argument for excluding such tastes from social choice. A second camp, following Gärdenfors and Sugden if I have them right, holds that the theorem reveals the entire preference-aggregation framework as the wrong home for rights: a right is not a veto over social states but an available strategy in a game, and the impossibility is an artifact of misformulation. Sen himself, as I read him, treats the result as a broad indictment of welfarism. Each camp has its formal machinery, and each seems to think the others have simply missed the point. My working hypothesis, formed over years of watching two laboratories with two correct models talk past each other, is that these camps are answering different questions — one about which aggregation rules are possible given fixed individual preferences, one about what rights are for — and that the debate is a heading under which they collide rather than a single disagreement. But I may be wrong: perhaps there is a genuine substantive dispute, for instance about whether the notion of a social state, or of individual preference, can bear the weight the framework places on it. Please diagnose what kind of disagreement this really is — verbal, substantive, two distinct questions under one name, or a sign that the original question was ill-posed — grounding the diagnosis in what the camps actually argue, and tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis, or why none should be expected.
