I'm taking a third-year seminar called "Assertion, Deception, and Trust" that straddles ethics and philosophy of language, and I've run into a dispute that seems genuinely stuck, and I'd like help diagnosing why rather than being told which side is right.

Here's the debate as it plays out in our seminar and, as far as I can tell, in the literature. One camp holds that there is a morally significant difference in kind between lying and merely misleading: lying violates the norms governing assertion directly (you assert what you believe false, thereby abusing the special license that assertion carries in cooperative inquiry), whereas misleading works through true assertions that trigger false implicatures, and implicatures are already provisional in a way assertions are not — hearers are, in some sense, supposed to keep their guard up about what was merely implied. On this view the deceiver who lies attacks a different and more fundamental trust than the deceiver who merely misleads, even if both produce the same false belief in the same hearer for the same reason. Jennifer Saul, Seana Shiffrin, and Andreas Stokke are the names that come up on this side, though they don't agree with each other on everything. The other camp — consequentialists and some others in our class — hold that the difference, insofar as it exists, is a fact about linguistic mechanics, not about morality: in both cases the speaker intends the hearer to end up with a false belief, foreseeably does so, and has no excuse; which channel the false belief travels through (what is said versus what is implicated) is about as morally relevant as whether the lie was spoken or written. We use the classic examples: the wartime case where you tell the pursuing soldiers "I saw him run toward the market" (true, but he ran there only because you forced him at gunpoint, and you say it to make them believe he fled on his own) versus telling them "He ran away on his own"; and the Kant–Benjamin Constant exchange about the murderer at the door, which is usually mangled but keeps resurfacing.

Here's what strikes me. Nobody in the seminar moves, but the arguments don't really engage: the moral-difference camp keeps describing the epistemology of assertion, and the no-difference camp keeps pointing out that the outcomes and intentions are identical. My working hypothesis, and I want it treated as a hypothesis, is that the impasse has a specific structural source: the moral question is parasitic on an unresolved semantic question — namely what assertion is and what norms it carries — so the two camps are smuggling in different answers to the semantic question and then talking past each other about the moral one. When the moral-difference camp says "lying abuses assertion," they're assuming assertion has a distinctive normative profile (a knowledge norm or sincerity norm that implicature lacks); when the no-difference camp says "same intent, same outcome," they're assuming that whatever norms assertion has are just generic norms of cooperative communication, so no channel is special. If that's right, the moral debate can't be resolved at the moral level; it would move only when the underlying account of assertion and implicature moves.

But I'm not confident. Alternative diagnoses I can see: (a) the two camps are using "morally different" differently — one means a difference in the pro tanto wrong (an additional wrong-making feature present in lying), the other means a difference in the all-things-considered verdict (how wrong the act is overall), so they can both be right and the disagreement is partly verbal; (b) the disagreement is really about trust — a substantive disagreement about what trust is and how many distinct kinds of it there are — and the talk about assertion is a proxy; or (c) it comes down to bedrock intuitions about particular cases (especially the murderer-at-the-door type) that neither side can argue for without circularity, which would suggest the debate is hard but not ill-posed.

Given all this, I'd like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is, supported by what each side actually claims rather than a caricature of either. And tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a worked-out account of assertion, a distinction between kinds of trust, an agreement on which sense of "morally different" is at issue, test cases designed to separate the pro tanto question from the all-things-considered question, or an argument that no progress should be expected. If you think my parasitic-semantic-question hypothesis is wrong, I'd genuinely like to know why, not just that it's incomplete.
