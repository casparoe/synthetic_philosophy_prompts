I'm taking an upper-level seminar called the Ethics of Eating, and for my term paper I want to work out what a theory of the comparative harm of animal-source foods would actually have to deliver before I commit to one. The question that got me here: nearly everyone in my seminar agrees that chicken is worse than beef per kilo, but for two different reasons. One group counts lives — a kilo of chicken costs on the order of one bird's entire life, while a kilo of beef is a small fraction of a cow's, especially once you remember the dairy herd standing behind the beef — and another group counts the quality of those lives, comparing broiler sheds with pasture. Meanwhile a friend who studies agriculture keeps pointing out that crop cultivation kills animals too: rodents and reptiles killed during harvesting, insects killed by pesticides, plus all the feed crops grown for the livestock themselves. So the theory also has to say something about the deaths embedded in plant foods and in animal feed, not just the animals slaughtered on purpose. Rather than jump to a verdict, I want the specification first.

Please write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of the comparative harm of foods must get right, which cases it must handle, which distinctions it can't collapse (for instance between how bad a death is for the animal and how bad its life was; between killing an animal and failing to bring one into existence; between per-animal and aggregate rankings; between the feed system and the slaughterhouse as sources of harm), and which empirical facts it can't contradict (yields per animal, lifespans, stocking densities, the fact that pasture is itself habitat for wild animals). For each requirement, give the case or the consideration behind it. Then do three further things, in descending order of importance to me. First, mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are prejudices of one camp — in particular, is demanding a determinate verdict between beef and chicken right now a genuine desideratum, or is it a refusal to take seriously the empirical uncertainty about invertebrate sentience and about the welfare of wild animals living on pasture and in crop fields? Second, flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, since if the spec itself turns out to be unsatisfiable, that is the interesting result. Third, briefly — a paragraph or a small table is fine — say how three rough candidates do against the list: a pure death-count view, a suffering-during-life view, and a total-welfare view that lets existence itself carry positive or negative value. Throughout, I'd most like to know which single requirement does the most work in eliminating candidates.
