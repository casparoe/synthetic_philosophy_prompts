I'm trying to work out what a theory of idealized preferences would actually have to deliver before comparing any of the concrete proposals, and I'd like the specification written out rather than assumed. The purpose to fix in advance: the theory is meant to supply the normative target for something like a fiduciary superintelligence that must act on our behalf once, without the option of coming back to ask us follow-up questions. So adequacy is relative to that use case, not to explaining everyday attributions of "what someone really wants."

Here is what I want: a requirements list for any theory of idealized preferences — whether of the coherent extrapolated volition family, of the ideal-observer family, or of the "full information" accounts of the good life — with the motivating case or consideration stated behind each requirement. At minimum the list should adjudicate the following, though I expect you to add items I haven't thought of:

1. Attribution: the extrapolated preferences must still be in some sense ours, not the preferences of a stranger who happens to be our epistemically improved counterpart. The standard worry is that if you give me enough information, time, and cognitive enhancement, the resulting agent may simply not care about the things I care about, and then whose preferences is the machine serving?

2. Non-circularity: the idealization conditions ("if we knew more, thought faster, were more the people we wished we were") must not already presuppose a substantive theory of the good, or the extrapolation becomes a rigged machine for producing whatever the theorist input. Relatedly, is there any way to state the conditions that is both substantive enough to filter out preferences formed under ignorance and neutral enough to avoid this?

3. Stability and path-independence: the output should not depend arbitrarily on the order in which information, reflection, and coherence are added — whether you cure my ignorance first and then my akrasia, or the reverse. If no version of the theory can guarantee this, I want that said plainly.

4. Aggregation: once individual volitions are extrapolated, what does the theory owe us about combining them — genuine overlap, a bargained compromise, majoritarian aggregation, or something else — and which of these is compatible with the machine not riding roughshod over durable minorities?

5. Content constraints: whether the output must cohere with the corrected factual and moral beliefs of the extrapolatees or is permitted to override them, and what that choice costs.

For each requirement, tell me which camp's prejudice it might be rather than a genuine desideratum, flag any pair or subset of requirements you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable (that unsatisfiability result, if there is one, is what I most want to see), and say which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate theories. Where the requirements come from the literature on idealization in well-being — the objections that idealized preferences can be alien to the person whose good they are supposed to constitute, or that the idealization procedure cannot be both illuminating and action-guiding — you can attribute the argument to its usual proponents in a sentence, but I don't want citations or reading lists, just the reasoning.

Since the specification involves judgment calls about which requirements are genuine, please indicate how confident you are in the classification of each requirement as non-negotiable versus negotiable, and single out the one point in the whole answer you are least sure of. If you think one of my five desiderata is actually a mistake — something no theory should be held to — say so rather than accommodating it.
