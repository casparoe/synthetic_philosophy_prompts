I sit on the advisory board of a national science funding agency (think a smaller NIH/DOE hybrid — we fund big multi-year programs, not individual grants). Next month we have to decide whether to greenlight our director's proposal for a fifteen-year flagship program to computationally design enzymes that break down plastic in seawater. It's a moonshot, let's be honest. The agency has run flagship programs before, and the staff analysts have helpfully tallied the record: of the seven previous ones, six were quietly wound down with little to show, and one was a genuine success. So the base rate is ugly, and the analysts memo basically says: on the outside view, this thing fails.

The director — who is honestly the sharpest person I know in this field — pushes back with what I gather people call the inside view. She doesn't dispute the tally; she argues that each of the six failures died for specific, nameable reasons (one had no industrial partner, one was launched before the key measurement technique existed, one got killed by a change of government), and that this program differs in each of those respects, plus the underlying science has crossed a real threshold recently. She also points out, a bit sharply, that an agency that always deferred to the outside view would never have funded the one that worked, since its odds looked just as bad at approval. And a third of my board colleagues grumble that "staged funding" — the obvious compromise — is just cowardice dressed up as rationality.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before the meeting. It should recommend one concrete decision — full funding, rejection, or staged funding with explicit kill criteria (say what they'd actually be) — and give the reasoning. Along the way it has to grapple with the reference-class problem: which comparison set is even the right one? All flagship programs ever, flagship programs in adjacent fields, or only programs led by someone with her track record (she's run two big ones, one succeeded one failed)? The analysts and the director are implicitly using different classes, and I suspect that's where the real fight is. And it should treat the strongest point on whichever side loses honestly — including her jab that the outside view, applied consistently, would have killed the one program that worked.
