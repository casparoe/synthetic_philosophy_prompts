Last month I was appointed to the clinical ethics committee of our regional hospital network, and I have discovered that nearly every question reaching us reduces to the same one: when may a loss be imposed on some for the sake of a benefit to others? The chair has asked each member to submit a written statement of the reasoning behind their positions, and when I sat down to draft mine I found that my verdicts across our cases do not obviously follow from any principle I can state. A colleague told me my difficulty is an instance of what philosophers call the separateness of persons, but I want to work out what my own judgments commit me to rather than adopt a label. Here are my verdicts; I would like to know whether a single principle captures them.

(1) During a shortage of a chemotherapy drug, we could give four patients the full dose or split the same supply among eight, each receiving a slightly less effective but still substantially beneficial dose. Splitting: fine, and I would go further and call it required.

(2) The network could trim a small amount from every patient's stay — marginally worse amenities, somewhat longer waits — none of it large enough for any individual patient to notice, and use the total to fund a safety program preventing several deaths a year. Fine.

(3) To test our reasoning, a member proposed a hypothetical: identify the staff member with the best tissue match and require a bone-marrow extraction — a day of significant pain and a small residual risk — over his explicit objection, in order to save five strangers. Not fine, and not close.

(4) The same member then offered a variant, structured so that the aggregate arithmetic comes out at least as favorable as in (3): levy an afternoon's pay on all five thousand staff, without asking anyone, to fund the same treatment for the same five strangers. Fine — and I cannot say what distinguishes it from (3) except that no one person bears much of it.

(5) Cancelling all elective surgery for six weeks, which significantly sets back each of roughly three hundred patients, in order to free capacity that will save perhaps six lives. Fine, but I am markedly less comfortable than in (2), and again I can locate the difference only in the size of each person's loss.

The case I keep going back and forth on: compulsory blood donation, under which every adult in the region gives one donation a year — an hour, a needle, a day of mild weakness, no consent sought — to keep the blood bank supplied. On some days this strikes me as obviously parallel to (2) and (4); on others, as a step toward (3).

Is there a principle that captures all five verdicts, stated precisely enough to decide new cases? If so, what does it say about the blood-donation case, and what does it predict for one we have not yet discussed: requiring two hundred staff to accept a permanent night-shift rotation — a modest but genuine and ongoing burden on each — so that forty thousand outpatients each save, on average, four minutes per visit? If no principle can save all five, tell me which verdict you would overturn and why, and whether a principle that preserved every one of them would be worth its complexity or would instead amount to a rationalization of my hesitations. Please also state how confident you are in your answer and single out the point about which you are least sure.
