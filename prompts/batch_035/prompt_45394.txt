My reading group has spent three weeks on moral responsibility, and our last meeting ended in a stalemate over Frankfurt-style cases that I'd like your help sorting out. The case we used: a man, call him Jones, deliberates about whether to shoot a rival. A neuroscientist named Black has implanted a device in Jones's brain. Black wants Jones to shoot, and the device is set up so that if Jones shows any sign of tending away from the shooting — any flicker of hesitation — the device will fire and force Jones to go through with it. In the actual run of events, Jones deliberates, decides to shoot entirely on his own, for his own vindictive reasons, and pulls the trigger. Black's device never activates; Black never does a thing. Marta's opening move was that Jones is plainly blameworthy, yet he could not have done otherwise — any path away from shooting would have been intercepted — so the principle that moral responsibility requires the ability to do otherwise, the principle of alternative possibilities, is simply false, and with it the most popular route from determinism to the conclusion that nobody is ever responsible.

Dev refused to concede, and their fight went four rounds, none of them sloppy. (1) Dev raised what he called the dilemma: either Jones was going to decide to shoot no matter what, in which case Black's device is doing no work and the case just presupposes that Jones's decision is determined — presupposing exactly what the incompatibilist wanted to argue about, so the case begs the question — or Jones might genuinely have wavered, in which case Black has to intervene, and intervene before the decision, to stop the wavering, and then it isn't Jones but the device that settles the matter, so the case shows no such thing. (2) Marta answered that Dev's first horn conflates two things: the case needn't assume determinism, only that Black can predict Jones infallibly, and an infallible predictor who never has to act still leaves the decision entirely Jones's own in the actual sequence. (3) Dev replied that infallible prediction without intervening is the mystery: either Jones's deliberation is deterministic, which is the question-begging horn again, or there's some indeterminacy in him, in which case no device can be infallible without monitoring a prior sign and acting on it — and a device that acts on a prior sign has already intervened in the causal chain before Jones decides, undercutting the very responsibility the case was supposed to secure. (4) Marta's last stand was that even if Dev wins on the mechanics, there remains a cheap fallback on her side: whatever else is true, in every version of the case there is something Jones could have done otherwise — hesitated, triggered the device, formed the first stirring of a different intention — so alternative possibilities never fully vanish, and responsibility still needs them. But Dev said that was her concession, not her fallback: if all the freedom Jones has left is the freedom to trip Black's device, that is far too thin a liberty to ground something as serious as blame, and Marta had effectively admitted that responsibility doesn't need alternatives in any sense worth caring about.

I followed both of them most of the way and then lost the thread. Please adjudicate the exchange round by round: for each of the four numbered moves, say whether it succeeds, fails, or succeeds only against a weaker version of what the other side actually claimed, and justify each verdict. Then tell me who is more right overall, whether the Frankfurt case as described genuinely refutes the principle of alternative possibilities or merely reshuffles the burden of proof, and what, if anything, both of them are missing — in particular, whether their fight about alternatives is even the fight that matters for whether Jones deserves blame.
