I've spent about fifteen years in test engineering and QA for distributed systems — coverage metrics, test oracles, flaky tests, reproducibility, destructive testing — and my philosophy background is basically nil. A philosopher friend told me about the old verificationist idea that every truth is in principle checkable, and the result (Fitch's, apparently) that this, plus some innocuous-looking logic, collapses into: every truth is already known. Which sounds like a joke. Restate the whole thing in my vocabulary as honestly as you can — the modal 'in principle' as some unbounded testing budget or unbounded search, 'known' as verified by an oracle, contradiction as two tests that can't both pass, whatever fits. Then give me the residue report: what translates cleanly, what gets distorted to fit, and what has no expression at all in my vocabulary. The part I keep snagging on: the proof seems to need that if you can verify p and you can verify q seperately, you can verify p-and-q together. In my world that is exactly what fails — the first test can perturb the system, or the two checks need incompatible configurations, so compositional verifiability is an idealization no real tester satisfies. So my question is twofold: is the paradox basically telling us that the philosophers' 'knowable in principle' smuggled in that idealization, or am I reading my own assumptions into the translation? And if I've mislocated where the proof actually bites, say where it really does, in my terms.
