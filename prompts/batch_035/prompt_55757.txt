I run discussion sections for an undergraduate course on decision-making, and last week I ran a live dollar auction: highest bidder takes the dollar bill, but the second-highest bidder still pays what they bid, which is how the bidding blew past 90 cents. When I asked her why she'd bid past the point where any further bid loses money, one student offered the proverb "in for a penny, in for a dollar" — and it landed with the room, because it sounds like time-tested counsel rather than a rationalization. I want to grade her move rigorously rather than just saying "that's sunk-cost fallacy," and the proverb deserves better than the reflexive dismissal it usually gets.

Please take "in for a penny, in for a dollar" apart. What distinct, precise claims could that one line be making? I can think of several candidates, and I'd like you to lay out the menu — including whatever readings I've missed — and give a verdict on each. Among the possibilities: (a) a bare descriptive prediction that people will escalate once involved, which is a psychological regularity rather than advice and so can't justify anything; (b) a consistency norm about following through on what one has begun, which raises the question of whether entering an auction creates anything worth being consistent with; (c) a sound decision-theoretic threshold claim — that once the remaining position makes one continuation the increment-maximizing move, go all in — under which the proverb is sometimes rational but only because the past bids drop out of the calculation, the opposite of what the proverb seems to appeal to; (d) a claim about reframing sunk costs as a deposit already paid toward the prize, which is arithmetically confused; (e) a reputation or deterrence claim, on which paying to stay in signals that you cannot be entrapped and changes opponents' behavior in future games; (f) anything about option value — that continuing preserves the chance of eventual recovery, which in a dollar auction is exactly the structure that entraps. For each reading: is it true, and does it actually justify continuing at the point she said it — leader and follower both still in, bids at 80 and 90 cents, second-place bid forfeited, a 5-cent minimum increment, and no collusion? Then answer the question I actually care about: which reading do people most likely intend when they deploy the proverb mid-escalation, is that reading one of the defensible ones, and does the proverb's rhetorical power depend on sliding between them — pointing at the rational readings (c) and (e) while doing the work of (d)? Conclude with whether there is any point in a dollar auction, given Shubik-style rules, at which continuing is genuinely rational on a defensible reading, or whether the proverb's folk-wisdom veneer is entirely unearned.
