I teach a second-year undergraduate seminar in ethical theory — 18 students, one 75-minute session, meeting twice a week. We have just finished a unit on consequentialism and are about to turn to Rawls. Design an in-class activity that makes students feel the force of the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism: the complaint that in aggregating welfare across lives, utilitarianism treats the boundaries between persons as morally less significant than the boundaries within a life — as though trade-offs that are perfectly legitimate when a single person makes them about her own future could be made legitimately by others about her, so long as the totals come out right. Students should come away seeing that the case for aggregation across persons has to be argued on its own terms, and cannot simply be inherited from the respectability of prudence within one life.

The activity should have two phases. In the first phase, students should make trade-offs within a single life — accepting present costs for later benefits in a way that feels unremarkable to them, so that the legitimacy of intra-personal trade-offs is established from the inside, by their own choices. In the second phase, the same arithmetic should be re-imposed across persons: a collective decision in which a large benefit to most of the group is funded by a concentrated burden on a few, with the total welfare unquestionably higher than under the alternative, so that the utilitarian verdict is not in doubt — what is in doubt is whether the same reasoning that everyone accepted in phase one licenses it here. Every student should either cast a real vote or occupy a role carrying real (small) stakes, and the design should make it vivid that in phase two the people who benefit and the people who pay are different people, whereas in phase one the beneficiary and the payer were the same person at different times.

Please supply: the complete setup and materials; the precise rules of each phase, including how payoffs are denominated — no real money, so choose something like raffle tickets toward a modest prize or a course-internal currency, and briefly defend your choice; a minute-by-minute timeline for the full 75 minutes; and a debrief script that maps specific moments in the activity onto the components of the objection — why intra-personal trade-offs are prudentially rational, why inter-personal aggregation cannot simply borrow that rationale, and what the burdened minority in phase two possessed that no temporal stage of a single life possesses, namely a standpoint from which to complain. Anticipate what will predictably go wrong on the first run — for instance strategic voting, students who have already read Rawls playing to the intended answer instead of to the incentives, or a designated minority that turns out not to mind its burden — and give concrete handling for each case. Finally, be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: real separateness of persons is a fact about whole lives rather than about an hour, and any in-class currency students can win or lose is a smallness they will notice. Where the game cannot carry the idea on its own, state plainly what the facilitator must supply in the debrief.
