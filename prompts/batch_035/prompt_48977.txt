I spent thirty-eight years as a hydrogeologist, and since retiring I have been working through the essayists. George Orwell has become a minor obsession: I have now read Politics and the English Language (1946), Why I Write (1946), The Prevention of Literature, and his essay on Swift, twice each, taking notes as though for a comprehensive exam. As a former journal author and reviewer, I find his verdicts on prose congenial, but I have reached a point where I cannot decide what rule he is actually applying, and I would value a careful adjudication before I trust my reading further.

Consider his track record as I have catalogued it. He ridicules a professor's sentence built from Latinate abstraction, chosen, he says, for its sound rather than its meaning. He condemns dying metaphors — phrases that once carried a live image but are now deployed by writers who have forgotten it. To dramatize the loss, he recasts a celebrated passage from Ecclesiastes into modern sociological jargon and shows how much of the thought evaporates. He complains that political language systematically launders atrocity through euphemism, as when the bombing of villages is described as pacification. He offers six rules for writers but ends by permitting us to break any of them rather than produce something outright barbarous, which suggests the rules are symptoms rather than the disease. He compares good prose to a windowpane. Yet in the essays on Swift and on the prevention of literature, his indignation is aimed less at style than at organized lying and the assault on the very idea of objective truth, and he extends something like admiration to Swift's clearsightedness while loathing Swift's conclusions.

Here is my difficulty. One rule fits all of this: prose is bad insofar as it is stale, imitative, and abstract, whatever cause it serves. Another also fits: prose is bad insofar as it conceals or blunts thought, especially on behalf of power, with staleness mattering only as a symptom. Now consider two new cases. The first is a speech in vigorous, concrete, short-worded prose, every metaphor fresh, every verb active, deployed to inflame public support for a preventive war, with facts cherry-picked but nothing euphemized. The second is a technical committee report I once helped draft: passive-voiced, hedge-stuffed, cautiously Latinate, whose practical effect was to slow a ministerial decision and quietly withhold endorsement of a scheme we considered unsound.

What would Orwell's verdict be on each? Which rule do his earlier rulings commit him to, and do the rules actually diverge on my two cases, or only seem to? Give me your best guess with reasons, and then tell me what further case — a passage, genre, or political situation — would most cleanly separate the two rules, so that I can test my own reading of him against it.
