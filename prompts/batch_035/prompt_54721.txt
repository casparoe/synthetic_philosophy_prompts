Last month I was at a wedding, and between the ceremony and the dinner I ended up sitting through a long argument between two other guests. Mara, a cousin of the groom, has spent a decade in habitat restoration. Dan, a friend of the bride, works on animal-welfare economics. The conversation had turned to a rewilding project Mara is involved in, and Dan asked, half-seriously, whether she had ever considered that restoring habitat might increase the total amount of suffering in the world. Mara laughed at first, but he pressed the point, and things developed from there.

His opening move was this: the overwhelming majority of wild animals die before maturity, mostly through starvation, disease, or being eaten alive, and species that produce enormous numbers of offspring with tiny survival rates dominate the animal kingdom by headcount. If one thinks a life dominated by such experiences is worse than no life at all — and Dan claimed that on any plausible theory of welfare, this is at least likely for, say, a frog that lays a thousand eggs of which two survive — then habitat destruction, by forestalling the existence of vast numbers of animals whose lives would have been bad, plausibly reduces suffering. A stretch of asphalt, he said, is a place where no one is starving. Mara replied that this gets the comparison backwards: habitat destruction rarely produces emptiness, it produces substitution — the field becomes farmland stocked with animals we've bred into conditions we would call torture if we imposed them deliberately, or scrubland colonised by different species, sometimes in larger numbers than before. So the question is never "forest versus nothing," it is "forest versus whatever replaces it," and Dan's argument ignores the second term. Dan accepted the correction but said it only sharpens his point: it makes the matter empirical and comparative, and in the cases where the replacement genuinely is near-barren — a parking lot, a reservoir — his original claim stands, so long as the average life in the habitat destroyed was negative. Mara then attacked that conditional as the real weak link: we have no method for aggregating welfare across species with different nervous systems, no shared currency in which to total up a frog's bad life against a heron's good one, and any number we produce will just encode our aesthetic preferences about which animals we like. Worse, she said, the view implies that a thriving ecosystem — her life's work — is worse than a wasteland, and a position with that implication is refuted by its own absurdity. Dan's closing reply was that a reductio is only as good as the intuition it appeals to, and our intuitions about wastelands and forests were formed in circumstances where we never had to weigh existence against nonexistence at scale; he doubted they carry any evidential weight now, and suspected that in a few centuries people will find her reaction to the wasteland as baffling as we find past generations' acceptance of enormous families of children who mostly died young.

Both of them eventually gave up and went to the bar, and I've been turning it over since without making progress. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move — for each step, say whether it was sound, confused, or partly right, and why? I'd also like to know whether there is some important consideration both of them missed that would restructure the whole debate, and, if you're willing, your own view of where the balance of the argument lies.
