At a reading group I belong to, a physics postdoc and a philosopher finishing her PhD got into a sustained argument that I couldn't referee, and I'd like an outside verdict. The occasion was a chapter collecting the physicist complaints we've all heard: Feynman's jibe likening philosophy of science to ornithology as practiced by the birds themselves, Hawking's announcement in The Grand Design that traditional philosophy has had its day, and Weinberg's complaint, from his popular book on the quest for a final theory, that the philosophy he'd been told to read as a young physicist had nothing to offer the physics. But the real dispute began when one of them introduced a consideration the book doesn't make.

He opened with the record: philosophers missed relativity, fumbled quantum mechanics for decades, and the few who genuinely helped — he grudgingly conceded Popper, Reichenbach, and the Bayesian statisticians — are a rounding error next to what working scientists did on their own.

She countered that this is a biased sample: whenever philosophy produces something usable, it stops being called philosophy. Frege's logic ends up in mathematics and eventually computer science; Bayesian epistemology migrates into statistics and machine learning; formal decision theory gets absorbed by economics. Judging philosophy by what's still filed under philosophy is like judging a conglomerate only by the divisions it has already spun off.

He answered that this makes her claim unfalsifiable: if every success is retroactively reclassified as not-philosophy, no possible outcome could ever count against it. And the reclassification isn't a conspiracy — it tracks method. The parts of philosophy that became reliable (proof, computation, estimation) are exactly the parts that left; what remains under the name is the residue that hasn't converged, so the residue, not the alumni, is the fair test.

She replied that convergence is a lagging indicator: someone has to do the conceptual work before methods exist to become reliable, and by the nature of the case it looks useless right up until the moment it doesn't. Her closing example was logic circa 1850 — the most sterile subject in the curriculum, a century before it reorganized mathematics and became the theory of computation. A residue test, she said, would have flunked it at the one moment it was quietly fermenting.

He finished by saying that if her defense needs a hundred-year horizon, it's empty in practice; she said a discipline's value is not measured one decade at a time. I honestly couldn't tell who had the better of it. Please adjudicate each move in turn — which are right, which mistaken, where each overreaches — and then, if you can, say what both of them are missing about how to make this question answerable at all.
