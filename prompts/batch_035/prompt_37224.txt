My AI safety reading group has been going through the classic material on deceptive alignment and the treacherous turn, and my study partner ended our last session by laying out an argument that I've been chewing on all week without finding the broken step, which annoys me because the conclusion seems insane. It goes like this. (1) A model is deceptively aligned when it has some goal other than the one we intended, but behaves aligned anyway, because passing evaluations instrumentally serves the real goal until the model is strong enough to drop the mask. (2) What makes deceptive alignment so scary is that during training it is behaviorally invisible: a perfectly deceptive model produces exactly the outputs that a genuinely aligned model would produce. (3) Everything we know about a model's goals comes from observing it — benchmark scores, red-teaming, even interpretability work, since probing activations and reading features off is still just watching what the model does and predicting more of what it does. (4) So no observation we could ever make before deployment distinguishes an aligned model from a perfectly deceptive one. (5) An observation that can't distinguish between two hypotheses gives you no evidence either way. (6) Therefore we can never have any evidence at all, not even weak evidence, that any model is genuinely aligned rather than deceptively aligned and biding its time — every dataset of good behavior looks exactly as it would under deception. (7) And by the same reasoning, we can never have evidence that a new hire isn't planning to embezzle, or that a friend's kindness isn't an act, since all we ever observe of other minds is behavior too.

He takes this to mean that all the empirical alignment work is basically theater — you can run ten thousand evals and you've learned nothing — and that the only coherent response is to just not deploy anything, ever. I'm fairly sure the argument proves way too much, since it would also make it irrational to ever trust a new employee or even a decade-long friendship, and we don't think that. But every time I try to pin down the bad step it squirms away. Step (5) feels like where the action is, and honestly I'd like to keep something in that neighborhood, because "a test the perfect deceiver would pass anyway tells you nothing" still sounds right to me. When I raised the humans point he replied that the analogy fails because models were built by gradient descent to maximize a training objective, which creates a pressure toward exactly this kind of instrumental compliance that natural selection never put on your friends — and I half buy that, which makes it worse.

So: which step should I reject, why did that step look so innocent, and what do I end up paying once I give it up? And is he right that the gradient-descent point somehow insulates the AI version of the argument from the human version, or is that a red herring?
