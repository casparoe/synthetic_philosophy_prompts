So my roommate and I watched the Coen Brothers' A Serious Man again last week and I haven't been able to stop thinking about it. Quick summary in case it's fuzzy: it's about Larry Gopnik, a physics professor in a 1960s Minnesota Jewish suburb, whose life falls apart in every ordinary way at once -- wife wants a divorce for another man, tenure is threatened by someone writing slanderous letters, his brother is a mess, his kids ignore him, he's broke from paying lawyers. He keeps going to the rabbis asking basically "why is this happening to me, what did I do?" and gets nothing back -- one tells him a weird story about teeth, another just tells him to be a good person, the senior rabbi won't even see him. And then the film ends with a tornado bearing down on his son right as Larry gets a call about his medical results. It's basically the Book of Job as suburban comedy, but God never speaks from the whirlwind, the whirlwind just shows up.

Lately I've been reading around online about what philosophers call the "divine hiddenness" argument -- the idea that if a perfectly loving God existed, He wouldn't stay so hidden from people who sincerely want a relationship with Him, since you can't have a relationship with someone who never shows up. And it hit me that this movie is kind of the perfect dramatization of that. Larry isn't an atheist, he's not even much of a doubter, he's a guy who keeps showing up at the door asking to talk and getting silence. There's even that scene where a Korean student bribes him and the whole thing turns on whether we can really know anything, Heisenberg's uncertainty principle, etc -- the film keeps mixing up physics uncertainty with "why won't God answer me" uncertainty, which feels like part of the point.

So what I want is a proper reading of the film through this hiddenness idea. Like: does the movie agree with the hiddenness argument (no loving God is on the other end of Larry's calls), or does it show something the argument misses? The rabbis' non-answers are interesting to me -- the senior one tells him about meeting a rebbe in his youth and being told to "receive with simplicity" whatever happens, which the film treats as almost a revelation to us but gives Larry nothing he can use. And the ending, with the tornado, feels like the film finally answering -- badly. Also the weird prologue with the dybbuk, which never connects to the rest, maybe on purpose? I'm not writing an essay or anything, it's for arguing with my roommate, who thinks the movie is just nihilism and the hiddenness stuff is overthinking it. Where does the film actually land, and where does it resist being read this way?
