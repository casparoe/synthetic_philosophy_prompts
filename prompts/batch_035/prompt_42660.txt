Construct a thought experiment that stress-tests lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism at its weakest joint: the placement of the threshold at which a pain ceases to be commensurable with pleasure.

Two views are at issue. Lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism: pains below a certain severity can be traded off against pleasures on a common scale, but a pain above that threshold cannot be offset by any amount of pleasure, however large; such pains dominate the calculus. Symmetric total utilitarianism: pleasure and pain are commensurable, and the right option is whichever yields the largest net balance of the two across everyone affected.

The scenario must satisfy the following specifications:

1. A realistic setting — a municipal decision, a clinic's policy, or the like. No trolleys, no science fiction, no world-historical stakes.
2. It must present two options on which the two theories deliver opposite verdicts.
3. The divergence must turn on a single variable: whether one person's pain sits just above the lexical threshold. Everything else must be held fixed across the two options.
4. The pleasure at stake must be large only through aggregation — many people each forgoing a modest, ordinary enjoyment — never through stipulated magnitudes. Build the total out of units a reader can intuitively price, such as an afternoon's enjoyment per person, and do not describe any quantity as vast or incalculable.
5. The scenario must be closed to obvious patches: the person in pain cannot avoid the exposure at reasonable cost, and the many cannot obtain equivalent enjoyment elsewhere for free. It must also be purged of non-welfarist inputs — no rights, no desert, no blame — so that welfare is the only thing either theory can register.
6. The scenario itself must be a single paragraph.

After the scenario:

- Walk through each theory's verdict and the reasoning that produces it, checking the scenario against the specifications as you go.
- Identify which feature of the case is doing the work, and show what happens when that one pain is nudged to just below the threshold: the two theories should converge, and you should demonstrate that they do.
- Give the strongest response available to the lexical-threshold negative utilitarian — disputing the threshold's placement, biting the bullet, or amending the view — and tell me which of these you find most promising, and why.

A note on what counts as cheating: the case must not stipulate its verdicts. Do not describe the pain as beyond compensation or as the kind no pleasure could offset; describe it concretely — its character, duration, and intensity as the sufferer would report it — and let the verdicts follow from the theories. Finally, target the boundary: I want a case in which the pain sits near the threshold, not one in which it is comfortably trivial or obviously catastrophic. What I am testing is whether the lexical threshold behaves as a stable feature of the view or dissolves into arbitrariness precisely where it is supposed to matter most.
