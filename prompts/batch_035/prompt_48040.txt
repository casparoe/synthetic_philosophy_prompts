I would like to know whether the following configuration has an established name, either in the literature on vagueness or in logic and semantics more generally.

I have been reading my way into the sorites paradox and the two main reactions to it. On one side, epistemicism says there is an exact cutoff between heap and non-heap and we simply cannot know where it is. On the other side, the view I take to be called supervaluationism says the predicate is indeterminate: no particular number of grains is the boundary. What struck me is a further claim that I keep encountering on the second side. The same theory apparently endorses the sentence "there is a number of grains such that a pile with that many grains is a heap and a pile with one fewer is not" — since every admissible way of sharpening the predicate draws a line somewhere, the sentence comes out true on every sharpening and hence true overall — while the theory refuses to endorse any instance of the form "such-and-such a number is the cutoff." The theory thus asserts an existential while disowning every one of its witnesses.

Once I noticed this, I began seeing the same shape elsewhere. Before a fair lottery draw, it is settled that someone will win, though it is not settled of anyone in particular that they will. And in the old debate about future sea battles, I understand that a disjunction can be treated as settled even while neither disjunct is. Someone told me that the vagueness literature distinguishes two readings of the existential quantifier — one ranging over the ordinary candidate objects, one somehow ranging over candidate meanings themselves — but I cannot tell whether that is a real, named distinction or whether my informant was gesturing at something else.

So my questions are these. Does this configuration — a theory's endorsing an existential while rejecting, or being unable to state, any of its instances — have a recognized name in logic or semantics? Within the vagueness debate specifically, is the status of the "there is a cutoff" sentence a standard point of contention, with standard objections and replies I should know about? How exactly does the supervaluationist's position here differ from the epistemicist's, given that both accept the unqualified existential and disagree only about whether it has a knowable witness? Is the phenomenon, in the end, merely a scope or reading ambiguity in the quantifier, or is something deeper at stake? I recognize that the configuration is adjacent to the problem of the many, but as I understand that puzzle it concerns an overabundance of candidate objects, whereas here there seems to be exactly one candidate, so to speak, that no candidate determines. If there is no established term for the configuration I have described, I would be grateful for a coined one, with a definition and some boundary cases; a bare label would not help me much, since what I want is a precise account of what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
