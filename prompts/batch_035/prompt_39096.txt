I lead a small estimation team at a mid-size engineering firm — our whole job is telling other departments how long projects will take and what they'll cost. I got sold on the outside view idea after reading some forecasting stuff: don't reason forward from the details of YOUR project, start from how similar projects actually went, because the details are mostly wishful thinking. Sounds great, but every time I bring it to a meeting it dies one of two ways. Either someone says "every project is different, you can always find a reference class that gives you whatever number you wanted," which... I don't have a good answer to. Or someone says "the whole point of our expertise is the inside view — if we can't use what we know about OUR parts supply chain, what are we even being paid for," which I also don't have a good answer to. And there's a third thing I can feel but can't name, where the team defers way too much to whichever engineer is most confident in the room, which seems like the opposite of epistemic modesty but I'm not sure the outside-view stuff actually says anything about that.

So I want a procedure we can actually run inside an estimation meeting — one hour, engineers with zero patience for philosophy, a number has to come out the other end. Not a lecture on reference classes. Something like: steps in order, when the outside view is allowed to override inside reasoning and when it isn't, how to handle the reference class selection problem (how many projects before a class isn't gerrymandered, what to do when two plausible classes give very different numbers), what actually counts as "we fixed the thing that made the last three projects late" as opposed to a story about how we fixed it, and how to fold in the room's individual estimates without the loudest person just winning. Mark clearly where the procedure runs out and it becomes a judgment call I have to own as the lead — I don't want it pretending to be mechanical where it isn't. Also tell me where it will predictably misfire; I'd rather know now.

Then run it on three of our actual kinds of jobs: (1) a cooling retrofit that's late because of a parts supply issue we think we've genuinely solved, (2) an internal software migration where the reference class of enterprise migrations says 2x over budget every single time but we're using a new method the vendor swears changes the game, and (3) a first-of-its-kind pilot for the company — some AI-based inspection thing — where I honestly cannot find a reference class that isn't cherry-picked to give the answer someone wants. At least one of these should come out with an answer I won't like — I suspect the migration one, since I already committed a number to the client — and tell me what I'm supposed to say to my team when the procedure contradicts me.
