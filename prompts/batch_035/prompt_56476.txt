I teach a second-year module on epistemology, though my own research lies elsewhere, so Fitch's paradox of knowability sits at the edge of my competence rather than at its centre. A colleague who knows of the module sent me a chapter from a popular trade book on the limits of knowledge, thinking it might suit my students. Its summary of the paradox reads: "In 1963, Frederic Fitch published a short proof that verificationism — the once fashionable doctrine that a statement is meaningful only if it can in principle be verified — is false. The argument runs that if every truth could in principle be known by someone at some time, then every truth is in fact already known, which is obviously absurd. So either every truth is already known, or else there are truths that could never be known even in principle. The proof is purely formal and goes through in any system of logic, which is why it is usually described as having dealt verificationism a fatal blow. The lesson we are left with is that reality outstrips any possible inquiry: some truths are forever closed to us." Before I put this in front of students, I would like an audit of its fairness. I count five distinct assertions in the passage. Please give me exactly five numbered points, one per assertion in the order they appear, each a sentence or two stating whether a logician working on the knowability paradox would accept it, wince at it, or reject it. Two half-memories of mine should be folded into whichever points they belong to, if they belong to any: first, that Dummett-style anti-realists lean on intuitionistic logic and that this matters to how the proof goes through; second, that some philosophers responded to the paradox not by abandoning the knowability principle but by restricting which truths it covers. I am inclined to trust the chapter's confident tone, which is precisely why I would like the audit. Please give the five points only, with no introduction or conclusion.
