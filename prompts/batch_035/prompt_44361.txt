I direct a five-person risk-analysis unit at a state agency that publishes a quarterly bulletin ranking the hazards facing our region — flooding, wildfire, dam failure, contamination of the water supply, and newer entries such as cyber incidents at public utilities. Each hazard line carries a probability, and the numbers are a mess. Some come from frequency data (vehicle and workplace accident rates), some from engineering models (dam breach analyses), and some are one-off judgments by our senior analyst, such as a figure for the regional water authority consolidating within a decade. We were recently embarrassed in a legislative hearing when a member asked what the "100-year flood" figure means for a specific river gauge next spring: two of my analysts gave incompatible answers, one treating it as a long-run frequency and one as a degree of belief, and neither addressed the fact that the public will read "1% per year" as a claim about next year at that spot.

Please write a review procedure my team can run on every probability statement before it goes into the bulletin, built on the actual differences between the interpretations of probability: relative frequency in a reference class, degree of belief — and whose belief, anchored to what evidence — and, if we ever want to invoke it, single-case propensity. The procedure should consist of ordered steps. For a candidate statement, it should first settle what kind of quantity the number could be: is there a defensible reference class with real frequency data behind it, is the event a token one-off, is the number anchored to a model or to a person. It should then specify what grounding and what checks each kind requires — for example, that a subjective number be attributed to a stated body of evidence and, where possible, scored later against outcomes for calibration. It should mark where the procedure terminates with "publish no number, use words," and where it stops being a matter of facts and hands the decision to an editor. I specifically want the reference-class problem named and handled honestly: when our dam-failure figure depends on whether the class is "earthen dams of this vintage nationally" or "earthen dams on comparable geology in this state," the choice changes the number by an order of magnitude, and no formula selects the class for us.

Constraints: a reviewer gets fifteen minutes per statement; the team is one statistician, two engineers, and two generalists, so nothing can assume fluency in measure theory or Bayesian formalism; and every published number must be traceable, because these figures get quoted in hearings, so each step should leave a one-line record of what was checked.

Then run the procedure on three statements from our current draft: (1) a 1% annual chance of a flood exceeding the 100-year stage at a named gauge, where the return-period figure comes from a national method but will be read as next year's risk at that location; (2) a 0.2% probability of a 1931 earthen dam failing within twenty years, where our two candidate reference classes disagree by an order of magnitude; and (3) the senior analyst's 30% figure for water-authority consolidation within a decade — a number I would like to keep, and on which I want the procedure applied honestly even if it strips the number or forces me to sign off on an explicit judgment call. Close by saying what the procedure cannot do — in particular, whether it can make two analysts converge on the same number for the one-off cases, and whether anything short of rewriting the bulletin can stop the public from reading every figure as a claim about next year.
