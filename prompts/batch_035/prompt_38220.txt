I'm a PhD student in economics, working at the intersection of welfare analysis and revealed preference theory; I'm fluent in that toolkit (preference orderings, utility representations, the welfare-theoretic practice of "correcting" preferences for misinformation or incoherence before using them in welfare judgments, social welfare functions, Pareto rankings) but my philosophical training is patchy. In a reading group I encountered Bernard Williams's distinction between internal and external reasons for action, and I want it translated into my vocabulary. As I understand it: an internal reason for an agent to do something is a consideration that connects to the agent's existing motivations through some route of correct reasoning — deliberation can extend, rearrange, and even create new motivations, but it has to start from something already in the agent's motivational set. An external reason would be a consideration that counts in favor of the agent's doing something even though no such deliberative route from that agent's motivations would ever reach it. Williams's stock case is a husband who has stopped caring about his wife: the externalist insists he still has a reason to treat her with consideration, while Williams's internalist says that claim is either covertly true because some concern of his can be reached from it by correct deliberation, or else empty.

Here is the translation task. Take the claim that an agent has most reason to choose a particular option. In my vocabulary, the natural first pass is: that option tops the agent's preference ordering. But my discipline distinguishes several orderings of an agent's options that can come apart: her raw revealed preferences, her stated preferences, her preferences corrected for misinformation and incoherence (as in the welfare-economic practice of laundering), other people's preferences over her options, and social rankings that aggregate everyone's preferences with whatever weights. So please map the two positions onto this apparatus. I would guess internalism says: an option is one the agent has most reason to choose only if it would top her own ordering after suitable correction — where the correction is meant to model Williams's requirement of a sound deliberative route. And externalism says: there is a fact about which option the agent has most reason to choose that outranks her own corrected ordering — a ranking of her options that counts in her favor but is not any preference ordering of hers, nor obviously anyone else's, nor a straightforward aggregation of them. My worry is that my vocabulary literally has no place for a ranking that is no one's: the only things in the toolbox that order an agent's options are her preferences (raw or corrected), somebody else's, or a social welfare function built out of preferences. So the externalist's claim may be exactly the thing I cannot so much as state.

Please do this in three parts, and be honest about the translation rather than smuggling in assumptions. First, restate the internalism/externalism debate as faithfully as you can in my terms — preference orderings, corrections, social rankings — flagging at each point where you had to choose a substantive interpretation that the philosophical debate itself leaves open (for instance, whether Williams's deliberative requirement is really equivalent to my full-information corrections, or whether it is weaker or stranger than that, since deliberation on his picture can transform motivations in ways my laundering procedure was never built to model). Second, give me the residue report: which parts of the debate carry over cleanly, which come over only distorted, and which cannot be expressed in the economic vocabulary at all; and where something cannot be expressed, say whether that reflects a blind spot in my vocabulary or unclarity in the original debate — whether the externalist is pointing at something real that preference-based welfare analysis cannot see, or whether the fact that I cannot state the claim without positing a ranking that belongs to no one is a sign that the claim was less clear than it looked. Third, and briefly, tell me what an economist who took the externalist verdict seriously would have to change in welfare practice, if anything. Please close with a one-sentence judgment as the final line, in the form Verdict: followed by your verdict on the residue question — whether it is better read as a limitation of the economic vocabulary or as an artifact of unclarity in the philosophical debate.
