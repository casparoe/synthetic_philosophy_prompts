I've recently been elected to the management committee of my apartment building, and a long-stalled dispute over the shared courtyard has landed on me. Residents want to grill on the roof, smoke on the balcony, and ride scooters through the courtyard at all hours; other residents want all three banned. What surprises me is that both camps argue in the name of Mill's harm principle: one side says no rule is justified without a victim, the other says the victim is all of us taken together. Before drafting any bylaw, I want to know whether the configuration underneath this argument has a name.

The configuration, described concretely: each individual act is not slightly harmful but, taken alone, harmless. One scooter trip at walking speed startles no one; one cigarette on the shared balcony changes nobody's lungs; one evening of charcoal smoke makes no difference anyone could detect. Yet the same acts, done by many people every day, produce a perfectly ordinary bodily harm — an asthma flare, a collision injury — and, crucially, no single instance can be identified as the one that caused it: remove any one scooter trip from the year's total and the outcome is exactly the same. I've already managed to sort out some neighbors. This is not paternalism, because the harm falls on others. It is not mere offense, because the end state is disease and injury rather than discomfort. And it is not the straightforward case my committee agrees is regulable, where each act does a small but perceptible harm to someone each time (the leaf blower at 7 a.m., every morning, annoyingly but detectably).

There are two further twists I keep chewing on. First, one member argues that an act making no perceptible difference to anyone's welfare cannot be a harm at all, so the aggregate harm has no wrongmakers, and the harm principle licenses nothing here — which suggests the principle goes silent precisely where modern regulation bites hardest. Second, another member likened it to driving home drunk at 3 a.m. on empty streets: no one is harmed, yet we punish it. I half-remember Mill himself discussing a man about to cross an unsafe bridge, and something about the sale of poison, which suggests he thought risk itself could count — but the risk case and my aggregate case feel like different animals wearing the same coat, and I don't know whether the literature treats them together or apart.

So: is there an established concept for exactly this configuration — individually harmless acts whose aggregate is harmful, with a victim that none of the acts alone wronged? If there is, I'm not satisfied with a bare name. Tell me where its edges are: how it differs from its nearest neighbors (free riding, the production of public bads, risk imposition that luckily never materializes, the small-but-perceptible-harm case), whether the aggregate version and the risk version are genuinely one problem or two, and what the standard answers say a harm principle must concede or absorb to handle it. If nothing established fits the configuration exactly, say so plainly, coin a term for it, define it, and give me a couple of boundary cases that test the definition. Throughout, please flag how confident you are in each identification — in particular, where you are naming a real, established concept and where you are carving the territory yourself — and single out the point in your answer you are least sure of. I would much rather have an honest hedge than a smooth-sounding but invented certainty.
