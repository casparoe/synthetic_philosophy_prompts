My neurologist mentioned a trial still in its planning stage: replacing those neurons, batch by batch, with synthetic cells. The catch is an engineering one — each batch needs about a week of offline calibration, running recordings of my past neural activity, before it can be brought online. So when a synthetic batch goes live, its first states were caused by a week-old recording of me, not by my living tissue in real time.

Construct a thought experiment around this: the replacement preserves overall function and, in the end, every affected neuron is swapped, but each handover is lagged — the new component's first live moment replays a week-old pattern rather than answering the present brain. Then walk through what a functionalist, a Parfit-style psychological-continuity theorist, and an animalist would each say about whether the person who wakes up at the end is me, and specifically whether the lag matters: is causal dependence at the moment of handover essential to gradual replacement, or is eventual functional equivalence enough? Constraints: the scenario itself should be one paragraph, set in an ordinary clinical setting, and the lag must come from the calibration requirement — no perfect copying, no whole-brain scanning, no exotic technology.

The point of the case, and what I need to decide whether I'd enroll, is to isolate whether the causal wiring at handover rather than the final functional profile is what my survival would turn on. So after the walkthrough, tell me which feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to each verdict if the lag is removed with everything else held fixed, and how someone who denies survival in the lagged version while granting it in standard gradual replacement would defend drawing the line there.
