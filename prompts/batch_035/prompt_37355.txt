I co-run a little discussion group — mostly people who worked on ML products and now read political philosophy on the weekends — and our next session is on whose values an aligned AI should actually serve. Last time we tried "what does alignment even mean" and it went of the rails into vibes, so this time I definately want a sorting instrument: a decision tree that places people on an actual position about value aggregation, built from concrete cases rather than from labels people already identify with. The positions I want as candidate leaves: the personalized-pluralist view (every user gets an AI serving that user's own values), the majoritarian view (one shared AI reflecting pooled majority opinion), liberal neutralism (a thin set of values everyone could in principle accept, with individual-rights trumps layered on top), the trustee/epistocratic view (the lab, or some constitution-drafting process, fixes the values, on the theory that it's accountable in a different way than a poll is), the cosmopolitan-aggregation view (everyone affected counts equally, not just users or paying customers), and the proceduralist view (values come out of a bargain or a procedure — think Nash bargaining or a veil-of-ignorance setup — rather than out of a survey). If some combination of answers lands somewhere nobody actually defends, say so inside the item where it would have had to split, rather than papering over it, and if two of these positions would answer all the questions identically, flag that too — that's exactly the kind of thing I want to argue about in the session itself.

Build the tree from the logical space, not from the history of the literature, so every branch question has to be a case someone can answer without knowing which position it leads to. The angles that matter for our group: whose preferences even enter the pool (users only? everyone affected? future people? animals?); whether it's okay for there to be many AIs with different values, or whether there has to be one shared set; and when the pool itself conflicts, what settles it — raw stated opinion, values after idealized reflection (the 2am pizza-ordering self versus the New Year's-resolutions self), some thin overlapping consensus, or whoever built the thing. Seed cases you can draw on, or improve on: a solid majority of a chatbot's users in some country considers a practice deeply wrong while the lab's home country treats it as a basic right — whose values does the shared bot serve there; one user asks their personal assistant to treat scripture as authoritative while another asks theirs for relentless secular skepticism — fine, or does the pluralism itself smuggle in a value; and someone asks an AI to write a maximally persuasive op-ed for their side — do the values of the readers who'll be persuaded by it count in the pool or not.

On format: give me exactly five numbered items, nothing before item 1 and nothing after item 5. Each item is one branch question, stated as the concrete case, followed in the same breath by which positions from my list (or "unnamed") fall on each side of answering it one way or the other. One or two sentences per item, so keep each case crisp — I'd rather have five sharp questions than a taxonomy essay. No intro, no conclusion, and no "well, it depends on how you define values" hedging; the whole point is to force the splits.
