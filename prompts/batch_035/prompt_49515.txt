I live in a forty-unit condo building, and I've been on the board for two years, which has gotten me thinking about how we charge for shared things. We do it at least four different ways, and I can't tell whether there's a principle underneath it all or just habit.

Repairs to the roof, the lobby, and the hallways come out of the general fund, which every unit pays into at a flat rate — the same dollar amount for the studio on the third floor as for the three-bedroom penthouse twice its size. Nobody chose that; it's in the original declaration from 1978. Our gym used to work the same way, but last year, after owners who never use it complained that they were subsidizing the heavy users, we installed fob readers and switched to per-visit fees, with the money going back into the general fund. The guest parking spots we rent out at market rate to whoever books first, including outsiders, and the profit subsidizes everyone's dues. And the bike room is left unlocked while the storage cages are individually locked and assigned by annual lottery.

Here's what's bothering me. When the roof leaks, no one can be excluded from the repair, and no one can use more of it than anyone else. Once the gym was fobbed, we could finally track who uses what. When we rent parking to outsiders, we're not sharing among ourselves at all anymore — we're selling. So the same group of people is simultaneously splitting costs equally regardless of use, charging by use, and operating as a small business. When I raised this at a board meeting, one member said flat dues are fair because you can't keep anyone out of a good roof, and another said per-visit gym fees are fair because everyone should pay for what they take. Both sounded right to me, and they can't both be the principle.

So my question is this: does our building's setup actually presuppose some consistent view of when a shared cost should be split equally versus charged by use versus sold — something about the nature of the thing being shared, or about fairness, or nothing at all? Is the mix defensible because the goods really are different in kind, or is it just that each rule was made at a different moment by a different group of annoyed people? And here's the part I'd like a definite answer to rather than a menu: if you had to pick one principle the building should commit to going forward, which would it be, why that one, and what would change about our actual rules — especially the fob system — if we adopted it?

Some of this is practical, but I'm more interested in whether there's a real line here: is there a genuine difference between things people share because they can't practically be divided up (the roof, the hallways) and things people share only as a convenience (the gym, the guest suite, the parking), or is that a line we're drawing rather than finding? If the line is real, tell me what it is in plain terms. If it isn't, tell me why the fob decision nevertheless felt obviously right to almost everyone at the time.

I'd like a committed answer: take a definite position and defend it, don't lay out the options and leave me to choose. Write in American English. And I should say upfront that I have no background in economics or philosophy — if there's a standard name for what I'm circling around, go ahead and name it, but don't assume I've read anything.
