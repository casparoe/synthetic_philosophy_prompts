I'm putting together materials for a reading group on peer disagreement, and the centerpiece I want is a mock adversarial-collaboration document. The setup: two philosophers who've been arguing about conciliationism vs. steadfastness for literally a decade — one thinks that when someone you judge to be your epistemic peer disagrees with you, you should move your credence at least partway toward theirs; the other thinks you're often permitted (or even required) to hold your original view and just treat their dissent as noise. Their original fight started over a now-infamous seminar incident: they both worked the same tricky conditional-probability problem, got different answers, each checked the other's work and found nothing wrong, and then spent months fighting over whether the fact of the disagreement itself gave them each reason to hedge. (The steadfast one turned out to be right about the math, which he has never once let anyone forget.)

Draft the joint statement they'd produce after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want a numbered section of claims both can sign — think: things about what peerhood actually amounts to, why disagreement in arithmetic feels different from disagreement about politics or wine, that neither side is committed to the absurd extremes (total credence-splitting with anyone who disagrees, or never moving an inch no matter what), and so on. Then a disagreements table where the residual disagreement is stated in wording each side would actually accept for its own position — no slanted phrasing where the conciliationist's view is described as "epistemic self-flagellation" or the steadfast theorist's as "arrogance" (they've tried both).

Then the cruxes. My suspicion is the big ones are (a) whether the mere fact of the peer's dissent is evidence about the object-level question, or only about what you should believe about the object level (if that distinction even holds up), (b) the self-undermining problem — conciliationism disagrees with steadfastness, so does the conciliationist have to split the difference with the steadfast theorist about conciliationism itself — and (c) whether "you should give some weight to the peer's view" is compatible with "you should keep your original credence," i.e., whether some of this fight is verbal. For each crux, give a concrete case or argument both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance — the Monty Hall / solved-math-problem cases are fair game, as are cases about experts (two equally good doctors, two forecasters) and cases where the disagreeer's belief was formed via some suspicious route. If you think part of their dispute really does dissolve once the words are cleaned up, say which part and why; the two of them would want that on the record. Format: numbered agreed claims first, then the table, then cruxes with predictions. Please make both sides look good — they'll each read the final draft before signing, and they're both insufferable about being strawmanned.
