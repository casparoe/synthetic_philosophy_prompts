So I write about science and ideas for a mid-size outlet, and my editor greenlit a piece I'm calling "does the gig economy exploit you, or just annoy you." The framing I want to run with is a credence, which is maybe a weird way to structure a magazine piece but whatever, it worked for the climate guys. So here's what I want from you: an actual number for the probability that a typical full-time gig worker in a rich country — say a rideshare driver in the US — is exploited in something close to Marx's sense, and then the full decomposition of where that number comes from. Not a hedge, a number.

Here's the twist I want built in, because I don't think this exact scenario has been done to death. Imagine a hypothetical platform where every single task is priced by a live auction, so at any moment the worker captures pretty much their exact marginal product — no surplus being siphoned off, at least not in any obvious way. Call it MarginalProductDrive or whatever. The question I keep chewing on is whether that platform kills the exploitation charge entirely, or whether it just relocates it. My hunch is the answer depends on a bunch of separable sub-questions, and I want you to make those explicit and put a rough probability on each. Like: does exploitation even need anything resembling the labor theory of value, or can it survive being recast as something about distributive shares or domination? Does the "no meaningful exit" condition still hold when the worker technically has other jobs available? And — this is where I suspect the story actually is — is alienation doing completely different work than exploitation, such that you could have a driver who is not exploited but is maximally alienated, or vice versa? Because algorithmic management feels like it should intensify the alienation side (the app owns the product, the route, the rating, the customer relationship) while maybe muddying the exploitation side.

So give me the sub-questions with a probability and one line of justification each, tell me how they combine into your final number, and then tell me which sub-question you'd most want resolved before you'd trust the headline. Also be honest about which one you're basically guessing on. And if your answer is going to start with "Marx distinguished four types of alienation" and then just walk through them — I can get that anywhere, my readers can get that anywhere, the whole point of this piece is to get past it.
