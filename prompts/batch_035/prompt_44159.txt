Argued with my friend Dara until 2am last night about dutch book arguments and I'm still annoyed. Her position: if your credences violate the probability axioms, a bookie who knows only your credences can offer bets you'll accept at prices guaranteeing a net loss, and that's what incoherence IS — a structural vulnerability, whether or not any bookie ever shows up. I raised the usual stuff: you can just refuse bets; risk aversion and nonlinear utility break the identification of credence with betting price; the bookie's epistemic access to your credences is a weird thing to bake into the setup. She had a reply to each and eventually retreated to the logical reading — the book is just a way of displaying an inconsistency, and complaining that nobody in real life accepts both sides of a sure-loss bet is like refusing to read a proof because you don't like the font. What I can't figure out is whether she's qualified the arguement into something that can't lose, and if so whether that's even a defect for an argument of this kind or just what normative arguments are like. So: what would actually count against her view? I'm especially interested in the diachronic versions — bets placed at different times, conditioning, whether the agent can see the book coming — and whether those collapse the pragmatic and logical readings back together somehow. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be clear about which ones a committed defender like her would conceed versus dismiss as missing the point.
