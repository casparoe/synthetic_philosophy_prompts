Here's the pedagogical target. The aggregation problem. The thing I want them to *feel* is the tension between two instincts: (a) that when you've got one person facing a very bad outcome versus a large number of people each facing a tiny burden, something has to give — at some point the many small burdens seem to outweigh the one big one — and (b) that contractualism, as Scanlon sets it up, is meant to resist exactly that kind of adding-up, because it compares complaints one person at a time and the individualist restriction is supposed to stop you from summing across lives. What I'd love is for them to hit this themselves: they make a series of pairwise choices, each of which seems obviously right at the time, and only at the end do they notice where they've arrived — basically they've walked themselves into endorsing aggregation, and then they have to figure out which step they want to take back and what it costs to take it back there. If you can get the structure of a transitive aggregation–style sequence into the game mechanics (i.e., the slippery slope built out of choices they individually endorse, not a lecture about it), that's ideal.

Practical constraints: 12 students, one 90-minute block, a normal seminar room, whiteboard, whatever printable materials you'd like, no real money or real stakes beyond token payoffs (points, bragging rights, chocolate — your call, but keep it simple). The game part should take maybe 50-60 min with the rest for debrief. No lecture up front — they shouldn't get told what the point is until the debrief, and even the debrief should mostly be them reconstructing what they did and where it went, with me only supplying names for the moves they already made.
