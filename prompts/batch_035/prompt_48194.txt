I'm a final-year PPE student taking a seminar on the history of economic thought, and in two weeks I'm presenting a paper defending a particular reading of the socialist calculation debate. My thesis is that Mises's 1920 calculation problem and Hayek's later knowledge problem are two different objections that get run together, and that only the second one survives the algorithmic socialists' reply. My sketch: Mises argued that without market prices for capital goods a planner cannot compute an efficient plan, but that objection, taken literally, is about computation — and between Lange's trial-and-error market socialism in the 1930s, Kantorovich's linear programming, and modern computing, it looks answered in principle. Hayek's 1945 argument, I want to claim, was never about computing power: the knowledge that prices aggregate is dispersed, tacit, and partly not articulable until the moment of use — the particular knowledge of time and place held by a machine operator or a shop floor manager — so no archive of data, however large, contains it, and a plan computed from data that doesn't yet exist is built on a fiction. I argue that this survives Cockshott and Cottrell's 1993 revival of detailed computer planning, because their case rests on treating economic knowledge as a stock of recorded information rather than a process of discovery — that's the crux, and it's the part I'm least sure of.
