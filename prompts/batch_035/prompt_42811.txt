My roommate is really into decision theory (endless LessWrong rabbit holes, says the word "updateless" maybe forty times a day) and he's got an argument that's been bugging me for a week. Background, since he made me memorize the setups: Counterfactual mugging — Omega flips a fair coin in secret. If it comes up heads, Omega gives you $1000 iff you would have paid $100 in the tails case; if it comes up tails, you're simply asked to pay $100 for nothing. Before the flip, the policy "pay" nets you +$450 in expectation, so the updateless agent pays in the tails world. Fine, I accept that. XOR blackmail — a predictor knows whether there's damaging dirt on you (50/50) and sends you a letter iff (there's dirt XOR you would pay upon recieving a letter). If the letter shows up, you can pay $1000 to prevent publication; publication costs you $10,000; if no letter shows up and there's dirt, it just gets published anyway. Run the policies ex ante: a payer pays $1000 exactly when there's no dirt and eats publication when there is; a refuser only ever eats publication when there's dirt. Refusing comes out ahead by $500 in expectation, and my roommate agrees the updateless agent refuses. So far so good.

Here's the part that's eating me. (1) Updatelessness just says: pick your policy before the uncertainty resolves, by expected payoff across the ways things could go, and follow it. (2) Applied to blackmail it says refuse, and the reason it says refuse is that the payer's payments never once stop a publication — a payer's letter only ever arrives in no-dirt worlds, so every dollar he pays is for nothing. (3) Now look at the mugging. In the tails world, the payment is likewise for nothing: the coin is already tails, the $1000 was never on the table, Omega's opinion of you is already fixed. (4) So if "your payments are for nothing" settles blackmail for refusing, it settles the mugging for refusing too. (5) But refusing the mugging is exactly what boring old causal decision theory says, so updatelessness only gives its fancy answers when nothing's at stake and collapses into CDT the moment things get weird. (6) Hence, he concludes, either pay both or refuse both, and since nobody sane pays the blackmailer, you should refuse the mugger too.

Something in there is doing illegitimate work but every time I try to pin it down it squirms away on me. For what it's worth, I'd like to keep both the standard verdicts — pay the mugger, refuse the blackmailer — partly because everyone I've seen defend refusing the mugging had an axe to grind, but he's started saying I only like that pair because I've memorized the answer key. Which step should I reject, what exactly is wrong with it, and if rejecting it means biting a bullet somewhere else, I'd like to know how big the bullet is before I take it.
