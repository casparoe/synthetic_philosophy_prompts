I'm on my city's charter review commission, and in two weeks I have to present a proposal I've been championing: replacing our at-large city council seats with a sortition-based citizens' assembly -- members drawn by lot from the voter rolls, like jury duty but you can decline, serving a single non-renewable three-year term, paid well enough that ordinary people can afford to serve, with a small professional staff of policy analysts who brief but don't decide. The idea is to break the grip of the local party machines that have basically made council seats a career ladder for the same five or six families of local politicians. My angle is that sortition and term limits are two sides of one coin: the lottery guarantees anyone can serve, the one-term rule guarantees nobody builds a career out of it, and together they attack the thing elections can't touch -- the selection effect where the people who want power for a living are exactly the people who get it.

The audience is going to be brutal though. Half the room will be the city councilmembers themselves plus their aides, who obviously have a stake in the current system and will come armed with arguments about accountability and voter choice. The other half will be a contingent from the political science department at the state university -- these are people who study democratic institutions, some of them have done work on citizen assemblies in Ireland and elsewhere, and one of them in particular is a skeptic who thinks deliberative bodies are great for legitimacy but terrible for actual governing, that random citizens will just defer to whoever staffs them. And the mayor, who keeps bringing up the 1970s-era arguments about competence and "you wouldn't want a jury doing surgery" -- though I suspect he's really worried about his own patronage network.

Draft my prep sheet. Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll actually come, phrased the way each group would put it (the council people will frame things one way, the political scientists very differently, and they'll talk past each other). For each, tell me what I can genuinely concede -- I want to be honest, some of these objections are just right, like the one about concentrated staff power -- what I have to hold, and where my reply is weakest so I'm not blindsided. Rank them by how much damage they could do to the proposal's chances, not by how philosophically interesting they are. And include the question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks, because I've never had a good answer to it and want one before the meeting. Don't give me a wishy-washy "here are some possible responses" list -- commit to specific replies and tell me which lines I should actually hold if pressed, even the uncomfortable ones.
