I'm a journalist who mostly covers science and ideas for a regional outlet, and because of some pieces I did on the transplant waitlist I got asked to sit on the community advisory council of our regional organ procurement organization. Now the council has an actual fight on its hands. Our state legislature is likely to take up a bill next session that would create a five-year pilot program paying living kidney donors a fixed payment of around $50,000 in cash, on top of covering lost wages and travel, and the OPO board wants a written recommendation from us before it decides whether to endorse the bill, oppose it, or stay neutral. The council is split badly. One of the nephrologists points out that people on the waitlist are dying at a rate of about thirteen a day nationally and thinks anything that adds donors is worth trying, while a minister on the council says flatly that paying for kidneys turns the bodies of the poor into supply depots for the rich, and a couple of members keep citing the old Titmuss blood study and worrying about crowding out altruistic donors the way paid blood may have crowded out volunteers. Someone also keeps bringing up Iran, which has run a paid kidney system for decades, though none of us really knows what to make of the evidence there.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before our vote next month. It needs a concrete bottom line -- endorse, oppose, endorse with conditions, or recommend neutrality -- with reasoning the other council members could actually respond to, and it should take seriously the strongest point on whichever side loses, not just restate the winning case. Where it helps, engage the commodification literature: what's supposed to be distinctive about markets in body parts versus, say, paying people for dangerous work or for plasma (which the US already allows), and whether the in-kind versus cash distinction does any real work or is just symbolic. Also please be careful to flag where the empirical ground is settled versus where it's genuinely contested among people who know the topic -- e.g. what's actually known about whether compensation crowds out or crowds in donors, and where the crowding-out worry rests more on the blood analogy than on kidney evidence -- and don't present a contested reading as consensus.

One formatting thing: our council chair hates documents that look like slide decks, so no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. Continuous prose only, like a proper letter. Also if you do lean on a contested reading of the crowding-out research or on the Iranian experience, say so plainly rather than slipping it in as fact -- I'll get an angry email from the nephrologist if I recieve a memo that papers over the disputes.
