I manage a four-engineer team at a mid-sized company, and I have modest discretion over our hiring funnel — I can add or drop at most one screening stage per quarter, with no budget for outside tools or consultants. We keep having the same argument: does our take-home assignment actually distinguish strong engineers from weak ones, or does it just filter for candidates with spare evenings? Please turn the economics and evolutionary biology of costly signaling — the core idea that a trait functions as a signal only if it is differentially costly, cheaper for genuinely high-quality candidates to produce than for low-quality candidates to fake, together with the way signals decay into wasteful arms races once everyone learns to produce them — into a decision procedure I can run on any proposed screening requirement before adopting it: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a no, and where it hands the decision back to me because the question is one of values rather than facts, such as how many good candidates we're prepared to lose to a signal that genuinely works. The procedure should be runnable in under an hour per stage by me and a recruiter, it should distinguish requirements whose cost the candidate pays from assessments whose cost we pay, and it should be honest about the fact that we can never empirically validate our screens, since we only ever observe the candidates we let through. Then run it on three of our current ideas: an eight-hour take-home, preferring candidates with public open-source contributions, and a live whiteboard-coding round I'm personally attached to — and don't spare the last one if the procedure kills it.
