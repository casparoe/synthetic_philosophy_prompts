I run a weekly reading group on AI alignment — about a dozen grad students plus a couple of motivated undergrads, 90 minutes per session. Next session's topic is the claim that you can get safety out of debate: put two AIs on opposite sides of a question, let a human judge pick the winner, and truth supposedly wins because lying is harder than truth-telling. The worry we're reading about is obfuscated arguements — arguments that look perfectly fine, are checkable in principle, but have a flaw buried somewhere in the middle that takes way longer to find than the whole thing took to produce. If a debater can crank those out, debate stops being a shortcut around verifying everything yourself, and the safety story wobbles.

I want an in-class game where they run into that rather than hear about it. Half-formed idea, feel free to improve or discard: some of them play debaters who score points for convincing the judge regardless of truth, and the key move is that the "arguments" are things I hand out on paper in advance — like multi-step word problems or short chains of reasoning where exactly one step is quietly wrong — so the debaters don't need to be geniuses, they just need to defend the handout. Judges get a limited cross-examination budget: some number of questions or some number of minutes, not enough to re-derive every step. I've never run anything like this and I don't trust my instincts on the payoff scheme, so please actually design it.

Things I care about:

- No real money, no laptops. Paper handouts are fine, and I can prep flawed arguments beforehand, so you can tell me what shape they should take (how many steps, what kind of error, how deep to bury it).
- The debaters shouldn't be able to just win on charisma or volume. I want the game to isolate the verification asymmetry itself: producing a plausible-looking 15-step argument is fast, checking all 15 steps is slow.
- Participants shouldn't have read the obfuscated-argument stuff beforehand or at least shouldn't have the label in their heads while playing — they should notice the problem before anyone names it.

Give me the setup, roles, rules, payoff scheme, and a timeline that fits 90 minutes. Then a debrief that maps what happened onto the debate-safety claim — including both directions: where the game shows debate working (an honest opponent pointing at the bad step is a huge shortcut) and where it shows the loophole. Also: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (my guesses are that judges rubber-stamp anything confident, or conversely that a sharp student sniffs out the planted error in thirty seconds and the whole point evaporates — tell me how to tune the difficulty, maybe with a couple of ready examples of arguments at different burried-error depths), and an honest note on which feature of the real problem a classroom version just can't reproduce, since I'm the one seeding the flaw and there's only one of it.
