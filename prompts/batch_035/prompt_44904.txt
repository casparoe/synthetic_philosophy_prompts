England funds its universities through income-contingent loans: the state lends each student the full cost of tuition, repayment is a fixed percentage of earnings above a threshold, nothing is collected from people who stay below it, and any balance still outstanding is written off after thirty or forty years depending on cohort. Economists have long noted that in cash-flow terms this behaves almost exactly like a tax on graduate earnings, yet the government insists on calling it a loan, and surveys suggest the debt framing itself deters some applicants in a way a "graduate contribution" might not. Germany and the Nordic countries, by contrast, charge no tuition at all and fund universities out of general taxation, including from taxpayers who never attended one. What theory of what higher education is does each arrangement presuppose? The English system looks internally split: pricing tuition at full cost and recovering it from graduates presupposes that a degree is a private investment whose returns the graduate, rather than anyone else, should pay for; meanwhile, writing off the balances of low earners out of public funds presupposes something more like a public good, or a state-provided insurance against the investment going badly. Reconstruct the implicit theory in each system, distinguish what each arrangement strictly presupposes from what it is merely compatible with, and say where the practices are philosophically underdetermined, such that several theories of the good at issue would license them unchanged. Does the insistence on the "loan" label presuppose something a graduate-tax framing would not — say, about individual responsibility for one's own future earnings — or is the difference merely rhetorical? And what would actually have to change in each country's arrangements if the presupposed view were replaced by a rival one, for instance the view that most of the wage premium attached to a degree is positional: a return to out-competing others for scarce jobs rather than to anything learned?
