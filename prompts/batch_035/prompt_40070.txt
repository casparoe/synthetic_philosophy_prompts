I teach an upper-level bioethics course, about 18 students, one 90-minute seminar, and next week is on advance directives and whether your earlier self really has the authority to boss your later self around. Design an in-class game for me. The core tension I want them to feel is this: a person writes a directive while competent, later becomes severely demented and seems content (or even happy) in ways they'd have found horrifying, and the caregivers have to decide whether the old self or the current self gets to call the shots. Please structure it so the students actually make these calls under some pressure — maybe split them into "earlier selves" who write binding instructions for a staged future scenario, and "care teams" who later get the case with details that pull against the directive, with real-ish stakes like points or enforceable rules rather than just discussion. The earlier selves shouldn't know exactly how the later self will end up feeling when they write the thing — that epistemic gap is half the point. Then a debrief that maps what happened onto the arguments people actually make in this literature: precedent autonomy, the idea that the earlier self's settled values should win, the counteridea that there's no real "same person" to have authority over, and the experiential-vs-critical interests distinction if you can work it in without lecturing. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I assume some students will just play contrarian or refuse to engage with the staged stakes), and be honest about which part of the philosophical problem the game can't actually reproduce — e.g. that no classroom version makes the later self's welfare really hang on the outcome. Act as an experienced facilitator who has run participatory ethics exercises like this before: brainstorm the design first, then give me the final package with rules, timeline, materials, payoff scheme, debrief plan, and the caveats. Keep the rules simple enough that I can actually run it cold.
