I edit a functional-annotation database for engineered microbes, and our team has a standing quarrel about what to record as the function of a transplanted gene: the job it was designed to do, the job its donor-lineage ancestors were selected for, or whatever it actually contributes to the recipient cell's metabolism. The quarrel is really the old philosophy-of-biology dispute about biological function wearing a lab coat, and I want to turn it into a decision tree we can walk through at an upcoming workshop where biologists and philosophers will both be present.

Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of famous names: first fix the diagnostic questions, then show which combinations of answers land where. The entry point should be a commitment nearly everyone at the workshop will grant — say, that hearts, enzymes, and eyes are paradigm bearers of functions — and every internal node should be a question about a concrete case that a working biologist could answer without having read a word of the literature. Someone who has never encountered the word etiological should be able to walk the tree and end up at a leaf.

At minimum, the cases feeding the nodes should include: a newly arisen mutation that improves blood pumping but has not yet undergone any selection; a vestigial structure in a lineage where it no longer does anything useful; a co-opted trait whose current use differs from the use it was originally selected for; an enzyme designed and synthesized in a laboratory, with no evolutionary history whatsoever; a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of an organism assembled by a freak accident, with neither history nor design behind it; and, if you think it belongs on the tree, the adaptations of a cancer-cell lineage, where a trait benefits the lineage rather than the organism. At minimum, the leaves should include the strong etiological view on which a trait has a function only where selection explains why the trait is present, the weak etiological view on which function derives from ancestral contributions to fitness without requiring that selection explain persistence, Cummins-style causal-role analysis, the biostatistical or normal-function approach, the organizational or self-maintaining-systems approach, a two-concept hybrid that assigns distinct notions of function to distinct explanatory jobs, and a deflationary option on which function talk is a dispensable shorthand. You may add, merge, or rename leaves beyond this minimum if the logical space demands it; expect a tree with six to nine leaves.

Three further requirements. First, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate: if no case-question drives a wedge between two positions, say so plainly rather than inventing a fake diagnostic, and identify what case would be needed to pull them apart. Second, note any region of the logical space that no leaf occupies, and diagnose it in a sentence or two — is the unoccupied combination incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored? Third, keep the questions self-contained: no node may ask whether the respondent accepts a selected-effects theory or anything else that presupposes knowing the labels in advance. The point of the instrument is to sort my colleagues by what they say about cases, not by which school they were trained in, and a philosopher and a biologist who land on the same leaf should be able to see from the path they took that they agree for reasons they did not know they shared.
