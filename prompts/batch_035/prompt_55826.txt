I was elected to the borough council last May, and I have been asked to draft the recommendation of our commemoration working group before it goes to full council in June. I would like you to draft that recommendation for me, and I want to be candid about why the matter has become personally relevant: my partner and I bought our first house in March, on a street named after the man at the centre of the case, and our daughter starts in September at the school he endowed. The name on my deeds, and in all likelihood on her exercise books, is the very name this working group must decide about.

The case: in the market square stands a marble statue of Josiah Grindle (1821–1889), erected by public subscription in 1894. Grindle built much of this town — the waterworks, the free library, the almshouses — and his name is carried by my street and by the Grindle Academy. He also owned four mills in which children worked twelve-hour shifts, and during the strike of 1868 he replaced his workforce with imported labour and had the organisers prosecuted. Local opinion is divided and heated. The working group has narrowed the options to: (a) retain the statue in place with a recontextualising installation; (b) relocate it to the museum courtyard with full interpretation; (c) remove it to storage. We must also advise, as a separate question, whether the street and school names raise the same issue or a different one.

Please take the role of a political philosopher who works on commemoration and public memory, and think the matter through before drafting — in particular, work out what functions the statue and the names actually serve (record, honour, ornament, inheritance?) and which of those functions the record of the mills puts pressure on. Then produce the recommendation: one option, clearly chosen, with a concrete timeline the council could act on this term, and a short, honest treatment of the strongest consideration behind the option you reject — not a strawman. One request on epistemics: wherever the recommendation rests on a claim about the ethics of commemoration, mark whether that claim is broadly settled among people who work on the topic or genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as if it were the consensus; I am aware, for example, that the claim that honouring a historical figure need not amount to endorsing him is itself disputed, and I want the report to be honest about wherever it leans. I have deliberately not said which option I favour, because I do not yet know; the discomfort of living on his street cuts both ways, and I do not want the report to inherit it. Finally, a hard limit: the recommendation must not exceed 600 words, excluding headings.
