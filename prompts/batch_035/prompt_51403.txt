My friend took a philosophy of mind course in which they read Dennett on the intentional stance, and since then she has been handing down verdicts on whether various systems "really believe" anything. Her rulings so far: a bimetallic-strip thermostat, no — she thinks Dennett's own favourite example overreaches; a hand-coded chess engine whose search runs on a hand-written evaluation function, yes, it believes its queen is under attack; a deep reinforcement-learning agent trained from scratch on a video game, yes; a bee navigating by a time-compensated sun compass, yes; an ant following a pheromone trail, even one that leads it in a pointless loop, no — though she will grant it a desire for food; a self-driving car's pedestrian tracker, yes; a pocket calculator, no; a cat, yes, obviously. Next term she has promised verdicts on three new cases: (a) a chess-playing system that is nothing but an enormous lookup table, billions of position–move pairs recorded from human grandmasters, with no search, no evaluation function, and no run-time learning; (b) a smart-home controller whose single running estimate of room temperature, updated from several sensors, drives a dozen different behaviours — heating, blinds, ventilation, load forecasting, occupancy alerts; and (c) a large language model assistant. Predict her verdict on each, lay out the rule or rules she seems implicitly to be running that best fit the record so far, be honest about where the record underdetermines the answer, and tell me which further case would most cleanly separate the candidate rules.
