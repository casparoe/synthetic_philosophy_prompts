My colleague Daniel has firm views about when someone else's probability should simply become your own, and for some weeks now we have been testing them case by case. His verdicts so far:

1. A casino die about to be thrown, with the house's published table giving each face a chance of 1/6: he said his credence in six should be exactly 1/6, because that is the chance, full stop.
2. A fair coin already flipped and hidden under a cup: he said his credence in heads should be 1/2 — but when I asked whether that was because the chance of heads is 1/2, he said no; the chance is now 0 or 1, we merely do not know which, and 1/2 is just the credence his ignorance leaves him with.
3. The weather service's forecast of a 30% chance of rain tomorrow: he set his credence at exactly 0.3, and when I pressed him on whether tomorrow's rain has an objective chance in the way the die does, he said he did not care what we called it — the forecaster's number was the best available handle on the question, so he takes it as his own.
4. A bookmaker's odds on a horse, implying roughly a 0.2 probability of winning: he refused to set his credence at 0.2. The number, he said, is shaded to guarantee the book a profit whatever happens; it is evidence about the market's opinion, and you weigh such evidence, you do not adopt it.

Next week we plan to put three further cases to him, and I would like to predict his answers in advance.

A. A sports analytics firm with a publicly verified record of calibration over two full seasons publishes a 60% probability that the home team wins tomorrow's match. If pressed, the firm would concede that the outcome is, in principle, fixed by the current state of the world plus the laws; there is no quantum coin toss anywhere in the causal chain. Will Daniel set his credence at 0.6? And will he say the match has a chance of the home team's winning in the same sense the die does?

B. A careful historian who has spent years in the relevant archive tells him she would put about 30% on the unsigned letter being the ambassador's. The letter was either written by her or it was not; nothing about it is chancy now. Will he adopt 0.3 as his credence, or will he treat the historian's number the way he treated the cup coin — as something his own credence may respect but is not obliged to take over?

C. The same weather service, the day after: looking back, there was about a 30% chance of rain here yesterday — issued for a town where Daniel could easily look up what actually happened but has not. Does he adopt 0.3 about the past?

What I want from you is this: (i) predict his verdict on A, B, and C; (ii) set out the candidate rules that fit his track record so far — there should be at least two, and you should say which verdicts each rule reads differently; (iii) for each of the new cases, say which of those rules it splits, insofar as it splits any; (iv) give your best guess with reasons, being honest about where the track record underdetermines the answer; and (v) invent one further case of your own that would most cleanly separate the rules you have been working with. Someone at our reading group remarked that Daniel's first verdict is just the Principal Principle and that the trouble starts after it; you may make use of that observation or ignore it entirely.
