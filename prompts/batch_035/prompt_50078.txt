My cofounder and I have decided to dissolve our company, and rather than hire a mediator we agreed to have the split computed by some explicitly stated rule, chosen before we know which of us it favors. Reading up on this, I've discovered that axiomatic bargaining theory is exactly the field that studies such rules, and that the two most famous candidates -- the Nash bargaining solution and the Kalai--Smorodinsky solution -- disagree about the fair division in cases where one party's outside option is much better than the other's. Before I commit to either, I want the specification, not the sales pitch.

Write the desiderata: what any adequate solution to a bargaining problem has to deliver, with the motivating case behind each requirement. I take it these will include at least the following, though you should add, subtract, or refine as you see fit: Pareto optimality (a rule that leaves value on the table wastes the whole point of the exercise); symmetry (when the parties are interchangeable, the rule shouldn't be able to tell them apart); invariance under positive affine rescaling of utilities (if one of us reports our payoffs in utils and the other in thousands of utils, the physical split shouldn't move -- though tell me whether you think this is a genuine constraint or a commitment to a controversial theory of interpersonal utility comparisons); individual rationality (nobody walks away worse than their disagreement point); independence of irrelevant alternatives (if a third possible agreement drops off the table and the old solution is still available, the rule shouldn't switch to a different one); and monotonicity of some form (if one party's best possible outcome improves while everything else stays fixed, that party shouldn't end up worse off).

For each desideratum, tell me: what scenario makes it compelling (the Nash vs. Kalai--Smorodinsky divergence over problems where the feasible set is unbalanced in a particular way is the natural test case, so use it), and whether it is non-negotiable or instead a camp prejudice dressed up as a requirement -- I have come to suspect that independence of irrelevant alternatives, for instance, is defended with examples that quietly smuggle in an assumption about why the alternative disappeared.

Most importantly, address joint satisfiability. My understanding is that Nash proved his four axioms pin down a unique solution, and that Kalai and Smorodinsky showed that adding a monotonicity condition to that list is impossible -- that you must give up independence of irrelevant alternatives to get monotonicity. If that is right, then the desiderata list is itself a theorem-shaped object: some subset of the things we intuitively want cannot all be had, and choosing a solution is really choosing which axiom to sacrifice. Spell out that tradeoff as concretely as you can, including what the sacrifice looks like in an actual division problem like mine.

Then, and only briefly: how Nash and Kalai--Smorodinsky each do against the list, and whether there is a third contender worth knowing about that satisfies a different defensible subset. I care more about the criteria and the impossibility result than the scorecard -- the scorecard I can do myself once the spec is fixed.
