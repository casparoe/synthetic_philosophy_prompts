I'm a software engineer, philosophy is very much not my day job, so bear with me. I got into the vagueness literature because of a work thing: we run a moderation classifier that outputs a toxicity score, and product wanted a hard cutoff above which a post gets flagged. Obviously some posts are clearly fine and some are clearly toxic and there's a big murky middle, and I got curious about whether there's "really" a fact of the matter about the last post that isn't toxic yet. That led me down the rabbit hole of epistemicism vs supervaluationism, and now I'm stuck on something meta.

From the outside the debate looks like this. Epistemicists say every vague predicate has perfectly sharp boundaries -- there IS a last non-toxic post -- we just can't know where the line is, because our ignorance is in principle, not just in practice. Supervaluationists say a vague language admits multiple precisifications, and a sentence like "post #472 is toxic" is true only if it comes out true under all the admissible sharpenings, false if under none, and neither if it's borderline -- so "there is a last non-toxic post" comes out TRUE on their scheme even though no particular post is the last one, which honestly makes my head hurt. Then there are the various "the predicate is just unsettleable" / degree-of-truth takes in the background, but let's keep it to the main two.

Here's what bugs me. This fight has been going on for decades, everyone knows all the moves, and as far as I can tell nobody has changed sides since roughly forever. And the weird thing is that both camps seem to agree on the observable surface: neither side claims they can point at the cutoff, both agree the Sorites premises all feel true, both agree the paradox needs some kind of answer. It feels less like a physics disagreement and more like my team's recurring arguments about whether something is a "product decision" or an "engineering decision" -- where after an hour you realize nobody disagrees about the facts, they disagree about whose column on the org chart the question lives in.

So what I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict on who's right. Is the epistemicism/supervaluationism dispute substantive, verbal, or two-questions-under-one-name? Some concrete candidates I've been mulling:

1. Maybe it's secretly a metaphysics vs semantics split -- epistemicists are answering "how is the world" (there's a boundary, full stop) and supervaluationists are answering "how does our language work" (truth talk distributes over precisifications), and these are compatible until you insist they're answers to the same question.
2. Maybe it turns on what you think an account of meaning owes you. Epistemicism looks like a "the bookkeeping is fine, the fault is in us" move -- it keeps classical logic intact and says the mystery is epistemic. Supervaluationism keeps the idea that vague predicates are somehow indeterminate in a robust way but pays with a nonstandard truth predicate. Is the whole fight then just about which bill you'd rather pay?
3. Or maybe it's bedrock: the intuition driving the supervaluationist is something like "it's crazy to think the universe carves heap-shaped joints," and the intuition driving the epistemicist is "it's crazy to abandon bivalence for ordinary words," and neither intuition is the kind of thing that can be argued FOR, only from.

Also, and this is the part I actually care about most: given whatever diagnosis you land on, what would even count as progress here? In my world a stalled argument ends when someone ships a formal model everyone grudgingly adopts, or when a new use case forces a decision and one side's framework is obviously more workable. Is there anything analogous -- a sharper notion of what a "precisification" is, or what "in principle unknowable" is supposed to mean when the boundary was never represented anywhere in the first place? Or is this one of those cases where the right answer is that the question is ill-posed and progress means changing the subject?

And one naggling side question: my instinct as an engineer is that in my moderation system the cutoff is a choice WE make, so both camps seem to be theorizing about a kind of boundary that doesn't exist in any system I've ever built. Does that instinct track anything in the actual debate, or am I just rediscovering some standard position and mangling it?
