As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I recently moderated an exchange between two sources that I'd like your help evaluating. The topic was special relativity and the philosophy of time — specifically the line of argument, associated with Rietdijk and Putnam in the 1960s, that the relativity of simultaneity forces us to accept a "block universe" in which the future is as fixed and real as the past. Please take on the role of an expert in philosophy of physics, brainstorm the issues privately before answering, and then adjudicate the exchange move by move.

The two parties were a physicist, Dr. L., and a philosopher, Prof. M. Their exchange went roughly as follows. (1) Dr. L. began with Penrose's version of the thought experiment, sometimes called the Andromeda paradox: two people pass each other on the street, walking in opposite directions; because they move at slightly different velocities, their planes of simultaneity, extended to a galaxy millions of light-years away, slice that distant world at moments that differ by days or weeks. So an event that lies in your future can be "now" for someone else, and since there is no privileged inertial frame, no observer's simultaneity is more correct than anyone else's. If what is real is not relative to an observer, he concluded, distant future events must be real simpliciter, and the future is fixed — determinism at one stroke. (2) Prof. M. replied that this smuggles in the very metaphysics it claims to establish: simultaneity in special relativity is a coordinate convention, a bookkeeping choice about how to label distant events, and nothing about a convention can settle what exists. A hyperplane of simultaneity is a slicing of spacetime, not a cut between the real and the unreal. (3) Dr. L. countered that if Prof. M. treats simultaneity as mere convention, then the presentist's "cosmic now" — the totality of events presently existing — must be defined relative to some frame, which privileges it arbitrarily, contradicting the relativity principle that grounded the whole argument. Presentism, he said, simply cannot be made relativistically respectable. (4) Prof. M. invoked Howard Stein's response: define becoming not over hyperplanes but at a single spacetime point — what is "real" for an event is what lies in its past light cone. That is frame-independent and respects the causal structure, so presentism survives in a pointwise form, and no conventionality is needed. (5) Dr. L.'s closing jab: if "the real" for me is confined to my past light cone, then most of what I naively call the present — everything outside my immediate neighborhood — is unreal for me, which seems to hollow out the doctrine until it says almost nothing; a view that only saves itself by shrinking, he said, has conceded the substantive point.

Please evaluate each of the five moves: say whether it is clearly right, clearly mistaken, or somewhere in between, and briefly justify each rating. Then give an overall verdict on who argued better, and identify what, if anything, both parties overlooked — in particular, whether any positions they never considered (such as intermediate views between a full block and pointwise becoming) would dissolve parts of their disagreement.
