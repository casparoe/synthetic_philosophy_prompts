Got into it with my friend Marcus over beers last night about trolley-type cases and I've been chewing on his argument all day. He's trying to get me to admit that the deontology I claim to believe in is either fake or secretly about nothing. It goes like this.

(1) The reason it's wrong to kill one to save five is that the one is being used — treated as a mere means, sacrificed for other people's benefit.

(2) But in the bombing case the deontologist has to allow — pilot blows up the munitions factory, foresees a few civilians will die, calls it tragic but permissible — nobody is being used. The civilians aren't a means to anything; their deaths are a side effect.

(3) So the whole difference between murder and permissible killing comes down to whether the deaths are "in your plan" or merely foreseen.

(4) But what's in your plan is just how you describe the action to yourself. Same bomb, same bridge, same dead civilians — describe it one way and it's permissible, describe it another (say, you time the strike so the civilian deaths demoralize the enemy) and it's murder.

(5) If moral constraints flip on self-description like that, then the constraints don't actually protect anyone. They only police how you talk to yourself while doing the identical thing.

(6) So either you give up the idea that constraints protect people (in which case why have them), or you admit they're constraints on descriptions, in which case it's all mental bookkeeping, and a utilitarian who happens to describe his killings as side effects gets a full license.

And then he leans back like he's won. The thing is I think there's a slide in there somewhere — something moves between "the deaths are in your plan" and "the deaths are described as in your plan" — but every time I try to pin it down he says the deontologist has no grip on the intending/foreseeing distinction that's independent of the agent's own description anyway, so the slide is licensed. Which, ugh.

Where does this actually go wrong, if it does? Walk me through the steps, tell me which premise to give up, and what it costs to give it up. For what it's worth I'd rather keep (1) than the permissibility of the bombing case — if deontology has to choose between those two I'm genuinely not sure which side I'm on. And is he right that (4) follows from (3)? That feels like the load-bearing move, but I can't say why it fails without just asserting that intentions are facts about you rather than facts about your word choice.
