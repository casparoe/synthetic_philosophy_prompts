My philosophy of biology reading group has spent two years arguing past each other about free-floating rationales — Dennett's idea that natural selection can install reasons in organisms that no mind, not even the organism's own, ever represented. Think of the wasp that stings a cricket in exactly the right ganglia to paralyse it and keep the food fresh for her young: there's a rationale in there somewhere, but nobody ever worked it out or acted on it. One member, a committed selectionist, holds that these reasons are fully real and literally tracked by selection, and that uncomprehending competence is everywhere in nature. Another, a committed representationalist, holds that reasons only exist where something could in principle represent them, and that reason-talk about nature is a helpful metaphor that trades on an ambiguity between causal history and genuine justification — his slogan is no reasoners, no reasons. They've finally agreed to try an adversarial collaboration, and somehow I got asked to draft the joint statement.

Write me that document. It needs four parts. First, the claims both can sign — about what selection actually does, about the causal-historical explanations of adapted traits, and about what the wasp does and doesn't, in any sense, understand. Second, the precise disagreements, each written twice: once in wording the selectionist would accept for her side, once in wording the representationalist would accept for his. Third, the cruxes. I suspect one is the co-extensive traits problem: hearts were selected for pumping blood, not for making noise, yet every heart in the ancestral population made noise, so what fact, if any, fixes what the selection was for? Another might be what to say when a scientist finally states a rationale that had gone unrepresented for millions of years — did the reason exist all along, or only once someone represented it? For each crux, include a concrete case both sides agree bears on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance, before the case is discussed. Fourth, a seperate section on whether the same notion transfers to reinforcement-learning systems that acquire policies whose rationale no engineer ever articulated — the selectionist thinks it's just the same phenomenon over again, the representationalist is unsure, so the document should make them take a position and record what each side thinks hangs on it. And if part of their dispute strikes you as purely verbal, say which part and why — that would genuinely help, they have lost whole evenings to it.
