I'm in a graduate seminar that straddles philosophy of action and behavioral decision theory, and the topic this term is weakness of will. Before I wade into the literature proper, I want to work out what a theory of akrasia is actually supposed to accomplish — I'd rather have the specification in hand than discover three papers in that the authors were aiming at different targets. So: write me the list of requirements an adequate account of weakness of will has to meet, with the case or consideration that motivates each one. At minimum the list should cover the phenomena any account owes us: genuine conflict at the time of action (the agent who judges, then and there, that one option is better and does the other anyway — not merely a change of mind between Tuesday and Thursday); the empirical results on hyperbolic discounting and preference reversal, where smaller-sooner rewards are chosen over larger-later ones and the valuations demonstrably cross over time, plus the precommitment behavior these models predict; and the marginal cases that sort accounts from each other, like inverse akrasia (the Huckleberry Finn-type case, where the agent judges the "weak" action worse and it turns out all-things-considered better) and the question of whether compulsion and addiction still leave room for akrasia or crowd it out.

Alongside the requirements themselves, I want two judgments from you. First, say which requirements are non-negotiable and which are really the preferences of one camp dressed up as conditions of adequacy — I suspect, for instance, that demanding consistency with the economic model of the agent as having coherent momentary preferences is negotiable, but tell me if you disagree. Second, and this is the part I most want to see: flag any pair of requirements you think cannot be jointly satisfied. The tension I have in mind is that if the hyperbolic-discounting story is taken as the complete account, the within-time conflict that seemed essential to akrasia gets redescribed as between-times preference reversal, which looks like it collapses akrasia into ordinary sequential change of mind — but tell me whether the tension is real or whether a hybrid can hold both. Also indicate which single requirement does the most work excluding candidate theories.

Finally, a brief scorecard, since it will help me calibrate the list: how do the Socratic view (no weakness of will, only ignorance of the good), Davidson's account of acting against one's all-things-considered judgment, Ainslie-style accounts built directly on hyperbolic discounting, and divided-mind or partition views each fare against your requirements? Keep the scoring short — a sentence or two per view — because the weight should sit on the criteria and their motivating cases; the scorecard is for my own checking, not the main deliverable. A table is fine if you find it cleaner.
