I am a third-year philosophy undergraduate currently taking a seminar on classical Indian epistemology, and we have just finished a unit on the Nyāya theory of the pramāṇas. I was particularly struck by our discussion of śabda, testimony, as a source of knowledge. As I understand it, the Nyāya view is that a statement generates knowledge only when it issues from a speaker who is both competent, in the sense of knowing what they are talking about, and trustworthy, in the sense of having no intention to deceive; whereas the Mīmāṃsā theorists, as our lecturer explained, defended the authorlessness of the Vedas precisely so that scriptural authority would not need to rest on any human speaker at all. We also touched on some of the stock puzzles: whether words apparently heard in the sky, or speech merely repeated by a parrot, could count as testimony in the relevant sense.

For my essay I would like to attempt something a bit speculative: imagining how Gaṅgeśa, the founder of the Navya-Nyāya school, would have responded to large language models. Suppose a chatbot tells me that a medication I am taking interacts badly with another one, and suppose that the claim is in fact true and that the bot's designers had no intention to deceive anyone. Would Gaṅgeśa allow that my resulting belief counts as knowledge produced by a pramāṇa, or would he insist that testimony strictly requires a genuine speaker possessing the appropriate epistemic virtues, so that the bot's output is at best reliable information transmitted through an instrument — closer to an echo or a written record than to the utterance of a trustworthy person? I am especially interested in whether the Mīmāṃsā doctrine of authorless language could give him any wriggle room here, and in what his eventual answer would imply about how cautious ordinary users of such systems ought to be when they rely on them.

Please reconstruct Gaṅgeśa's likely position as faithfully as the Nyāya framework allows, flag any points where the tradition itself would genuinely be divided, and keep your whole answer to no more than 450 words, written in British English.
