Write a dialogue between two academics who have been asked to referee the same paper, which claims that Gen Z is more financially risk-averse than Millennials were at the same age. One referee, a quantitative social scientist, takes generations to be real historical kinds — cohorts whose members share formative experiences at a similar age — and holds that "generation" can legitimately appear on the right-hand side of a causal model, conceding only the familiar age-period-cohort identification problem and arguing that distinctive formative events (say, coming of age in the aftermath of a financial crisis) break the tie. The other referee, a philosopher drawing on Hacking's notion of interactive kinds, replies that "Gen Z" is partly constituted by its own label: the generational narrative is written retrospectively, people sort themselves into it, and what the scientist calls a formative effect is largely a self-identification effect, so the proposed tie-breaker is circular. Both characters should be competent and neither a strawman: the scientist should get real mileage out of the observation that cohort differences sometimes show up before any label for the cohort is current, and the philosopher should get real mileage out of the observation that age and period effects alone do not exhaust the data. Have them push each other toward the single point on which their disagreement really turns, and end the dialogue there, with that question stated and still open between them.
