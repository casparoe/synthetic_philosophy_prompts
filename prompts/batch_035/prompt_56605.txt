After a semester of phil of science I've landed on a version of falsificationism that I think is actually defensible, and I want to stress-test it. The standard logical version got wrecked for me a while ago (Duhem-Quine: you can always blame an auxiliary assumption, so no theory ever gets falsified cleanly). So here's the version I now hold: what makes a field scientific isn't a property of the logical form of its theories but a disposition of its community. There's at least one observable outcome such that, if it had come out the other way, the practitioners would have actually given the theory up, and everyone knows this in advance. The mark of science is a public, pre-committed willingness to die on a specific hill. Pseudoscience differs not in the content of its claims but in the fact that no possible result would ever make its adherents let go — every outcome gets absorbed one way or another.

What would count against this? I want the defeaters sorted by decisiveness, from fatal through serious to merely awkward. Some things I'm already half-worried about: (a) fields that look scientific in every other respect but whose practitioners basically never abandon anything — are there real cases, or is this only a philosopher's toy? (b) the reverse: communities that genuinely do drop theories when the evidence turns, but that we'd hesitate to call scientific for independent reasons (a marketing team that A/B tests ruthlessly and discards campaigns on the numbers?); (c) the criterion makes "scientific" a property of a group at a time, so the same theory could be science in one decade and not in another, depending on who's practicing it — bug or feature? And (d) the obvious embarrassment: my demarcation criterion looks itself unfalsifiable, since any putative counterexample can be argued away as misjudging the community's true dispositions. Is there some way to state the criterion so it sticks its neck out, and if not, should that bother me — or is it fine, given the criterion was never supposed to be an empirical claim in the first place? Along the way, please be honest about which objections a committed defender of this view would concede as counting against it and which they'd just bite the bullet on.

Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence — the single most dangerous thing for my position — then give the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate my question back at me.
