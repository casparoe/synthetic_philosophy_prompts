I'm the managing editor of my high school's student newspaper, and I chair its six-person editorial board. Three weeks ago, one of our editors published, under our standing rule that any editor may run a piece "at their own editorial discretion," an article about a teacher's mid-year departure that rested on two anonymous student sources. In the aftermath, it came out that four of the six of us had had private doubts about running it: each had assumed that, if the piece were really a problem, one of the others would have said something, and none of us knew it was coming until it went live. Our faculty adviser has asked the board to decide, at Friday's meeting, whether to change the standing rule, and it falls to me to circulate the decision memo beforehand.

Please draft that memo with the following sections, in this order:

1. Recommendation (about 100 words): a clear bottom line the board can vote on, including the exact text of any proposed amendment.
2. What happened (about 150 words): a neutral, non-defensive account of the incident that the board could also share with our adviser.
3. The structural problem (about 250 words): an explanation, in general terms, of what it is about the current rule that makes this outcome likely to recur. The board should come away seeing this incident as an instance of a pattern, not a one-off caused by one editor's bad judgment.
4. The strongest case for keeping the current rule (about 150 words): the honest version, not a strawman. The rule exists for reasons, and the board should see plainly what we give up by changing it.
5. Proposed amendments (about 200 words): the concrete rule change or changes, with draft language the board could adopt as written.

A last request: in the Recommendation, be decisive. This board has a history of splitting the difference with vague compromise language, and I want a memo that forces a real choice on Friday.
