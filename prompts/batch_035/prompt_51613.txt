I write for the ideas-and-science section at a mid-size outlet, and I've been toying with a piece that uses the Challenger launch decision as a worked example of a problem from formal social choice theory -- the discursive dilemma, a.k.a. the doctrinal paradox. Before I commit to the framing I want to see whether it actually holds up, so I need the analysis run both ways, not just as a cute illustration.

Quick setup in case you want the facts pinned down. The night of January 27, 1986, on a teleconference between NASA managers in Huntsville and Morton Thiokol in Utah, Thiokol's engineers recommended against launching if the O-ring temperature was going to be below 53°F, the temperature of the coldest previous launch. The forecast for the next morning was far colder, around the freezing point. A NASA official on the line made no secret of being appalled by the recommendation, Thiokol asked to go off-line, and in a private caucus -- managers only, engineers excluded, at least as the main decision-makers -- the recommendation flipped: the data on cold was now officially inconclusive, the burden had shifted onto the engineers to prove it was unsafe, and Thiokol recommended launching. There's a well-known moment where a senior manager told the engineering VP to take off his engineering hat and put on his management hat, and an engineer's warning memo from the previous summer had literally predicted a catastrophe of exactly this kind. The launch went ahead the next morning at about 73 seconds of flight.

Here's the theory I want you to apply. Judgment aggregation: a group has to adopt collective yes/no judgments on some premises and a conclusion that's logically entailed by them (if it's too cold for the O-rings and it will be that cold at launch, then don't launch; or in court versions: if there was a contract and it was breached, then liability). The discursive dilemma is that majority voting on the premises can force a collective conclusion that a majority of members actually rejects, so the aggregation procedure itself -- premise-based, conclusion-based, or otherwise -- becomes the decision-maker. What strikes me about Challenger is that nobody's technical judgment changed between the first vote and the caucus; what changed was which question was being aggregated and who counted as the group. That looks a lot like the agenda-setting / sequence-of-votes results people in this literature talk about, but it also looks like something the theory has trouble with, because the formal setup assumes roughly anonymous, sincere voters submitting judgments to a fixed agenda, and here the re-definition of the electorate midstream, plus NASA acting as pressure rather than as a voter, plus the flip in where the burden of proof sat, all seem to do work the aggregation rules don't capture.

So: give me a careful mapping first -- who the individual judges were, what the premises and conclusion were, which aggregation procedure each "vote" embodied, and how the caucus amounts to changing the procedure rather than any judgment. Then tell me honestly where the discursive dilemma lens explains something that a story about hierarchy and customer intimidation does not -- and where it misdescribes the episode or goes silent, which I suspect is a lot. Finally, run it back the other way: what does January 1986 show about the theory? Is this a confirmation, an anticipated case, or a case that reveals a missing piece, and if the last one, what would have to be added to judgment aggregation to handle it? And please include a section on what would change your mind about all this -- which alternative framing, or which feature of the episode I've got wrong or left out, would most plausibly make you conclude the discursive dilemma is just a bad metaphor here rather than the actual structure of what happened. Also flag where you think your own analysis is most likely to fail; if the mapping leans on a contested reading of the historical record or of the formal results, say so instead of papering over it.
