Afterwards a guy from my cohort, also physics, tried to give me the rundown: "Humeans think laws of nature are just descriptions of the regularities we happen to have observed -- like 'all copper spheres conduct electricity.' Nothing in nature makes anything obey a law, the universe is basically a giant accident, and any pattern that happens to hold counts as a law. Since it's all just curve-fitting, Humeans also can't explain why induction works at all. The view got pretty much refuted back in the day because of this."

Now, I know from the seminar that Humeanism is supposed to still be a serious position -- we read Lewis's stuff about it and the professor treats it as live -- so something in his summary must be off. Can you go through it piece by piece and tell me which parts a Humean would actually accept, which parts conflate Humeanism with some simpler "whatever regularity we've seen is a law" view, and whether the induction objection lands the way he thinks? His "any pattern that happens to hold counts as a law" bit seems especially suspicious -- surely that would make some boring accidental generalization a law of nature, and I'd guess the view has a smarter answer than that, since I vaguely remember something about systems of axioms being involved. Also not sure if the "giant accident" framing is fair or just how opponents like to make it sound.
