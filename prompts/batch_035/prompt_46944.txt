I review papers for a mid-tier journal in evolutionary psychology — my PhD advisor got me roped into it, and now roughly one adaptive-hypothesis paper lands in my queue each month, usually something on the pattern of "trait x evolved because of benefit y," where x can be anything from gossip to morning sickness to why humans like spicy food. My actual training is in statistics and cognitive modeling, not organismal biology, so I can evaluate the math fine but I freeze at the adaptation question itself. My advisor was a huge Gould person and made me read the spandrels paper early on, and I absorbed the vibe: I'm suspicious of just-so stories, I know the words exaptation and byproduct, I get that a trait being useful today doesn't mean it was selected for that use. But the critique never came with anything I can actually run on a submission, and "I smell a Panglossian story" isn't something I can write in a review.

So what I want is a decision procedure, not a lecture: exactly five numbered steps, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no concluding paragraph. It should give me the questions to ask of a paper in order, what counts as an adequate answer to each question, at which steps an inadequate answer means I should reccomend reject or major revisions, and where the procedure has to give up and hand the call over to my judgment because the issue is framing or values rather than facts. It has to be usable in a single evening by someone who is not a developmental biologist, so no step can require me to know the pleiotropic effects of a particular gene or the phylogeny of the trait offhand. Two things it has to handle without breaking: papers arguing that a trait is a byproduct rather than an adaptation (I don't want a procedure that reflexively says "insufficient evidence of selection, reject," because sometimes the byproduct story is the well-supported one and deserves publication), and papers where the adaptive hypothesis is well-formulated but completely untested — whether that deserves to see print is genuinely unclear to me. If it helps to have a concrete target to calibrate against, imagine a submission claiming pregnancy nausea is an adaptation that shields the fetus from plant toxins in the first trimester.
