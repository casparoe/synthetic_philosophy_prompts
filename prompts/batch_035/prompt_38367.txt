You are a political philosopher with deep expertise in the ethics of paternalism, choice architecture, and the nudging debate — you know the literature on libertarian paternalism, soft versus hard paternalism, and the standard objections about manipulation and autonomy, and you can apply those distinctions to concrete institutional cases. I want you to work through the following carefully and in stages: brainstorm before you commit, show the reasoning, and don't sand off genuine uncertainty.

I am the faculty representative on a mid-sized university's Behavioral Policy Review Board, a standing committee that approves, rejects, or approves-with-conditions administrative proposals that shape how students and staff make choices. We have accumulated a track record of decisions, and next month we face a new proposal. I need a briefing paper that predicts our verdict and, more importantly, works out what our precedents actually commit us to.

Our track record, in chronological order:

1. Approved: the main cafeteria was redesigned so that salads and fruit sit at eye level by the entrance while desserts sit on a low shelf at the far end. No items were removed and no signage was added.
2. Approved, with the condition that the opt-out link appear on the same screen and the change be announced in the offer letter: new employees are automatically enrolled in the pension plan at a 3% contribution rate, overridable with one click during onboarding.
3. Rejected: making the cafeteria dessert station request-only, so that a student who wants dessert must ask a server for it. All items would have remained available and displayed.
4. Rejected: a campus-wide email reporting, accurately, that the large majority of colleagues in each building had already received the flu vaccine, with a booking link included.
5. Rejected: automatically enrolling all first-year students in the peer-mentoring program, with an opt-out that required emailing the department administrator directly.
6. Approved: changing the default meeting length in the shared calendar template from 60 minutes to 25 minutes, overridable for any individual meeting.

The new proposal, from the residence office, aims to reduce dormitory energy use. Students would be enrolled by default in a floor leaderboard that displays each room's monthly energy consumption against the floor median, with rooms identified by number. Opting out requires a single click in the housing portal and is invisible to floormates. The change was announced in the monthly housing newsletter, which internal data suggest roughly one in ten students opens.

Please do the following:

First, articulate at least two distinct decision rules or principles that each fit our entire track record — every approval and every rejection — and that are genuinely different, not just notational variants of each other. For each rule, say what it predicts for the new proposal and which of our precedents is the closest analog under that rule. I suspect the interesting tension is between rules that focus on the mechanism by which an intervention works (for instance, whether its engine is social comparison or emotion rather than reordering what is easy) and rules that focus on the character of the escape route (how hard, how public, how interpersonal it is to get back to the original option set). But do not feel bound by my hunch.

Second, give me your best prediction of the board's verdict — approve, reject, or approve-with-conditions, with the conditions specified — and defend the prediction, saying honestly where the precedents underdetermine it.

Third, tell me which of our precedents the new case is genuinely most like, and acknowledge that the answer may differ depending on which rule the board is implicitly running.

Finally, design one further case — a hypothetical future proposal — that would best separate the candidate rules from one another, and explain what verdict under that case would falsify each rule. That is the case I will want in my back pocket for the meeting.
