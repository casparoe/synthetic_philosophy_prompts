So my roommate and I got into a three-hour argument last night (over dishes, of all things, but it drifted) about what you should do when someone as smart as you, with the same evidence, disagrees with you. My position was that if I've worked through the math myself, twice, and my equally math-savvy friend looks at it and says I'm wrong, I'm entitled to mostly keep my confidence — I've seen the actual proof, she's just a data point. My roommate thought this was arrogant and kept deploying this line she picked up from a philosophy of science course: "evidence of evidence is evidence." Her move was: the fact that my friend disagrees is evidence that my friend has evidence against my conclusion I haven't seen, and evidence of evidence is evidence, therefore I have to significantly lower my confidence. She acted like the slogan just settles it in one step.

The thing is, I suspect that one sentence is doing a bunch of different jobs and we were talking past each other the whole time. Like, one reading is almost trivially true (anything that raises the probability of there being supporting evidence raises the probability of the conclusion, a bit). But other readings seem way stronger — that higher-order evidence can *override* what I've directly checked, or that it "screens off" my first-order reasoning somehow — and those seem like they could actually be false, or at least not follow from the catchy version. And there's a weird self-undermining angle too: if I follow her rule all the way, I end up believing something that I myself think my total evidence weighs against, which feels like a kind of incoherence people complain about in this literature (epistemic akrasia I think it's called?).

Can you lay out the distinct precise claims "evidence of evidence is evidence" could be making — from the harmless near-tautology to the radical readings — as a list I can actually use in round two with my roommate? For each reading, say whether it's plausible, and crucially whether it actually gets her the conclusion she wanted (that I must substantially conciliate, not just nudge my confidence a hair). And if some readings are true but useless for her argument while the useful ones are dubious, I want that said plainly. Also flag where, if anywhere, following the slogan forces you into that akratic/"believing against your own lights" position. Please answer in American English.
