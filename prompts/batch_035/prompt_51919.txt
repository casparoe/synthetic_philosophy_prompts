ok so i fell down a plato rabbit hole recently. in the meno, socrates compares true opinions to daedalus's statues: they're worth a lot but they run away on you unless you tie them down, and tying one down is working out the reason why it's true. i want a villanelle out of this. the two refrains should be (1) the opinion slipping off again and (2) the asking-why that hauls it back — so the form does the argument, not just the words. every stanza the belief escapes and gets dragged home, and only in the last stanza, where the two refrains finally stand side by side, does it stop running. that closing pair is the moment a true belief turns into knowledge. dont mention plato, daedalus, or the meno anywhere in the actual poem — someone who knows the passage should recognise it without a name tag, and someone who doesnt should just get a good poem about a thought that wont stay still. one more thing: the tether shouldnt come off smug. the frustrating part of the meno is that socrates never really shows you HOW to tie the knot, only that it needs tying, and id like the poem to leave that open too.
