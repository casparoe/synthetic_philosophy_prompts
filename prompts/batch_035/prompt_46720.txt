everyone in my evo psych seminar keeps waving around the spandrels paper to shut down adaptationist talk, and I want to know how much work that analogy is actually doing. the line is: just because a trait looks designed for a purpose doesn't mean it was selected for that purpose, look at San Marco -- the spandrels under the dome are so lavishly decorated that you'd swear the cathedral was built to house those mosaics, but actually they're just the leftover spaces where the arches had to meet, and the decoration came after, painted onto whatever was there. from this my classmates conclude that basically any psychological trait we'd want to give an evolutionary story (music, religion, disgust at norm violations, whatever) is probably like that: a byproduct riding along on something selection built for other reasons, so all these adaptive explanations are just-so stories. audit the analogy for me. first give me the mapping -- what's the arch supposed to be, what's the spandrel, what's the later decoration (is that supposed to be exaptation? cultural overlay?), what's the whole cathedral. then the breaks: spandrels are architecturally forced once you commit to arches holding up a dome -- is every biological byproduct like that, or can selection build things with lots of neutral slack where nothing is forced? and does the fact that the decoration was added deliberately, with intent, by the artists even fit a process with no designer at all? lastly tell me whether the "it's all spandrels, so back off adaptationism" conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole time. and if the cathedral is a bad map somewhere, what would work better?
