In the Kant reading group I belong to, we spent last semester on the ethics of assertion, working up to the 1797 exchange with Benjamin Constant where Kant bites the bullet on the murderer at the door and insists that even then you may not lie. One member of the group, let's call her Marta, defends Kant's position in discussion but refuses to state her principle outright; she says verdicts on cases are all she'll commit to, and then the principle should be extracted from those. Her track record so far, from several long evenings of arguing: that exaggerating your managerial experience on a résumé is wrong; that telling a nosy neighbor a misleading but literally true thing -- saying she hasn't seen a certain visitor today when in fact she saw him yesterday -- is fine, indeed clever; that telling her dying father that his other son had pulled through the operation, when he hadn't, was wrong even though the truth would have hastened his despair; and that giving an armed mugger who was demanding her PIN a fake one was permissible. For what it's worth, she was visibly uneasy about the last one but wouldn't retract it, and she got visibly irritated when someone pointed out that her résumé verdict and her mugger verdict are hard to square with Kant's own absolutism about lying proper.

The new case, which comes up at our meeting this week, is the classic one updated slightly: a man with evident murderous intent is at her door asking whether her houseguest -- someone he has threatened by name and whom she believes is hiding upstairs -- is inside. She is not the target; the danger is to the guest. But there is a wrinkle of Kant's own devising: there is some real chance the guest slipped out the back window minutes ago, and the direction she would misdirect the man toward is the direction the guest would have fled in, so that a lie could conceivably deliver the guest to him. Marta has to say whether she lies, tells the truth, or gives another one of her literally-true evasions.

Before the meeting, I want to be prepared to see the structure of her position rather than just argue about the case. Predict Marta's verdict, and lay out the candidate principles her track record is jointly consistent with -- I can see at least three worth taking seriously: that wrongdoers forfeit their right to truthful assertions from us; that veracity is a prima facie duty overridden only by preventing grave and reasonably certain harm to someone; and that lying becomes permissible in self-defense but not, or not clearly, in the defense of others. Say which of these best fits her earlier verdicts, note where they converge and diverge on the new case (the uncertainty about the guest's location is where I suspect they come apart), and give me your best guess at her answer with your reasons. Then tell me which single further case would best discriminate between the two strongest candidate principles, and what verdict on it would decide which one Marta is actually running.
