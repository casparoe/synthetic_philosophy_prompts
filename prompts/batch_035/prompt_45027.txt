I'm a philosophy PhD student working on population ethics, and I'm trying to figure out my actual position on strong longtermism — the claim that shaping the very long-run future matters overwhelmingly, because of the number of potential future people — by taking verdicts on concrete cases rather than arguing about totalism in the abstract. My verdicts so far: paying large, certain costs now to prevent a one-in-a-million civilizational collapse this century is clearly worth it; I also think it's right to refuse funding a grant that would reduce extinction risk by something like one part in a billion, in favor of deworming thousands of children today, even though the expected number of lives at stake in the grant is astronomically larger; it's clearly wrong to harvest a living person's organs to bring down the probability of extinction by a genuinely minuscule amount; yet I support imposing real, diffuse costs on people now to prevent a political or environmental lock-in that would permanently degrade every future generation, even when the chance of the money mattering is comparably small; and I think we should reject the argument that the best thing for the future is to save and invest for a few centuries before spending anything, while admitting I can't say where it goes wrong. Is there a single principle behind all this — maybe a probability threshold below which tiny chances of astronomical stakes drop out of deliberation entirely, a contrast between imposing risks and generating benefits, a person-affecting proviso, or some combination — and if not, which of my verdicts should be abandoned? Please state the principle precisely, note which case motivates each clause, tell me honestly which verdicts it cannot save, and say what it implies about a further case I keep going back and forth on: diverting a vaccine shipment from a poor country today to a research program with roughly a one-in-a-hundred-million chance of preventing an engineered pandemic that ends civilization.
