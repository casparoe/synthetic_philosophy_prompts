I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and for the past few years part of my beat has been the restitution debate — the Benin Bronzes, the Parthenon Marbles, human remains policies, the slow-motion reckoning of the encyclopedic museums. I've been sitting in (on the record) on the restitution board of a large European museum, which I'll leave unnamed here since my editor hasn't run the piece yet. The board has now decided enough claims that its case law is starting to look like a jurisprudence, and I'm writing an essay about what principle — if any — it is actually running. Before you answer, I'd like you to take on the role of a specialist in cultural property law and museum ethics: please think carefully and methodically, and begin with a short brainstorm of the candidate decision rules that could fit the board's track record before committing to any prediction.

The board's decided cases, roughly in order. (1) It returned a collection of ancestral human remains unconditionally to the descendant community that claimed them, processing the claim directly with the community and without requiring any involvement from the community's national government. (2) It refused the long-standing claim to a celebrated classical frieze acquired in the early nineteenth century under a permit from the then-ruling empire, citing both its reading of the permit's validity and the frieze's place in the museum's "world cultures" galleries. (3) For a large group of royal objects seized in a documented nineteenth-century punitive expedition — the archetypal colonial-loot scenario — it transferred title to the source state but kept the objects physically on renewable long-term loan, with a jointly curated gallery. (4) It returned a single antiquity unconditionally after provenance research established it had been clandestinely excavated after the source country's patrimony law took effect. (5) It refused a diaspora community's claim to ethnographic objects bought outright from a dealer more than a century ago with no evidence of coercion, stating that object claims must be channelled through states.

The new case, on which I expect a decision within months: a set of ceremonial regalia seized during a colonial punitive expedition in the 1900s — fully documented, and arguably lawful as war booty under the law of its day. It is claimed by the traditional council that is the institutional successor of the raided community; the national government endorses the claim but insists any return go to the national museum in the capital, which plans a flagship exhibition. The traditional council, meanwhile, wants the regalia returned to active ritual use — which would mean no public display, no loan-back, and handling standards the museum's conservators would never sign off on. My questions: What will — or, if you prefer, what should — the board decide if it wants to be consistent with itself? Which precedent is the closest analog? It seems to me that at least two rules fit the track record equally well — something like "coercive context of acquisition triggers some form of return regardless of formal legality" versus "title moves but possession is retained wherever the objects anchor a major gallery" — and that these diverge sharply here. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, tell me what principle the board would be adopting with each available choice, and then give me the hypothetical next case that would best expose which rule the board has really been running all along — I'd like to end the essay on it.
