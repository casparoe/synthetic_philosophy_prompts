For most of my working life I ran a laboratory in visual and auditory psychophysics. Since retiring I have been reading systematically in the philosophy of mind, and a question about my own former trade now bothers me more than anything I confronted at the bench.

Here is the practice that puzzles me. When my colleagues and I wanted to know when a subject experienced something, we asked, and recorded the answer. In temporal-order judgment tasks, subjects report which of two flashes came first, or whether a beep and a flash were simultaneous, and the community tabulates those reports as measurements of the relative timing of experience itself. That protocol appears to presuppose that an experience has a determinate time of occurrence — that for each perceptual episode there is a fact of the matter about when it happened, which the report more or less accurately discloses. Yet the same community treats a family of results as established in which this looks untenable. In the color phi phenomenon, described by Kolers and von Grünau in the 1970s, a blue dot is shown, then a red dot some distance away; subjects report a dot moving smoothly between the two positions and changing color midway — changing, that is, at a moment before the second dot, the only possible cause of the color change, was ever displayed. In the cutaneous rabbit, taps delivered at the wrist and elbow are felt as a regular sequence hopping up the forearm, including at locations never touched. In backward masking and the flash-lag effect, content presented later demonstrably alters what a subject reports about an interval already past. The literature accommodates such results under two pictures, usually discussed under Dennett's labels. On the first, the brain edits the content before the moment of experience: consciousness is a staged show, and the subject's report is veridical about an experience that was, so to speak, doctored in advance — a revision on the model of Stalin's show trials. On the second, the experience really did unfold as it initially seemed, and what is corrected afterwards is the record: the subject's memory of the experience is overwritten, on the model of Orwell's Ministry of Truth. Philosophers call these the Stalinesque and Orwellian options, and Dennett's own conclusion, if I understand it, was that the choice between them is ill-posed — that there is no determinate moment at which the edit occurs, because the temporal ordering of content is simply not a fact the brain's operations respect.

My question is about what the discipline, and the practices downstream of it, actually presuppose — not about which picture is true. There seem to be three commitments in circulation. First, the tabulation of temporal-order judgments appears to presuppose that experiences have determinate onset times, which sits closest to the Stalinesque picture. Second, the interpretation of postdictive findings often treats the subject's report as a narrative reconstructed after the fact, which sits closest to the Orwellian picture. Third, outside the laboratory, human-factors experts testify in accident cases about when a driver perceived a hazard, and engineers set the timing of collision warnings on the assumption that there is a fact of the matter about when a person consciously registered a stimulus; juries are invited to believe in timestamps of experience.

What I would like from you is a reconstruction of the implicit theory, or theories. Does the practice of timestamping experience strictly presuppose that there is a fact of the matter about the time of an experience, or is it compatible with several views of the timing of experience, so that the discipline could remain agnostic between the Stalinesque and Orwellian pictures? Is the apparent tension between the first two commitments real, or can both be subsumed under something more neutral — an effective quantity in the physicist's sense, defined by convention relative to a measurement procedure rather than discovered, the way one speaks of an effective temperature or an effective mass? And if the discipline did adopt one of the two pictures outright, what would have to change: in the design and interpretation of temporal-order experiments, in how postdictive results are described, and in the forensic practice of testifying to times of perception? I ask partly out of curiosity and partly from a professional's discomfort: I spent decades taking down numbers whose presuppositions I can no longer articulate, and I would like to know whether my younger self was doing metaphysics without a license, or merely applying a harmless convention.
