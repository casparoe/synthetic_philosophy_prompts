I'm a PhD student in health policy (dissertation on the ethics of scarce-resource allocation, heavy on the separateness of persons literature), and through a connection of my advisor I ended up with a seat on the ethics advisory panel of our regional health authority. The panel gets called in whenever there's a serious drug or equipment shortage, which lately is 3-4 times a year, and every single time we relitigate the same fight. Half the panel wants to just run the utilitarian arithmetic -- maximize total QALYs or lives saved, done -- and the other half keeps insisting that something is wrong with adding up claims across separate patients, that a bigger total doesn't automatically trump. Nobody on the panel knows this is a live debate in ethics going back decades (Nozick's side constraints, Taurek's 1977 paper on whether numbers should count, Scanlon's contractualism in What We Owe to Each Other), so the fights are loud and unproductive. The chair has asked me, since I "know the philosophy," to draft a decision procedure the panel can actually use.

Here's what it needs to be. Something a group of clinicians and one lawyer can run in a 90-minute emergency meeting, with no philosophy background required: an ordered list of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and points at which the procedure halts and says either "aggregate, the numbers decide" or "stop, this is a case where the separateness of persons blocks pure aggregation" -- plus explicit spots where the procedure has to hand the question back to the panel as a values question it can't settle, since I don't want to smuggle my own view in as pseudo-procedure. My fear is that any simplification either collapses back into straight utilitarianism with extra steps, or becomes so hedged it never tells anyone anything. So I'd also want you to say which distinctions from the literature absolutely must survive the translation (I suspect the distinction between trade-offs within one life and trade-offs across lives is one, and maybe something like Scanlon's line about the relevance of numbers once harms are comparable, but I'm not sure) and which debates can safely be dropped for this purpose.

Then run the procedure on three cases so I can see whether it actually works. Case one: we have 40 doses of a chemo drug; one late-stage patient needs the full 40-dose course for a modest extension of life, or we can split it to give 40 patients a partial course with meaningfully lower survival odds each. Case two: a single ECMO machine free up, two eligible patients, one a 25-year-old with better survival probability and one a 55-year-old with slightly worse odds and two young kids. Case three: a fixed annual budget line where we can either fund a wildly expensive gene therapy for one child or top up insulin supplies that will run out for a few hundred patients mid-year. For at least one of these, tell me honestly if the procedure gives an answer I'm going to hate -- I lean anti-aggregation, and I'd rather find out now than during an actual shortage. And please flag where the procedure will misfire or get gamed, and what it flatly cannot do.
