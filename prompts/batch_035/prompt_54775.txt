The essay question was:

"C. S. Peirce and William James are both labelled pragmatists, yet they appear to offer very different accounts of truth. Explain the difference between Peirce's account of truth and James's account, and assess the objection that pragmatist theories of truth confuse truth with what it is convenient or comfortable to believe."

And the student's answer reads:

"Peirce's pragmatism begins from a rejection of Cartesian doubt. In his 1877–78 papers on the logic of science he argues that inquiry begins with the irritation of doubt and ends in the settlement of belief, and his pragmatic maxim holds that the whole meaning of a conception consists in the practical effects we conceive the object of that conception to have. Applied to truth, this yields the view that a true belief is one that satisfies — one that proves advantageous to our thinking — and that the real is whatever the community of inquirers agrees upon. Since Peirce identifies the real with the object of the final opinion, truth for him is simply whatever the community of scientists actually converges on, which is also why he regards the method of science as the only legitimate method of fixing belief: it is the only one whose success does not depend on the idiosyncrasies of individual thinkers.

James takes over this approach and applies it directly to truth itself. For James, truth is not a static property that a proposition either has or lacks; it is something that happens to an idea. An idea becomes true insofar as it is verified, insofar as it leads us usefully through experience and can be assimilated with the rest of our beliefs. James illustrates this with the example of being lost in the woods: the belief that a path leads to a house is true just in case following the path actually gets us there. Truth is thus a species of the good for James, and he is happy to allow that a belief's being comfortable to hold can contribute to making it true. This is exactly why his critics charged that pragmatism is a philosophy for people who want to believe whatever suits them.

The objection that pragmatism confuses truth with what it is convenient to believe has more force against Peirce than against James. Because Peirce makes truth depend on whatever the scientific community accepts, a consensus that is widespread but mistaken would count as true on his view, which seems clearly wrong. James avoids this problem because he ties truth to successful verification in experience rather than to what anybody accepts. Peirce himself saw the danger here: he eventually renamed his own position pragmaticism precisely to mark a clean break with James's version, regarding it as a corruption of what he had intended."
