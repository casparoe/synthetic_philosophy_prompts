I'm a graduate student in aesthetics, and I've spent the better part of this year trying to write a seminar paper on the aesthetic testimony problem, only to realize I can't even tell what the paper should be about because the debate itself seems to be going nowhere. To set it out as it looks from where I sit: the pessimist holds that you cannot properly come to know aesthetic facts on the say-so of others — you have to experience the work yourself, and at best testimony of a work's merits gives you something second-rate, like justified belief or knowledge of the critic's opinion. The optimist points out that we constantly and quite properly learn from critics, friends, teachers, and guidebooks that Guernica is harrowing or that a building is inelegant, and that the rest of our epistemic life would be crippled by the kind of caution the pessimist demands here. Both sides know the standard moves by now. When pressed, the pessimist concedes that testimony can transmit propositional knowledge-that and retreats to insisting that what it can't give you is acquaintance-based warrant, or the ability to make and defend the judgment yourself, or the kind of understanding that first-hand engagement provides; the optimist then presses on whatever that "something else" is supposed to be and finds that either it can be gotten second-hand after all or it was never a kind of knowledge in the first place. There's also the disanalogy anxiety: we're perfectly happy to take testimony about ordinary perceptible properties — that the shirt is red, that the engine is loud — so the pessimist owes some reason why beauty or elegance is different, and the usual answers (aesthetic concepts are response-dependent, there's a norm against believing on testimony alone in the aesthetic domain, the properties are ones you must be able to recognize yourself) each get a predictable counter. It strikes me that nobody on either side has changed their mind about anything in a long time, and I want to know why.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis because I genuinely don't trust it: the fight isn't really about testimony at all, but is a proxy war over whether possessing an aesthetic concept requires perceiving instances of the property — roughly, whether 'elegant' or 'moving' is more like 'red' or more like a theoretical term you can master from a textbook — and each side's epistemology falls out of a prior, unargued commitment on that semantic question. But I can see at least two rivals to this diagnosis, and I'd like them taken seriously. One is that the disagreement is verbal: the parties are using 'know' differently, or have quietly different objects in view (the proposition that the work is beautiful versus what it's like to encounter the work as beautiful), so that both are right and the "conflict" is a shadow cast by a shared vocabulary. The other is that it's not epistemology but value in disguise — that the pessimist is defending a norm of aesthetic life, something about the point of engaging with art at all, and the felt resistance to second-hand aesthetic knowledge is just the residue of a practical commitment that has been dressed up as an epistemic limitation. It could also just be a hard substantive disagreement about which there's nothing more to say, but that's the diagnosis I most want a reason to accept or reject, not a default.

What I want from you is a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement itself, not a survey or a verdict on who's right. Tell me which of these readings (or some fifth one) the impasse actually supports, drawing on what each side actually says when they make their best moves rather than their slogans. If you think it's the semantic story, explain what would show it — for instance, whether a psychological finding about how people actually acquire aesthetic concepts, or a distinction between concept possession and epistemic warrant, would dissolve or merely relocate the dispute. If you think it's the verbal story, say what distinction would let the two parties see they were agreeing. And if you think it's genuine and substantive, tell me honestly what progress should even look like — a new kind of case, an analogy with a resolved debate elsewhere in epistemology, a formal model of when transmission of warrant fails — or whether the question is ill-posed in a way that means the debate should be ended rather than won. If it helps, I'm inclined to think testimony from someone whose taste I've calibrated myself over years is different in kind from testimony from a stranger, but I've never managed to work out whether that conviction is an insight or a symptom of exactly the confusion I'm trying to diagnose.
