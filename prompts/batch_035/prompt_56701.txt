I teach an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar on perception, and I am writing the final examination. I want a single essay question on Molyneux's problem — the congenitally blind man, newly able to see, presented with a sphere and a cube he already knows by touch — but one that cannot be answered by reciting the standard positions. My students have already read the classic dialectic (the empiricist pessimism, the rationalist optimism about shared spatial structure), so a question that merely asks "what would Leibniz say and why?" would reward memory rather than understanding. The question I have in mind should be built around a case I have constructed, which I would like you to incorporate verbatim in spirit as the exam stimulus: a (fictional) study in which a group of patients who gained sight after prolonged congenital blindness perform at only slightly above chance on the classic sphere-versus-cube discrimination without feedback, yet reach near-ceiling accuracy after a single feedback trial — and, strikingly, transfer that accuracy immediately to a novel pair of objects they had never touched, a pyramid and a cylinder. The exam question must force candidates to say what this pattern would settle, if anything, and in particular to distinguish three different things "solving Molyneux's problem" could mean: an empirical psychological claim about de facto performance, a conceptual claim about whether tactile and visual representations share a common spatial format, and a claim about the temporal structure of learning — including whether one-shot transfer of a discrimination rule is evidence of pre-existing cross-modal correspondence or merely of very rapid recalibration that the traditional debate never contemplated. Candidates who treat the hypothetical data as simply "confirming" or "refuting" one of the historical parties should lose marks.

Please deliver three things. First, the exact wording of the question, including the stimulus as a short paragraph the candidates would read, phrased so that it is answerable in ninety minutes without any knowledge beyond my course. Second, a model answer of about six hundred words that itself models the distinctions I want: it should say explicitly what follows from slightly-above-chance baseline performance, what follows from one-trial learning with transfer, and what does not follow from either. Third, a marking scheme built around five must-hit points totalling twenty marks, with the marks each is worth, a one-line note on how candidates typically go wrong on each point, and a closing statement of what separates an A answer from a B answer — I expect the difference to lie not in coverage but in whether the candidate recognises that the empirical result underdetermines the conceptual question, and can say why. Please write everything in British English.
