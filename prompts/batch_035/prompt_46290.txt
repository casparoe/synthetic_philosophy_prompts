Here's the question I wrote:

"According to classical Nyāya, what makes something a pramāṇa? Explain the Nyāya thesis that the truth of a cognition is intrinsic (svataḥ prāmāṇya) while its falsity is extrinsic (parataḥ aprāmāṇya), and contrast this with the Buddhist position. Then explain why Nyāya insists that upamāṇa (comparison) is a distinct pramāṇa rather than a species of inference, using the standard example of learning what a gavaya (wild ox) is."

And here's what the student wrote:

"A pramāṇa is any cause of true cognition, so for Nyāya the four pramāṇas — perception, inference, comparison, and testimony — are just the four kinds of causes that reliably produce knowledge. This means a cognition, when it arises, is not yet knowledge, but becomes knowledge once its truth is established.

Nyāya holds svataḥ prāmāṇya, which follows directly from their doctrine that every cognition is self-luminous (svaprakāśa): just as the cognition reveals its object, it also reveals its own truth to the knower, so no external check is needed for validity. The Buddhists, especially Dharmakīrti, argued the opposite: a cognition is like a lamp that must illuminate itself before illuminating other things, and since it does illuminate itself, its validity is self-evident, hence intrinsic. So the Buddhist position is that validity is intrinsic and invalidity is intrinsic too, whereas Nyāya says only validity is intrinsic and invalidity is extrinsic, since falsity has to be discovered through subsequent experience of a defect, like touching the rope you mistook for a snake. This asymmetry is a little odd but Nyāya defends it by saying that a cognition arising from proper causal conditions naturally produces true knowledge, while errors are caused by defects like bad lighting, so truth is the default and error needs explaining.

On upamāṇa: the classic case is that a city-dweller hears from a forest dweller that a gavaya is like a cow, then later goes to the forest, sees the animal, and knows 'the gavaya is like the cow.' Nyāya says this knowledge of similarity can't be reduced to perception, inference, or testimony, so comparison is a separate pramāṇa. Honestly this seems like a weak spot in their system: once you actually see the gavaya, ordinary perception of the two animals gives you the similarity directly, so Nyāya should have conceded that upamāṇa is redundant. I think they kept it mostly to round out the list at four."
