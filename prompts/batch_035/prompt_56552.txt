Construct a thought experiment in which infinitism earns a distinctive verdict about justification — a realistic scenario in which a belief is justified only because its supporting reasons form an infinite, non-circular chain, and in which foundationalism and coherentism each struggle to say the belief is justified without resorting to moves they normally avoid. Constraints: the setting must be ordinary (for example, a reliability engineer tracing a fault through layers of subsystems, or an auditor verifying provenance along a supply chain), with no ideal reasoners, demons, or stipulated omniscience; and the chain must consist of reasons the agent could possess in Peter Klein's sense — merely available rather than actually endorsed — rather than reasons whose existence is simply asserted. The chain must also fail to bottom out: no member may be plausibly self-warranting, and no member may draw support from any belief outside the chain, so that coherence with a wider web cannot come to the rescue. After one paragraph of scenario, walk through the verdict each of the three theories delivers and why; identify which structural feature of the case is doing the work; explain how a foundationalist would attack the case (for instance, by appealing to dogmatism about some member, to reliabilist warrant for the non-basic links, or by denying that merely available reasons justify); and state whether removing that feature collapses the case into a standard illustration of the Agrippan trilemma. The case is intended as a teaching device for an advanced undergraduate seminar on epistemic justification, so its verdicts must emerge from the theories' own commitments rather than being stipulated.
