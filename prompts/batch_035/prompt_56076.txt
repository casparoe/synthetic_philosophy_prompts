I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a feature about the many-worlds interpretation and why it keeps resurfacing in popular culture. A science blog I read for background sums up the quantum immortality argument like this: 'Quantum mechanics tells us that whenever a quantum event has more than one possible outcome, the universe splits into parallel copies, one for each outcome. So in the many-worlds picture, there is always some branch in which the experimenter survives even a game of quantum Russian roulette. Since the conscious observer cannot experience branches in which they are dead, subjectivity continues only along the branches where they live, which means survival is guaranteed from the first-person perspective. An appropriately designed quantum gun would therefore be perfectly safe for the person holding it, and by repeating the experiment many times, an experimenter could prove the many-worlds interpretation to themselves — something no ordinary experiment can do. By the same reasoning, physics guarantees that none of us will ever really die, because for any fatal event there is always some branch in which it fails to kill us.' I suspect this packages some genuine and genuinely strange ideas together with some sleights of hand, but I don't trust my own ear for which is which, and I don't want to build a feature on a strawman. Please audit the passage sentence by sentence. Tell me which claims a physicist working on quantum foundations would accept as stated, which are simplifications a popular treatment might be forgiven, and which misrepresent the position — paying particular attention to where the article slides from what quantum mechanics itself says to what one interpretation adds, to what happens to the argument once one takes the Born rule weights of the surviving branches seriously rather than just counting branches, and to whether the assumptions about consciousness and personal identity that the argument needs are part of physics or an additional philosophical wager. Finally, tell me whether the article's two headline conclusions — that the experiment is 'perfectly safe' and that physics guarantees personal immortality — follow even from those parts of the summary that survive your audit, or whether each depends on an error you have identified. I'd like to come away knowing exactly what I can responsibly put in my article and what I have to flag as the blog overreaching.
