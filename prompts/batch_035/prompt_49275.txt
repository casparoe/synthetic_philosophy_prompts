For most of my career I have defended a version of luck egalitarianism, and I would like a disciplined assessment of its exposure. The version I hold is roughly this: justice requires eliminating disadvantage that traces to brute luck, but not disadvantage that traces to genuine choices, where a choice counts as genuine not because it is metaphysically free but because it was made against a background of options and information a reasonable person would accept. I have also helped myself, at various points, to Dworkin-style hypothetical insurance arguments to convert brute luck into responsible choice, and to a sufficiency floor to blunt the worst cases. Each qualification was added to handle an objection; I no longer have a clear view of what remains testable.

Tell me what would actually count against the position. I do not want a parade of the standard objections; I want them ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with the ranking argued rather than asserted. Include the sorts of things I have come to worry about myself: whether the view collapses into prioritarianism or sufficiency once the qualifications accumulate, so that nothing turns on the luck/choice cut anymore; whether the hypothetical insurance device can be disciplined by anything (would actual people's insurance choices be admissible evidence, or is the fiction answerable to nothing?); and empirical developments that bear on the cut, such as the finding that traits like risk tolerance and conscientiousness are substantially heritable, which seems to geneticize the very dispositions the view treats as choice. Also tell me what the view predicts about our considered judgments in concrete cases — the uninsured smoker, the person born with expensive tastes cultivated over decades, the couple who refuse insurance for religious reasons — and which of these predictions distinguish it from its rivals rather than being shared by them.

Finally, be honest about the dialectical situation: which defeaters would a committed defender such as myself concede as counting against the view, and which would I predictably explain away? Have I qualified the position into something that risks nothing, and if so, is that a defect in a view of this kind, or just what maturity looks like in political philosophy? I am not looking to be reassured; if the honest verdict is that only the sufficiency floor is doing real work, I would rather know that now.
