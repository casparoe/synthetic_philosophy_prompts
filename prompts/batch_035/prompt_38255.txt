I'm a research engineer on an interpretability team at a mid-sized AI lab, and I've been asked to write a short essay for an internal workshop on whether our evals roadmap rests on unrealistic assumptions. The topic became unexpectedly relevant last week: a proposed evaluation of one of our models was rejected on the grounds that we couldn't say which internal state the evaluation was actually tracking, and my manager asked me to articulate in writing whether that kind of gap is a problem we should expect to eventually solve or one we should learn to design around. Below are my notes; I'd appreciate comments before I turn them into the full essay.

The core of my argument is that the project of "ontology identification" — recovering humanly meaningful concepts like deception or intent from a model's internal representations — is not merely hard but impossible in a strong sense, because of what alignment researchers sometimes call inaccessible information, following Paul Christiano: information that is easy for a learned system to extract and act on, but that humans cannot efficiently verify or even specify without recapitulating the system's own ontology. My reasoning: (1) if humanly natural concepts were the most efficient way to organize the predictive work a large model does, we would expect its activations to cluster along our concepts, but there's no reason gradient descent should favor our ontology over cheaper, alien ones; (2) even where something like "deception" is partially recoverable from a model's internals, what makes the recovered feature an implementation of deception rather than a mere correlate of it is a fact about how the model would behave in counterfactual situations we cannot enumerate; (3) so the best we can hope for is leaky, context-bound approximations of human concepts, and our evaluations should be redesigned around behavioral stress tests rather than readings of internal state. A colleague raised doubts: interpretability work has already turned up features that behave like human concepts well enough to steer models with, so perhaps "impossible" should just be "expensive." I'm also unsure whether the distinction between a fundamental limit and an engineering difficulty even makes sense here — any fundamental-looking limit could in principle be dissolved by humans adopting the model's concepts as their own, and I don't know whether to count that as success or failure. Finally, I worry the argument equivocates between "the model's ontology differs from ours" and "the model's ontology is inaccessible to us," since those come apart for representations that decompose into human-understandable parts.

Please comment on the notes: which of the numbered steps hold up, which are weak, whether my colleague's objection defeats premise (1), and how I might sharpen or reformulate the distinction between engineering difficulty and a fundamental limit.
