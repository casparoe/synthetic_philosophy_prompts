I am preparing a position paper on the ethics of collective memory for a workshop that will include both philosophers and practitioners of transitional justice, and before I send anything out I would like an audit of whether the views I have settled on form a coherent package. Here is the set, as I currently hold it.

1. I follow Margalit in thinking that the duty to remember historical wrongs is fundamentally communal rather than universal. It runs through thick relations — the ties among the members of a community, extended across generations — so that a wrong done to my own community's dead imposes a duty on me, while a comparable wrong done to strangers' ancestors imposes, at most, a claim on my concern. Only radical evil on the scale of genocide plausibly generates a duty that reaches humanity as a whole.

2. I hold that collective memory is partly constitutive of communal identity. What a community remembers of its past is not an inventory it happens to carry but something closer to a definition of who it is; a community that lost a founding memory would not be the same community minus one item, but a somewhat different community.

3. I also accept Margalit's own proviso that remembrance can serve either as the cement that binds a community together or as sand thrown in its members' eyes — and I believe, from watching how memories of past victimization were mobilized in the breakup of Yugoslavia, that a substantial amount of what presents itself as dutiful remembrance is in fact sand. In such cases I side with letting the memory decay, even where the wrong remembered was entirely real.

4. I take the South African settlement to be the model of dealing with an atrocious past: amnesty conditioned on full public disclosure, truth exchanged for immunity, no forgetting required. And yet I also think the Spanish pact of forgetting was, in its historical circumstances, a defensible arrangement, and I resist treating it as a mere failure of nerve.

5. I am sympathetic to Rieff's skepticism about remembrance as policy: I do not think remembering past atrocity has any reliable tendency to prevent its repetition, and I suspect the cult of memory can itself become a source of fresh harm.

6. Finally, I hold that a community's duty of memory is discharged institutionally — through memorials, curricula, days of remembrance — rather than by each member privately. The individual's obligation is at most one of participation; personal forgetting does not breach the communal duty.

What I would like is a diagnosis of which of these commitments pull against each other, how badly, and what the cheapest repair to the set would be. For each genuine tension, tell me what would have to give and what that concession costs; for each pair that only appears to be in tension, show why it is not; and at the end, tell me which single change would resolve the most. I should say plainly that I do not myself know where the deepest problem lies, so please do not shape the analysis around a conclusion you assume I have already reached.
