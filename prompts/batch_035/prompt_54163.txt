I've been a regular reader of the LessWrong-adjacent corner of the internet for over a decade, and somewhere along the way I became convinced by the Lucas–Penrose line of argument: that Gödel's incompleteness results show human mathematical understanding can't be mechanized, because for any consistent formal system we can construct its Gödel sentence, see that it's true, and the system can't. I've read through the standard back-and-forth a few times, and I've noticed that defenders of the argument have accumulated a set of qualifications -- that the relevant human is idealized, that the claim concerns in-principle limits rather than current hardware, that the human only needs to see the system's soundness rather than prove it, that the argument targets systems we can *know* to be sound rather than systems as such. My friends in our rationalist reading group think these qualifications have quietly converted the thesis into something that no observation or result could ever touch, while I suspect at least some of them are load-bearing and genuine.

So: what would actually count against my view? I want the full map of its exposure, ranked from fatal through serious to merely awkward. Include the obvious candidates -- formal points about whether seeing the truth of a Gödel sentence for system S presupposes more than S's consistency, or whether humans can even coherently be attributed the "sees it's true" premise when they err -- but also the less-discussed ones: what it would mean for the argument if human arithmetical belief turned out to be formally inconsistent or unsound in describable ways, whether a machine that was itself unsound but happened to assert the right Gödel sentences would be a counterexample or an irrelevance, and whether the "proves too much" style of objection (that parallel reasoning would establish things about chess programs or calculators that are clearly false) lands. For each, tell me whether a thoughtful defender of the Lucas–Penrose view would themselves concede it as pressure, or would treat it as resting on a misreading, and be specific about which of the accumulated qualifications is doing the insulating in each case.

I'd also like you to play it straight on the meta-question, since this is the one my reading group keeps pressing: after all the qualifications, is there any conceivable development -- mathematical, computational, or empirical -- that would move a reasonable holder of this view, and if not, is that a defect in the view or just the ordinary condition of a metaphysical thesis about minds? If you think the view is genuinely well insulated, say so plainly and tell me whether the insulation comes from the qualifications or from the structure of the argument itself. I'm not looking to have my view confirmed; I'd rather have an honest accounting of what I've actually bet on than win the argument at meetup.
