I write literary fiction, and I've been invited to run a single 90-minute session at a residential writers' retreat for twelve adult participants, all of them serious amateurs or early-career novelists. I want to design a session that makes them feel, in their bones, a problem in the philosophy of value that I keep running into in my own work as a novelist: the possibility that "better than" is not transitive — that a piece of writing can be better than another, which is better than a third, and yet the third can be better than the first, once the comparisons are made across a long enough spectrum of small changes. I have in mind the structure of the classic spectrum arguments in value theory, though I do not want the session to be a lecture about those arguments; I want the problem to erupt out of the participants' own aesthetic judgments before anyone names it.

Here is the raw material I can bring: before the retreat, I will write a chain of prose passages — say eight to twelve versions of the same 200-word paragraph — each one a small, locally defensible improvement on the last. The early versions would be flat and inert; each step trades a little plainness for a little vividness, specificity, and rhythm; and the terminal version would be unmistakably overwrought — the kind of maximalist prose that most working novelists at a retreat like this would privately find embarrassing. I can calibrate the steps so that each adjacent pair presents a genuinely easy call, while the endpoints present an easy call in the opposite direction.

Please design the full session for me. I need: the precise sequence of what happens, minute by minute; how the pairwise judgments are actually collected (ballots, a show of hands, written rankings at the start — I want genuine private commitment to some judgments before others are made, so participants can't retroactively rationalize); how to sequence the adjacent-pair comparisons against the endpoint comparison so that the contradiction is discovered rather than announced; what to do when the group splits on a pair; and how to run the debrief so that it maps specific moments in the session onto the parts of the philosophical problem — pairwise better-than, the transitivity assumption, the difference between "better in every relevant respect I can name" and "better all things considered," and the question of whether the endpoints are genuinely comparable at all.

I would also like you to be candid about two things. First, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this: which step is most likely to collapse into general discussion, how participants will try to escape the trap (claiming they never really preferred the middle version, refusing the endpoint comparison, redefining "better" mid-session), and how I should handle each escape attempt without bullying them. Second, and most important: which feature of the philosophical problem this activity cannot reproduce. My paragraph chain is a series of aesthetic judgments made by fallible perceivers, and I suspect that whatever contradiction emerges might be explained away as perceptual error or context-dependence rather than as genuine intransitivity in the better-than relation itself. Tell me honestly where the activity is doing real philosophical work and where it is only simulating the problem, and what I, as the facilitator, must supply in the debrief that the exercise cannot generate on its own.

Do not design anything that requires money, technology beyond a whiteboard, or participants to have read anything in advance.
