I am an undergraduate currently enrolled in a seminar on logical empiricism, and our final assignment is to stage a philosophical dispute between two positions in a way that a reader could not immediately tell which side the author favors. I have chosen the dispute over the analytic–synthetic distinction after Quine's attack on it in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism," but I want the dialogue to be anchored in a concrete episode rather than an exchange of position-paper abstractions. I would like you to write that dialogue for me as a draft I can work from.

The setting: two graduate students, one a convinced Quinean and the other a defender of a broadly Carnapian position, are arguing late at night in 2011, shortly after the OPERA collaboration announced that neutrinos appeared to have traveled faster than light. (As it turned out, the anomaly was traced to a loose fiber-optic cable, but the two students do not know this yet.) The Quinean seizes on the episode to argue that it vindicates the rejection of a sharp analytic–synthetic line: when an anomaly of that magnitude arrives, nothing fixes in advance which of our beliefs must be given up — the physicist can revise the theory, question the experiment, or, in the extreme case, revise even the mathematics or logic used to frame the laws. Everything is revisable in principle, and the difference between "factual" and "definitional" claims is a matter of degree of entrenchment in the web of belief, not a difference in kind.

The Carnapian should not concede. Her strongest available reply, as I understand it, is that this sort of revisability simply does not touch the analytic–synthetic distinction once it is properly drawn: the choice between linguistic frameworks is a pragmatic one, guided by convenience and fruitfulness rather than truth, while questions posed within an adopted framework have factual answers. On her view, the Quinean has shown only that framework choice is not itself answerable to evidence — which Carnap never denied — and has mistakenly concluded that the internal factual questions and the external pragmatic ones collapse into a single continuum. She might add that even the radical option the Quinean gestures at — following Hilary Putnam's old suggestion that the anomalies of quantum mechanics might be met by revising the distributive law rather than the physics — is better described as adopting a different language for doing physics than as discovering that logic was "false," and that the Quinean picture has trouble saying what distinguishes that move from ordinary theoretical revision.

A few specific requirements for the dialogue:

1. Both interlocutors should be equally competent, and each should land at least one point that the other has to actually address rather than talk past — for instance, the Quinean pressing on what work "pragmatic choice" is doing if no constraint of truth or justification applies to it, and the Carnapian pressing on how the Quinean's degree-of-entrenchment picture could ever explain why it is rational to keep believing arithmetic while revising the neutrino result.

2. They should each explicitly connect the neutrino episode to their general position, and disagree about what the episode is evidence of, rather than treating it as mere backdrop.

3. At some point they should test their views against a humdrum candidate for analyticity — a truth like "no bachelor is married" or "all bachelors are unmarried" — since my professor has warned us that the famous examples and the scientific examples can pull in different directions, and I want to see how each side handles that tension.

4. The dialogue should end unresolved, but with the disagreement narrowed to a precise question the two of them can state and agree on — something like whether the analytic–synthetic distinction, once reformulated as a distinction between internal and external questions, still does the work the original distinction was supposed to do.

Please do not have either character quote specific passages verbatim, and do not have them attribute exact quotations to Quine or Carnap; paraphrase their positions in the characters' own words. The dialogue should run roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words, in a natural but elevated conversational register appropriate to two philosophy graduate students. I plan to use it as the backbone of my term paper, so please make sure the arguments attributed to each side are ones that a careful scholar would recognize as fairly representative, even though the characters speak in their own voices.
