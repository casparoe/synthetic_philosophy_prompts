My partner and I have been overhauling the yard, and somewhere in the process I realized I don't know what my own views on killing insects and small animals amount to. Rather than argue about it in the abstract, I've started keeping a list of my gut verdicts on concrete cases. Killing the mosquito that has just landed on my arm and is about to bite: obviously fine, not a flicker of hesitation. Hunting down the one in the bedroom at two in the morning so that I can sleep, and swatting it against the wall: also fine, though the paint did not survive. The spider in the shower, which was minding its own business: some years ago I would have washed it down the drain without a thought, and I find I can't do that anymore — it gets cupped and carried out. The ants trailing across the counter into the sugar jar: spraying them is fine. Those same ants, followed back outside to the nest under a stone in the far corner of the garden, where they bother no one: pouring boiling water on them, which a neighbor cheerfully recommended, struck me as grisly, and I wouldn't do it. Termites in the joists: the house gets treated, whatever it costs the colony. The mouse in the pantry: a snap trap is fine, but the glue boards the hardware store pushes felt plainly wrong, and I've quietly stopped buying them.

Here are some more, and this is where I can feel my verdicts pulling against each other. The wasp nest on the porch beam, right where the kids come and go: I had it destroyed without a moment's unease. A nest of the very same wasps in the corner of the shed, a building I open perhaps twice a year: I left it, and tearing it out would have felt gratuitous. Aphids on the roses: I'll spray, but with the soap spray; the one time I reached for the broad-spectrum bottle that would have taken out the ladybirds and, presumably, the bees along with the aphids, I put it back on the shelf — it suddenly felt like the outdoor-ant case, killing a great deal that had done nothing. The cat with a mouse: I don't blame the cat, and I've made a few half-hearted lunges to intervene, but what I feel watching her is not what I would feel doing it myself. And then the verdict that embarrasses me: the mosquito-control district sprays the entire neighborhood every summer, which must kill thousands of times more insects than every personal case of mine put together, and I have never given it a second's thought — it doesn't even register as a decision, whereas the ladybirds stopped me cold. My rough attempt at a principle goes something like this: it is permissible to kill when something is genuinely threatening my household's health or doing real damage; it is impermissible to kill merely for tidiness or presence; and it is never permissible to make the dying slower or more painful than the situation requires. But I can feel this creaking, because I can't tell whether the principle is supposed to be about what the killing is for or about what the dying is like — my verdicts seem to switch keys between the two.

The case I keep going back and forth on, and the one I'd most like your account to say something definite about: in late autumn a cluster fly gets in. It does nothing — it just sits drowsily at the window — but if I carry it out, it dies of the cold within a day; if I leave it in, it lives for weeks; if I swat it, it's over in an instant. Killing it quickly feels more merciful than putting it out to freeze, and yet more like a betrayal than either, since it never threatened anything. So here is what I'd like from you. Take my verdicts as the data and tell me whether a single principle, or a small set of them, captures the pattern — stating each clause precisely and naming the case that forces it in. Then tell me what the resulting account predicts on cases I haven't listed: a restaurant kitchen with roaches behind the fryer; a farmer choosing between a targeted insecticide and a broad one against cutworms; live-trapping the pantry mouse and driving it to a field two miles away, where I suspect it mostly feeds owls. And close honestly: which of my verdicts, if any, cannot be accommodated without making the principle ugly, and in each such case whether you would keep the principle and revise the verdict, or the other way around.
