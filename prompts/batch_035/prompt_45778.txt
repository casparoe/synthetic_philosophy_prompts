I'd like a prediction, and I'd like it structured so I can see the reasoning, because I'm testing whether my friend's use of the word "power" has stable content.

Background: after a term of reading Foucault together — Discipline and Punish and the first volume of the History of Sexuality — a friend and I have fallen into a routine. Whenever we encounter a social arrangement, I ask her whether it counts as power in what she takes to be the distinctively Foucauldian sense: not power as prohibition or brute force, but power as productive, working through subjects rather than merely on them. Her verdicts so far, with the glosses she gave:

1. A panoptic prison — inmates permanently visible to an unseen warden, discipline maintained without bars or beatings. Verdict: yes. The arrangement makes the prisoner his own overseer.
2. A knife-point mugging. Verdict: no. That is force operating on a body, not power operating through a subject.
3. A monarch's edict making heresy capital, enforced by occasional spectacular public execution. Verdict: no. That is the repressive, sovereign model — the scaffold rather than the timetable.
4. A school that grades, ranks, and publicly posts the rankings, turning pupils into comparable quantities. Verdict: yes.
5. The confessional, where absolution is conditional on speaking one's desire aloud. Verdict: yes. The desire is not merely revealed but constituted, she says.
6. A village where nothing is recorded, no one examines anyone, and nobody is in charge — but standing depends entirely on accumulated face-to-face gossip, and everyone self-regulates accordingly. Verdict: yes. This one surprised me: no tower, no dossier, no institution at all, and she still said yes.
7. A national census that counts and classifies the entire population for purposes of taxation, never touching anyone's behavior. Verdict: no. The state learns things, she said, but the population learns nothing about itself — nothing is formed.

Tomorrow our reading group takes up a new case and I want to predict her verdict before she gives it. The case: a government switches organ donation from opt-in to opt-out. No surveillance, no examination, no sanction, no exhortation — the form is simply redesigned, and donation rates jump because almost nobody ever touches a default.

Here is what I want from you:

(a) The candidate rules that fit her seven verdicts. I can see at least two in the vicinity: one on which power requires subjects to internalize a gaze or a standard, and one on which power is any structuring of a subject's field of action that operates through the subject's own motives rather than through force or external sanction. As far as I can tell, both predict her record exactly — though I have not been careful about cases 2 and 3, where a threat plainly does work through the victim's own fear, and yet she said no. Spell out the rules you see, sharpen them where they need it, and say honestly whether some verdict fits one better than the other.

(b) Predict her verdict on the defaults case under each candidate rule. The interesting feature of the case, as I see it, is that nothing is internalized — no one adopts a norm or comes to self-monitor — and yet the field of possible action is massively restructured, by way of subjects doing nothing at all. If that observation is right, the rules should split on it. Give your best guess with your reasons, and say which feature of the case your guess turns on.

(c) Name the further case — one we could actually pose to her next week — that would best discriminate between the candidate rules you found, and explain why it discriminates where the defaults case might not.

(d) Tell me how confident you are in your best guess, and single out the one thing about my report of her verdicts that you are least sure of — for instance, a verdict you suspect reflects the wording I used in describing the case rather than her considered view.
