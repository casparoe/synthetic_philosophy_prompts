I'm putting together a unit on presentism for a historiography seminar -- mostly the standard stuff, Butterfield's whig history, the contextualists arguing you can only judge an actor once you've reconstructed the norms they were reasoning within, versus people who think moral distance is just an excuse factory. To keep it from being bloodless I'm pairing Octavia Butler's Kindred as our test case, and I want to write up a reading to hand out, but I'm genuinely torn about what the novel is saying.

If you don't remember it: Dana, a Black woman in 1976 Los Angeles, keeps getting pulled back in time to an antebellum Maryland plantation to save the life of Rufus, her white slaveholding ancestor. She can't let him die without erasing herself, and she can't leave, so she has to live inside the institution she's been taught to condemn.

Here's my problem. The novel seems to run both sides of the presentism debate at once. Dana arrives with something like the presentist's confidence -- she knows slavery is evil and expects that knowledge to hold up -- but surviving forces her into something suspiciously like the contextualist's position: she learns the internal logic of the Weylin household, stops being shocked, starts calculating what she can and can't say, even develops a weird protectiveness toward Rufus that she herself finds disturbing. That reads like an argument that moral judgment from the outside is a luxury that dissolves on contact with the past's actual texture. But then Butler keeps pulling the rug out: Alice's death, Sarah the cook's resigned compliance that Dana can't bring herself to condemn from her perch of untested 1976 morals, and the ending, where the past literally takes Dana's arm off. Is Butler saying contextual understanding is real but doesn't add up to excusing anything? That understanding and forgiveness are separable in a way the historiography literature often conflates?

I'd also like the reading to take seriously a disanalogy that I think matters and that my students will miss: Dana isn't a historian reading sources, she's a body in the past. The contextualist historian has to work to reconstruct the norms; Dana gets them beaten into her, which is a different epistemic route to the same "understanding." Does the novel therefore vindicate the contextualist in a way no archive could, or does it cheat -- knowing a regime from inside as a victim isn't the neutral immersion contextualism imagines? And Kevin, her white husband, stranded for five years, comes back having drifted toward the master's worldview without noticing. His case seems to be the dark twin of contextualism: absorb enough context and the presentist's standards don't just get complicated, they decay. Is that Butler's real answer to the debate?

Give me a reading that takes the historiography seriously -- Butterfield, Skinner-ish contextualism, whoever else belongs -- and tells me what the novel dramatizes that the methodological literature can't, where the novel resists the reading, and which scenes I should actually point the students to. I don't want it reduced to "novel says slavery bad"; obviously it says that. I want the sharper thing the book seems to know about what happens to judgment when you can't keep your distance.
