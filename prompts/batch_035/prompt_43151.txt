So this is a bit embarrassing for someone who teaches political philosophy, but a line I've leaned on for years is starting to bother me, and I want to figure out whether it's actually an argument or just a noise people make. The line is the classic: "if you don't vote, you can't complain." Last week at the department a grad student who abstained in the local elections was grousing about the school board, and a colleague shut her down with that line plus something about her having had her chance in November. She just kind of deflated, and I realized I couldn't say what, if anything, the line had actually established. It's clearly doing *some* work — people don't reach for it for nothing — but I can't pin down what the sentence is even claiming, and the readings I can think of don't all pull in the same direction.

Here's my attempt so far, so you can see where I'm stuck. It might be a normative claim about standing: abstaining is a waiver, and having waived participation you've forfeited the right to criticize the outcome, the way you can't complain about a movie you chose to walk out of. Or a causal claim: the outcome is, in some sense, self-inflicted for you specifically, since you passed on your one chance to affect it. Or an epistemic one: voting is the minimal homework that earns you the floor. Or maybe it's not normative at all — just a hypocrisy-tracking observation that non-voters who complain are being inconsistent somehow, and the inconsistency does the dismissive work. Some of these look flat-out false (the school board race wasn't within any margin her one vote would have touched), some look plausible but too weak to justify shutting her down, and one or two might actually hold up. And there's a wrinkle: the same colleague is against compulsory voting, so I'd also like to know whether the reading that best supports dismissing the student, taken seriously, slides into a case for making voting mandatory — or whether the slogan can be true in the complaint-foreclosing sense while staying neutral on compulsion.

So what I'm after is a menu, not a ruling. Lay out the distinct precise claims the one-liner could be expressing, from the trivial to the substantive; say for each whether it's plausible, and crucially whether it would actually license dismissing the student's complaint (a reading can be true and still not get you that). If you can, also tell me which reading people who repeat the slogan most likely have in mind, since I suspect it's not the one doing the real work. Keep it in plain language — I'd like to be able to hand this back to my colleague over coffee without a lecture's worth of apparatus.
