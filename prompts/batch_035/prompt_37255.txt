I belong to my school's philosophy society, and for two terms now we have been stuck on a single question: are beliefs real things in the head? One faction holds, roughly with Dennett, that attributing beliefs and desires is adopting the intentional stance — a prediction strategy that tracks real patterns in a person's behavior, rather as a physicist posits a center of gravity: not a little object hidden inside the body, but not a mere fiction either, since the pattern is out there in what the person does and says. The other faction holds, roughly with Fodor, that if beliefs are to do any causal work they must be concrete states of the brain, presumably sentence-shaped; and they suspect that the pattern-talk is just instrumentalism in fancier clothing. Both sides know the standard moves by heart, nobody has moved since October, and our adviser has stopped pretending a meeting is going anywhere. My own hypothesis is that the dispute is at bottom verbal: that the two factions mean different things by 'real' — one meaning something like 'predictively indispensable,' the other 'eventually identifiable with a neural structure' — and that this is why their arguments never actually collide. But I worry this lets both sides off too easily, since there may be a hidden empirical question underneath, namely whether the brain ever contains anything sentence-like at all. Please diagnose the disagreement for me: is it verbal, substantive, a case of two questions being run under one heading, or a standoff between starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? Support the diagnosis with what each side actually claims, not just with labels, and then tell me honestly what would count as progress — a conceptual distinction, a kind of finding, a better-framed question — or whether we should expect none, and why.
