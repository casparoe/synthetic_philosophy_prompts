I am a palliative-care physician. My working vocabulary consists of the terms I use every day on the ward: decision-making capacity, goals-of-care conversations, proportionality of expected burden to expected benefit, the distinction between withholding and withdrawing treatment, the doctrine of double effect as it figures in opioid dosing and terminal sedation, medical futility, and the framing of a death that follows a refused intervention as allowed rather than caused. I am fluent in that vocabulary and have no background in early modern philosophy. I have recently read Hume's posthumously published essay on suicide, in which he argues that ending one's own life, when age, illness, or calamity have made it a burden, is no greater an offense against God, against one's duty to oneself, or against one's duty to society than diverting a river from its course or taking a medicine when nature provides the means. I do not want a summary in Hume's own idiom; I want a translation into mine.

Restate each of his main lines of argument in the clinical vocabulary I actually use. Treat the religious argument about providence and our license to disturb nature, the argument about courage, prudence, and duty to oneself, and the argument about the point at which one ceases to be useful and becomes a burden to society as separate translation problems, and be candid when one of his premises has no clinical counterpart at all — do not manufacture a strained equivalent just to fill the slot. Keep the translation honest in the other direction too: do not smuggle in the assumptions of my field, such as that patient autonomy is the operative value or that death is presumptively a harm to be postponed, and present them as though they were Hume's.

Then give me the residue report. Which of his claims translate cleanly? Which translate only with distortion — for instance, does anything in my vocabulary reproduce his observation that we never in fact preserve life but only postpone death within limits nature has already fixed, given that in my world the refusal of treatment is treated as categorically unlike an act that ends life? And which of his claims cannot be expressed at all? I notice, for example, that my specialty constitutes an intermediate institution between continuing to live in misery and dying, one that Hume's dichotomy of enduring versus ending has no room for; I want to know whether that reflects a gap in his argument or a distortion forced by my vocabulary. I also want the capacity question addressed: Hume presumes a reasoner deliberating about his own good, while my vocabulary contains an entire gatekeeping apparatus for asking whether the deliberator counts as such — which framework does the residue favor there?

Finally, tell me which way the residue cuts overall. Is there something in Hume's position that the clinical vocabulary is failing to carry — something the translators of my field into ethics quietly dropped? Or is the residue evidence that his argument was less clear than it looked, in particular whether the theological premise, once removed, leaves the prudential and social arguments strong enough to stand on their own? I would rather leave the ward puzzled than leave it with a translation that flatters my habits.
