I am a retired seismologist who has spent the past year reading political philosophy, and a habit of mind from my old trade keeps intruding on the reading. For thirty-five years I inferred unobservable quantities — the depth of a hypocentre, the moment of a rupture — from arrays of noisy surface instruments, and I cannot rid myself of the conviction that the will of the people is the same sort of thing: a single latent quantity, existing out there prior to any instrument, which our institutions merely detect, more or less well. On that reading, populism seems to me nothing more than the sensible engineering position — the demand that we replace the distorting old pendulums of party and parliament with direct measurement — and a mature democracy would look something like a modern seismic network: frequent referendums, opt-in citizen panels polled weekly, confidence intervals on the popular will published alongside the unemployment figures. When two readings disagree — a referendum result contradicted by the polls a year later — that looks to me like ordinary instrument error, to be dealt with by calibration, larger samples, and more careful wording, not as evidence against the quantity itself. My son, who read politics at university, tells me that philosophers regard this whole picture as not merely optimistic but somehow conceptually confused, though he cannot say clearly why, and mutters only that the median voter theorem is not the whole story.

So my question is this: granted that the instruments need refinement, is the remaining opposition to direct measurement of the popular will anything more than status-quo bias on the part of a professional class grown fond of its antique apparatus? If there is a genuine conceptual error somewhere in my seismological picture, I should like it located precisely, not waved at.
