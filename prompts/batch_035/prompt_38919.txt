I've been working through Douglas Hofstadter's Metamagical Themas material on superrationality and I can't decide how much of the argument I actually accept. In the Platonia dilemma, an eccentric trillionaire gathers twenty people and announces that if exactly one of them sends him a telegram, reverse charges, by noon the next day, that lone sender will receive a billion dollars; if more than one telegram arrives, or none at all, nobody gets anything. Hofstadter's superrational reasoning holds that identical reasoners confronting a symmetric problem will converge on the same answer, so each participant should evaluate strategies under the constraint that all twenty are choosing alike — which, once mixed strategies are admitted, licenses each person to send a telegram with probability one-twentieth rather than either the near-certain ruin of everyone sending or the free-riding hope that everyone else stays silent. The companion Luring Lottery that Scientific American actually ran in the 1980s, where a one-million-dollar prize was divided by the total number of entries and a randomly chosen entry won it, drives at the same point: the superrational reader follows Hofstadter's dice-rolling scheme and effectively submits a single postcard with a small probability, while the game-theoretic reader floods the lottery — some entrants famously wrote down a googolplex of entries — and reduces the prize to a minuscule fraction of a cent. My friend thinks all of this is seductive nonsense: there is no causal channel between my telegram and anyone else's, so treating my decision as evidence of theirs, let alone as somehow determining theirs, is magical thinking dressed up in recursive language. My credence that superrationality gives the correct account of what an idealized rational agent should do in a one-shot symmetric dilemma like Platonia is roughly 0.45; hers is roughly 0.9.

Please give me your own number, but more importantly decompose it. The terms I would expect to see, roughly: the probability that decision-theoretic rationality should be assessed from a standpoint of logical or functional correlation between agents rather than purely causal channels; the probability that the symmetry premise holds — that identical reasoning genuinely entails linked choices absent any causal connection; the probability that the mixed-strategy extension of superrationality is principled rather than an ad hoc patch bolted on after the pure cases misbehave; and the probability that the intuitions doing most of the argumentative work in the two-person prisoner's dilemma actually generalize to Platonia's twenty-person, only-one-winner structure, which is arguably a different beast since the cooperative outcome must be asymmetric by construction. Feel free to add or drop terms as you see fit, assign each a rough probability with a one-line justification, and show how they combine into your final credence.

Then tell me which term you are least confident in, what evidence or argument would move it the most, and where you think my friend and I are most likely to be talking past each other given that her credence is nearly double mine. Is our disagreement really about causation and evidential versus causal decision theory, or about something upstream — such as what the word "rational" is even supposed to track in a one-shot interaction we will never repeat, where there is no one to reciprocate, punish, or learn from our choice?
