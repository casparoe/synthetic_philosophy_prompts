Quick background: I'm a freelance science journalist, been on the quantum-foundations beat for roughly a decade now, mostly for outlets that make you explain entanglement in 800 words next to a stock photo of a shiny atom. Over those years I have basically watched the same fight happen at every conference and in my inbox: one physicist telling me Bell's theorem proved the world is nonlocal, full stop, and another telling me that's journalistic laziness — that what Bell actually ruled out was "local realism", and that you can hang onto a perfectly local world if you're willing to give something else up instead (retrocausality, many worlds, or the nastiest option, superdeterminism). I've quoted both camps, both camps have emailed me corrections, and I've slowly come to beleive that a decent chunk of the argument is about which words we're allowed to use rather than about the physics.

So here's the situation. A nonprofit I do bits of work for — they fund science communication projects — has agreed to pay two physicists, one from each camp, to sit down for a proper adversarial collaboration, and I'm the one facilitating / ghostwriting it. They've never agreed on anything in public before, and my pitch to the funder was that the argument itself is the story, told properly for once.

What I need from you is the draft joint statement I'll take into their first working session: the document the two of them could conceivably both sign after a good-faith collaboration, written so that each side would accept the wording of its own view even where they still disagree. Please structure it as:

1. A numbered section of claims both could sign — about the theorem itself (what the observed correlations force, what the experiments actually established, which loopholes really got closed and when), about the different things "locality" and "realism" could each mean here, and about which popular framings of Bell are simply wrong no matter which camp you ask.

2. A disagreements table: the exact residual disagreements, each phrased so its own side reads it and says yes, that's my position. Candidates they'd probably fight over: whether Bell's derivation needs any realist assumption at all, whether superdeterminism is a scientific position or a rejection of the scientific method dressed up as one, and whether the many-worlds escape counts as "local" in any sense a general reader could be told about. But let the two camps' actual positions drive the table, not my guesses.

3. The cruxes — sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp — and for each, the argument, experiment, or future development both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. I know some of these will end up being philosophy rather than measurement; for those, have each side say what physics has to look like in fifty years if they're right.

4. A closing section on what's at stake for people like me: if part of the argument turns out to be verbal — say, about whether "local" means no superluminal signalling, no superluminal causation, or Bell's technical notion of local causality — say which part is verbal and why. And then an actual recommendation on whether the phrase "local realism" should be banned from science coverage. My editor would genuinely take that recommendation, which is the closest thing to a real-world consequence this project will ever have.

A few constraints. No citations, no bibliography — I'll handle sourcing, and honestly I don't want you inventing references. Keep it signable: neither side gets to smuggle its view in as an "agreed" claim, and neither gets its view strawmanned. And crucially, don't resolve the debate for them — the whole point is a document two people who've been sniping at each other through a science journalist for years could both actually put their names to. If you think one camp is just right, bite it down; the deliverable is the collaboration, not the verdict.
