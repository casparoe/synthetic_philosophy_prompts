The exam question was:

Distinguish etiological (selected-effect) accounts of biological function from causal-role accounts, and illustrate the difference with the heart and its pumping of blood. Then apply both accounts to the following case. Certain beetles of the Namib Desert have wing covers whose surface is patterned with hydrophilic bumps and waxy troughs; the bumps condense droplets from coastal fog, and the droplets roll down the beetle's angled back to its mouth. Suppose that the wing covers originally evolved in the beetle lineage as armor for the folded flight wings, and that only much later, in the population ancestral to today's Namib beetles, did individuals whose wing covers condensed fog more efficiently survive and reproduce better, so that the present condensation efficiency was shaped by that selection. (a) On each account, does the wing cover have the function of condensing water for the beetle? Does it have the function of protecting the wings? (b) Explain how each account handles malfunction, e.g., a heart that fails to pump.

The student's answer was:

Etiological accounts look to the history of a trait: the function of a trait is the effect for which it was selected in the organism's ancestors, which is why Neander and Millikan's view is often called the selected-effect account. Causal-role accounts look at what a trait does now: for Cummins, the function of a trait is whatever the trait currently does that increases the organism's fitness. This is why the heart's function is to pump blood on both views — pumping is what hearts were selected for, and pumping is also what hearts do that keeps the organism alive.

For the beetle: on the etiological view, the wing cover's function is to condense fog. The question says the ancestors with better condensation were selected for that, so condensing water is the effect the trait was selected for, and that settles it. Protecting the wings cannot also be its function, since the trait was most recently selected for condensation, and the function tracks the most recent episode of selection. On the causal-role view the wing cover also has the function of condensing water, because condensation contributes to the organism's water budget, and the water budget is something researchers analyzing the beetle's physiology would pick out as a system-level capacity of interest. So the two views agree on the beetle, at least about condensation.

On malfunction, the etiological account clearly does better. A heart that fails to pump still has the function of pumping, because the function was fixed by selection in the ancestors, not by what this particular heart is doing now, so it counts as a malfunction rather than a heart without a function. The causal-role account handles this equally well: a heart that fails to pump is malfunctioning because pumping is its causal role and it is failing to perform it, in the same sense in which the etiological account says it is malfunctioning.
