I work on temporal aspects of visual perception, and over the last few years I've been talked into what I take to be the minority position on the color phi phenomenon: the Stalinesque one. When two stationary dots flash in the same position a couple hundred milliseconds apart — the first red, the second green — most people report not two flashes but a single dot that moves and changes color midway. On the Orwellian picture, the subject first experienced two discrete flashes and the brain subsequently rewrote the record, leaving memory saying there was a smooth red-to-green transit. On the Stalinesque picture, endorsed by Dennett's Multiple Drafts model, the delay between stimulus and experience is not wasted time: the brain exploits it, filling in the trajectory and the mid-flight color shift before the content becomes available for report, so no memory revision is needed because there was never an experience of the raw sequence to revise. I find the Stalinesque story right, and I want to stress-test it.

Tell me what would count against it. Specifically:

1. Which empirical findings would genuinely damage the Stalinesque view, and how would each do its damage? Consider real-time measures that bypass retrospective report — eye movements, rapid motor responses, emotion-driven physiological markers locked to stimulus onset — and whether there is any pattern in them (e.g., early responses tracking the raw stimulus rather than the constructed transit) that the Stalinesque view cannot accommodate while the Orwellian view predicts it. Consider also the family of postdiction effects the view is typically run together with: the cutaneous rabbit, backwards masking, the flash-lag effect, and Libet's claims about the late arrival of consciousness and its subjective antedating. Are there dissociations among these phenomena that would force the Stalinesque camp to splinter — cases where the delay-based filling-in story works for one effect but visibly fails for another?

2. What does the Stalinesque view predict that its Orwellian rival does not? I'm interested in whether there is any observable difference between pre-experiential construction and post-experiential memory revision at all — neural traces that are genuinely perceptual (adaptation, motion aftereffects from the illusory transit, remapping effects) versus genuinely memorial — or whether the two views are empirically indistinguishable by construction. If they are indistinguishable, say so plainly rather than manufacturing tests.

3. Which deeper commitments does my position stand or fall with, beyond the phenomenon itself? For instance: the intuition that there is always a fact about "what it was like" at a given moment; the unity and seriality of consciousness; the assumption that the temporal order of experience must match either the order of stimulation or the order of the eventual record. If some of these were to give way, would that merely bruise the Stalinesque view or break it?

4. Here is the objection I most want addressed head-on: Dennett himself argues that once you reject the Cartesian Theater, the Orwell–Stalin dispute dissolves — there is no determinate fact about whether the "editing" happened before or after "the" experience, because there is no single place where content becomes conscious. If that retreat is available, it looks like my position can never lose: any apparent counterevidence gets absorbed by the claim that the question was ill-posed. Assess honestly whether the Stalinesque view, defended this way, has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so, whether that is a defect for a theory of this kind or simply the correct refusal to answer a question built on a broken presupposition. If you think there is a version of the view that remains genuinely at risk, say what keeps it at risk.

Please rank the defeaters you identify from fatal to merely awkward, and distinguish those a committed defender of the view would concede as counting against it from those they would dismiss as question-begging. Do not give me a bibliography — argue the case directly. I'd also like you to state how confident you are in each of your main claims and single out the point you are least sure of.
