I'm a philosophy PhD student (early modern, mostly Descartes-adjacent stuff) and I got roped into teaching a unit of our department's Philosophy and Literature course next semester. The other grad students grabbed Frankenstein and the obvious dystopias, so I ended up with The Tempest, and I want to teach it against Montaigne's essay on cannibals, since the received story is that Shakespeare lifted Gonzalo's commonwealth speech more or less straight out of it (Florio's translation). The problem is that when I actually sat with the essay, it seemed to me Montaigne is doing something that the proto-cultural-relativist label papers over, and that's what I want the reading to be about.

Here's the tension as I see it. Montaigne gets filed under relativism because of the famous observation that we brand other peoples' customs barbarous merely because they aren't ours. But the essay's actual argumentative work seems to be done by a universal standard: the cannibals aren't just equally valid relative to their framework, they're closer to nature — genuine in war, no commerce, no rank, none of the artificialities — while Europe has drifted into corruption and is therefore worse, by a standard that's supposed to bind everyone. That looks like naturalism, not relativism; relativism is just what it looks like sitting next to how bad sixteenth-century Europe was. So the first thing I want the reading to take seriously: is the essay relativist at all, or is it a nature-based universalism that only reads like relativism in context? And then, what does the play do with this inheritance? Gonzalo delivers the Montaignevian speech and gets heckled for it almost in the same breath, and Prospero's whole regime rests on a nature/degeneracy distinction — Caliban is, in Prospero's telling, bad by birth and beyond improvement. So is Prospero basically doing Europe's "nature" move on Caliban, thereby exposing Montaigne's standard as a tool of power? Or does the play quietly endorse that standard?

The scene I keep coming back to, and the one I'd want the reading to center on, is where Caliban says the only profit he ever got from being taught Prospero's language is that he now knows how to curse. That cuts against both readings — language doesn't merely civilize, it also arms resentment — and I think it might be the place where the play is doing philosophy the essay doesn't. But I might be over-reading one short exchange. So: give me a reading of the play through this tension in Montaigne, be honest about where the reading strains (including whether hanging this much on the cursing speech is too much), tell me which scenes to put on the syllabus page, and don't treat either the essay or the play as a gloss on the other. My students will have read both texts, and I want to walk in with something thats actually arguable.
