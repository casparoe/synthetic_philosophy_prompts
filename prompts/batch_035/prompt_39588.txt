I've been reading around the old argument — it goes back at least to Rousseau, and was revived in different ways by Marshall Sahlins and Jared Diamond — that the shift from foraging to farming was a disaster for the people who lived through it. I can't settle it at that level of generality, so instead I've written down my gut verdicts on specific cases, and I want to know whether there is a coherent principle behind them or whether I'm just oscillating.

My verdicts:

1. The original transition: for the typical person alive at the time, and for many generations after, adopting farming made life worse — narrower diet, more disease, longer hours, and for the first time being governed by strangers. Judged from the standpoint of those who lived it, the transition was a mistake. That verdict feels solid to me.

2. The Industrial Revolution: on the same measures (real wages, adult height, urban life expectancy, child labor), the first two generations of factory workers seem to have been no better off, and possibly worse, than the rural people they left. Yet I do not want to call industrialization a mistake — it feels like a painful phase on the way to something good. I can't justify treating case 1 differently without feeling arbitrary, and this is the pair that bothers me most.

3. Inequality: what seems genuinely new and bad in the farming world is not captured by averages at all — it is permanent, heritable hierarchy, where being at the bottom is a condition you are born into and cannot leave. Yet the status differences inside a foraging band — the best hunter, the person whose word carries weight — do not strike me as the same kind of bad, and I don't know what marks the line: heritability? enforcement by specialists? scale?

4. Early farming villages: when I picture a small Neolithic community of roughly equal households, before chiefs, priests, or states, I judge that life as better than foraging — more predictable food, and still nobody ruling anybody. So my verdict in case 1 apparently was not about cultivation as a technique at all; it was about something that came later. But then I am unsure what the "it" was whose mistake-status I was pronouncing on: the adoption of farming, or the social evolution that followed it.

5. Numbers: when I think of the transition as more people existing who otherwise would not have, most of them living lives worth living, my mistake verdict wobbles; when I picture an individual farmer's worn joints and recurring famine, it comes back. I don't know which of these I actually hold, and I would like you to tell me which one my other verdicts commit me to.

What I want from you:
