I work as a risk analyst for a municipal water utility, and after a long argument with a friend — a devoted precise Bayesian who insists every rational credence is a single number — I've become sympathetic to imprecise probabilities instead: representing my uncertainty with intervals or sets of distributions rather than point values. But I can't turn sympathy into decisions. Please build me a decision procedure my team can actually run: when to treat a question as one of ambiguity rather than just risk, how to elicit and represent our uncertainty when experts disagree or models diverge, and crucially, what to do when our imprecise credences fail to determine a unique choice — which decision rule to apply (something like maximin over the set, or only ruling out options no distribution in the set favors, or something else), and when to just pick. Our decisions recur monthly, the stakes range from routine maintenance budgets to drought contingency planning, and the procedure has to be usable by analysts without formal training in a day or two. Run it on three cases: approving a new treatment plant sized under deep uncertainty about regional demand growth, setting an allocation between two watersheds under climate-model disagreement, and one routine case where the imprecise machinery turns out to be overkill. Mark honestly where the procedure just relocates judgment rather than eliminating it, and tell me where it will misfire — including the places where my Bayesian friend would say the whole apparatus collapses back into precise probabilities anyway.
