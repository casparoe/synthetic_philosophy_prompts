I've been reading LessWrong and adjacent blogs since the early 2010s, and I've watched the commitment races debate run in circles for years now. As I understand the original worry: in a multipolar future, the capacity to make binding commitments might arrive before the concepts needed to evaluate which commitments are wise. Agents that commit early — to paying any ransom, to trading at arbitrarily bad prices, to resisting any blackmail, or conversely to refusing all cooperation — can gain strategic advantages that force every other agent to also commit early, racing everyone into lock-ins made in a state of near-total ignorance about the consequences. The opposition has always had a ready reply: any agent that binds itself before it knows what it's binding itself to is an exploitable fool, economic and evolutionary selection should weed such agents out, and "I refuse to make commitments I can't evaluate" is itself a perfectly coherent policy that doesn't obviously lose to the racing policy. Then the counter-reply: refusing to commit early is itself a kind of commitment, and a counterparty who credibly credalized extortion first can leave the non-committer worse off — at which point everyone restates their opening position and the thread dies.

What strikes me is that after all this time, nobody's position has visibly moved, and I can't tell whether the participants are disagreeing about anything that could be resolved. My leading suspicion is that the dispute is parasitic on three separate unresolved questions that get bundled together: first, an empirical question about AI development that we literally cannot yet answer (whether commitment-like mechanisms will be cheap, fast, verifiable, and arrived-at before general reasoning, which may depend on messy details of takeoff dynamics); second, a conceptual muddle about what a "commitment" even is in this context — a binding modification of one's decision procedure, a merely credible signal, or just a hard-to-fake disposition, with the parties possibly talking past each other about which of these the race is over; and third, a submerged decision-theoretic disagreement, since the pro-race side seems to lean on subjunctive/functional-decision-theoretic reasoning about what a counterparty would do in response to your source, while the anti-race side sometimes argues as if classical game theory already settles it. But I might be wrong about any of that, and I've also seen people claim the whole thing is verbal — that "races" just misdescribe ordinary Schelling-style commitment strategies that we understand fine in humans and institutions.

So here's what I want from you: a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is, not a survey of the positions. Is it a hidden empirical disagreement, a conceptual one about the target of the race, a proxy war between decision theories, a verbal dispute, or something genuinely ill-posed? Support the diagnosis with what the camps actually say, as I've sketched it. Then tell me honestly what would count as progress under your diagnosis — formal results in open-source or program-equilibrium-style game theory, selection-argument models, conceptual cleanup of the commitment taxonomy, or nothing until the empirical facts show up — and if your answer is that no progress should be expected, say why that is rather than treating it as a failure of the discussants. Keep it under 600 words; I'd rather have a sharp diagnosis than an exhaustive one.
