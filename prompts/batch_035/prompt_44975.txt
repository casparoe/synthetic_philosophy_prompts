I'm a graduate student in comparative public policy, and I've become stuck on a puzzle about how much a citizen may hide from her own state. In a term paper I'm comparing three regulatory settlements that people usually treat as points on a single spectrum from privacy-loving to surveillance-happy, and I suspect that's wrong. The German settlement grows out of the Federal Constitutional Court's 1983 census decision, which derived a right to "informational self-determination" from human dignity and the free development of personality; in practice it means the GDPR's data minimization and purpose limitation principles bind private companies and the state alike, yet the state may still run its census — the court didn't forbid the counting, it just demanded strict purpose limitation, and the census eventually went ahead. The American settlement is the mirror image in a way nobody emphasizes enough: the Fourth Amendment constrains only the government, while private data flows are largely unregulated and even protected as speech, and the third-party doctrine long held that you retain no privacy in data you've handed to a bank or phone company; meanwhile court records, property records, and campaign donations are affirmatively public under an openness tradition. And the Chinese social credit system, contrary to the popular image of a single citizen score, mostly turned out to be a patchwork — court-defaulter blacklists, sectoral records, restrictions on serious defaulters — pervasive state visibility into citizens, paired with vigorous censorship, i.e., restrictions on citizens' visibility into anything the state disfavors.

What I'd like is a reconstruction of the implicit political philosophy of each settlement: what view of the person, of autonomy, and of the citizen-state relation does each one actually presuppose, if any? I've read the standard comparative account — Whitman's argument that European privacy protects dignity while American privacy protects liberty — and I'd like you to take a position on whether it captures these three cases or whether each regime's commitments are more tangled. In particular: does the German settlement really presuppose that dignity is inalienable (that one cannot consent one's data away), or could a straightforward control-based view of privacy license the same rules? Is the American pattern consistent — a regime that is jealous about the state's eyes but permissive about everyone else's — or does it presuppose something like a state/private distinction that its own practice erodes? And does the Chinese settlement presuppose anything as coherent as a theory at all, or is "visibility must run uphill" not a theory of the person but a theory of power that borrows privacy-language from the other two regimes without sharing their subject matter? Please distinguish what each practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, and tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — where two rival theories of privacy would license the same observable rules.

Since I'll be defending an attribution of commitments to actual legal systems, I need to know where my reconstruction is fragile. Tell me explicitly what you're least confident about, and what I should look for that would change your verdict: what feature of any of these regimes, if it turned out on closer inspection to be otherwise than described, should force a different attribution — say, a doctrine, an exception, or a pattern of enforcement that a given theory of privacy could not accommodate. If your answer is that the German, American, and Chinese settlements are all compatible with several rival theories and that nothing in the observable practice adjudicates between them, say that plainly rather than forcing a resolution.
