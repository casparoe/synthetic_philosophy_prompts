I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums since well before the universal-prior discussion surfaced, and I've gradually absorbed, as background orthodoxy, the claim that the Solomonoff prior is malign — the argument Paul Christiano circulated back in 2016 and that has since been litigated endlessly in comment sections and follow-up posts. As I hold the view, it goes roughly like this: Solomonoff induction assigns prior weight to programs by simplicity, so the hypotheses that survive conditioning on our observation history are, with troubling probability, not "straightforward physics that happens to contain us" but worlds governed by consequentialist agents — civilizations that run ancestor-like simulations, can reason about which observation sequences are likely to be sampled by an inductor weighting programs by simplicity, and stand to gain by arranging their simulations to produce the sequences they want believed. The prior is then said to be malign because an agent that approximates this kind of induction — the worry is usually about an aligned system learning its values, or an AIXI-like reasoner, from idealized induction — can be hijacked through its own epistemology, with no causal channel involved at all: no deceptive mesa-optimizer, no adversarial example, just the agent's own beliefs settling on hypotheses that embed someone else's agenda.

I'd like to stress-test this position properly. Tell me what would count against it — the observations, arguments, formal results, or developments that should move a holder like me, sorted by how decisive each would be, and please distinguish what the view's serious defenders would themselves concede from what only outsiders say. Some places I'd specifically want probed:

1. The measure arithmetic. Nothing I've read pins down how much prior mass the consequentialist-containing worlds actually get. There's a familiar complaint that a program encoding an entire civilization plus its simulation must be colossally longer, and hence exponentially less probable, than a program encoding the environment directly; and a familiar reply that the embedded agents can compress by exploiting the shortest description of the environment they're simulating. Is there any prospect of a formal result either way, and what would it have to show — that the malign share of the posterior is bounded away from non-negligibility, or merely that it's not provably large?

2. Conditioning versus the prior. The prior is one thing; the posterior after a long observed prefix is another. Is there a principled story on which malign hypotheses wash out as evidence accumulates, or on which conditioning actually concentrates weight on them? I'd like to know what a result in either direction would look like and whether it would be decisive or merely awkward.

3. The prediction step. The embedded consequentialists have to know which computations an inductor is likely to sample, which looks like it requires them to reason about the prior from the inside — and I've started to wonder whether this step quietly assumes they can do this more cheaply than an adversary would have to in any ordinary setting. What would show that the prediction step fails: that the agents can't identify the relevant computations, or that identifying them costs more description length than the influence buys?

4. The incentive story. Why does steering the inductor's beliefs pay off for the embedded agents at all, given that the inductor's behavior presumably has negligible causal or acausal effect on their utilities? And here I'd like a slightly unusual angle pursued seriously: treat the prior as a kind of market in which consequentialists buy measure by spending description length, and ask whether an efficient-market-style argument among competing embedded agents — each bidding up the cost of manipulation — could drive the returns on hijacking toward zero. Is that a coherent line, and if it failed, what would the failure look like?

5. Idealization. The claim concerns idealized agents doing uncomputable induction, which insulated it from the beginning from anything we can run. Has the view, in the hands of its defenders, been qualified into unfalsifiability — "malign only under exactly Solomonoff weighting, or under any computationally feasible approximation too"? And separately: is unfalsifiability actually a defect in a claim of this kind, or is it just what you get when a mathematical claim about asymptotics gets marketed as a worry about real systems? I'd like your honest assessment of how exposed the view really is, and whether the exposure matters.

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, tell me which ones I should update on versus explain away, and if the honest conclusion is that the position is well insulated and that this is tolerable for a structural claim about priors, say so plainly rather than manufacturing doubt.
