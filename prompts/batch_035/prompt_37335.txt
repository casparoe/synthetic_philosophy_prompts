I'm in a political philosophy course and we just finished the luck egalitarianism unit (Cohen, Dworkin, Arneson, the whole brute vs option luck thing), and a friend in my section has an argument I genuinely can't find my way out of. Here it is as he states it:

(1) Luck egalitarianism says justice requires neutralizing the effects of brute luck, but leaves people to bear the consequences of their own choices -- that distinction is supposed to be the whole point of the view. (2) But choices don't come out of nowhere: they flow from your preferences, values, ambitions, how risk-tolerant you are, basically your character. (3) You don't choose your character -- it's genes plus upbringing plus formative stuff that happened to you, i.e. brute luck. (4) If a choice is fully determined by factors you didn't choose, then you're no more responsible for it than for your eye color, so it's brute luck for purposes of the theory. (5) Therefore every "choice" is brute luck all the way down. (6) So luck egalitarians are commited to compensating for literally everything -- the gambler who blew his savings, the person who took the fun low-paying career, the person whose tastes got expensive -- and the view collapses into plain equality of outcomes, which no luck egalitarian actually wants. (7) So luck egalitarianism is either false or empty.

The conclusion feels too strong, but each step looks fine on its own? My TA hinted there's an equivocation somewhere but wouldn't say where. My instinct is to go after step 4, but whenever I try, my friend asks whether I'm saying some people are just born responsible and others born irresponsible, and that handing people wildly different fates on that basis is fine -- which sounds like it gives away the whole egalitarian motivation. The other route I can see is denying (3) or resisting the slide to (5), like maybe there's a difference between the ingredients of a choice being unchosen and the choice itself being unchosen, but I can't articulate it without it sounding like hand-waving.

So: which step should I reject, and what exactly does rejecting it cost? If the answer is that the luck egalitarian has to keep the spirit of (4) but weaken it somehow, I want to see what the weakened version looks like and why it isn't ad hoc. Also, we discussed the harshness objection in seminar (that Scanlon example about the guy who could've bought insurance and didn't) and I can't tell whether that's a totally seperate problem for luck egalitarianism or whether it's a symptom that something in the argument above already went wrong. Sorry for the wall of text, any help appreciated.
