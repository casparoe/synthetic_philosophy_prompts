In a statistical mechanics seminar this week, a classmate shut down my question with the slogan that entropy is disorder. I had asked about a supersaturated solution in which salt crystals spontaneously appear: the ions seem to organize themselves, going from scattered particles to an ordered lattice, while the second law says entropy only increases. He gestured at the lattice and said that crystals are ordered and therefore low-entropy, so — I never quite caught what followed. I suspect the slogan is doing several distinct jobs at once, and I want a disambiguation rather than a lecture on entropy in general.

Please lay out as a menu the different precise claims the sentence could be expressing, and for each say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or straightforwardly false. I can see at least these candidates, but add, merge, or correct them as needed: (a) a pedagogical heuristic that holds only for the textbook case of a gas spreading through a container; (b) a stipulation, in which "disorder" is just shorthand for multiplicity — the logarithm of the number of accessible microstates — with no claim that this tracks any everyday notion of messiness; (c) a substantive identity claim, that thermodynamic entropy and intuitive disorder co-vary, so that tidy/untidy and low-entropy/high-entropy always move together; (d) an epistemic reading on which entropy measures an observer's ignorance or the information required to specify a state; and (e) any further reading you think is actually in circulation.

For each reading, I especially want to know whether it answers my crystal question. My tentative sense is that the right answer turns on the difference between the entropy of the system and the entropy of the system plus its environment: the lattice is locally ordered, but the released heat of crystallization more than pays for it, so total entropy rises. If so, say so plainly — and note, if it is so, that the slogan actively obscures the very distinction that resolves the puzzle. Finish by telling me which single reading my classmate would have needed for his reply to work, and whether, on that reading, his reply was actually correct.
