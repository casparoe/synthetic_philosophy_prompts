so, context: i'm a novelist, mid-list, working on a book where one character has been diagnosed with schizophrenia, and i keep getting pulled in two directions about how to write her. at a dinner party a psychiatrist friend told me the right frame is: write her exactly as you'd write a character with diabetes. chronic illness of an organ, managed with medication, and it's no more a comment on her soul or her character than a diabetes diagnosis would be. she says stigma comes from treating it as different-in-kind, and the diabetes picture is how you fix that. this analogy gets used a LOT in anti-stigma writing, the "depression is like diabetes" line, chronic disease of an organ like any other.

but i'd also been reading szasz, the myth of mental illness guy, and he would say the diabetes picture is precisely the thing to be suspicious of. as i understand him his claim is that illness proper requires a bodily lesion or malfunction you can point to, and that calling behavior and suffering "illness" without that is a metaphor that got literalized -- he somewhere compares belief in mental illness to belief in witchcraft, a whole institutional apparatus built on a category error. on his view my friend's analogy doesn't extend a concept, it smuggles one in.

i'm not asking you to referee the dinner party. i want an audit of the analogy itself, since it's basically the hinge my book turns on. give me:

1. the correspondence table. what plays the role of the organ, the insulin deficiency, the medication, the doctor, the diagnosis, the course of the disease? fill it in as charitably as possible for the psychiatry side, including where the cell fills in only loosely.

2. where it breaks. i know every analogy breaks somewhere -- what i need is which breaks matter for the use the analogy is being put to here, which is: how should i write this person, sick or strange? e.g. does the fact that the "disease" is defined by behavior and reported experience rather than a measurable physiological marker matter for the writing question, or is that only a problem for some other question i don't care about? szasz's own counter-picture (mental illness as the modern witchcraft) -- does it survive the same audit, or is it the identical trick pointed the other direction, taking the loosest part of its source (witchcraft as socially-constructed persecution) and importing it where it does no work? he obviously wants "moral problem in living" to follow, and i can't tell if that follows any better than "organic disease" does.

3. the verdict. does my friend's practical conclusion -- write her as sick the way you'd write a diabetic, not as strange, not as a message -- survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? and if the diabetes frame dies, what's left? is there a better analogy for a novelist in my position, or should i just accept that i'm writing one person and not a case?

sorry this got long. it's just that how i answer this determines the whole last third of the book, and everyone i ask in person has a dog in the fight.
