I'm a graduate student taking a course on mechanistic interpretability, and my safety research group has spent this whole term stuck on a foundational question that keeps derailing our project on anomaly detection: when we say that a model is using the same mechanism on a new input as it used on the inputs we validated, what are we even asking for? The group has hardened into two camps. The structural camp says sameness of mechanism is a fact about the computational graph: an input runs through the same mechanism iff the same circuit — the same set of weights, or some permutation or re-parameterization of them — carries the information that determines the output. They are fond of the observation that the weights of two networks trained separately can be related by a permutation of units, which they take to show that structure rather than coordinates is the natural object. The functional camp says what matters is the counterfactual profile: intervene on the relevant input feature and check whether the intermediate quantities and the output respond as they did on the validation distribution; two mechanisms are the same when they are interchangeable in the causal structure, even if implemented by different weights, or conceivably different architectures. Both camps agree about the textbook cases — a network trained on modular arithmetic that turns out to use Fourier components is using a different mechanism than one that memorizes a lookup table, and everyone is comfortable saying so — but they cannot agree on the cases our project actually turns on.

Those cases are where the arguments stall. Consider: (a) the same circuit fires on a new input, but the surrounding context routes its output nowhere — ablating it costs nothing on this input though ablating it cost a lot on the validation set. Did the mechanism stay the same while the model stopped using it, or does 'using a mechanism' already smuggle in the functional notion? (b) A different circuit produces the same counterfactual profile the original had under validation — the model, after fine-tuning, achieves the same competence by what looks like a different route. The functional camp wants to call that the same mechanism (same role), the structural camp refuses (different graph), and neither side budges. (c) The same circuit fires with the same causal profile, but the input distribution has shifted so far that the feature it tracks no longer means what it meant — same mechanism, or different? Every meeting we relitigate these, and I've noticed something suspicious: each side seems to win on the cases it got to define first.

My working hypothesis is that the two camps are answering different questions under one label — the structural camp is offering identity conditions for a model's internals, something like a metaphysics of mechanisms, while the functional camp is really answering an epistemic question about what an auditor needs to know to generalize a safety claim from a validation set to deployment, and for that purpose interchangeability may be all that matters and graph identity may be more than is needed. I suspect that if we saw this clearly, half of our arguments would dissolve, but the important half — the cases where the two notions genuinely come apart, like (a) and (c) — would not, because there something real is at stake for what our detector should flag. Please diagnose this disagreement: is it verbal, are the sides answering different questions, or is there a substantive hidden question — perhaps about how to individuate mechanisms across distribution shift — doing the work? Engage the actual positions and the three cases rather than surveying the literature, and tell me honestly what would count as progress here: a sharper definition, a benchmark that forces the cases apart, or an admission that mechanism-sameness is a family of resemblance notions with no single criterion, and what our group should do in the meantime.
