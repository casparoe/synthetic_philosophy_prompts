For a seminar on the philosophy of emotion and affect, I need to work out what a theory of pain's badness has to accomplish before I can usefully compare the leading candidates. Please write the desiderata list. It should cover, among other things: pain asymbolia, where patients report feeling pain of ordinary intensity yet are unbothered by it; the pain-science practice of measuring pain intensity and unpleasantness on separate scales, and the dissociations that practice presupposes; chronic pain without any corresponding tissue damage; masochistic contexts in which nociception seems welcomed rather than shunned; and cases like heartbreak or grief that share pain's felt badness while lacking its bodily-sensory phenomenology. It should also cover the distinctions a theory must respect — sensory-discriminative versus affective components of pain; pain being bad versus pain merely causing things that are bad; bodily damage being bad versus the experience of damage being bad — and any methodological constraints, e.g. that the theory must explain rather than stipulate the connection between pain's phenomenal character and its badness. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it and say what a theory that fails it gets wrong. Then identify which requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — I take pain asymbolia together with the intuition that pain's badness is intrinsic to the experience to be a pinch point, but I'd like your view. Finally, briefly score three contenders against the list: an evaluativist view on which pain is bad by representing bodily damage or disorder, an imperativist view on which pain commands protective action rather than representing anything, and a view on which badness is conferred by a general valence mechanism that pain merely feeds into. A table is fine, but the requirements and their motivations are the part I most want.
