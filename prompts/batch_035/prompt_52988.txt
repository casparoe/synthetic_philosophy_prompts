I'm a software engineer by training, and I've been trying to get my head around some of the arguments used in AI safety discussions. There's one I keep running into called the strategy-stealing assumption, and I want to check whether I've understood it correctly before building any further reasoning on top of it.

Here is my current understanding: the assumption says that if a misaligned AI has some strategy available for gaining power over humans, then that same strategy is in principle available to humans too, since the underlying capabilities come from the technology rather than being exclusive to the AI. If that's right, it seems to follow that an unaligned superintelligence can never have a structural edge over us — anything it can do, we could do just as well by adopting its playbook.

If that's correct, then I'm having trouble seeing why alignment researchers still describe unaligned superintelligence as an existential risk rather than as a logistics problem. Wouldn't it be enough to build a careful monitoring setup: observe everything the AI attempts, identify whichever strategies actually work, and then execute the same strategies ourselves with a human approving each step? In software terms, it sounds like we could treat the AI as a staging environment — let it discover what succeeds, and mirror the winning commits into production under human control. The AI's speed advantage would be neutralized the moment we deploy the same strategy, since both sides would then be running equivalent plays.

So my question is: given the strategy-stealing assumption, why isn't "copy whatever the AI does, but with a human in the loop" considered a sufficient response to misalignment? And where exactly, if anywhere, does my reading of the assumption go wrong? I suspect I'm missing something, because the argument feels almost too clean, but I can't locate the gap on my own.
