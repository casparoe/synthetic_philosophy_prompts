I've been rereading the Brothers Karamazov chapter where Ivan tells Alyosha his "Grand Inquisitor" poem, and I can't get it out of my head, so I want to do something with it. Could you write me a short story that transposes the Inquisitor's logic onto a setting Dostoevsky couldn't have imagined, instead of just retelling his scene in sixteenth-century Seville?

Here's the setup I have in mind. In a near-future country recovering from decades of famine and civil war, a government program called something like the Provision (your choice) has ended hunger by doling out exactly what each person needs, when they need it — food, housing, jobs matched to temperament, even marriages the matching system quietly arranges because they statistically last. Nobody starves, nobody riots, and nearly everyone reports being content. The program's aging chief architect, a sincere and exhausted man who genuinely loves the people he administers, is our Inquisitor. One day a stranger arrives who somehow breaks the system's predictions: people around him start refusing their rations, leaving assigned partners, asking for work nobody assigned them. The architect has him quietly detained, and in the detention cell delivers his own version of the Inquisitor's speech — bread, miracle, and authority transposed as guaranteed needs, algorithmic certainty, and freedom from choice — while the stranger, like Christ in the original, barely speaks at all. I'd like the story to end the way the legend-within-the-novel ends, with a gesture instead of a rebuttal, but you can decide what gesture lands here.

A few requests. Please don't make either man a cartoon — the architect has to be genuinely sympathetic and his case genuinely strong, not a strawman for freedom-speeches. Work in the three temptations from the wilderness somehow, but don't label them with a neon sign; let a reader catch it. And after the story, add a short note on the interpretive choices you made: for instance, whether you read the Inquisitor as Dostoevsky's mouthpiece, as Ivan's creation that backfires on Ivan, or as something Dostoevsky deliberately leaves unresolved, and where your story leans on a contested reading rather than the standard one — I gather scholars genuinely disagree about how much the Inquisitor speaks for Dostoevsky himself, so please flag which of your choices are like that rather than presenting one reading as settled. Write in American English, and don't worry about keeping my plot sketch if something better occurs to you, as long as the core confrontation between guaranteed contentment and unmanageable freedom survives.
