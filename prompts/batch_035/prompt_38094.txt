Please describe the most important implications of the existence of free-floating rationales — facts that explain a system's features or behavior by appeal to purposes or reasons that no thinking agent ever represented, intended, or deliberated about — for the practice of interpreting and amending inherited institutional rules. As a running case, consider a small nonprofit whose bylaws, drafted decades ago by founders no longer living, contain provisions whose original purpose nobody now remembers but which happen to produce good effects today: for instance, a staggered board structure that, whatever it was originally meant to achieve, now turns out to stabilize leadership during periods of funding uncertainty. In your answer, please address (1) whether the provisions' current beneficial effects can count as genuine reasons for retaining them, given that no one intended or foresaw those effects; (2) how this bears on the distinction between justifying a rule and merely explaining its persistence; and (3) whether a board member who comes to realize that the rationale for a provision is, in this sense, free-floating is thereby required to weigh it differently from a rationale that is known to have been deliberately designed — for example, whether the board must treat the accidental benefit as weaker evidence, or as no reason at all, when deciding whether to preserve or rewrite the provision.
