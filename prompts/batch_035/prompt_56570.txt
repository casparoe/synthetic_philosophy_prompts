The debate over procreative beneficence seems to me to have stalled in a revealing way. On one side, defenders of the view that prospective parents have (at least prima facie) a moral reason to select, among embryos they might implant, the one expected to have the best life -- this is typically framed as an application of liberal principles to new reproductive technology, sharply distinguished from state-run, coercion-backed eugenics. On the other side, critics, especially from disability scholarship and from thinkers worried about the social meaning of selection, argue that the distinction between liberal and old-fashioned eugenics is thinner than advertised: that judgments about which traits count as conditions worth avoiding are socially saturated, that screening practices express a devaluation of existing disabled people regardless of anyone's intent, and that a practice can be formally voluntary and still converge on a coercive social norm about what kind of people should exist. Each side grants the other's surface claims -- yes, selection differs from coercion; yes, disability classifications embed values -- and then nothing moves. Is this a substantive moral disagreement, a verbal one about the word "eugenics," or a case where the sides are answering different questions (one about the obligations of individual parents in a fixed social context, one about what a practice does when generalized)? Where exactly does the load-bearing disagreement lie, and does the empirical claim that expanding selection actually worsens the situation of disabled people matter as much as both sides seem to assume? Diagnose the structure of the dispute, drawing on what the participants actually argue rather than on caricatures, and tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress -- a conceptual distinction, evidence, a reframing -- or whether the impasse reflects a genuine bedrock difference in starting commitments that no argument should be expected to resolve. And state plainly what would change your own diagnosis: which consideration, if it were established or refuted, would lead you to a different account of what the disagreement is about, and where your reading is most likely to go wrong.
