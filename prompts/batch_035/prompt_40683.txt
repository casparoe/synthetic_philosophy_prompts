I'm a junior in high school, and in our after-school philosophy club we recently debated whether it is rational to fear death. At least four separate people tried to end the argument with the same one-liner, which I now understand traces back to Epicurus: 'death is nothing to us.' The way it was deployed went something like this: the dead no longer exist; nothing bad can happen to someone who doesn't exist; therefore death can't be bad for the person who died, and so fearing it (or feeling grief on someone's behalf) is a kind of confusion. I had the strong sense that a great deal of philosophy was being compressed into one short sentence, but I couldn't articulate on the spot which parts were doing the work. What exactly is that slogan claiming? It seems to slide between several distinct theses: that death is not an experience and so cannot be felt as bad; that death itself is bad for no one, as opposed to the process of dying, which is a separate question; that a person cannot be deprived of goods by their own nonexistence, since there is no subject left to suffer the deprivation; and perhaps something stronger still, a general principle that no event can be bad for a subject unless that subject exists to be harmed. I would like you to set these candidate readings out as a list I can bring back to the club, and for each one tell me whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or actually false, and whether it supports the conclusion my clubmates wanted to draw from it.

There was one exchange I especially want you to address, because it seemed to expose a crack in the argument. A member objected that even if death is painless, it still deprives you of every good experience you would otherwise have had — losing the decades you might have lived looks bad for you, even if there is no 'you' around afterwards to feel the loss. The reply was that, by the same logic, the endless stretch of nonexistence before our birth should have been bad for us too, and nobody apparently finds that alarming; she mentioned that this point goes back to Lucretius. No one had a ready answer to where that leaves the slogan. So in addition to the list of readings, please say which of them survive the deprivation objection and which don't, whether the pre-birth comparison genuinely undermines the deprivation view or rests on some asymmetry that needs to be argued for, and finally which reading the people who repeat the slogan most plausibly need if their argument is to go through. I would rather have a careful menu of interpretations with honest verdicts than a single authoritative gloss.
