I'm preparing a term-paper proposal (roughly 6,000 words, one semester) on the aggregation problem in Scanlon's contractualism, and I want to build it around a suspicion I haven't yet managed to state cleanly. My half-formed idea is this: the standard debate assumes there is a single question — do the numbers count? — but I suspect it in fact runs together two distinguishable questions. The first is a within-person question: does the fact that many people would each suffer a given burden raise the strength of any one individual's complaint, as compared with a situation in which only that one person would suffer it? The second is a cross-person question: can a larger number of individually weaker complaints, taken together, outweigh a smaller number of individually stronger complaints, or do comparisons of strength simply trump headcounts? It seems to me that the familiar test cases are constructed so that an intuition about one dimension is routinely taken to settle the other: in the transplant-style case, where harvesting one person's organs would save several dying patients, most people's verdict is meant to show that headcounts can't add up across persons; in the case where a minor inconvenience to one person is set against an equally minor inconvenience to each of a great many — the sort of case involving distributing a good like a television broadcast or a sports broadcast to a tiny audience versus a vast one — the intuition seems instead to concern how the availability of alternatives changes what any single person can reasonably reject. If those are genuinely different mechanisms, then some of the standard exchanges between contractualists and their critics may be talking past each other.

Please turn this into a proposal. It should include: a precise statement of the question, formulated so that it has a determinate answer rather than inviting a survey; an explanation of why the main existing positions leave it open — I have in mind at least the view that what matters is the strength of the strongest individual claim, the appeal to the relevance of what could have been done instead, and the skeptic's line that interpersonal comparisons of complaint strength are too indeterminate to carry the weight the theory puts on them — but you should treat these as positions to be characterized in the text rather than as positions to be sourced; a method section saying what I would actually do, which I expect to involve designing pairs of cases that vary one of the two dimensions while holding the other fixed, and I'd like you to say what such pairs would have to look like if they are to be probative rather than merely clever; a statement of the possible conclusions and what would decide among them; an honest account of what would count as the project failing, including the possibility that my two questions cannot in the end be pulled apart; and a realistic assessment of scope for one semester. Do not include a bibliography or citations by name — I will fill those in myself — and do not include verbatim quotation from any text.
