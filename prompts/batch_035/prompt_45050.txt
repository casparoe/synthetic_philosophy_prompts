I direct the clinical ethics consultation service at a large academic medical center, and a substantial share of my consults — several a month, arriving with hours to spare, families in crisis, clinicians wanting an answer rather than a seminar — turn on the same underlying question: what makes a person dead, and who gets to say so. Our institution follows the whole-brain standard under the model statute dating from the early 1980s, but my clinicians keep colliding with its philosophical fine print, and I need something more usable than "call ethics."

The recurring collisions are these. First, families — often, though not always, on Orthodox Jewish or certain conservative Christian grounds — who hold that death is a cardiopulmonary event and cannot accept that a warm, pink, perfusing body with a beating heart is a dead person. Second, donation-after-circulatory-death cases, where death is declared minutes after the heart stops, which quietly trades on the gap between irreversibility (the heart cannot restart) and permanence (it will not restart because no one will attempt to restart it). Third, the kind of case the chronic-brain-death literature presses: patients maintained for months, and in a handful of documented cases longer, gestating a fetus, healing wounds, mounting fevers in response to infection — which makes it awkward to tell a bedside nurse that this is merely a corpse with artificially circulating blood.

That last point deserves emphasis. The standard rationale for equating whole-brain death with death is that the brain integrates the body into a single organism, so that the destruction of the entire brain marks the loss of the organism as a whole. The well-known critique of this rationale, associated with the neurologist D. Alan Shewmon, argues that the somatic integration visible in these long-maintained patients persists without the brain, through non-neural routes; you need not accept his conclusion to notice that our staff notice the tension.

Please write me a decision procedure my service can run repeatedly under real constraints. Specifically:

- The questions to ask, in order, and what counts as a sufficient answer to each — enough to distinguish, at the bedside, a factual dispute (is the standard met? is the test valid?) from a conceptual one (is the standard the right one?) from a purely values question (what should be done given the determination?);
- Where the procedure yields a determinate stop and hands the decision to a person, and it must be honest about which of its steps are judgment dressed up as procedure;
- What it cannot settle and should not pretend to settle.

Then run it on three consults: (a) a family citing religious law rejects the brain-death determination for their thirty-year-old son and demands that ventilation continue indefinitely; (b) a patient declared brain dead at twenty-two weeks' gestation, whose partner and parents all want somatic support continued toward fetal viability — a request the OB service is willing to honor, and which several of our ICU nurses find morally incoherent if she is already dead; (c) a surrogate authorizing donation after circulatory death who asks, in effect, how long we wait before calling it, and whether being sure enough means the heart could not restart or merely that no one will try.

Include at least one case where the procedure delivers an answer I will not like, and say so plainly. And I do not need a reading list or case law survey — I need something that works at the bedside, reasoned from what is above.
