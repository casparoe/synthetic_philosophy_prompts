I'm a graduate teaching assistant for a philosophy of biology course, and I'm preparing a diagnostic handout for our section on biological function. The running theme of the unit is the split between etiological (history-based) and causal-role (performance-based) accounts of what a trait's function is, and I want the handout to sort students by which combination of commitments they actually hold, not by which label they find most attractive. Please write a decision tree that does the sorting. Start from a single entrance question and branch only on questions a student could answer without knowing any of the literature's terminology — every branch node should be a concrete case with two or three plain-language options phrased about the case itself (for example: for a cave fish's vestigial eyes, the student chooses between the eyes still having the job of seeing even though there is nothing to see, versus no light, no seeing, and therefore no job).

The cases I want used as branch points, with room for one or two of your own if a split needs them: rudimentary eyes in cave-dwelling fish that live in total darkness and never see; a mutation fixed by drift in a population, which turns out to aid digestion of a food source the population has just switched to, before any selection has acted on it; the panda's enlarged wrist bone that it uses to strip bamboo; the small bones of the reptilian jaw that became our middle-ear ossicles; a dog breed's skull shape produced by artificial selection for a breeder's aesthetic preference; and a hormone-secreting tumor that ends up regulating something in the body. The branching questions should make the underlying distinctions surface on their own: does a trait's having a function depend on its history at all, and if so on whose history — selection by nature, selection by breeders, or mere ancestry? Can a trait have a function it never performs, or one it has never yet performed? Can something have a function while being bad at it, and if not, where on the tree do attributions of malfunction become impossible?

Build the tree from the logical space of answers first, then locate the named positions within it, giving each leaf the position's name where the literature provides one (selected-effect etiology, modern-history accounts, Cummins-style causal-role, propensity or dispositional accounts, hybrid or two-level views) and marking it "unnamed" where it doesn't, along with one sentence on that view's most awkward commitment. Note any cells that remain unoccupied and say whether you think nobody holds them because the view is incoherent, unappealing, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate — I suspect causal-role and propensity accounts collapse together whenever the trait is currently performing well, and I'd like to know whether you agree. End the handout with a single machine-readable line beginning "Summary:" that states, in one sentence, which single case ended up doing the most sorting work and why.
