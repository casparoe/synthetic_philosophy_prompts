I'm a junior analyst at a foreign-policy think tank, and after a recent promotion I'm the one drafting the first-pass memos on whatever crisis is in the news that week. The way my shop argues is almost entirly historical analogy: every memo leans on some "lesson of history." Last month a senior colleague built a whole section of a memo around the Thucydides Trap (the Graham Allison thing — rising power threatens ruling power, war in 12 of 16 historical cases), and a reviewer shredded it in the margins. Then I personally got burned when I wrote a paragraph calling a sanctions standoff "Munich all over again" and the historian on staff replied with three bullet points about how 1938 actually worked, none of them flattering to me. So I've been asked (read: told) to come up with a house procedure for how we handle analogies — and honestly I'd want one anyway, because I can no longer tell when these comparisons are doing real analytical work and when they're just rhetoric with a bibliography attached.

Here's my current thinking, which you should feel free to correct: (1) analogies mislead when they match surface features — strongman leader, territorial demand, rising power — rather than causal structure, like what the actors can actually see, what they have to lose, what their domestic politics let them do; (2) analogies mislead because they're chosen by the advocate, not by the case — people reach for whichever famous episode supports the policy they already wanted, so "Munich" shows up to justify acting and "Vietnam" shows up to justify not acting, and neither is really an inference; (3) the more famous an analogy is, the less trustworthy it is, because fame tracks drama and how often it's been invoked, not typicality. That last one I'm least sure of. I've been skimming an old book by two political scientists, Neustadt and May (Thinking in Time, 1986), but I can't tell how much of their advice survives contact with a fast-moving news cycle, so don't feel bound by it.

What I want is a decision procedure my team can actually run on a memo draft in about half an hour before it goes up to the director. Steps in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure ends with "cut the analogy" versus "keep it, but reframe it as one data point among several," and — crucially — the places where it has to hand off to a human because the question is one of judgment, not facts. Then run it on three cases from our recent drafts: (a) a director's claim that a trade-and-territorial dispute with an authoritarian government is Munich 1938 and any concession just invites the next demand; (b) a colleague dismissing a proposed humanitarian intervention as "another Vietnam" — I suspect that comparison is doing less work than it looks like it's doing, and I want the procedure to tell me honestly; and (c) the Thucydides Trap framing applied to US–China, where I'd like to know whether the famous 12-of-16 number should scare us or whether it's mostly an artifact of how the cases were picked. At least one of these should come out the way I don't want it to — I'd rather find that out now than in a room with the director. And please be blunt about what the procedure can't do, including whether it can ever actually stop a director who outranks its conclusion.
