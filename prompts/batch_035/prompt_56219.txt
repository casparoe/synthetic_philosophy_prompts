I am preparing a fifty-minute section of an undergraduate philosophy of science course on the underdetermination of theory by evidence, and I am asking not for the explanation itself but for a plan of it. The students are second- and third-year philosophy majors who have just completed a unit on Duhem-Quine holism, and my experience is that they arrive primed to conflate the two theses.

Please structure the plan around the following. First, the order of ideas: which component must be in place first for the thesis to register as a claim about the structure of evidential support rather than as a sigh of epistemic resignation, and why that dependency runs the way it does. The plan should draw a hard line between underdetermination and the auxiliary-hypothesis holism they have just learned; I want the students to leave able to say what the difference is, not merely to have been told it. Second, the carrying example: I would like it built on something in the spirit of Reichenbach's distinction between differential and universal forces, or a structurally equivalent device of your devising — two rivals that agree on every possible observation and yet disagree about something the students can state precisely. It must not be one of the stock cases: no Newtonian world supplemented by undetectable forces, no polynomial curve-fitting, no brain in a vat. Say what the example must have and what it must lack, and why it is the one element that carries the whole section. Third, the misconception map: I anticipate at least (a) the slide from no evidence currently distinguishes them to no possible evidence could — the contrast some of the literature marks as transient versus permanent underdetermination — (b) the collapse of the thesis into blanket skepticism, and (c) the thought that a simplicity tie-breaker dissolves the problem. Place each misconception at the point in the sequence where it is best headed off, rather than waiting for it to surface. Fourth, a closing question I could ask that would reveal whether the students have understood the thesis rather than merely found it depressing. Finally, name what I should openly decline to cover at this level — I am inclined to say the status of parsimony, though you may disagree — and indicate how I should say so without inviting the students to file the whole topic under unanswerable.
