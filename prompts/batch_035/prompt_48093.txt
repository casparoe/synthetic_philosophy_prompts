Every year I teach the philosophy of math seminar, and every year the axiom of choice unit ends with me sort of shrugging at my students, which is honestly not what they're paying tuition for. Two of my colleagues have been in a standing argument about AC for a decade now. One of them thinks it's basically self-evident once you phrase it as "for any collection of nonempty sets, you can pick one element from each" — reasonable! — and puts his credence that AC is true at about 0.8. The other one cannot get past Banach-Tarski, nonmeasurable sets, and the well-ordering of the reals, and puts it at 0.15, and I strongly suspect what's really going on is that she finds those results ugly, though she'd deny that to my face. So here's what I want from you: your actual credence that the axiom of choice is true. Not consistent, not independent of ZF — Gödel and Cohen settled all that, and I always have to look up which of them did which direction, feel free to enjoy that about me — but true, whatever that's supposed to mean for a set-theoretic axiom. And I want the number decomposed, because the bare figure is useless to me. Off the top of my head the terms would include: the probability that set-theoretic claims have determinate truth values at all, i.e. some stripe of platonism; given that, the probability that the iterative conception of the set-theoretic universe is the right conception versus some rival like limitation-of-size or a more conventionalist picture where axioms are stipulations; given the iterative conception, the probability that choice comes out true on it; and whatever other terms you think belong in there (I can imagine someone adding a term for whether "true" for an axiom is even the same question as "true" for a Goldbach-style claim — include it if you beleive that). Give me a rough probability and one line of justification for each, then show me how they combine into your final number. Then two more things: tell me where you think my two colleagues' disagreement actually lives — my current theory is that it's not about mathematics at all but about how much weird consequences are allowed to count as evidence against an axiom, i.e. whether "this implies a decomposition of the sphere into finitely many pieces and two copies of the sphere" is a cost or just a curiosity — and tell me which term of your own decomposition you're least confident in and what would actually move it. And if your real view is that it's all stipulation and truth doesn't apply here, fine, I've heard that at enough dinner parties, but then assign a probability to THAT view being right and run the decomposition through it anyway.
