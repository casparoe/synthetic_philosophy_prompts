I'm taking a course on rational choice theory, and we've spent two weeks on whether rationality attaches to individual acts or to plans. Rather than argue about theories in the abstract, I've tried to fix my verdicts on concrete cases, and now I'd like to know what principle, if any, captures them — or which verdict I should give up. Here they are.

1. Parfit's hitchhiker, with a near-perfect predictor: the driver will pick him up only if she predicts he will pay, and once he's in the car, paying gets him nothing further. I cannot bring myself to call the agent who pays irrational. Paying seems like the rational thing to have made sure he'd do.

2. The same setup, except the driver is a Good Samaritan who picked him up out of kindness, without thinking about whether he'd pay. Here paying seems admirable but not rationally required; if anything it's mildly irrational.

3. Kavka's toxin: you get a million dollars tonight if by midnight you intend to drink a toxin tomorrow that will make you ill but not harm you permanently. My verdict: it is rational to form the intention, but when tomorrow comes, drinking the toxin is irrational. If someone could collect the million while merely fooling the billionaire about his intentions, that would be the best and fully rational outcome.

4. Ulysses having himself bound to the mast: obviously rational to order the binding. And once bound, his straining against the ropes isn't an exercise of rationality, but it also isn't a failure of it.

5. A gym-goer who signs a contract forfeiting $5,000 to a friend unless he exercises: rational. A gambler who pays a hypnotist to make him feel nausea at the sight of a casino: I also want to say rational, even though what he's buying is, in effect, a permanent incapacity for rational choice at the table.

6. A union leader with a genuinely hair-trigger temper wins concessions a calm bargainer couldn't. He is irrational but lucky. But a leader who deliberately stops practicing self-control in order to acquire that temper, precisely to be a stronger bargainer — that, I keep flip-flopping on. Acquiring the disposition looks rational; the resulting agent looks defective; and I can't tell which judgment should win.

The tension I've noticed is between 1 and 3. To defend my verdict in the hitchhiker case, I seem pushed toward evaluating whole plans or dispositions from the standpoint of the earlier choice point. But that kind of account appears to vindicate the toxin-drinker as rational too, which I deny. Conversely, act-by-act evaluation keeps 3 and cleanly condemns the toxin-drinker, but it also condemns the hitchhiker who pays, and it makes my endorsement of the hypnotist's client in 5 hard to sustain.

So: is there a single principle that delivers all six verdicts, or close? If not, which should I abandon, and at what cost? Please state the principle precisely, show which verdict motivates each clause, and tell me what it says about two cases I haven't listed: the hitchhiker where the predictor is only 80% accurate, and a state that staked its deterrence on a promise of massive retaliation which no longer serves any purpose once deterrence has failed and it has been attacked anyway. I'd also like an honest accounting of which of my verdicts your favored principle simply cannot save.
