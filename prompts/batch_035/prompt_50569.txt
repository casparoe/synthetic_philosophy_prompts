So I'm a retired geochemist, 38 years of isotope dating, recently gone soft in the head for philosophy books and meditation retreats. On the last retreat a guy gave me an argument for something he called tranquilism, and I can't find where it breaks -- and it had better break, because the endpoint is monstrous. Here it is as best I can reconstruct:

(1) The only thing that ultimately matters about a mental life is the presence or absence of suffering (craving, restlessness, distress); everything else is decoration.
(2) The "good feeling" of getting what you wanted is nothing over and above the temporary switch-off of the craving; there is no positive ingredient, just a negative being removed.
(3) So a mind in permanent, complete tranquility has everything that matters.
(4) A mindless universe also contains no suffering, and nobody in it is deprived of tranquility or misses anything.
(5) If nothing of what-matters is lost either way, then a universe of perfectly tranquil minds, a universe of happy flourishing minds, and a dead universe are all equally good.
(6) So painless extinction of all sentient life would not be a loss, and is arguably the ideal.

The retreat guy swears he doesn't accept (6) and says tranquilism is a claim about your own mind, not about worlds. But I've stared at this for a week and I can't see which premise he can reject without giving up the whole view. My hunch is that something slides between (3) and (5) -- it feels like the argument quietly changes what "what matters" is a property OF, maybe like confusing an intensive property with an extensive one, if that analogy even makes sense. But I'd honestly rather deny (2) than accept (6), and I want to know what that costs me.

Give me your diagnosis as a table with one row per premise (1 through 6), with columns: (a) verdict -- keep, reject, or "this is where the flaw is"; (b) one or two sentences on why; (c) what rejecting it costs, i.e. which other premise or principle you have to weaken to let it go. After the table, a short plain-prose paragraph on this: suppose someone keeps everything up to (5) but digs in and refuses (6) only, on the grounds that preventing suffering counts but creating happiness doesn't have to. Is that just an ad hoc patch, or is it actually a coherent position? Weirdly, that asymmetry seems almost right to me -- having a kid you expect to be miserable seems clearly wrong, having a kid you expect to be happy seems fine but optional -- but maybe I'm being talked into a monster by my own intuitions now.
