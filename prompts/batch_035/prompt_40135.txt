In a course I help teach, we keep circling the old debate about whether the adoption of agriculture made human lives worse — the one running from Sahlins's "original affluent society" through Diamond's essay calling farming our worst mistake — and I've noticed it never goes anywhere. One side points to the paleopathology: declining stature, enamel hypoplasias, heavier infectious-disease markers in early farming skeletons, plus the claim that foragers worked less and enjoyed more varied diets. The other side answers that the comparison is rigged — that we're comparing an idealized Pleistocene forager with the least healthy farmers archaeology happens to preserve — that forager life expectancy was grim, that sedentism and storage predate farming (the Natufians come up every time), and that if agriculture were really so bad it is hard to explain why any group ever stuck with it. Everyone in the room seems to accept the same archaeological record, and yet nobody has budged in years. My working hypothesis is that this is not really an empirical dispute at all, but a disguised normative one — a proxy war over whether civilization was worth it — with the two sides tacitly answering different questions, namely "were the first farmers worse off than the foragers they replaced?" and "was the switch bad for the species or for humanity's descendants?" Please commit to a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is and defend it, and say what, given that diagnosis, would genuinely count as progress. Give your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
