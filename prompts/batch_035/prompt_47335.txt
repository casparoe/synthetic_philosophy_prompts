In a reading group I help run, a debate about whether human preferences can be inferred from behavior has been going in circles for two years without anyone conceding anything, and I would like help diagnosing what kind of disagreement it actually is.

On one side sit the people from machine learning. They point to inverse reinforcement learning and its descendants: given enough behavioral trajectories, they say, we can recover something like the reward function a person is optimizing, and more recent work on preference learning and assistance games shows how to keep uncertainty about the human's objectives and update on their choices instead of pretending to a point estimate. On the other side sit people trained in philosophy and behavioral economics. Their objections have become familiar: behavior underdetermines preference, since many different reward functions are compatible with the same actions; people are not consistent optimizers, so the inference inherits their noise and their biases; and, in the spirit of Amartya Sen's old complaint against revealed-preference theory, much of what humans do expresses habit, commitment, or adaptation rather than preference, so treating every choice as evidence of what a person values builds a mistake into the method.

What frustrates me is that neither side actually disputes any of this. The learning side concedes the indeterminacy as a matter of mathematics — they will tell you themselves that adding a potential-based shaping term to a reward function leaves behavior unchanged, so rewards are identifiable only up to such transformations — and they concede that humans are noisy and biased. Their reply is that none of this threatens the engineering goal: a model that imitates a person well, or that plans well under uncertainty about the person's reward, is succeeding at what it was built for. The skeptics concede that the engineering works fine for teaching a system to drive or to summarize; their reply is that the interesting question was never how to clone or assist behavior, but whether the recovered construct deserves to be called the person's preferences at all, and that answering it requires premises about the person — that she is roughly optimizing, that her circumstances are not so distorted as to make her adaptations masquerade as values — which no quantity of trajectory data can supply.

So here is my hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis: the two sides are answering different questions under a single name. The learning side is answering an identification question — what can in principle be recovered from behavioral data, as a matter of mathematics and statistics — while the skeptics are answering a constitutive question — what it takes for a state of a person to count as a preference rather than a habit, a policy, or an accommodation to pressure. If that is right, the debate should be restructured rather than won: each side gets its question, and the apparent contradiction dissolves. But I can see one place where the questions might genuinely collide. If the assistance-game people are correct that acting well on a person's behalf requires taking a stance on how approximately rational she is, then the constitutive question leaks into the engineering one, and the split into two independent questions is not clean.

Is my diagnosis right, or is there a substantive disagreement hiding here after all — perhaps a hidden empirical one about how far human behavior actually is from optimal, or a difference about what counts as data, with one side admitting only trajectories and the other insisting on testimony, reflection, and the person's own endorsements? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a new mathematical result on identifiability, a better behavioral model of humans, an explicit agreement to rename one side's construct so the two questions stop sharing a word, or an argument that no progress should be expected because one of the two questions is ill-posed? I want a diagnosis supported by what each side actually says, not a survey of the field, and an honest verdict on whether anything could move the people involved.
