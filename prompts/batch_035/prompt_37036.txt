I serve on the ethics and compliance committee of a mid-sized international humanitarian NGO. We operate in about a dozen countries, and our largest program is in a post-conflict state in the Sahel, where customary chiefs control access to villages and district officials control the checkpoints our supply convoys must pass. Our country director has for two years run an informal practice he calls "relationship maintenance": modest cash honoraria to chiefs who host our distribution events, in-kind gifts (a ram, cloth) at ceremonies marking the start of a project, and small "expedition fees" paid at checkpoints, which the officials there treat not as solicited bribes but as their ordinary perquisite of office. He insists this is local reciprocity, that refusing it would brand us as insulting and untrustworthy, and that no villager has ever been denied aid over it. One of our major institutional donors was told about the practice during an audit, and is now threatening to withdraw funding unless we adopt a zero-tolerance anti-corruption policy: no payments or gifts of any kind to any public official or customary authority, full stop, with mandatory reporting of any request for one. The country director responds that the donor's policy is written for a world where every transfer is either a market transaction or a bribe, and that in his operating environment it would shut down the program within months — and, worse, would hand program access to whichever (less scrupulous) agency replaced us.

The full board has asked our committee for a written recommendation on the donor's proposed policy, and we are divided. Three positions have crystallized:

1. The absolutist position: a payment to someone holding public or quasi-public office, made because of that office, and intended to affect what they do in it, is corruption by definition — whether it's culturally expected is irrelevant to whether we should participate in it. Definitions shouldn't bend to accommodate practice, and once we admit "local norms" as an exception, we have no principled place to stop.
2. The contextualist position: "corruption" is not a culture-independent natural kind. What the donor calls a bribe functions in that setting as the entry fee to a patronage network that is simply how governance works there; what the donor's own home-country lobbying and revolving-door practices amount to is arguably worse, just laundered through legality. Imposing the donor's categories worldwide is less an ethical stance than a form of institutional ethnocentrism.
3. A compromise position some staff have floated: adopt the zero-tolerance principle for anything involving coercion or denial of aid, but create a formally budgeted, dual-authorized "community engagement" line for ceremony gifts and hospitality under a fixed monthly cap, disclosed to the donor, so that the practice is transparent, bounded, and reviewable rather than driven underground.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before our meeting. Take the role of a political philosopher with applied ethics and development-practice experience who has seen donor compliance regimes both succeed and misfire. Think carefully before committing to a recommendation — first map what actually makes a transfer corrupt (the role of office, reciprocity norms, harm, secrecy, whether it entrenches a gatekeeper), and test each of the three positions against that. Then recommend one course of action (adopt, reject, or adopt with modifications — be specific about what the modifications would be), including criteria field staff could actually apply when facing a checkpoint demand or a chief's expectation, and what our escalation and review triggers should be. The memo must engage the strongest argument on the losing side honestly rather than caricaturing it, and should address the contextualist challenge directly: if we reject the claim that local norms redefine corruption, we need to say what universal standard we are applying and why it isn't just the donor's. Keep it to something the committee can read before Thursday: roughly 900 to 1,300 words.
