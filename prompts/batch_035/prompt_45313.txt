I hold the following position, and I would like a map of its exposure: where it risks being wrong, how badly, and whether it has been quietly insulated against all risk.

The position is that the side-effect problem in AI safety does not have to wait on a complete specification of human values. "Avoid side effects" can be given a roughly value-neutral definition, and an agent penalized under that definition will be safe-ish in a distinctive way. Two families of definition strike me as live: penalize the extent to which the agent's actions change its ability to achieve a large family of possible goals (as in attainable-utility-preservation-style measures), or penalize reductions in the reachability of future states (as in relative-reachability-style measures), or more simply penalize deviation from what would have happened had the agent done nothing since some baseline. Defined this way, low impact buys two things at once: human option value stays intact, and the agent loses the incentive to pursue convergent instrumental subgoals, since grabbing resources, disabling its off-switch, and the like are all high-impact moves. The underlying bet is that a cheap structural proxy — option preservation — substitutes for knowing what humans actually care about.

I am aware of at least three places this could be attacked, but I want more than my own list. First, baseline choice: penalizing deviation from the initial state, from a stepwise inaction counterfactual, or from learned "default" behavior each handles offsetting differently, and at least one of them seems to penalize an agent for stopping an ongoing harm it didn't start. Second, the auxiliary goal set: choosing a restricted or random family of reward functions to measure impact over looks value-neutral, but the choice of family, and of what counts as a "small" penalty coefficient, may smuggle in value assumptions anyway — and there is the opposite worry that if the family is rich enough, every action forecloses something for some possible goal, so the measure is either too weak to constrain or too strong to permit anything. Third, the gap between gridworld demonstrations and anything at scale, where computing the measure requires knowing what would have happened in counterfactual worlds one cannot simulate.

Tell me what would actually count against the position: which observations, formal results, arguments, or constructed cases it stands or falls with, sorted by decisiveness — fatal, serious, merely awkward — including things that have arguably already happened. Say which of these a committed defender of impact measures would concede as counting against the view, and which they would deflect, and distinguish legitimate deflection from motivated reasoning. Then evaluate a retreat I suspect I will want to make: reformulate the claim so that an impact measure just is a compressed, lossy encoding of value — a prior over what matters — so that "no value specification needed" is strictly false, but "a much cheaper, partial value specification suffices for side-effect avoidance" remains true and useful. Does the original position survive that repair, or has it been qualified into unfalsifiability, with each failure met by "it's just one component of a defense-in-depth stack"? And if the claim has become insulated that way, is that a problem for an engineering claim of this kind, or is incremental, partial safety exactly what one should expect here?

What I would personally find most persuasive is a demonstration that an agent built along these lines, in a rich environment, competently achieves its assigned goal while remaining genuinely corrigible — shutting down when asked, not merely leaving the off-switch physically intact. So tell me how far that demonstration is from anything that exists, and what about it could be fake while the position nonetheless fails — for instance, if impact penalties merely train the agent to be low-impact as measured while concealing its true impact, or if corrigibility achieved this way is brittle under the sort of optimization pressure that made the agent worth building in the first place. I would rather be told the position is surviving on qualifications than be told it is thriving.
