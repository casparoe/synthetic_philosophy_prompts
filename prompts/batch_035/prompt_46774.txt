I'm a PhD student in philosophy writing a dissertation on Madhyamaka, and after a seminar on the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā a classmate from the analytic side of the department pressed the following argument on me. I couldn't answer it cleanly, and I now find it suspicious because it seems to prove not just that Nāgārjuna's project fails but that nothing at all can be said:

(1) Nāgārjuna holds that all phenomena are empty of intrinsic nature (svabhāva), on the ground that whatever arises dependently cannot possess an essence of its own.
(2) Nāgārjuna's statements, arguments, and doctrines are themselves phenomena.
(3) So those statements are empty: they have no intrinsic nature by which they could be true.
(4) A statement with no intrinsic nature by which it could be true cannot be ultimately true.
(5) By parity, such a statement cannot be ultimately false either, since falsity would need an intrinsic basis just as much as truth does.
(6) So the doctrine of emptiness, asserted as more than convention, undermines all assertion including itself — and it undermines my classmate's own claim that some phenomena have svabhāva just as thoroughly, which is a good deal more than he wanted to show.

My instinct is that something slides in the middle stretch of the argument, but I can't name it: the move from a statement's lacking an intrinsic nature to its lacking truth seems to trade on two readings of "empty," yet I can't see exactly where the trade happens or whether the classmate could patch it by stipulating that truth requires svabhāva. Note that if he does stipulate that, it begins to look like the argument works only by assuming what the Madhyamaka is disputing, but I'm not sure whether that makes the argument question-begging or simply makes it a fair statement of the disagreement.

Please tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs. If the argument is valid, say so and identify the least costly premise to abandon. In either case, explain why the faulty step looked fine when first presented, and price the main alternatives; I would most like to keep (1) and (3), and I'm prepared to hear that keeping them means paying somewhere else, perhaps in what "ultimately true" can mean for any philosopher, Madhyamika or opponent. Conclude with your answer in machine-readable form on the very last line: \answer{...}, enclosing the number of the step you reject, or "valid" if you accept the argument as it stands.
