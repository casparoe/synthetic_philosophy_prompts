I've been trying to work out what I actually believe about the boundary between legend and history, and rather than starting from any theory I've been recording my gut verdicts on particular cases. Here they are.

The Trojan War: I say there's a historical kernel. There really was a fortified Bronze Age city at Hisarlik, destroyed by violence roughly in the period the poems imply; the Hittite archives mention a western Anatolian state called Wilusa (which fits 'Ilios' about as well as anything does) and a distant power called Ahhiyawa (a plausible rendering of 'Achaeans'); so I think Mycenaean Greeks probably warred on that coast at some point. What the poems narrate — a decade-long siege over Helen, the gods taking sides, the catalog of ships — I take to be transformation, not memory.

The Trojan Horse: pure invention, and it doesn't trouble me at all to hold this while holding the war itself to have a kernel.

King Arthur: essentially legend. He first appears in texts written centuries after his supposed floruit, in a period that had every incentive to invent a resisting war-leader. I'm suspicious of the hypothesis, which I know is out there, that he was a real Breton commander named Riothamus, attested in a letter of Sidonius Apollinaris and in Jordanes campaigning in Gaul around 470 — suspicious partly because it's the kind of identification that gets made by wanting there to be a kernel. Meanwhile I do accept that the battle of Mount Badon was a real event, because Gildas, writing within living memory of it, treats it as one — he just doesn't name a commander.

The Xia dynasty: I say it probably existed as a real Bronze Age polity, even though its attestation is only in texts compiled up to a millennium later — the same order of lateness as Arthur's. I know the standard move is to cite the Erlitou site culture as a plausible material correlate, but honestly I suspect I'd hold this view without the archaeology.

The Exodus as a mass departure from Egypt: no kernel in the form described. The Merneptah stele does put a people called Israel in Canaan by around 1208 BCE, but I read that as consistent with origins wholly inside Canaan.

The Vinland voyages of the Icelandic sagas: historical, full stop. L'Anse aux Meadows settled it for me, and I notice that I treat the confirmation of the setting as having confirmed the voyages.

The fall of the Burgundians in the Nibelungenlied: kernel, because the historical destruction of the Burgundian kingdom in the 430s is attested near-contemporarily, and the epic preserved the place and the cast of peoples, however transformed.

Atlantis: no kernel, and I hold this more confidently than any of the above, even though Plato is a nearer and more authoritative source than anything behind Arthur or the Xia.

Here's my problem. My tentative principle is something like: a legend carries history only where it's anchored by independent attestation of the setting or events, and the anchor transfers to the frame, not the narrative. But I can see this doesn't straightforwardly deliver my Xia and Arthur verdicts — both are late-attested, only one has an anchor, and I'm not sure which one. I also can't decide whether Vinland vindicates the principle or shows I'd have gotten it wrong before 1960, when nothing about the sagas was anchored by anything.

Please tell me whether there's a principle — yours or a repaired version of mine — that captures these verdicts. State it precisely enough to generate new verdicts, tell me which of mine it cannot accommodate and which I should give up, and say what it predicts for cases I haven't listed, such as the Geats of Beowulf, the Dorian invasion, or Robin Hood. Finally, tell me whether the fact that my confidence is graded and shifts with each new anchor, rather than falling on a sharp boundary, means there is no single principle here — or whether it just means the principle should be probabilistic.
