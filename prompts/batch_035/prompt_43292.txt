I've noticed that I have fairly settled reactions to claims that germs, climate, or geology did something in history, but I can't articulate what drives them, and I'd like help finding the principle, if there is one.

My verdicts, case by case. When historians write that the eruption of Tambora in 1815 caused the year without a summer, the crop failures, and the famine that swept New England and much of Europe in 1816, that seems to me unobjectionable, even illuminating — and I don't even mind when a book treats the volcano as a kind of protagonist, with victims and a trail of destruction, as though it had walked through the decade. When a historian says the Black Death so depleted labor in Western Europe that serfdom loosened there, that also seems fine: a bacterium as an engine of social change, with the interesting rider that the same bacterium apparently entrenched serfdom east of the Elbe, which I take to show that the germ set conditions and institutions responded. When I read that smallpox did more than Cortés to bring down the Aztec empire, I believe it, and it doesn't feel as though it lets Cortés off; the epidemic and the treachery seem to belong to different ledgers, both true. When a book argues that geography — continental axes, domesticable animals, the resulting diseases — explains why Europeans colonized the Americas rather than the reverse, I'm mostly fine with it for crops and germs, though I get uneasy when it slides from explaining why the epidemics traveled in one direction to explaining why the West won in full. When a commentator says that the multi-year drought preceding the Syrian civil war was the spark that ignited it, however, I bristle: it feels like laundering the regime's responsibility, and the drought's severity owed something to human water policy in the first place. And when someone attributes Russia's political culture to its climate, I find that not just wrong but somehow obscene — the point at which environmental explanation becomes determinism.

That leaves tensions I can partly see and probably others I can't. The smallpox verdict and the drought verdict both shift responsibility from human actors to non-human causes, yet one feels fine to me and the other feels like an alibi, and I can't say what marks the difference. I accept the Tambora famine claim without asking which governments handled the harvest failures better, while rejecting the drought account partly because it ignores how the Syrian regime managed the crisis — which looks inconsistent. And I notice that I tolerate the personified volcano but would wince at a museum plaque crediting smallpox with the conquest of Mexico, even though that is roughly the claim I endorsed.

So: is there a principle — or a small set of candidates — that captures when a non-human cause may be cast as a historical actor and when that move degenerates into determinism or apologetics? Please state each clause precisely, say which case motivates it, tell me what it predicts on cases I haven't listed (for instance, whether it licenses saying that cooling climate ended the Norse colonies in Greenland, or that coal deposits explain why the Industrial Revolution happened in Britain — a claim I keep going back and forth on), and say honestly which of my verdicts it cannot save and what I should give up instead. I'd also like its verdict on whether the timing of the French Revolution may be attributed to the bad harvests of the late 1780s: my instinct says that is closer to Tambora than to the Syrian drought, but that instinct is exactly what is under examination here.
