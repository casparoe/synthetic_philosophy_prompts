Three weeks ago my brother was declared brain dead after an aneurysm rupture, and we consented to organ retrieval while his heart was still beating; since then I have been unable to stop interrogating the definition of death that made that decision coherent, and I want to work through it using the hardest public case we have: the Jahi McMath case. The facts as I understand them, which I will state so we share them: in December 2013, thirteen-year-old Jahi McMath suffered massive bleeding and cardiac arrest following surgery at Children's Hospital Oakland; she was declared brain dead three days later under California's neurologic criteria, a judge affirmed the declaration, and after a legal fight the hospital released her body, on a ventilator, to her mother's custody. The family, on religious grounds, held that she remained alive so long as her heart beat, and she was relocated to New Jersey, which accommodates religious objections to neurologic death declarations; her body was maintained for over four years, during which it reportedly went through puberty, until a second death certificate was issued there in June 2018 after complications from surgery. So one human organism acquired two death certificates, in two states, four and a half years apart, and in between it grew. Analyze this episode through the lens of the competing definitions of death — the whole-brain criterion, the circulatory criterion, and the higher-brain or personhood criterion — and run the analysis in both directions. For each criterion, say what it implies about what Jahi was in the interval: a corpse receiving futile treatment, a living but irreversibly unconscious patient, or something the criterion simply has no vocabulary for; be disciplined about what each criterion actually claims (e.g., whether whole-brain death is meant to track the death of the organism as a whole or has drifted toward something else), and flag where a criterion goes silent rather than forcing an answer. Then say what the case does to the criteria: does the two-certificates fact show that death is legally stipulated rather than discovered, does the maintained body's pubertal development embarrass the whole-brain rationale or merely reveal that some somatic integration does not require the brain, and does the suspicion that neurologic criteria were partly consolidated to serve transplantation explain anything about how the case was handled? Include the wrinkle my own decision turned on: donation after circulatory death, where the heart is stopped and death is declared after a few minutes on grounds of irreversibility — whether the organism-as-a-whole rationale that supports brain death for donation also supports that waiting period, or whether the two donation paths lean on incommensurable rationales while pretending to share one. Start with your bottom line — which criterion, if any, emerges from this case intact — in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
