I run the puzzle segment at my school's weekly math club — about fifteen students aged thirteen to seventeen, most of them with no formal logic beyond what they've absorbed from riddles, and I get thirty-five minutes and a whiteboard. Next week I want to present the muddy children puzzle: a group of children come back from playing outside, some number of them have mud on their foreheads, each child can see every other child's forehead but not their own, and the father announces in front of everyone that at least one child has mud. The children, all perfectly logical, are then asked in rounds whether they know their own status, and the muddy ones eventually work it out from the silence of earlier rounds. I can do the induction myself, so I'm not asking you to solve the puzzle; I'm asking for a plan of how to present it.

Concretely: in what order should the pieces be introduced, and what does each depend on? My instinct is that they have to accept the round structure and the publicness of the father's statement as fixed rules before the induction can even begin, but I'm not confident how to motivate that without it feeling arbitrary. Which version of the puzzle should carry the session — one muddy child, two, three — and how many rounds should I actually play out at the board; what must the chosen version have, and what should it avoid? Which misconceptions should I expect, and at exactly which point in the sequence should I head each one off? The one I've already hit when explaining this to a friend is that the announcement tells the children nothing, since everyone can already see mud on someone; and I suspect there's a related confusion between a child knowing that another child can see mud and the stronger fact that the first child knows the second knows that at least one child is muddy. For the closing check, I was considering asking what would change if the father had told each child privately instead of announcing it — is that the right probe for whether they've grasped the difference between everybody knowing something and whatever it is the public announcement creates, or is there a better one? And what should I openly decline to cover at this level — I assume the formal semantics and anything requiring infinitely nested knowledge statements, but correct me if that's the wrong line to draw.

One last thing, since I've never taught anything before: tell me where this plan is most likely to fail — the specific point in the sequence where I'd most plausibly lose the room — and what I should watch for during the session that would tell me to revise the plan on the spot. And if you think any part of my framing is mistaken, say so directly rather than building the plan around my errors.
