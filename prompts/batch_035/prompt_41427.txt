I keep running into these two ideas in AI safety writing and I can't for the life of me get straight how they relate: ontology identification and inaccessible information. The way I understand it (and correct me if im off), ontology identification is the problem that we can only specify an AI's goals in human terms — "protect the diamonds", "be honest" — but the AI learns its own world-model, which carves the world up whatever way was useful for prediction, and we then face the task of finding, inside that alien carving, the stuff our words were pointing at. Inaccessible information is the companion idea: there's information that would matter enormously to us — about what the AI is "really" up to, about how its world-model divides things — that we can't read off in practice; if we can get it at all, it comes second-hand from other AIs.

To sort this out in my head I invented a three-way split of how the lookup can go wrong:

(a) translation failure — the AI does have a concept matching our "diamond", we just can't find it in its internals;
(b) carving failure — the AI's world-model has no single thing corresponding to our concept (maybe our "diamond" comes out as several of its concepts, or straddles a couple of them);
(c) access failure — we could in principle locate the concept, but only by using information about the AI's internals that is inaccessible to us in practice.

And I have three hunches, which I don't trust: (a) is ordinary interpretability work that gets easier as the field matures; (b) is permanent and is really about the indeterminacy of our own words — a friend of mine says its just Quine's gavagai point in a lab coat, is she right?; and (c) gets worse as the AI gets smarter, since smarter agents end up with weirder world-models.
