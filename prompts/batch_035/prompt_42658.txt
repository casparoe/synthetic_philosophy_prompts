I'm a retired geophysicist who's spent the last few years reading philosophy and economics for fun, and honestly I'm a bit out of my depth here, so bear with me. My son gave me a popular book on social choice (I won't name it since I'm about to be unkind about it) that summarizes Amartya Sen's "liberal paradox" roughly like this: Sen proved in the 1970s that you can't have a society that is both free and efficient. If each person gets the final say over even one personal choice — what they do in their own home — then society can no longer respect unanimous preferences, and since unanimity is about the weakest efficiency requirement there is, freedom and efficiency turn out to be strictly incompatible, full stop. The book runs Sen's example: a prudish person and a lewd one sharing a society with one copy of a notorious obscene novel. Each cares mostly about the other's behavior — the prude wants nobody to read it, the lewd one wants the book kept away from the prude — so their private choices spill over into each other's business, and honoring both people's liberty produces an outcome everyone agrees is worse. Hence, the book says, economists after Sen concluded that rights and welfare have to be kept in completely seperate boxes, and any political philosophy trying to combine them is confused at the foundation.

Something about this bugs me but I can't locate it. I come from a field where a proof is a proof, so part of me wants to just accept the result and move on, but the jump from one formal theorem to "liberal society is incoherent" feels like the kind of move I'd have flagged in a seminar. Is this summary fair?
