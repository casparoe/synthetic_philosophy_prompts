In my reading group on classical pragmatism we have been working through Peirce's 1878 essay on making our ideas clear, with its account of truth as the opinion that inquiry, pursued far enough, would eventually settle into, alongside James's later Pragmatism, where truth is instead something that happens to an idea: an idea proves itself by leading us fruitfully through experience, by its practical payoff, and truth is a name for that proven process. So far, so standard. The trouble started with an analogy a senior member of the group uses to argue that James, properly understood, is a relativist about truth.

The analogy is the money analogy. A banknote, she says, is not good because it matches some gold in a vault somewhere; it is good because it circulates, because people accept it, because it lets you actually buy things. Its value consists entirely in what you can do with it inside a functioning economy. Beliefs, on the Jamesian picture, are like that: a belief is true not by corresponding to some reality hidden behind experience, but because it works within the ongoing traffic of our dealings with the world, guiding us from one bit of experience to another without letting us down. And now the conclusion she draws: since currencies are community-relative — what is legal tender in one economy may be worthless in another — truth on James's account is community-relative too. Different communities can run different truth-currencies; there is no supra-communal fact of the matter, any more than there is a currency that is intrinsically, community-independently money. She adds, against Peirce, that his story about the eventual agreement of all inquirers is just the fantasy of a single world currency, and that he smuggles in the very correspondence-style realism about a final opinion that the money picture is supposed to have replaced.

I want to know how far the analogy actually holds, on both sides of the Peirce-James divide. Please lay out the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the role of the banknote, the accepting merchant, the issuing authority, redemption, counterfeiting, inflation, the economy as a whole. Then identify where the mapping breaks, and — this is the part I care most about — separate the disanalogies that matter for the relativist conclusion from the ones that don't. For instance, ordinary currencies have a central issuer and deliberate design in a way that, arguably, the circulation of belief does not; whether that helps or hurts her case is unclear to me. Also consider whether the analogy, once audited, might fit Peirce better than she thinks: does his long-run convergence story have a natural money-theoretic reading, or is it precisely the feature of inquiry that no financial system exhibits? Finally, tell me whether her community-relative conclusion survives the disanalogies you find, or whether the analogy was doing the work of carrying it along; and if there is a better analogy for James's view of truth — something other than money that keeps what she likes and drops what breaks — say what it is and why. You may assume I know the general shape of both philosophers' views, so spend your words on the audit rather than on background.
