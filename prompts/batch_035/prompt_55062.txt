I'm a retired seismologist who's gotten hooked on philosophy since I stopped going into the office, and I keep circling back to futarchy — Robin Hanson's proposal that elected legislatures should only decide what we want to maximize (some welfare measure, say) while prediction markets work out which policies will actually move the needle, the idea being that traders who bet on outcomes lose money when they're wrong, unlike pundits. What I want to understand is what this scheme quietly takes for granted about knowledge. In my old field we had pretty much the opposite arrangement: nobody bet on whether an earthquake forecast was right, it all went through peer review, which is pure cheap talk, and that somehow worked for centuries.

So here's my puzzle. Futarchy seems to presuppose that credences are the kind of thing you can read off someone's willingness to lose money — that market prices are measurements of belief the way a thermometer measures temperature. It also seems to presuppose that values and beliefs can be cleanly separated, that a crowd of self-interested traders aggregates into something like public truth, and that a rich fanatic who cares more about a policy passing than about winning his bet can't just buy the price — though I gather Hanson's reply is that such manipulation hands free money to informed traders, which always struck me as assuming the thing to be proven. And the same economists who like futarchy also believe ordinary financial markets bubble and herd.

Which of these commitments does futarchy strictly need, which does it merely sit comfortably with, and where is it underdetermined enough that a different epistemology would license the same design? What would have to change if you swapped in a deliberative view where talking is the point rather than a cost? And is my peer review comparison fair, or am I comparing apples to oranges?
