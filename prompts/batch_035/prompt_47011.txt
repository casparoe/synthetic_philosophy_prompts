I'm a second-year undergraduate taking a course called Philosophical Methods, and our professor has students run one of the weekly sections themselves in the second half of the semester. I've been assigned the unit on intuition pumps, built around Dennett's idea (mainly from his 2013 book Intuition Pumps and Other Tools for Thinking) that thought experiments are tools that get built, deployed, and — crucially — misused: some earn their persuasive force by isolating exactly the feature that matters, while others smuggle in their conclusions by exploiting surface features we respond to automatically, the way a skilled salesman frames a choice. I have 16 classmates and a 75-minute section, and the professor has given me one condition: the point has to show up in what happens during the session, not in anything I say up front, since the reading was already assigned and everyone will arrive knowing what an intuition pump is supposed to be.

My idea is to have them be pumped without knowing it. I'd split the room in half without announcement: each half silently reads a different one-page case, casts an anonymous verdict (a forced choice plus a confidence rating), and then — this is the reveal I want the activity to be built around — the two cases turn out to be twins, identical in every respect the philosophical theory cares about but differing only in packaging: the vividness with which one party in the case is described, which grammatical person the case is written in (does it matter whether it says "you" instead of "a surgeon"?), which detail comes first in the paragraph, and so on. I have a rough plan for a second phase after the reveal, where small groups try to construct their own minimal pair — a case and its doctored twin — to pull the verdict of the other groups in a direction the groups get to choose, and then we inspect which doctorings worked. But I don't know how to sequence all this, how to word the cases so that the cover story holds (I'm worried people will smell a rat the moment verdicts are collected anonymously, since that's not how a normal discussion section starts), or what to do with the confidence ratings — it would be a much better activity if the people whose verdicts were manufactured were also the most confident, but I don't know how to arrange that and don't want to fake it.

So I'd like a full plan: the two cases (on whatever topic you judge gives the cleanest packaging effects, though I'd rather stay away from trolley problems since they've seen those to death), the anonymous voting mechanics, the timeline for 75 minutes, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the real distinction between a thought experiment that earns its force and one that merely exploits our reflexes — including how to handle the objection I expect from the one classmate who will say that survey judgments in a classroom tell us nothing about how intuitions function inside an actual philosophical argument. I also want an honest note on which feature of real intuition pumps the activity can't reproduce (I suspect it's something about how the classic ones do their work slowly, embedded in pages of dialectic, rather than in one shot), plus what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover mid-session. Please don't have me lecture for more than five minutes at any point, and don't plan anything that requires me to deceive anyone about anything after the reveal happens — the manipulation has to be fully exposed by the end of the section, since the professor was explicit that the session is only defensible if everyone leaves knowing exactly how they were steered.
