I'm on the board of a 240-unit housing co-op and I chair the rules committee, which means I'm the one who has to referee a steady stream of proposals about what members can and can't do on their own property. It's mostly small stuff but it never stops: some members want to ban smoking on private balconies because the smell drifts into neighbors' windows; a petition with forty signatures says the member who painted her shutters electric pink has to repaint because it ruins the look of the courtyard; and there's a current push to make compost bins mandatory, supported by basically everyone except two holdouts who think it's gross. Each of these is a fifteen-minute fight at a monthly meeting, the elected board has to sign off on whatever we recommend, and right now the only decision procedure we really have is whoever shows up angrier. Nobody on the committee has a philosophy background except one guy who took a semester of Habermas and won't shut up about it.

I took a philosophy of economics course years ago and Sen's liberal paradox has been living rent-free in my head since: roughly, that there's no social decision rule that both respects everyone's preferences (the Pareto condition) and gives each person final say over at least some private choices in their own protected sphere (his minimal liberalism) — you can construct preference profiles where the two collide head-on. I've become convinced there's something in there for us, but obviously Sen wasn't writing about housing co-ops. So what I want is for you to translate the paradox into an actual decision procedure my committee can run on a proposal in about fifteen minutes: how we'd define a protected personal sphere in a co-op and who gets to draw its boundary (I suspect the balcony smoking case shows the boundary is quietly doing all the work, since almost everything leaks some smell or sound or sight across it), at what point other members' preferences about conduct inside someone's sphere become legitimately relevant, and what we're supposed to do when a large majority feels strongly about something squarely inside one person's sphere — which is exactly the collision Sen describes, and where I suspect the procedure has to stop and hand things to a straight vote because it's a values question the framework can't settle for us. Be explicit about which steps are just judgment calls dressed up as procedure; I'd rather know that than pretend we have something airtight.

Then run it on the three cases above. For each one, tell me plainly if the procedure gives an answer I won't like — I'll admit up front I want the smoking ban, and I personally can't stand the pink shutters, so if my preferences and the procedure's output conflict I want that called out rather than smoothed over. And for each verdict, tell me what would have to be true, or what argument I'd have to accept, for it to come out the other way — I want to know where my application of Sen is most likely to go wrong, not just get an answer. Finally, flag whatever cases the procedure genuinely can't handle, where his framework runs out and we're back to ordinary politics.
