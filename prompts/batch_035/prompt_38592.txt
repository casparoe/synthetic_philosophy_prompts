My ethics reading group has spent two terms on markets in repugnant goods — kidney sales above all, with side trips into vote-selling and price gouging after disasters — and the discussion has stalled in a way I find instructive. The pro-market side holds that since unpaid donation is already permitted, the ban on compensation simply sets the price of a kidney at zero and produces chronic shortage and avoidable deaths; that paternalism toward the poor would-be seller is no better justified than paternalism toward the paid research subject or the paid surrogate; and that the exploitation and desperation worries could in principle be met with regulation — single public purchaser, mandatory counseling, lifetime follow-up care. The anti-commodification side replies that some goods are changed by being sold — that a kidney transfer is not the kind of transaction money can mediate without degrading it — and that consent extracted by desperation is not the sort of consent that legitimizes a market in the first place. The frustrating thing is that everyone in the group already knows all the moves, each side freely concedes the other's strongest facts (the shortage is real; the desperate would be overrepresented among sellers), and nobody has budged in months.

My working hypothesis is that the sides are not really disagreeing about kidneys at all but about what the baseline is: the market side compares a world of regulated sales to the actual world of shortage and black markets, while the anti-commodification side compares it to an idealized sphere of medicine organized by gift and need that market access would corrode — so the very same policy registers to one side as removing a lethal restriction and to the other as installing a corrupting institution. Is that diagnosis right, or is there something else doing the real work: a hidden empirical question about what a well-regulated system would actually produce, or a hidden conceptual question about what the degradation of a practice could even consist in, or bedrock intuitions about the body and desperation that neither side can argue for? Please support your diagnosis with what each side actually says, and tell me what — a distinction, a piece of evidence, a reframing of the question — would count as progress given that diagnosis, or else argue that none should be expected.
