I'm a science-and-ideas writer (behavioural econ beat, mostly), and in a few weeks I'm giving the toast at the retirement dinner of the woman who basically trained me: Eileen, who for thirty years ran a two-week summer fellowship teaching journalists how to cover negotiation and conflict without becoming the story. Her signature move, first morning of every cohort, was the dollar auction. She'd hold up a real twenty, run it as a live auction with the one rigged rule — when the hammer falls, the second-highest bidder also pays whatever they bid — and then stand there with a completely blank face while two reporters who write about sunk costs for a living bid each other up to 14, 18, and once, gloriously, past forty dollars. Then she'd pocket the money and say nothing about it until the last day of the course. The dinner room is split exactly in half: former fellows who have all personally lost money to her at that podium and get misty about it, and her kids and sisters and grandkids, who have never bid on anything more combative than a house and find the whole auction story a little weird and honestly a little mean until you get the point. So the toast has to land for both halves at once — the fellows need it to be a real argument about what she taught us, and the family can't be sat through a lecture with the words "escalation of commitment" anywhere in it.

Here's the part I can't figure out and want your help with. She always insisted the lesson was NOT "know when to quit" — she called that the greeting-card version. Her version was that when you're standing at the podium at nineteen dollars with eighteen-fifty already sunk, quitting feels like a fresh, free decision to throw money away, while staying in feels like the only responsible thing left, and that knowing all about the game does basically nothing for you while you're actually standing there. So a toast that ends with tidy wisdom about walking away would be exactly the thing she spent her career mocking, and she'd hear it from the grave. What I'd love is a toast that somehow does the thing instead of describing it — builds a little escalation of its own (praise stacked on praise the way every retirement toast does, each speaker topping the last, we've all done it), and then visibly stops, takes the loss, and sits down early on purpose. Five minutes max. There's one true story I want built in somewhere: a fellow named Dave who quit the auction at nine dollars just to be out of it, and how afterward she matched his nine out of her own pocket, taped both bills to a card, wrote something on it, and he still has it on his office wall. And one detail for the fellows in the room: in thirty years of running that game she never once mentioned that it was invented by an economist named Shubik back in 1971 — she let every cohort think it was hers, and nobody ever dared ask. Family should leave having FELT what it's like at that podium, not having been told about it. It can't be mean, and it absolutely can't be smug — she had a phrase for smugness, she called it bidding with other people's money.
