I write long-form pieces on science and ideas for a Sunday broadsheet, and I'm at work on a feature about epiphenomenalism — the view that conscious experience is a real by-product of brain activity but does nothing in return: the physical world is causally closed, and experience never pushes back on it. The feature turns on what I regard as the view's most uncomfortable corner, the paradox of phenomenal judgment. If my experience of the burning stove is causally idle, then my sincere utterance "that hurt" has a complete physical causal ancestry — sensory nerves, motor cortex, vocal cords — to which the painfulness itself contributed nothing. The report would have come out word for word the same had the experience never occurred at all. Yet such reports are the very evidence we go on when we attribute consciousness to others, and even when we believe in our own. I want to put this corner on stage, and for that I need a thought experiment built to order rather than the usual furniture.

Construct the scenario to these specifications. First, it must be anchored in ordinary clinical or experimental neuroscience of the kind I could describe in a magazine without losing readers: blindsight, split-brain reporting, anaesthesia with a button to press, seizure auras narrated afterwards — that register. No zombies, no inverted spectra, no molecule-for-molecule duplicates, no demons or perfect simulators, and no appeal to exotic physics. Second, the paradox must be generated, not announced: the scenario should be written entirely in third-person observational terms, and at its climax the subject must be asked — by an experimenter, or by herself — whether her report would have been the same without the experience, at which point it is the theory, not the scene-setting, that supplies the troubling answer. Third, keep the scenario itself to a single paragraph; the analysis comes afterwards.

After the scenario, do the walkthrough. Explain what the epiphenomenalist must say, step by step, about the causal ancestry of the subject's report, and where exactly the experience drops out of the story. Then say what an interactionist or dual-aspect theorist would say differently, and — this matters — where those rivals would still agree with the epiphenomenalist, so the contrast isn't exaggerated. Identify which single stipulated feature of the case is doing the work (I suspect it is causal closure of the physical, but tell me if it's something else), and describe what happens to the case if that feature is removed. Finally, give me the strongest objection a committed epiphenomenalist would raise against the case rather than accept the paradoxical reading — perhaps something about the causes of a report coming apart from what the report is about — and say honestly whether the case survives it; if it doesn't, tell me that plainly rather than papering over it. The piece is aimed at lay readers who have never heard the term epiphenomenalism, so define any term of art on first use, and bear in mind what the case is for: a sidebar that lets a general reader feel the problem arrive, rather than being told about it.
