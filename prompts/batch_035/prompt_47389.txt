I'm a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, and I came up through the Sequences and the standard community lore that goes with them — including the lore that "group selection" was a mid-century evolutionary-biology confusion, that it was mostly cleared up by gene-level thinking and inclusive fitness, and that anyone invoking it is probably rationalizing. I've never actually examined that lore in detail. Separately, I work in machine learning, and I suspect the units-of-selection debate translates into my home vocabulary far better than any of the blog-level retellings suggest. So here is my request.

Restate the units-of-selection debate — the arc running from Wynne-Edwards's defense of traits that regulate group populations, through George C. Williams's 1966 clampdown on group-advantage explanations and Richard Dawkins's gene's-eye framing of replicators and vehicles, up to the multilevel-selection revival associated with Sober and Wilson, with inclusive fitness and the Price equation as the common mathematical currency — in the vocabulary I actually use day to day: credit assignment, reward signals, gradients, baselines and variance reduction, parameter sharing, multi-agent training under team-level rewards, and the gap between the training loss and the thing you actually care about. As a fluency check: I've implemented policy-gradient methods, I understand why baselines exist and what variance does to learning, I know what happens when the only reward signal is observed at the level of a team rather than an individual agent, and the law of total variance is not intimidating to me. I have no formal training in evolutionary biology beyond what has filtered through blogs and popular books.

Please treat this as a translation exercise with a residue report at the end, not as an explainer pitched at a general audience. For each major position in the debate, tell me (a) what translates cleanly, (b) what translates only if distorted, and (c) what has no expression in my vocabulary at all. A few specific adjudications I want along the way:

1. Gene-level selection looks to me like per-parameter credit assignment — the claim that selection "really" acts at the gene level reads as the claim that credit is assigned per parameter. Does that mapping hold, or does it quietly smuggle in the gene-level view by fixing the parameterization before the translation begins? Is there an argument in the debate about what corresponds to choosing the parameterization?

2. Group selection looks like the case where the only reward is a team reward: the gradient signal is coarse, the variance is high, and agents inside the team that free-ride get more precise individual-level signal, so within-team selection drags parameters toward selfishness faster than between-team differences can accumulate — with migration between groups acting like a mixing process that destroys the between-group component of variance. Is that a faithful rendering of the standard objection, or a different objection that just happens to be easy to say in my vocabulary?

3. Inclusive fitness and relatedness look like claims about correlated parameters — shared weights across agents, such that a signal generated by another agent's performance nonetheless updates my parameters. Does Hamilton's rule survive translation as something like a condition on when an indirect gradient term outweighs a direct one? Does the distinction between direct and inclusive fitness correspond to anything I distinguish, or does it blur?

4. The Price equation looks like the law of total expectation applied to fitness, with the group-structured version a variance decomposition into a between-group and a within-group component. If that is right, then the accounting can be done at any level and will agree — the decomposition is a reparameterization. So my question: given that invariance, what exactly were Wynne-Edwards's opponents objecting to? Was the dispute substantive, or was it about something that the equations don't settle — something like Williams's apparent principle that adaptation at a level requires selection at that level, and the Dawkins line that a gene's "interests" are what a trait is ultimately for? In gradient language I cannot state "what the trait is for the sake of" without stipulating an objective function, and stipulating the objective function looks question-begging on precisely the point the debate was about. Tell me whether I've located the residue correctly, or whether that's an artifact of my translation rather than a genuine feature of the original.

Then the verdict I actually want: when the translation fails, is it because my vocabulary lacks a concept that biology earned the hard way — something like a canonical level at which the "point" of a trait is fixed — or because the biologists were arguing about something less substantive than it looked, and the community lore I inherited is correct but for reasons nobody in the retellings ever states? And if some of the residue points the other way, I want to know which parts of the rationalist-community take I should downgrade my confidence in.
