My brother-in-law and I got into it over Thanksgiving about what it actually means to be free. He's basically a live-and-let-live type: as long as nobody is physically stopping you from doing something, you're free, full stop. I don't think my gut agrees with him, but honestly my gut doesn't agree with itself either, so last week I wrote down my honest verdicts on a bunch of cases, and now I want someone to tell me whether there's a principle underneath all of them or whether I just have to bite the bullet and drop some.

Here they are. An addict who wants to quit and can't: unfree, even though there is no person on earth stopping him from quitting. A guy who signed a noncompete that keeps him from taking a better job across the street: unfree, and I don't care that he signed the thing voluntarily. An undocumented cook whose boss could get her deported any time he feels like it, so she works unpaid overtime with the threat just hanging there even though he's never actually said it out loud: about as unfree as it gets, and notice nothing has actually been *done* to her yet. A woman living under an old-fashioned tyrant who happens to like her personally and would never dream of touching her: I still want to say she's unfree. A hiker who might get mauled by a bear on the trail: not unfree, just unlucky. A person who might get mugged walking home at night: I keep flip-flopping — it feels different from the bear, but also somehow different from the tyrant. A guy who's broke and is technically "free" to eat at the fanciest restaurant in town: I want to say not really free to eat there, even though the maître d' would happily take his money. And a woman who's emotionally left her husband but hasn't filed the paperwork because her whole town would turn on her — gossip, shunning, her own family cutting her off: she hasn't technically been stopped by anyone either.

The closest I've gotten to a principle on my own is something like: you're unfree when there's some *particular somebody* who has it in their power to mess with you on a whim, and you both know it. That covers the cook, the tyrant's favorite, and maybe the noncompete guy, and it rules out the bear. But then what do I do about the addict, where there's no somebody at all, or the restaurant guy, where the only obstacle is being broke? And why does the mugger case feel like it belongs with the bear rather than with the tyrant, even though a mugger is definately a somebody? Can you state a principle — or a couple of candidate principles — that captures as much of this as possible? I'd want you to tell me which clause is doing which work, what it predicts about a couple of cases I haven't listed, and, honestly, which of my verdicts you think just have to go. If your answer is that my gut is running together two or three genuinely different things and slapping the word "unfree" on all of them, that's an acceptable answer too, as long as you tell me how to split them up and which of my cases lands in which bucket.
