I write fiction for a living, and my brother, who is in seminary, has produced an argument, drawn from Augustine's treatises on lying, that I cannot dismiss and cannot accept. Augustine, as I understand him, defines a lie as speaking contrary to what one holds in one's mind, with the intention of deceiving; he excludes jokes, because the jester's manner shows he means no deceit even when what he says is untrue; and he holds that lying is never permissible — not even to save an innocent life, since the evil lies in the kind of act it is, not in its consequences. My brother's argument:

(1) A lie is the utterance of assertive sentences contrary to the speaker's mind, presented as true to the hearer. (2) Lying is intrinsically wrong — wrong in virtue of the act's nature, so that no outcome, not even the preservation of a life, can make it licit. (3) When I write that my heroine buried two husbands outside Jaffa, I utter assertive sentences contrary to my mind: there is no such woman, no such town, and I know it. (4) The only differences between me and an ordinary liar concern outcomes and inner attitude, and by (2) differences of outcome cannot affect deontic status. (5) My reader is not deceived the way the jester's hearer is not deceived, since the covers, the table of contents, and the surrounding conventions announce that this is invention; but an immersed reader does take each sentence as true while reading it, and many readers of historical fiction leave believing false things about the period, which I foresee and write on regardless — and knowingly causing false belief is what makes lying the wrong it is. (6) Therefore writing fiction is lying, and by (2) intrinsically wrong; and my life's work is an extended sin.

I am confident the conclusion is false, but each time I locate a step to reject, I find I have rejected something I need. If I deny (1) by requiring an intention to deceive, then the foreseeing clause of (5) seems to reinstate the problem through the back door. If I deny that the act is the same kind as the liar's, my brother demands the differentia, and all I can offer is a convention — the tacit preface "all of the following is invented" — which he says is itself a difference of context, not of act. If I lean on Augustine's exclusion of jest, he replies that the jester's hearer is in on it at the moment of utterance, whereas my reader believes my sentences as she reads them. Tell me which premise or inference to give up, why it looked unobjectionable, and what rejecting it costs — I would most like to keep the intrinsic wrongness of lying and the innocence of my craft, and I am prepared to hear that I cannot have both.
