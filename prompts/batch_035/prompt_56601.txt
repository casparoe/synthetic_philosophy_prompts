My seminar has been stuck for weeks on the Judy Benjamin problem, that old puzzle of van Fraassen's about how you should update on a conditional, and I'm starting to suspect the disagreement among us isn't really a technical one, so I'd like a diagnosis of what kind of impasse this actually is. The setup, in case you know it under another name: a soldier is dropped somewhere at night and has no idea where she is. HQ radios in and tells her, first, that she's definitely not in Yellow territory, so she splits her credence equally between Blue and Red territory. Then HQ adds that, if she is in Red territory, the odds are 3 to 1 that she's in Blue territory rather than some leftover corner of Red. (The story gets silly here but the structure is what matters: an initial uniform distribution, then a single conditional communication that only carries information about what to believe given Red.) The natural proposal, associated with Lewis, says she should end up with something like 2/3 credence in Blue and 1/3 in Red, but it's never been obvious which formal rule gets you there or why that rule is the right one, and since the 1980s people have kept inventing update rules for this case -- Jeffrey conditioning on carefully chosen propositions, imaging, splitting the difference between conditionalizing and leaving well enough alone -- and every rule has both defenders and a counterexample that the other camp finds compelling but the first camp finds question-begging.

Here's my hypothesis about what's going on, which I want you to evaluate rather than just confirm: the whole debate is haunted by a choice that nobody gets to make after the fact. The proponent of any update rule has to smuggle in an extra assumption about how much evidential weight the conditional statement carries -- how far down from the antecedent's having probability one the conditional's information reaches. Bayes' theorem just doesn't say anything about a bare conditional, and the triviality results seem to show that no proposition can play the role a conditional would need to play, so in a sense there's nothing there to conditionalize on. One half of my seminar thinks this means the puzzle is genuinely open and we're waiting for the right formal object, a random variable or a distribution over update rules or something; the other half thinks it's a pseudoproblem, that the story is underspecified in a way that makes any answer as good as any other, and that the decades of proposed rules are people disagreeing about how to idealize a situation that was never well-defined to begin with. A third person just mutters that the odds language in the story is ambiguous between a conditional probability and an odds ratio conditional on a proposition, and that if we cleaned up the statement the puzzle would evaporate.

So: is this a substantive disagreement about a formal question we haven't cracked, a verbal dispute about what it means to accept a conditional, a clash of idealizations masquerading as a clash of results, or an ill-posed question that keeps generating papers because nobody will say out loud that the story needs more information than it contains? Diagnose it using what the various camps are actually committed to, not a survey of who proposed what. And then tell me what would count as progress -- a theorem, a stipulation, a decision to treat the answer as conventional -- or whether I should expect this to stay exactly where it is for another forty years, and why.
