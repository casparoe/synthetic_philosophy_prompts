here's my position on scientific realism, written up for our reading group — please tell me whether it hangs together before I present it. (1) The no-miracles argument is basically right: the predictive success of mature theories would be miraculous unless they were at least approximately true. (2) The pessimistic induction is also basically right: phlogiston, caloric, the ether — most past theories that were once taken seriously turned out false, so ours probably will too. (3) So I've landed on structural realism: what no-miracles actually supports is approximate truth at the level of structure, and structure is just what survives revolutions — Fresnel's equations carried over into Maxwell's theory even though the ether died. (4) But I'm an epistemic structural realist, not an ontic one: objects are real and have intrinsic natures, we just can't know anything about those natures beyond their structural properties. Electrons aren't merely nodes in a relational network, even if everything we know about them is structural. (5) That said, whenever I actually look at how group theory functions in particle physics — particles getting individuated by symmetry representations — I find the ontic version weirdly tempting. (6) And I dont think any of this weakens ordinary scientific realism: the no-miracles argument still gives me reason to believe in the Higgs boson as a real object, not just as a position in a structure.

Which of these commitments are genuinely in tension, which only look like it, and what's the cheapest fix for each? Rank the tensions by severity instead of listing them all as equally bad. I genuinely can't tell whether (5) is a real problem or just an itch I should ignore, and I'm not sure what (3) leaves (6) standing on.
