I serve on the governance committee of a mid-sized professional society — roughly 900 members, most of whom know each other at least by reputation — and we've been tasked with recommending a change (or no change) to how we elect the society's president. We currently use plurality with a runoff if no candidate clears 40%, and our last election produced a messy situation: four candidates, a clear Condorcet winner by the pairwise comparisons we informally reconstructed afterwards, and a vocal faction claiming their members "wasted" their first preferences and effectively elected their least-favored candidate under the runoff. One of our committee members, a game theorist, has been citing the Gibbard–Satterthwaite result at every meeting — as he puts it, every voting rule with three or more candidates that isn't a dictatorship is in some situations manipulable, so chasing a "game-proof" ballot is a fool's errand and we should simply pick the rule that behaves best on the profiles we actually see. He concedes he can't reconstruct the theorem's exact hypotheses from memory, and the rest of us can't either, so we can't tell how much our specific setting — a small electorate where nominations are pre-screened by a committee, voters can be indifferent, and everyone will see who ranked whom — escapes or falls under it.

The committee is currently split three ways. One faction wants instant-runoff voting, arguing it removes the two-round turnout problem and lets members vote sincerely. A second wants Borda counting, on the theory that manipulation, while possible in principle, is hard to coordinate in a 900-person society where rankings are public afterward and reputations matter. A third wants to sidestep the whole question by having the nominations committee always narrow the field to exactly two finalists, so the theorem's domain condition fails and simple majority voting is strategy-proof in the classical sense — though two of us worry this just relocates the manipulation to whatever process picks the finalists, and makes the nominations committee de facto kingmakers. There's also a lingering question of whether we should even treat strategic voting as a defect to be engineered away rather than an ordinary part of political life that norms can absorb: our bylaws already say members should vote "in the interest of the society," which some read as a duty of sincerity.

Please draft the written recommendation I'll circulate before our next meeting. It should recommend one concrete procedure (or explicitly defend the status quo), explain in terms our non-specialist members can follow why the impossibility result does or doesn't settle the question, engage the strongest version of the argument for whichever option you reject, and be candid about what your recommended rule will still get wrong — including whether the "narrow to two finalists" gambit is a genuine escape from the impossibility or a shell game. Bear in mind that whatever we recommend requires a two-thirds vote at the annual meeting, so the memo needs to be persuasive to members who will never read the theorem, only its consequences.
