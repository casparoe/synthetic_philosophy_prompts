so, background: i'm a retired analytical chemist. 34 years running a trace-contaminants lab, mostly chromatography and mass spec work, and since retiring i've gotten properly into philosophy — reading whatever the library has, following my nose. i've hit the knowing-how / knowing-that literature recently and it's driving me a little crazy, because i keep thinking: none of these people have ever had to train a new hire at a bench. in my world the distinction isn't a puzzle, it's payroll — i paid people for knowing how, and it was a different thing entirely from the ones who merely knew that.

before i go deeper into this literature i want what we'd have called a method validation plan. you never started running samples on a new instrument until you'd written down what counts as the method working: detection limits, spike recoveries, blanks. so write me the spec sheet for a theory of practical knowledge — what an adequate account of knowing-how has to deliver, which cases it can't flub, which distinctions it has to respect. and to be clear about what the theory is FOR: i want one good enough to explain how skills actually get taught, transferred, and lost, not one that's good enough to win a seminar argument.

i'll seed you with the cases that feel load-bearing to me, from years at the bench, but add your own:

- the trainee who can recite the SOP for the extraction step backwards and still loses half the analyte every single run, versus the tech who never read the SOP and nails it — and then, when you ask her what she does differently, gives you an answer that's provably false (we checked on video) but whose coaching works anyway
- the person who can smell when a distillation is about to go wrong, or the old-timer who can eyeball a chromatogram and say "that column's dying" before any metric has moved — skill with no accessible propositional content, and no amount of interrogating the expert retrieves any
- transfer: my knowing how to run the assay on the old instrument, and then the "identical" replacement arrives, and for two weeks i'm suddenly mediocre again. does the theory have an account of what carried over and what didn't, or is that somebody else's problem?
- improvement by practice: after year one i was measurably better at the prep work. did i come to know something new, or did i just change, the way a muscle changes? the theory has to say something here and not wave at "habit"
- the difference between losing the skill and never having the occasion to use it — i haven't run a manual Kjeldahl in 25 years but i'm oddly confident i could

for each requirement on the sheet, give the case or consideration that forces it — no bare assertions of what a theory "must" do. then the part i actually most want: tell me which of the requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which are, on your view, just prejudices of one camp dressed up as demands (i can't tell anymore whether "knowledge must be accountable to reasons" is a real constraint or just the intellectualist's hobbyhorse, and ditto for whatever the anti-intellectualists insist on). flag any subset that might not be jointly satisfiable — if the spec is impossible, that's a finding, and say it. and finally, which single requirement, enforced hard, would knock out the most theories on offer? i suspect it's the false-coaching one — the case where the explanation is wrong but the teaching works — but i want your view, argued, not just agreed with.

no reading list at the end please, i want the spec not a syllabus. a table is fine but the reasoning behind each line matters more than the table. and keep it concrete — if a requirement can be stated in terms of something that could happen in a lab, state it that way. i've read enough philosophy now to know that abstractions are cheap.
