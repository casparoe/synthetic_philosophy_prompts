Please argue that the so-called gender-equality paradox—the finding that occupational segregation by gender tends to be more, not less, pronounced in countries scoring higher on gender-equality indices—is best read neither as evidence about innate sex differences nor as evidence about culture in the usual sense, but as an artifact of what those indices measure. Your argument should rest on a distinction between barrier-removal equality (no formal discrimination, equal legal rights and access) and cost-structure equality (whether the career options actually open to men and women carry the same marginal costs in money, time, and flexibility). Then develop the following consideration, which is not standard in the literature: in affluent egalitarian welfare states, wage compression and high marginal tax rates mean that a household can raise its standard of living only by concentrating, rather than balancing, its labor-market investment—one partner pursuing one of the small number of very high-variance, "greedy" careers while the other absorbs domestic costs—and a household with children will typically assign the absorbing role to the lower-earning partner, which in turn tracks who took leave and worked part-time in the child's earliest years. On this reading, greater occupational segregation can coexist with, and even be partly produced by, greater formal equality. Two constraints: your argument must explain why this cost-structure explanation is not merely another socialization story—say what would distinguish the two conceptually or empirically—and it must confront the strongest objection from the nature side, namely that the pattern is most pronounced in precisely those societies where material constraints are least binding, so that one would expect cost-based mechanisms to be at their weakest rather than their strongest.
