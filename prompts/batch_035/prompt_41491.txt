Before I commit to any of the proposed resolutions of Bertrand's paradox, I want the specification that any such resolution has to meet, stated as a list of desiderata. My sense is that the standard lines of response each quietly fail one requirement or another, and I'd rather see the failure pattern made explicit than watch another round of the usual debate.

The context, so we're agreed on the object: for a circle with an inscribed equilateral triangle, the question "what is the probability that a randomly chosen chord is longer than the triangle's side?" receives, by equally plausible applications of indifference, the answers 1/2, 1/3, and 1/4, depending on how the chord-generating process is parametrized. I don't want the familiar tour of the three constructions; I want the requirements list, with the case or consideration behind each entry, and each marked either non-negotiable or a preference of one camp.

The requirements should at least engage the following, though I'd welcome additions:

First, a resolution must earn its uniqueness rather than stipulate it. The most common move is to say that the problem is ill-posed until the sampling procedure is specified. But the entire dispute lives in the phrase "a chord is chosen at random," so a resolution that simply demands a procedure has to say something further: either what in the original wording, if anything, fixes the procedure, or why nothing does and indifference therefore assigns no probability at all. "Specify the experiment" is not an answer to the paradox; it is a restatement of it, and I want a requirement that marks the difference.

Second, description invariance. Bertrand himself, in the same book, gave a companion example in which two apparently equally valid derivations of the expected side length of a randomly inscribed equilateral triangle yield different numbers. There the difficulty cannot be blamed on an underspecified generating process in the same way, since the object is fixed and only the derivations differ. A resolution that handles the chord problem by pointing at procedures must either extend to this case or say honestly where its method runs out.

Third, coverage of the wine/water paradox, in the form introduced by van Fraassen: given that the ratio of wine to water in a mixture lies between bounds, indifference applied to the ratio rather than to its reciprocal yields inconsistent conclusions about, say, the probability that wine predominates. A resolution owes us a principled account of which parametrization is privileged and why, or a demonstration that none is and the principle yields nothing here. This, I suspect, is the requirement that does the most to exclude candidates, and I'd like your view on whether that suspicion is right.

Fourth, consistency with the mathematics of infinite partitions. There is no uniform probability distribution on the positive integers or on the whole real line, so indifference as ordinarily understood cannot be applied there at all, and even on the reals it delivers only improper priors. A resolution must say what the principle comes to in these settings: whether improper priors are tolerable, whether indifference bifurcates into a finite case with content and an infinite case with none, or something else entirely.

Fifth, preservation of the working parts. Restricted symmetry reasoning underlies a great deal of respectable statistical practice — reference priors in the tradition associated with Jaynes's transformation-group treatment, and the invariance assumptions behind permutation tests. A resolution should not be so austere as to forbid these, or else it owes an explanation of why the practice succeeds when, by its lights, it has no right to.

Please write out the full list with these and any further desiderata you think the literature has missed, each with its motivating case, each labeled non-negotiable or camp preference, and — this is the part I most want — an assessment of whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable. If no resolution can satisfy them together, that fact is itself the result, and I'd want it stated plainly. Then, briefly and without belaboring it, indicate how the main strategies on offer — the dissolve-it-by-ill-posedness line, the symmetry and maximum-entropy approach, and the move to imprecise or sets-of-probabilities treatments — fare against the list, so I can see at a glance where each one breaks.
