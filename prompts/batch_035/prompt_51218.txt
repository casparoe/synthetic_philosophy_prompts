I clerk for a judge on the constitutional chamber of a small fictional country — call it Verania — that just came out of 22 years under an authoritarian government (the "Directorate"). The Directorate fell eight months ago, and we're drowning in cases from its era. The big one in front of our chamber now is a package of ~400 petitions from people who were criminalized under the Directorate's "Public Order and Loyalty Act." Some petitioners were grudge informers' victims: denounced by neighbors, convicted of "social parasitism" or "insulting the dignity of the state," sentences of a few years each. Others were border-shooting victims' families — people shot by guards under a statute that authorized deadly force against anyone crossing the northern frontier without papers. And a third group were hit by provisions of the same act that were harsh but arguably ordinary: expropriation of land from "enemies of the social order" without compensation, that kind of thing. All of it was formally enacted by the Directorate's rubber-stamp parliament, published in the gazette, applied by real courts with real procedures.

Our chamber has to decide, for each class of cases, whether the underlying provisions counted as *valid law* at the time — which determines whether the convictions were wrongful (and compensation follows) versus merely unjust-but-valid (which routes people to a separate clemency track, or nowhere). I've been reading up on the Hart–Fuller debate from the late 1950s and the post-war German grudge-informer mess, and my judge keeps asking me which side we should actually take. The positivist line — that these statutes were valid law, wretched as they were, and the remedy is prospective legislation plus clemency — seems honest but politically radioactive and honestly a bit grotesque for the border shootings. The Radbruch-style line — that sufficiently intolerable injustice just isn't law at all — feels morally right but I worry it's doing everything with one adjective ("intolerable") and gives us no way to explain why the expropriation cases, which are bad but not border-shootings bad, still come out as law. Fuller's procedural stuff about publicity, generality, congruence between official action and declared rule is interesting because some of the Directorate's worst practices (the informer cases especially) involved secret directives contradicting the published statutes — but I can't tell whether that's a real criterion or just a natural-law position wearing procedural clothes.

Draft the memorandum I'll circulate to the other clerks ahead of Friday's conference, where we need to recommend one of three options to the chamber: (a) retroactively void the convictions under the intolerable-injustice doctrine and compensate, (b) declare the provisions valid but legally defective in application, remitting cases for individualized review, or (c) hold them fully valid and route everything to the clemency commission the new parliament is setting up. The memo should recommend one option, give reasons the other judges could actually engage with, and — this matters — treat the strongest argument on the losing side fairly rather than caricaturing it (my judge hates strawmen). Two more things: please keep clearly separated what's genuinely settled among people who know this literature versus what's live controversy — I don't want to hand my judge someone's contested take as if it were consensus — and no citations or reading lists, just positions and arguments, we'll check sources ourselves. End the memo with a machine-readable bottom line, like a final line that says \answer{...} with the option letter and a short phrase. Keep it under about 1200 words and make sure the recommendation actually handles the three classes of cases differently if they deserve different treatment, since a one-size answer will get torn apart in conference.
