I'm a test-infrastructure engineer: I design CI pipelines, set merge-gate thresholds, chase flaky tests, and argue with release managers about error budgets. I'm fluent in probability theory, so marginal versus joint probabilities, conditioning, and dependence structures are all fine to use freely. I have no training in epistemology beyond having once read the encyclopedia entries on the lottery paradox and the preface paradox, and I want those two paradoxes restated in the vocabulary I actually use — and, more importantly, an honest report on what the translation fails to carry.

To fix the targets: in the lottery paradox, we have a fair lottery with a million tickets and exactly one winner. For any single ticket, the probability that it loses is overwhelming, so it seems rational to believe of each ticket, individually, that it will lose; yet it is irrational to believe that every ticket loses, since one must win. In the preface paradox, an author who has carefully checked each claim in her book believes each of them, but also — knowing her own fallibility and the base rate of errors in books of that kind — reasonably believes that the book contains at least one false claim, and says as much in the preface. Both paradoxes threaten the idea that rational belief is closed under conjunction: individually warranted beliefs seem to combine into a warranted belief in a falsehood, or at least into a combination the believer herself disavows.

Translate both into my vocabulary as faithfully as you can. I suspect some of this maps onto things my field already has names for: a test suite where each test passes with high individual probability but the joint probability of the whole suite passing is not high; merge gates that are just thresholds on a computed statistic; flake rates as per-test error probabilities; the difference between tests whose failures are independent and tests that fail together because they share an assumption or a fixture. Where it gets interesting to me is the preface case: I will happily sign off a release while telling the release manager that there is almost certainly a bug somewhere in it, and no one thinks this is incoherent — my sign-off is a decision under a threshold, not an assertion of the conjunction of all test results. So do the paradoxes even get a grip on the threshold-gate picture of confidence?

Then give me the residue report. What translates cleanly, what survives only with distortion (e.g., if you treat "believes that p" as "P(p) is above the gate's threshold," say so and say what that distorts), and what has no expression in my vocabulary at all. I'm particularly interested in whether the residue is mostly the normative notion of a belief's being *warranted* — as opposed to merely probable — together with the idea that some threshold-crossing state *ought* to be closed under conjunction. My vocabulary has thresholds and it has joint distributions, but it has no operator that takes a set of individually accepted claims and says which conjunctions I am rationally committed to. Is that a defect of my vocabulary, or does it show that the philosophers' closure principle presupposes an ambiguity — between a rule for action (ship when the joint pass probability clears the gate) and a normative notion of justified assertion — that the gate-and-threshold picture has already disambiguated, deliberately?

One more thing I want your view on, if it survives the translation: the two paradoxes are usually treated as a pair, but their structures differ. The lottery's "exactly one ticket wins" is a logical partition among mutually exclusive and exhaustive alternatives, while the preface's "there is at least one error" is an empirical expectation drawn from a base rate. Does that asymmetry show up in my terms — say, as the difference between tests whose outcomes are jointly constrained by a tautology and tests whose failures are merely statistically dependent through a shared fixture — or does the translation flatten the two cases into the same thing? If it flattens them, I want to know what my vocabulary lost.
