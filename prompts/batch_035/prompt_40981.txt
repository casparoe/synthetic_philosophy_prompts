Since the mid-2000s a bunch of big companies -- Google famously, but also Intel and various others -- have run internal prediction markets where employees bet play money (or real prizes) on things like whether a product will ship on time or how many units it'll sell. Managment isn't bound by the price but treats it as the best available forecast, sometimes against the loud objections of the project's own manager. And the futarchy proposal people argue about online is basically this scaled up to whole governments: vote on values, bet on beliefs. I keep wondering what this setup actually presupposes philosophically. Like, does treating a market price as "the firm's belief" presuppose a particular view of what a belief even is -- something you can aggregate out of many agents rather than a thing an individual or a committee holds? Does it presuppose that expertise is distributed rather than concentrated, and if the market keeps beating the named expert, what does that commit you to about the epistmology of expertise generally? I'm also puzzled by an apparent inconsistency: firms happily run these markets for forecasting but almost never for actual decisions. They'll ask the market "will this ship by June" but not "should we ship by June," even though the futarchy crowd insists you need conditional bets on the consequences of each option before the market is doing anything decision-relevant. Is the corporate version quietly presupposing something different about knowledge than the futarchist version, or are the companies just being squeamish about letting traders overrule managers? So what I'd like is a reconstruction of the implicit epistemology here: what the practice strictly presupposes vs. what it merely sits comfortably with, which rival views of expertise or group knowledge it rules out (does it rule out the idea that managerial judgement is a distinct source of knowledge, or can you be a market enthusiast and still believe that?), and where the practice is underdetermined enough that several theories of belief aggregation would license it. Also curious whether the play-money detail matters -- if employees have nothing real at stake, is the firm presupposing that sincere information disclosure is what matters, not willingness to put your money where your mouth is? Because that seems in tension with the whole futarchist bet-or-shut-up spirit.
