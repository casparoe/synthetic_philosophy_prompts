Science-ideas journalist here, the one with the monthly column on probability puzzles, and my editor finally greenlit one I've been pitching forever: the Judy Benjamin problem. Walk me through it, because I want to get the sidebar right, not just repeat the wikipedia version.

The setup as I'll tell it: Judy, a soldier, is dropped by parachute at night into territory divided between a Red army and a Blue army. She starts out roughly 50/50 on which side's territory she's in, and each side's territory has a Red-held part and a Blue-held part. HQ radios: we can't tell where you landed, but if you're in Blue territory, the odds are 3:1 you're in the Red-held part of it rather than the Blue-held part. That's her only information. The puzzle is what her new probabilities should be, and the fun part is that ordinary Bayesian conditionalization is useless here — she didn't learn any plain proposition, she learned a conditional, and its antecedent might well be false.

Please write me the ~600 word sidebar, for readers who know what a conditional probability is but haven't seen this puzzle, covering:

1. why conditioning literally has nothing to grab onto — there's no event E she's learned to condition on;
2. what look like the natural "admissibility" constraints on any sensible answer (the odds inside Blue territory should now be 3:1, and arguably the message shouldn't change her probability of being in Blue territory at all, since it only said what to believe *if* she's there);
3. the punchline: van Fraassen argued that minimizing relative information (cross-entropy) subject to those constraints gives an answer that seems to drag her probability toward one territory even though the message never said anything about which territory she's in, and later work by Grove and Halpern suggested the problem may just be underdetermined — the constraints don't pick out a unique update. Give the reader the intuition for why "change your beliefs as little as the constraints allow" is doing more work than it seems to, and why "the constraints don't pin down a unique answer" is even possible when they feel so obvious;
4. if the standard version of the problem has specific posterior numbers from the min-info solution, include them and sketch the arithmetic so readers can check it — and say plainly whether the answer looks right or wrong, since that's the hook of the column.

Write it like a magazine sidebar, not a lecture — no equations beyond maybe one line, and no reading lists, just the ideas. Use American English.
