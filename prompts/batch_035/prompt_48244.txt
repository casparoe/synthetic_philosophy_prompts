Write an essay on whether consequentialist cluelessness is actually a problem for consequentialism, or just for consequentialists. Here's what I mean: James Lenman argued that we're basically clueless about the remote effects of our actions — the butterfly-effect stuff, how the cashier line you pick ripples out through history, changes who meets who, which future people exist, etc. So the actual full consequences of anything we do are unknowable in principle. Some people take this to show that act consequentialism collapses as a guide to action. Others say it's just decision-making under uncertainty, which everyone faces, and expected value reasoning handles it fine (or as well as anything can).

Your essay should engage with the fact that cluelessness comes in at least two flavors worth distinguishing: (a) the unknowable ripple effects of tiny casual interventions, and (b) cases where we know an action has large effects but can't compare the value of the outcomes at all — like, the intervention saves lives but you can't say whether that's net good once you look at second-order effects. It's not obvious these should get the same treatment, and honestly part of your job is to figure out whether the distinction matters.

Then I want you to tackle a specific escape route: the move where you say fine, we're clueless about ripple effects, but some interventions are way more "robustly good" than others (the standard example being global health interventions that reliably save lives now), so we can rank actions by how good they are under the range of scenarios we can actually evaluate. Question: does this move really escape cluelessness, or does it just relocate it? Think about what happens when you combine cluelessness with the possibility of tiny probabilities of astronomically huge effects — if every ordinary action has some minuscule chance of triggering a catastrophe or preventing one, does "robustness" even make sense as a tiebreaker?

Don't end on some wishy-washy "more work needs to be done" note. Reach a real verdict and defend it — even if it's something like "cluelessness refutes consequentialism as a decision procedure but not as a criterion of rightness," or the opposite, or something weirder, as long as you actually argue for it. You can take a position on whether the distinction between criterion of rightness and decision procedure is doing real work here or is a cop-out. That's fair game. Just be decisive.
