Domain: individual contributions to collective harms and the inefficacy objection. Type: desiderata.

I keep having the same argument about whether my eating chicken, or flying to a conference, or not voting, actually makes me responsible for anything, given that each of those acts by itself makes literally no difference to any outcome — one burger doesn't change how many animals get raised, one flight doesn't change the climate, one vote almost never decides anything. And I've realised I can't even evaluate the answers people give me (you're still participating in something bad! do your fair share! expected value!) because I don't know what a good answer to this is even supposed to look like. So before I read anything else on it: write me the full spec — the requirements any adequate theory of individual responsibility for contributions to collective harms has to meet, with the case or consideration behind each.

Cases the theory has to at minimum handle, so you know what the requirements are requirements *for*:

1. Parfit's harmless torturers — a thousand people each turn a dial that makes the victim's pain imperceptibly worse, and the total effect is indistinguishable from straightforward torture, but no single torturer makes any perceptible difference.
2. The chicken step case — producers don't respond smoothly to demand, they respond in steps: buying one fewer chicken does nothing until enough people abstain to drop production past the point where some barn doesn't get refilled, or some truck doesn't get dispatched. So individual contributions are individually no-ops but collectively decisive in lumpy, unpredictable ways.
3. Voting — chance of being pivotal is tiny, payoff if pivotal is huge.
4. The compensator case — if I abstain from the harmful thing, someone else takes my place, so my abstention does no good and my participation does no harm. (Does the theory have to say the compensator and I are equally responsible, or can it avoid that?)
5. The bystander asymmetry — someone who never contributed at all vs. someone who contributed but made no difference. If the theory says the contributor did nothing wrong, it seems stuck saying they're no different from the bystander, which feels off.

Requirements to consider — but argue with me about which of these are genuine and which are just one camp's prejudices smuggled into the spec: the verdicts can't flip depending on whether the harm scales smoothly or in steps; the theory can't derive verdicts from the axiom "participating in a wrongful practice is itself wrong" (that's the thing to be shown, not assumed); it must give different or at least distinguishable verdicts for contributions that are imperceptible vs. contributions that make no difference at all (the torturers' dials vs. the chicken steps — are these even the same case?); it must say whether the wrongness attaches to my act, my disposition, or my participation in a practice, and defend the choice; it must not be so demanding that it entails every spare dollar goes to the most efficient reduction of collective harm; it must be usable by an ordinary agent who cannot solve the supply-chain game theory before dinner; and it must respect the difference between causing harm and merely benefitting from a system that causes harm, without collapsing them.

Sections, in this order and rough lengths:

- "What the theory is for" (~120 words) — fix the purpose, i.e. what this theory is supposed to be adequate *for*, since I suspect adequacy here is relative to what we want it to do.
- "The requirements" — the bulk of it, numbered, each with the case behind it and a verdict on negotiable vs. not.
- "Where the list fights itself" (~200 words) — I have a suspicion you can't jointly require that verdicts track actual difference-making AND that the theory correctly condemns the torturer and the chicken-eater, since in both cases there's no difference to track. Say whether that's right or whether I've set it up wrong.
- "The knockout requirement" (~100 words) — which single requirement excludes the most popular theories out there, and why.
- "Quick scorecard" (~200 words) — just three contenders: a pure difference-making/causal view, an expected-value view, and a fair-share view. Keep it brief, the requirements are the interesting part, I'll argue the scorecard with you later anyway.
