In my philosophy reading group we're making our way through Lewis's On the Plurality of Worlds a bit at a time. The guy who leads it has this line he trots out whenever a certain kind of question comes up: the actual world is just one possible world among others, and 'actual' works like 'here' and 'now' — it's indexical, so nothing makes our world special, any more than my city is special by being the one I'm in. Last meeting he used it to shut down two completely different things, and I want to know whether the same reading of that line can really do both jobs.

First shutdown, which I was fine with: someone asked what makes our world the *real* one, and he said that's like asking what makes my city the *here* one. Sure, OK.

Second shutdown, which bugged me: I brought up the fine-tuning thing — that it seems wierd the constants happen to fall in the range that permits life, and that this feels like something it's legitimate to be surprised about. He said the indexical point dissolves that too: in worlds with no observers nobody's around to notice, every world's inhabitants can call their world actual, so ours isn't special and there's nothing to be surprised about. Something felt sneaky in that move but I couldn't put my finger on it.

So what I want: lay out the distinct precise theses that could be packed into his one-liner — the cheap semantic one about the word 'actual', the full-on Lewisian one about the other worlds being just as real as ours, plus whatever else might be hiding in there (maybe something about 'real' not coming in degrees?). For each, say whether it's plausible and, more importantly, whether it supports the no-surprise move he made. My suspicion is the indexical bit gets him the first shutdown basically for free but does no work at all in the second one, and that the second is really a selection-effect claim — observers only ever find themselves in observer-permitting worlds — wearing the semantics as a costume. And if that's right, the selection-effect claim seems to not need possible worlds to be *real* in any sense whatsoever, so is he just dragging the ontology around for no reason? Please lay the readings out as a list I can bring to the next meeting.
