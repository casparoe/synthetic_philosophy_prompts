Two members of a policy reading group I sit in on, Dana and Marcus, spent most of the spring arguing about the international country happiness rankings, and their exchange got tangled enough that I genuinely can't referee it myself. The dispute had a measurement half and a normative half, and they kept sliding between the two.

(1) Dana opened by defending the rankings as the most honest measure of national wellbeing we have: rather than experts counting proxies, millions of respondents place their own lives on a scale from the worst possible life to the best possible life they can imagine, and the countries that come out on top year after year are exactly the ones with the institutions, security, and trust we'd independently expect to do well.

(2) Marcus answered that the whole instrument is mislabeled. The question captures a cognitive judgment — how your life compares to your own private conception of the best life possible — and that conception travels with culture: a society that socializes modest expectations will grade an ordinary life an eight, while an aspirational society may grade a materially better life a six, so cross-country comparisons partly rank national temperament rather than experienced quality of life. He added that the report's own supplementary data on daily emotions doesn't track the headline scores — some countries rate high on positive daily feeling but middling on the ladder, and others the reverse — which he took to show that whatever is being measured, it isn't anything unified called happiness.

(3) Dana replied that if the measure were mostly temperament, it wouldn't track objective conditions the way it does — income up to a point, low corruption, employment — and that the divergence between momentary mood and life evaluation is a familiar feature of the psychology of wellbeing, not a defect of the latter; arguably governments should care about life evaluation, not day-to-day affect, which is not what policy is for. She also noted that you can't dismiss a measure for correlating with income and institutional quality, since nearly everything good correlates with those.

(4) Marcus said that was precisely his point: if the ladder is carried mostly by income and institutional trust, it is an expensive opinion poll restating what national accounts already tell us, and its specifically hedonic content — felt experience — is exactly where it is weakest. He then turned to the normative question: even granting the measure were sound, topping a ranking is a pernicious target. A ranking is comparative, so if every country improved, half would still be below average; and aiming at a self-report invites the usual gaming — campaigns that coach the answer, migration policy that discourages the dissatisfied, national wellbeing branding of the sort that has already produced happiness ministries. Fix the conditions, he said, and let the number fall where it may.

(5) Dana answered that every policy metric can be gamed, GDP most obviously, and yet we retain them with safeguards; abandoning aggregate measurement altogether just leaves wellbeing invisible to ministries that will optimize whatever they can count. She also argued that anchoring each respondent to their own standard of the best possible life is a feature, not a bug: respecting people's wellbeing means asking them by their own lights, and substituting an external standard of what ought to satisfy them is paternalism in measurement's clothing.

(6) Marcus closed by saying that a per-respondent standard may enrich each individual self-report but empties the cross-country average of meaning, since you cannot average quantities each defined by a different zero and a different unit; and that Dana's own appeal to people's own standards cuts against using the result as a target, since standards are themselves formed by the very conditions under evaluation — the measure ends up partly certifying its own causes.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: for each of the six steps, say which specific claims are right, which are mistaken, and where someone is answering a stronger or weaker version of what was said. Then address the normative question on its own terms: is there any defensible sense in which a country should try to top such a ranking, as opposed to treating it as an output to be explained rather than aimed at? Finally, tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing that would restructure the whole disagreement.
