I am a philosopher of science who has long held a strong version of the multiple-discovery thesis: once the concepts, instruments, and open problems that make a discovery thinkable are in circulation in a community, the discovery itself is effectively inevitable, and who happens to make it first is of biographical but not philosophical interest. The familiar doubles and triples — calculus, natural selection, Neptune — strike me as evidence rather than coincidence. Tell me what would count against this view. I want more than the standard worry that we remember the multiples and forget the singletons: tell me how serious the base-rate problem really is, and whether my notion of the prerequisites "being in circulation" can be specified independently of the discovery that in fact eventuated — I suspect I have defined "ready to be discovered" so that it comes out true whenever a discovery occurs, and I want to know whether that qualification has rendered the view unfalsifiable, and whether unfalsifiability would be disqualifying for a thesis of this kind. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, including the cases an inevitabilist should concede: delayed discoveries such as Mendel's, long stretches in which the prerequisites were arguably in place and nothing happened, and instances where the "same" discovery made by different people would have differed in content that mattered. Indicate which of these a committed defender of the view would dismiss rather than concede, and be honest about whether what remains, once the qualifications are in, is a substantive historical thesis or a tautology dressed up as history.
