I sit on the committee of a national anti-doping agency that grants therapeutic use exemptions. The rulebook prohibits a list of substances for every athlete; the only door back in is a medical diagnosis. If a physician certifies a genuine condition — say, exercise-induced asthma — and the drug is administered at the standard therapeutic dose, we grant the exemption; the same drug, same dose, in an undiagnosed athlete is a doping violation. I've begun to doubt this rests on any coherent version of the therapy/enhancement distinction, and I want to know what our practice actually presupposes.

Two features of our cases worry me. First, the substance's physiological effect is the same whichever athlete takes it; all that differs is that a diagnosis intervenes. That suggests we define therapy as restoring the athlete to her own baseline rather than by reference to the substance or its effect — yet the same rulebook bans the substance in healthy athletes, which suggests we also assume a baseline-independent, species-typical level of function that any use of the drug would exceed. Second, the diagnosed condition is often produced by the sport itself: asthma is several times more common among elite endurance athletes than in the general population, and much of it is thought to be airway damage caused by years of heavy training. So "restoring the athlete to health" can mean restoring her to a baseline the sport itself destroyed and that her healthy rivals never had. It begins to look as though the diagnosis functions mainly as a license to enhance, and the implicit theory is just "a doctor was involved."

Please reconstruct the theory of the therapy/enhancement distinction our practice presupposes, if any; state it as precisely as you can; and say whether it survives the two tensions above. Then tell me whether the practice is underdetermined — whether rival views of therapy (restoration of individual normal functioning, restoration of species-typical functioning, mere medical authorization) would all license the same rulings — and what would have to change in our practice if we adopted a strict individual-baseline rule instead. Keep your answer under 500 words.
