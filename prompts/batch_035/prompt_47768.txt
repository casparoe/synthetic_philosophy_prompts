i'm a grad student TA-ing intro stats this semester while also sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group, and simpson's paradox has basically become my whole personality. after a long argument with a friend (she's the causal inference person, i'm more of a decision theory person) i finally wrote down what i actually believe about it, and she says i'm trying to have it both wayss but she couldn't pin down exactly where. here's the list:

(a) simpson's reversal is a trivial arithmetic fact about weighted averages — small groups with different sizes, that's the whole story — and it carries no deep lesson for statistics or epistemology. people who write papers about what it teaches us regarding causation are overreaching.
(b) in the kidney stone case (treatment A beats B on small stones roughly 93% to 87% and on large stones roughly 73% to 69%, yet loses overall roughly 78% to 83%) the disaggregated data are clearly what you should use to pick a treatment. and in the berkeley-style admissions case, the department-by-department data give the right verdict, namely that there's no bias. general rule: when in doubt, dissagregate.
(c) pearl is basically right that whether to use the aggregated or the disaggregated numbers is settled by causal structure — you condition on the partitioning variable when it's a confounder of treatment and outcome, and you don't when it isn't.
(d) i'm committed to total evidence: rational belief and decision should condition on everything you know, and finer-grained data is never worse than coarser-grained data.
(e) i think simpson's reversal shows that association is partition-relative — there's no fact of the matter about "the" correlation between two variables until you've picked a population or partition.
(f) the sure-thing principle is a genuine requirement of rationality: if you prefer A to B in every state, you must prefer A overall. so rational preferences, unlike observed frequencies, can never be reversed by aggregation.

my friend's actual complaint was about (f). she says i can't hold (f) alongside (e) and (b), because if associations flip under aggregation then the preferences built on them should flip too, and that's supposed to be a contradiction. i don't think it is but i cant quite articulate why. seperately, her advisor apparently claims (b) and (c) can't both be right — something about how in the berkeley case the aggregated data are the ones you want if your question is the total effect of sex on admission (since sex influences which department you apply to), and conditioning on department "explains away" the very thing you were asking about. that struck me as sophistry but i want to be sure before i dismiss it at the reading group.
