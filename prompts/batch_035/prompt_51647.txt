Retirement dinner on Friday, and I've been dragooned into giving the toast. The retiree is Dvora K—, forty years in our department, and a thoroughgoing Tolstoyan about art — as far as she was concerned, What Is Art? was the last word on the subject, full stop. Her creed, delivered many times at the coffee machine to anyone who'd stand still: a work succeeds only if it sincerely hands on a feeling the maker actually had, and it fails in four characteristic ways — by borrowing from other art, by imitating reality for its own sake, by showing off technique, and by just entertaining people. She enforced this on after-dinner speeches the way other people enforce parking. Once told a very famous visitor, to his face, that his remarks were counterfeit art. He laughed; she wasn't joking.

So here's my problem: the toast is itself a small work of verbal art, and she will be sitting ten feet away, judging it by exactly those standards. Every draft I produce sounds like the inside of a dust jacket, and I've come to realize the standard retirement toast is basically mode two from her list — imitation for its own sake: born in such and such a year, degrees, posts, a gag about the office, done. Its a genre built out of the very counterfeits she spent four decades diagnosing.

What I need from you:

1. A toast of about four minutes, in plain words, that transmits a real feeling about her rather than admiration for my phrasing. No borrowed literary ornament, no technique on display.

2. I want one laugh in it. On her own theory, is a laugh even permissible — or is getting one precisely the entertainment-counterfeit she warned about? If permissible, on what terms? My candidate bit is her old line that the two sincere sentences in philosophy are I don't know and you go first. Rule on whether that passes.

3. The room is mixed: family who've never read a word of Tolstoy, half the department, and two artists she famously wounded with this creed of hers, who will be listening to see whether I whitewash it. Don't whitewash it — she'd sooner forgive a bad joke than that.

4. For the concrete feeling at the center, the one story I keep circling back to: at a rival's retirement party years ago — a man she'd feuded with in print for thirty years — she cried in the middle of her own toast, and afterwards told me the feeling was real, so out it came. Build around that if it helps.

And definately tell me plainly if the whole project is incoherent — whether a speech composed to be sincere by somebody else's criteria can even be sincere. She'd want that question asked, not dodged.
