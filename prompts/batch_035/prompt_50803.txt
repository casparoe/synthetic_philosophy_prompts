I'm a high school junior enrolled in an elective called Paradoxes and Proof, and our final assignment is to write a philosophical dialogue in which two serious, evenly matched positions argue about a real problem — the rubric explicitly penalizes strawmen, so neither character can be the dumb one who exists to lose. My topic is the sorites paradox, and I'd like the two positions to be an epistemicist and a supervaluationist about vagueness. I've chosen these because they seem to disagree about something genuinely deep — whether vague predicates have sharp boundaries that we simply cannot know, or whether there simply are no boundaries to know — rather than about wording.

Setting: the two of them are standing over an actual pile of sand on a lab bench, perhaps a hundred thousand grains, and they remove one grain at a time. A third character, a skeptical lab assistant, runs a forced march: after every single grain removed, she asks "Is it a heap now?" and writes down the answer. The dialogue should be built around the following commitments and exchanges:

1. The epistemicist answers every question in the forced march, all the way down, with a flat "yes" or "no" — and he must own the consequences, including the claim that somewhere in the middle there was a last grain whose removal ended the heap, and that no amount of investigation could ever tell us which grain it was.

2. The supervaluationist refuses to play the same way: for the clear cases she says "yes" or "no" confidently, and for the borderline stretch she says the question is indeterminate — and she should be made to explain, in her own words, what she means: that a statement about a borderline number of grains is true only if it comes out true no matter how you make "heap" precise, false only if it comes out false on every precisification, and neither otherwise.

3. The epistemicist gets to make his sharpest point against her: that her view still contains a boundary — somewhere a state went from being one where "that's a heap" was definitely true to one where it wasn't definitely true, so she has only moved the sharp line one level up rather than eliminating it. She should have a real reply, not a shrug — presumably something about the difference between a line in the world and a line in our decision to stop using a word, and about keeping classical logic, including the law of excluded middle, while giving up bivalence for borderline statements.

4. The epistemicist should press on the explanatory emptiness of his rival's "no fact of the matter": the lab assistant, he'll say, was still writing something down after every grain; what fact made "yes" the wrong thing to write in the borderline stretch? And the supervaluationist should press back on his unknowable cutoff: a boundary that no possible evidence could ever locate, that changes with how the grains were counted, and that does no work in explaining anything about how we actually use the word.

5. At some point I want them to locate exactly where they have stopped disagreeing about facts and are disagreeing about whether the phrase "there is a fact of the matter" has any content beyond the facts they've both already conceded. Ideally one of them concedes something genuine — for instance, the epistemicist admitting that his position makes "heap" unlike almost every other predicate in the language, or the supervaluationist admitting that her apparatus gives no guidance about borderline cases to anyone who actually has to decide one.

6. Here is my own contribution, which I'd like you to work in as a twist rather than a gag: about a third of the way through, the lab assistant proposes a house rule to break the deadlock — "let's count it as a heap whenever every ordinary person, shown the pile in ordinary circumstances, would still call it one" — and demands they evaluate it. Both characters should take it seriously and reach different verdicts: the supervaluationist should recognize it as a precisification, but should be forced to admit that it doesn't come from nowhere, since "every ordinary person would call it one" is itself vague, so the rule doesn't resolve the indeterminacy so much as restate it at one remove; the epistemicist should argue that the rule at least yields sharp results in practice and that this is some evidence that ordinary speakers are implicitly more comfortable with boundaries than the supervaluationist claims — while privately disliking the rule because the boundary it draws is defined by what people would say, not by how many grains there are. The lab assistant should notice that her rule has been devoured rather than answered.

Requirements on the writing itself: both characters must respond to each other's actual arguments — no alternating monologues; each character's best point should be stated so well that a reader can't tell which side the author favors; the tone can be dry and a little wry, but the logic must be airtight; and it should end somewhere honest rather than with a tidy reconciliation — perhaps with them agreeing on the last clear case and disagreeing about whether that's where the interesting part begins. Aim for roughly nine hundred to twelve hundred words. Please do not insert citations, footnotes, or references to named articles or books — my teacher wants the positions to stand on their arguments, and I'll present it without a bibliography.
