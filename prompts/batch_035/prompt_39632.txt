A popular history of women in philosophy that I picked up recently devotes a chapter to Émilie du Châtelet, and it sums up her Discours sur le bonheur roughly as follows: du Châtelet published the essay in 1746, and it circulated to great acclaim in the Parisian salons. Its doctrine, as the book presents it, is that happiness is entirely our own making — we are the masters of our fate, and external circumstances count for almost nothing. To be happy, she says, we must free ourselves of all prejudice, look after our health, and acquire passions and tastes, though, in properly Stoic fashion, we must keep the passions firmly under the command of reason, an unruly passion being a disease of the soul. Most distinctively, she holds that illusions are essential to happiness: we ought to deceive ourselves about our talents, our loved ones, and our prospects, because seeing the world as it really is would be unbearable. The chapter adds the biographical note that gambling, love, and study were her own three ruling passions, and that she recommended gambling above all — nothing, in her view, secures contentment in old age like the gaming table. It closes by arguing that the essay refutes itself: a rational argument that concludes reason ought to serve self-deception is self-undermining, so the Discours is best read not as philosophy but as a memoir of a brilliant woman's moods, a proto-Romantic document rather than a genuine work of the Enlightenment.

I found the chapter persuasive, but a friend who knows the period insists it is careless. Could you go claim by claim and tell me which parts a scholar of du Châtelet would accept as accurate, which are oversimplifications a defender of the essay would want qualified, and which are flatly wrong — in particular the dating and circulation story, the reading of her "illusions," the Stoic picture of reason commanding the passions, and what she actually says about gambling? And separately: does the book's self-refutation conclusion follow from anything correct in its summary, or does it collapse once the misreadings are corrected? Please write your answer in American English.
