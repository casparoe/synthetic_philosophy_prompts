I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or academic game theory; my exposure to the subject comes from implementing things, not from reading proofs. As a side project I maintain a small library that analyzes normal-form games, and last quarter I added a pruning pass that iteratively deletes strictly dominated strategies. The pure-strategy version felt unobjectionable: if one pure strategy yields less than another pure strategy against every one of the opponent's strategies, no player who understands their own payoffs would ever choose it, everyone can see that, and deleting it is just bookkeeping. But the mixed-strategy version has started to bother me. When I let a strategy count as dominated if some probability mixture over the player's *other* strategies beats it against every opponent strategy, the pruning becomes dramatically more aggressive: several of my 3x3 test games, in which no pure strategy is dominated by any pure strategy and nothing at all seems pruneable at first glance, collapse all the way to a single surviving cell after a couple of rounds of mixed elimination. I'm also told that, for strict dominance, the order of elimination never changes the final result — which surprised me and made the whole procedure feel more robust than I expected, at least until the mixture issue resurfaced. The standard gloss I've encountered is that iterated deletion of mixed-dominated strategies is nothing more than common knowledge of rationality applied step by step — prune the irrational, recompute against the smaller game, repeat — and is therefore exactly as harmless as the pure version.

I want to be unpersuaded of that gloss, and I'd like you to argue me there. Specifically, please argue against the claim that iterated elimination of strategies dominated by mixed strategies is merely common knowledge of rationality in action, and hence against treating it as a rationality-based pruning rule in the same sense as its pure-strategy cousin. Two seed worries you should engage with rather than merely repeat. First, the aggressive-collapse observation above: a rule that deletes almost nothing when restricted to pure strategies but deletes nearly everything once mixtures are allowed seems to be doing far more than bookkeeping — my suspicion is that mixture-based comparisons quietly import something like equilibrium-flavored reasoning (an expectation over how strategies will be exercised) under the label of individual rationality. Second, and this is where I get shaky: I can see in a completely direct way why a player should never choose a pure strategy that some other pure strategy always beats. I cannot see in the same direct way why such a player is *required to be willing to randomize* over alternatives so that a mixture can serve as the dominating witness; "there exists a mixture that beats A against everything" feels like a different kind of fact about A than "B beats A against everything," and if the underlying rationality requirement does not obviously license deliberate randomization, then the iteration built on top of it loses its supposed foundation.

Please structure your answer in four sections, in this order. Section 1, "Position stated plainly": in at most four sentences, state exactly what you will argue, including whether you accept my proposed asymmetry between the pure and mixed versions or think both rest on the same footing. Section 2, "The argument": roughly five hundred words, organized as numbered steps, making the case that mixed-strategy dominance elimination is not justified by common knowledge of rationality alone; somewhere in this section, construct one small concrete game (payoff matrices of your own choosing) that illustrates the gap between the two versions. Section 3, "Strongest objection and reply": around two hundred words presenting the best defense of the standard gloss — for instance, that expected-utility maximization already commits a player to being indifferent over available mixtures, or that dominance by a mixture follows from pairwise comparisons plus coherence — followed by your reply to it. Section 4, "Bottom line": at most one hundred words on whether my solver's mixed-elimination pass should be presented to users as a rationality-based reduction, a purely computational heuristic, or something in between, and why.
