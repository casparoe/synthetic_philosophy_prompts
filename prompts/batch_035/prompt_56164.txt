I've spent enough time on Gödel's modal ontological argument to know that the live question isn't formal validity — take the derivations as correct — but whether it can be a good argument, and before I look at any more emended versions I want the standards written down first. The proof defines a Godlike being as one having all positive properties, moves from axioms about positivity to the possibility of such a being, and then, since necessary existence is itself taken to be positive, to a Godlike being's necessary existence; and it has since been shown that the original axioms also entail that whatever is true is necessarily true, which nearly everyone agrees is too high a price. Distill the demands on any argument of this shape into exactly five desiderata, judged for the purpose of persuading someone who is not already a theist. The pressures I'm aware of, which you should evaluate rather than merely transcribe: it must yield a unique maximally great being and not just some necessary existent, since abstract objects like the number 7 exist necessarily without being God; it must survive the mirror argument, running on negative properties toward a necessarily existent devil; it must not trivialize the modal logic it is couched in, as the original did; it must earn the possibility premise without smuggling a miniature ontological argument into the justification of that premise; and it must say whether Leibniz's insistence that God exist per se necessarily — necessary in itself, not merely entailed by something else that happens to be necessary — is a genuine requirement or a theological prejudice. Each of the five items should be a sentence or two: the requirement, the case behind it, and a clause on whether Gödel's original or a standard emendation satisfies it. Give the answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, no introduction or conclusion, in American English.
