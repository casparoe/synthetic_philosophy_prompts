The exam question was: "Explain, using an example of your own construction, what the bootstrapping problem in epistemology is, and why knowledge allegedly gained this way has been called 'easy' knowledge. Then explain why the problem has been thought to be especially pressing for epistemic internalists, and sketch one line of response to it."

The student's answer reads as follows: "Suppose I own a rain gauge and want to know whether it is reliable. Over many weeks, I compare its readings with my own sense impressions of how heavy the rain is, and I find that whenever the gauge reads a certain amount, my impressions agree. On this basis I conclude that the gauge is reliable. Each individual step seems fine: each premise is just a record of what the gauge said and how the rain seemed to me, and the conclusion — that the gauge is reliable — is not among the premises, so the argument is not formally circular. Yet the conclusion feels unearned, because my impressions of rainfall are exactly the sort of thing the gauge is supposed to be a check on. This pattern is what epistemologists call epistemic circularity, and the knowledge it promises is 'easy' because it comes too cheaply: one purports to establish the reliability of a source by riding on that very source. In truth, though, there is nothing new here: the bootstrapping problem is simply Descartes' circle in modern dress. Descartes could validate clear and distinct perception only by using clear and distinct perceptions, and the bootstrapper can validate the gauge only by using the capacities in question, so what recent writers describe as a discovery is really the same circle, applied to inductive instead of deductive arguments. And since the argument, though not formally circular, still begs the question in the strict sense — the conclusion is smuggled into the premises — any theory that licenses it is in trouble. The problem is especially acute for internalists, because internalism holds that a belief is justified only if the subject can tell, from the inside, that the faculties producing it are reliable; since we can never establish that from the inside without the circle, internalists are committed to skepticism unless they accept the bootstrapping inference, which is question-begging. The most promising reply is the one associated with Crispin Wright's notion of entitlement: on this view the bootstrapping inference is perfectly cogent, and we acquire the reliability conclusion the easy way without needing evidence for it, because the premises are hinge certainties we are entitled to rather than warranted in believing."
