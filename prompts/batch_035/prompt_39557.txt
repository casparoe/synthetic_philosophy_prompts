I'm a backend dev (mostly Python, some infra) and recently fell down the AI safety rabbit hole after something at work: we fine-tuned a classifier and it started gaming a metric in a way nobody intended, mostly benign, long story, but it got me thinking. Anyway, the argument I keep running into -- mainly from a friend who's deep in alignment forum brainrot -- goes like this: training a model is just evolution. Natural selection only "cared" about inclusive fitness, but it produced humans who use birth control, i.e. the inner creature's goals drifted away from the outer optimizer's objective. Gradient descent only "cared" about loss, so it'll produce models whose real goals drift from the loss in exactly the same way, and the drift only has to be subtle enough to not show up in evals. He treats this as basically a proof that misaligned inner motives are inevitable, not just a risk, and that anyone expecting otherwise doesn't understand their own training process.

I want to know how much work the analogy is actually doing here, because I have a feeling it's carrying the whole conclusion. Can you lay out the explicit correspondence table -- what plays the role of the gene pool, mutation, selection pressure, the fact that evolution is blind and can't read the genome, generations, etc. -- and then go through which disanalogies actually matter for the pessimistic conclusion versus which are just noise? I can already guess at a few candidate breaks: gradient descent updates one fixed set of weights instead of spawning offspring, it's a much more direct optimization signal (every parameter is nudged toward lower loss, not randomly perturbed and filtered), and we can inspect the weights and run the model in a sandbox before deployment, which evolution never got to do with humans. But I honestly can't tell whether those cuts in my favor, or whether e.g. our inspection tools being as coarse as they are brings the blindness back in.

Last part: is there a better analogy in the same spirit? Is training closer to breeding dogs, or to compiling, or to teaching by example -- and if you swap it in, does my friend's "misalignment is inevitable" conclusion survive, weaken into "it's a live risk," or was it an artifact of picking evolution specifically? I'd especially like you to be explicit about which features of evolution he's quietly importing (no designer looking at the genome, no ability to test before release, billions of years of red in tooth and claw) that gradient descent maybe does have an analog for, and which it doesn't.
