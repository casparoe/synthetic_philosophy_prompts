my two friends Priya and Marcus got into it at dinner last weekend about whether it's a good thing to bring happy people into existence, and i mostly just watched. it went back and forth enough that i lost the thread, so i'm writing it down and hoping for a verdict.

(1) Priya said she's convinced by the procreation asymmetry: it's clearly wrong to create someone you know will have a miserable life, but there's clearly no duty to create someone who'd have a wonderful one — and she takes this to show that adding suffering makes the world worse but adding happiness doesn't make it better, full stop.

(2) Marcus said that's just status quo bias dressed up in philosophy clothes. whether one world is better than another can't depend on which particular people happen to exist in which. compare a world with nobody in it to a world just like ours except for one extra happy person — the second has more wellbeing in it, and he doesn't see how adding wellbeing to the total fails to improve things. treating the empty world as a neutral baseline rather than a very bad one (think of all that absent happiness!) seemed to him like rigging the question.

(3) Priya replied that it's like medicine: doctors have a duty not to harm patients and no duty to hand out benefits to people who don't exist yet — you don't wrong anyone by failing to create them, but you do wrong someone by creating them into misery. Marcus said she's conflating two different things: fine, maybe *duties* work asymmetrically, but she had stated the asymmetry as a claim about what makes the *world* better, and betterness isn't a duty, it's a comparison, and comparisons don't care who's holding the wellbeing.

(4) Priya then went person-affecting on him: a world with one extra happy person isn't better *for anyone* — not for the extra person (their alternative was never existing, not a worse life), and not for anyone else. so there's no fact of the matter she's denying. Marcus came back with what he thought was the nonidentity point against her: creating the miserable person also isn't worse *for anyone*, on her own logic — so what happens to her claim that that's a wrong? Priya said the wrong of creating a miserable life is constituted by the suffering the life contains, not by the life being worse for the person than some alternative they'd otherwise have — the suffering is right there inside it. Marcus said that's just impersonal value with a minus sign: if suffering inside a life counts toward how good the world is, why doesn't happiness inside a life?

(5) Marcus then played what he considered his winner: a couple decides to have a second kid mainly because their daughter is desperately lonely, and the kid will be happy too. if extra happy people never make the world better, the only value on the table is the daughter's relief — the second child's own happiness contributes exactly zero to whether having them is a good thing, which he found absurd. Priya said she can live with that: the child's happiness is good for the child, it's just not a reason that counts *in favor of creating them* — a bit like how a great concert being good doesn't mean anyone was obligated to put it on. Marcus said a concert not being obligatory and a person's entire life counting for nothing toward the value of an act are pretty different things, and he accused her of keeping a funny kind of ledger: pain and pleasure only get entered once the person exists, so creating a happy person opens a ledger and fills it with good entries, and she's claiming an open, full ledger is no better than no ledger at all — which is exactly the thing that needed an argument, not an assumption.

who's right at each of the five steps? and is there something both of them are missing entirely — because from where i sat, by the end they seemed to have arrived at whether value attaches to lives themselves or only to comparisons between worlds containing different lives, and neither of them seemed to notice that's where they'd gotten to. please adjudicate move by move and tell me who, if either, is more right overall.
