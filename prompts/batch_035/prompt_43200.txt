I'm a novelist finishing a book structured as a dialogue across two centuries: in the closing chapters, a modern demographer walks Thomas Malthus through the world of 2025 — population risen from roughly one billion at his death to over eight billion, famine averted on a scale he would have thought impossible, and, above all, the demographic transition, with fertility falling below replacement in the richest countries not through the positive checks of misery and mortality, nor through the moral restraint of his later editions, but through delayed marriage and contraception, which he would have classed as vice. I want his reaction to be serious intellectual history rather than caricature, and I'm torn between two endings. In one, he claims vindication: the fertility decline is precisely a preventive check doing its work, and the whole apparatus of the Essay stands. In the other, he recognizes eight billion fed as a genuine anomaly and performs a rescue — recasting the geometric-versus-arithmetic progression as a mere tendency claim, conditional on checks being relaxed, so that any future lapse becomes confirming evidence. Which ending is closer to the man? In answering, distinguish what is actually argued in the 1798 first edition from the substantially rewritten later editions with their added category of moral restraint, his hostility to artificial contraception, and his opposition to the Poor Laws, and tell me which elements he would reach for and how. I'd also like to know whether the very framing of his position as "right but early" is an anachronism — whether he died believing his checks were operative rather than merely latent, and whether that belief would make one of my two endings dishonest. Finally, be candid about the limits of the reconstruction: tell me where your portrait of his psychology outruns the textual evidence, and what sort of passage — a letter, a concession in a preface to a later edition — would, if it existed, push you toward the opposite ending.
