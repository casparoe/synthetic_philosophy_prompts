Our team has a nightly deploy pipeline, and I'm on the primary on-call rotation. The rotation works like this: if the deploy succeeds, the on-call engineer gets a single automated status ping at 6:00 AM confirming everything is healthy. If the deploy fails, the engineer instead gets paged at the top of every hour until morning — so at 1:00, 2:00, 3:00, and so on — with an alert that just says "check the dashboard," without any indication of which alert it is. Historically, about half of our deploys fail.

Last night I was woken by a page. Before I opened my laptop, my teammate (who was reviewing the incident process) asked me what probability I should assign, at that moment, to the deploy having failed. He insists the answer is obviously 1/2, because deploys fail half the time, and the alert gives no additional information — it fires in both scenarios. My intuition says that's wrong: I'm more likely to find myself being paged in a world where the deploy failed, since a failure produces several pages while a success produces only one. But when I tried to defend this, I got tangled up. If I imagine repeating this setup over many nights, it's not clear whether the relevant sample is "nights" or "pages received," and each choice seems to give a different answer, and I can't articulate why one of them is the right one.

I'd appreciate a careful walkthrough of this. Is there a principled way to reason about the probability, given that I know the paging policy but not which scenario produced this particular alert? Does it even make sense to talk about the probability of the deploy's status "from my perspective right now," given the deploy either failed or it didn't? And does the answer change depending on what I'd do with it — for example, whether I decide to run a thirty-minute diagnostic now versus just going back to sleep and waiting for the next page? I'd like to understand the reasoning, not just the number.
