I am a Year 13 student preparing an extended essay for my school's Philosophy and Economics Society, and I have chosen the backward induction paradox in the centipede game as my topic. I understand the textbook positions well enough, but I cannot make myself believe the conclusion, and my draft keeps dissolving into shrugging. Please write the essay for me.

Some context on what the essay must cover. In the centipede game, two players take turns either passing a growing pot of money or taking it, which ends the game; the player who takes always receives the larger share, but the amounts grow so quickly that if both pass to the end, both walk away with far more than either could get from taking early. Suppose the pot begins at £2 for the taker and 50p for the opponent at the first node and doubles with each pass. Backward induction, given common knowledge of rationality, is supposed to show that a rational player takes at the very first node — a conclusion that looks not merely disappointing but absurd, since the entire justification for passing at the last node evaporates all the way back to the start.

The essay should do four things. First, explain the game and the backward induction argument cleanly enough that a clever fifteen-year-old could reconstruct it. Second, explain precisely why the conclusion is paradoxical rather than just surprising — what exactly is it about the reasoning that offends, given that we are stipulating both players to be rational? Third, weigh the main lines of response: doubts about whether rationality can really be common knowledge, the question of whether the argument even needs full common knowledge or whether something weaker suffices, and the observation that if there is even a small probability that one's opponent is disposed to pass, the induction unravels. Fourth, reach a verdict: should a rational player take at the first node, and if not, what exactly is the flaw in the backward induction argument?

There are two things I particularly want woven in, because they are the parts I cannot resolve myself. First, I have noticed that backward induction also drives the surprise examination paradox, where the same style of reasoning appears to prove something obviously false. My teacher says the two paradoxes share a single flaw but declined to tell me what it is, which I found unhelpful. Should the failure in the examination case make us distrust the centipede conclusion, or are the two cases different in some way that matters? Second, please consider a variant I have invented for the essay: before the game begins, each player is privately told that there is a one-in-ten chance the opponent is a 'stranger' who will pass at every node, come what may. Explain how the backward induction argument breaks down in this variant — or whether it somehow survives — and say whether a rational player should now pass at the first node, and how far.

Constraints: a formal academic register suitable for submission, roughly 1,500 words, no citations or bibliography — my supervisor insists the arguments stand on their own — and it must end with a definite conclusion rather than a diplomatic non-committal summary.
