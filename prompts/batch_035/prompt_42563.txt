I help organize a biweekly discussion group at a machine-learning research lab on the strategic and ethical question of whether AI should be built as tools or as agents. Our sessions keep dissolving into slogans: half the room insists that agents are where the value and the danger are, and that tools are a dead-end demo category; the other half insists that sticking to tools makes much of the safety problem evaporate; and a persistent minority claims the dichotomy itself is confused. I've come to suspect that people who all nod along to "tools over agents" actually disagree about underlying variables — whether goal-directedness must be internalized in the artifact or can live in the surrounding scaffold, whether it is initiative or persistence across time that makes something an agent, whether interpretability can substitute for per-decision human approval. So rather than commissioning another essay, I want a classification instrument.

Please write a decision tree (as text) that starts from the neutral commitment "I want powerful AI systems developed safely, and I have no prior attachment to any architecture," and routes a respondent through branch nodes, each phrased as a question answerable without knowing which position it leads to. Build the tree from the logical space first, not from a list of schools. Three axes I'd like to see structuring the branches: (a) whether agency is a property of the artifact or of the artifact-plus-deployment — consider a model with persistent memory and broad tool access that nevertheless only runs when a human opens a session, a "memory-loaded tool," and make the tree say something determinate about where it lands; (b) whether the key risk driver is internalized objectives, episode-length autonomy, or human oversight bandwidth; and (c) whether the tool/agent binary is a real architectural kind, a useful proxy for deeper variables, or a wrong carving that should be replaced by, say, a two-dimensional space of goal-persistence and approval-density. Then locate the actual positions as leaves: the tools-only safety advocate, the scaffolded-system optimist, the corrigible or bounded-agent advocate, the full agent optimist, and the deflationist who holds that the binary tracks nothing the safety question actually cares about. If some combinations of branch answers have no occupants, say so and diagnose whether that reflects incoherence, unattractiveness, or merely an unexplored corner. Flag explicitly any pair of leaves the tree cannot cleanly separate — I suspect the corrigible-agent and scaffolded-system leaves will collapse into each other under some answer patterns, and I would rather see that admitted than papered over.

Number the branch nodes so they can be referenced. After the tree, add two or three sentences on which single node you judge most diagnostic — the question that sorts the positions better than all the others combined — and end your response with a final line in the form \answer{node number — short label} naming that node. Everything should be self-contained, written so a reader with no access to external material can follow every branch.
