I sit on the clinical ethics committee of a hospital preparing to offer mitochondrial replacement, and I've been given ninety minutes at next month's meeting to bring the committee up to speed on the nonidentity problem. The audience: two obstetricians, a genetic counselor, a lawyer, a chaplain, and two lay members. No philosophy among them, and no patience for it either, though they're very good at noticing when an argument smells off. The occasion is real: we expect consults in which a prospective parent asks whether going ahead is fair to the future child, and the honest answer requires the committee to see that the child who exists if we proceed would not have existed otherwise — that the choice is not between a harmed child and an unharmed one, but between this child and no child.

Don't write the explanation itself; plan it. What has to come first, and what depends on what. Whether the carrying example should be a two-option fertility case in which each option yields a genetically different child, one with a modestly elevated risk of a manageable condition — my instinct is that Parfit's depletion case is too distant from our consults, but tell me if that instinct is wrong and what my case loses by being this close to home. The misconceptions I should expect: "no one has standing to complain, so nothing wrong was done"; thinking the whole issue is about consent; sliding from "the child wasn't made worse off" to "the child wasn't wronged"; the lawyer's urge to run it through wrongful-life litigation as if the doctrine settles the ethics. Where in the sequence each one should be headed off, and which one is best left until the end and turned into the closing question.

Two further requirements. First, I need to know where the session will force me past the problem into genuinely contested territory — whether a life can be worse for its bearer than never existing, whether impersonal principles are the way out or a bridge too far, whether the committee's rights talk can survive the problem or has to be suspended for these consults. Mark clearly, in the plan, where I'd be reporting a settled point and where I'd be taking a side, and tell me how to signal that distinction to a committee that will want "the answer" — the worst outcome is their leaving convinced there is a received solution when what I'd actually be handing them is one position in an open dispute. Second, say what I should openly refuse to resolve in ninety minutes, and how I'll know by the end whether they've understood the problem rather than merely been unsettled by it.
