I am a novelist three drafts into a comic novel about the philanthropic elite of a mid-sized American city, whose chapters follow the members of one family competing for a civic honor called the Citation for Exemplary Moral Character. To keep the world dense, I have been drafting in-world documents — award citations, donor newsletters, an agony-aunt column — and I now want the centerpiece: a syndicated self-help column, the sort that runs in a Sunday supplement between the crossword and the real-estate listings, advising ordinary readers on how to become moral saints in five manageable steps. The column should carry a title in the genre's register (something in the vein of "The Five Habits of Highly Effective Saints," though you may improve on it) and should consist of exactly five numbered tips, each a sentence or two long, with no introduction or conclusion.

The difficulty, and the reason I am asking you rather than drafting it myself, is that the satire has to be earned rather than sneered. My editor holds a PhD in philosophy and will check the piece against Susan Wolf's essay "Moral Saints," so the tips should follow the actual logic of moral sainthood rather than invent vices for it: the way each non-moral excellence — the piano, the sarcastic wit, the afternoon wasted in the garden — becomes suspect at the margin of one's time and attention; the way the loving saint's warmth and the rational saint's dutifulness each curdle when made total; the way the saint's friends find they enjoy the approval far more than the company. A reader who knows the literature should find the piece fair-minded underneath the jokes, and a reader who does not should simply find it funny. Keep it deadpan: the column itself must never signal that it is anything other than sincere, well-meaning advice.

Finally, since my editor checks everything: append a short note, no more than four sentences, flagging where the column takes liberties with the underlying ideas or leans on a contested reading of them. In particular, keep separate what is broadly settled among philosophers from what is genuinely disputed — for instance, whether Wolf's conclusion is that moral saints would be undesirable company or merely that sainthood is not required of anyone, and whether that distinction matters. I would rather cut my best joke than have my editor tell me the piece presents one side's reading as the consensus view.
