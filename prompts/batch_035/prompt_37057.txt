In organ transplantation, hospitals operate under the dead donor rule — vital organs may only be taken from someone already dead — but they apply two different tests for death depending on the donor. A patient on a ventilator who meets the neurological criteria is declared dead while the heart is still beating, and organs are procured at that point; the law there treats the irreversible loss of all brain function as death itself. A patient on the donation-after-circulatory-death pathway, by contrast, is declared dead when the heart stops, and surgeons wait only a few minutes — typically two to five — before procurement, even though at that moment the heart could in principle still be restarted by aggressive intervention; the policy is satisfied by circulation that will not return on its own, not circulation that no means whatsoever could reverse. What definition of death does this two-track practice presuppose? Is death being treated as a single biological event that both tests detect at different margins, or as a status partly fixed by convention — and if the latter, does the practice amount to choosing whichever criterion lets procurement proceed, then calling that death? Reconstruct the implicit concept, say whether the two tracks are genuinely inconsistent or reconcilable under one theory, and tell me what would have to change in practice if we insisted on a single unitary criterion — either strict irreversibility of circulation everywhere, or a whole-brain criterion everywhere.
