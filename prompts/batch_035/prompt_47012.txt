I'm a retired entomologist — 35 years mostly on water beetles, the last stretch doing molecular work — and since retiring I've gotten properly hooked on philosophy of science. I've written a draft of a short essay on the species problem and would love comments before I try it out on my old department's reading group. It's my first attempt at anything like this, so be honest but gentle.

Here's the draft:

---

**Species are real, but they're measurement-shaped**

Every working taxonomist eventually hits the moment where the species concept stops being a definition and becomes a nuisance. For me it was a set of populations of a water beetle along a lake system in central Europe: morphology said two species (the aedeagus differs and that structure is usually decisive in these beetles), mate-choice trials said one gene pool (they interbreed freely and the hybrids are fertile), and a mitochondrial phylogeny said three lineages, two of them cryptic and one apparently not even matching the morphological split. None of these results was wrong. They just didn't agree.

The usual philosophical move here is pluralism: there are several legitimate ways of carving organisms into species, and which one you use depends on your question. I used to find that comfortable. Now I think it understates the problem, because the standard framing treats the disagreement as being about borderline cases — the pluralist's species groupings mostly overlap, and the concepts differ on the margins. My claim is that in cases like my beetles the groupings don't merely disagree at the edges; they cross-cut each other. The morphological partition, the interbreeding partition, and the lineage partition are not refinements or coarsenings of one another — population A and B go together on one measure, A and C on another. And this matters because of a contrast with higher taxa: clades, whatever their problems, are nested. Whatever tree you accept, there is one tree, and the taxa sit inside one another like boxes. Species, at least under the familiar concepts, do not sit inside one another. There is no single partition of organisms into species waiting to be discovered, because the candidate partitions genuinely cross.

So my proposal: species are real but measurement-dependent kinds. They're like wetlands, actually — a wetland is a perfectly real thing, but its boundary depends on which delineation criterion you pick (hydrology, vegetation, soil), and the criteria disagree in ways that are not just imprecision. Nobody thinks that makes wetlands unreal or arbitrary. I suspect species are the same: each circumscription is tracking something objective (genetic exchange, morphological cohesion, lineage independence), but "the species" as a single unitary partition of life doesn't exist, and much of the species problem is the mistaken search for it.

---

What I'd like from you:

When biologists or philosophers talk about species concepts disagreeing, do they treat the resulting groupings as cross-cutting in the way I describe, or as mostly coinciding with disputes at the margins? I've read a little of the pluralism literature and I can't quite tell if my "no single partition" point is a genuinely different claim from pluralism or just pluralism said awkwardly by someone new to the terminology.

3. Anything in the biology I've gotten wrong or stated too confidently — I'm an entomologist, so I know the asexual-organism and bacterial end of this debate only secondhand, and I gesture at it nowhere in the draft because I don't want to embarass myself. Should I?

Since I'm new to philosophy, please tell me how confident you actually are in each of your claims (especially the ones about what the philosophy literature says), and single out the one point in your response you're least sure about — I'd rather know where the ground is soft than recieve a smooth answer.
