I work on the risk-scoring team at a consumer lender, and I'm the one who has to write up our internal fairness review, so I could use an outside referee. Last week two colleagues, Maya and Dmitri, got into it over our default-risk model and I honestly couldnt tell who was right.

Background: the model outputs a 0-1 score for the probability an applicant defaults within 24 months. It's used two ways — a cutoff at the decision layer (score above cutoff, application denied) and the raw score itself, which the pricing and provisioning teams also consume. The score is calibrated within each of the two demographic groups we track — reliability curves sit basically on the diagonal for both. But the groups default at very different rates, roughly 12% versus 4%, and at our current common cutoff the false positive rate for the higher-default group is about triple the other group's. So people in that group who would in fact have repaid are getting denied at 3x the rate of equally creditworthy people in the other group. That's the setup.

(1) Maya said this is a fairness failure, full stop. The model denies creditworthy people in one group far more often than equally creditworthy people in the other, and being "accurate on average" doesn't change that. She wanted error parity before launch.

(2) Dmitri said hold on — the score is calibrated in both groups, which we verified. Given that, and given the base-rate gap, unequal FPRs at a common cutoff aren't a bias in the model. They're what an honest score looks like when the groups genuinely default at different rates. If anything, the only way to get equal FPRs at a common cutoff is to make the model worse at predicting. He said she was asking the model to lie.

(3) Maya said she knows the impossibility stuff — you can't have calibration plus equalized error rates across groups when base rates differ, except in degenerate cases. Fine. But that's a menu, not a verdict: when the arithmetic forces a choice, somebody has to make the choice on the record, and her view is that the burden of our mistakes should not fall 3-to-1 on one group. Calibration tells you the score is honest relative to the world as it is; it says nothing about whether the world's base rates are the right thing to be honest about, and a group's default rate is partly an artifact of history anyway.

(4) Dmitri said fine — then do it with thresholds, not the score. Keep the score calibrated since pricing and provisioning live off it, and set group-specific cutoffs at the decision layer where they're visible and auditable. Then he made the move I really can't evaluate on my own: he said the impossibility result she kept citing doesn't even block what she wants. With group-specific cutoffs on a calibrated score you can equalize FPR exactly — what the result blocks is equal FPR *and* equal FNR at once. So, he said, cite it correctly.

(5) Maya conceded the threshold point but pushed back twice. First, she said equalizing FPR that way just relocates the disparity: the higher-default group's cutoff rises, more of its actual defaults get approved, and that cost lands on us as losses while the FNR gap opens up. So it's a real trade with a price tag, not a free fix, and he should say the price out loud. Second — the part that's been bugging me since — she said those extra approved-but-defaulting loans become next cycle's training data, so our own decision policy partly maintains the very base rate the score is "honestly" calibrated to. "Calibrated to the world," she said, does alot less work when the world is partly us.

They eventually gave up and got coffee, and I've been stuck since. Please adjudicate move by move — who's right at each step, not just overall. Specifically: is Dmitri's claim in (4) actually correct as stated, or is he overreading the theorem? Is Maya's feedback point in (5) fair, or does it smuggle in an assumption that the counterfactual policy would lower the base rate (it might not — approving more risky applicants in that group could just mean more defaults)? Is she even asking for the same thing in (1) as the thing she ends up defending in (5)? I have a feeling there's a slide in there but I can't pin it down. Also flag it if either of them makes an outright mathematical error anywhere, and tell me if there's something both of them are missing entirely.
