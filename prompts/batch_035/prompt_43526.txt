I'm running the discussion section for a second-year course in ethical theory. The lecture the students have already had covered the sheer variety of moral codes across cultures, along with the point — which most of them absorbed — that psychology's picture of the normal moral mind was for decades built almost entirely on studies of the students' own population: Western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic. My section is on what any of this is supposed to show about moral objectivity. The students will arrive with a half-formed syllogism — cultures disagree about right and wrong; where there is disagreement there is no fact of the matter; therefore there are no moral facts — plus, from the lecture, the unsettling corollary that even their conviction that moral questions have answers may itself be a WEIRD datum rather than a neutral vantage point. I want them to leave unable to state the syllogism without immediately asking which premise does the dying. Please design the section rather than write its content. Specifically: (1) the sequence — I suspect the distinction between disagreeing about values and disagreeing about non-moral beliefs that values ride on (about what an embryo is, what happens at death, what a promise does) has to be secured before we can even say what the anthropological record is a record of, but I can't decide whether the reflexive WEIRD point belongs at the start, as a disclaimer about our instruments, or at the end, turned on the students themselves; (2) the one carrying case — I've been considering a funerary practice in which the dead are consumed by mourners as an act of care, so that what strikes my students as desecration expresses exactly the value they think it violates — but tell me what such a case must contain and what it must not, since my worry is that a case that dissolves entirely into factual difference would teach them that all moral disagreement is just cognitive error, which is the misconception I most need to avoid; (3) the misconceptions in the order they will actually surface — the inference from disagreement to no-fact, the assumption that relativism is the only alternative once objectivism wobbles, and the astronomy analogy ("people disagreed about the shape of the earth too"), which they will produce on their own and find satisfying in a way that worries me — and where in the sequence each should be intercepted; and (4) a single question, answerable in one sentence, that would tell me whether a given student can distinguish diagnosing a disagreement from resolving one. Finally, tell me what to explicitly refuse to cover at this level — I'm inclined to exclude Mackie's argument from queerness and any self-refutation objection to relativism, but correct me if one of those is doing load-bearing work I'm not seeing.
