Write a mock software post-incident report, about 600 words, in the deadpan voice of an engineering team's public postmortem, about a bug in the human capacity for introspection. The conceit: the team treats introspection as an internal observability tool that reports on the causes of a user's behavior, and the report documents incidents where the tool's confident reports turned out to be fabricated. Use the standard postmortem sections — Summary, Timeline, Root Cause Analysis, Lessons Learned, Action Items — and make every incident correspond to a real, well-replicated experimental phenomenon: subjects explaining a choice that was secretly swapped by the experimenter and defending reasons for a decision they never made; people reporting elaborate reasons for choices that were actually driven by the position of the options in an array; split-brain patients fluently rationalizing actions initiated by the other hemisphere; people carrying out a posthypnotic suggestion and offering sincere explanations for why they did it. Do not name the researchers or cite the papers — describe the phenomena plainly enough that a reader who knows the literature can identify each one, and so the humor comes from getting the findings right rather than from inventing them. The root cause analysis should arrive at the actual lesson of that literature — that the reporting system has no access to the real causes and drafts a plausible narrative after the fact, without being able to tell the difference — but the postmortem must never state this outright; let it emerge from what the team does and doesn't count as anomalous. At least one Action Item should quietly concede that no fix is planned and the behavior is considered working as designed, and somewhere the report should perform the very confabulation it is documenting — e.g., a root cause confidently given for the incident that the timeline plainly does not support — without the team noticing. The reader who knows the cognitive science should find it fair and precise; the reader who doesn't should still follow it as a plausible-sounding tech postmortem.
