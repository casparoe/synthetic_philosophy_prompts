A friend of mine has been re-reading the Grand Inquisitor chapter of The Brothers Karamazov and now argues, straight-faced, that the Inquisitor is right and that Christ, by refusing the three temptations, overestimated humanity. His case:

(1) The value of a social order is fixed entirely by the wellbeing of the people living under it.
(2) A person's freedom is valuable only insofar as it contributes to that person's own wellbeing.
(3) For the great majority, freedom is a burden heavier than they can carry: offered a life of free choice or one of bread, certainty, and someone to obey, most end up happier in the second. That, he says, is the Inquisitor's whole anthropology.
(4) A small elite can bear the burden on the majority's behalf — manage the bread, the miracle, and the authority, and answer for the result.
(5) So the Inquisitor's corrected commonwealth is better for nearly everyone than Christ's kingdom of free souls, and — the step he insists on — better, full stop.
(6) The same reasoning vindicates any competent, benevolent totalitarianism. He accepts this cheerfully; I can't.

He adds that the Inquisitor's despair at the end of the speech — the confession that he no longer believes, that the correction of Christ's work was done out of pity rather than faith — only shows how costly his love of humanity is, and so strengthens the case rather than weakening it. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? If the only respectable exit is rejecting (2), I want to know exactly what that costs, since it seems to say that freedom can be good for a person in a way that makes no difference to her, and I can't tell whether that is a coherent position or just sentiment. But if you think the trouble is more basic — that nothing in (3) and (4) licenses the move to (5), or that the argument rests on a misreading of the speech itself — tell me that plainly rather than rescuing me by manufacturing a premise to sacrifice.
