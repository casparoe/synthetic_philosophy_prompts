I teach a seminar on moral status — animals, future people, ecosystems — and this year a student's final paper was about video games, which got me looking at how mainstream games actually handle the morality of killing their non-player characters. The pattern is oddly consistent across titles: many games install a conscience. Dishonored tracks your lethality as "chaos" and darkens the world and the ending accordingly; Fallout has a karma meter that shifts when you steal or kill; Bioshock makes your treatment of the Little Sisters — rescue or harvest — the hinge of its moral machinery; Red Dead Redemption 2 has an honor system that changes how the world's inhabitants greet you. These systems clearly treat some in-game deaths as morally weighty. Yet the very same games ask you to kill dozens, often hundreds, of hostile NPCs in ordinary combat with no moral charge whatsoever, and the games respawn vendors and townsfolk after scripted deaths as though nothing happened. So the practice, taken as a whole, is radically uneven about which fictional deaths count.

What theory of moral patienthood does this design practice collectively presuppose? Is it, roughly, that a character matters morally exactly insofar as the narrative frames them as mattering — that standing is conferred by authorial and mechanical framing rather than by any property of the character itself, so that the same bundle of polygons and dialogue trees is a murder victim on one street corner and legitimate target on the next? Is that coherent, or does it collapse into the admission that what the systems really track is the player's character — honesty, cruelty, self-restraint — rather than harm done to the characters, and can that view survive the fact that the designers deliberately engineered opportunities to be cruel precisely so that you could choose them (and even, in some games, be rewarded for choosing mercy in a way that makes virtue a route to loot)? I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, distinguish what the design practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and identify where it is philosophically underdetermined — where several rival accounts of the moral claims of fictional beings would license the same mechanics. Then tell me what a game built on a different presupposition would have to change: one, say, that treated every NPC as equally weighty regardless of narrative role, or one that abandoned moral tracking entirely as incoherent applied to fictions. My student wants to know whether the game industry has, without noticing, a worked-out position in the debate her paper was in.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reconstruction and reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
