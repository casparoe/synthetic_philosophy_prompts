I'm putting together a small zine-style collection of philosophy dialogues for our reading group (grad students from different fields, so keep it jargon-light but dont dumb it down). Write a dialogue between two epistemologists who get stuck together on a hiking trail in a country where neither speaks the local language well.

One is an anti-reductionist about testimony: she thinks testimony is a basic source of justification, on a par with perception and memory -- when you accept a speaker's word, that isn't an inference from other evidence. The other is a reductionist: he thinks whatever justification testimony provides has to bottom out in other sources -- mostly an induction about how often people's reports turn out to be true, plus on-the-spot monitoring of the speaker for signs of deceit or incompetence.

Here's the situation that sparks the argument. A local villager, clearly in a hurry, uses gestures and a few broken words of English to tell them the footbridge further up the trail has been washed out and they should take the long way around. They can't verify this from where they stand: the bridge is around a bend, the villager has no obvious motive to lie, and there's no time to hike up and check before dark. Both of them end up turning around. The question the dialogue has to work through is what exactly justified each of them in beleiving the villager: the reductionist should say it's his background track record with strangers' warnings plus the absence of red flags in this particular speaker; the anti-reductionist should say the acceptance is direct, and that checking for red flags only guards against defeaters rather than generating the justification in the first place.

Make them both good at this -- neither one a strawman. The reductionist should get real replies in: pressing her on what she'd say about the chronically gullible and the pathologically suspicious, on whether his monitoring story can explain cases where the speaker's evidence is invisible to the hearer, and on whether "basic source" avoids collapsing into either dogmatism or a disguised induction. The anti-reductionist should press back on circularity: he can't run his track-record induction without trusting testimony about the track record itself, and she should make that stick rather than let him shrug it off. Have them actually respond to each other's points -- no speechifying past each other. Aim for roughly 900 to 1200 words, and don't end it with a clean knockout; it's fine, better even, if each of them walks away having conceded something real.

After the dialogue, give me a structured summary as a JSON object with exactly these fields:

- "scenario": one-sentence description of the setup
- "reductionist_position": one sentence stating his view as the dialogue actually developed it
- "antireductionist_position": one sentence stating hers the same way
- "strongest_reductionist_point": the single argument he put best, in one sentence
- "strongest_antireductionist_point": the single argument she put best, in one sentence
- "concessions": an array of objects, each with "character" ("reductionist" or "antireductionist") and "conceded" (what that character actually gave up during the dialogue, or "none" if truly nothing)
- "unresolved": an array of the one or two questions the dialogue left genuinely open

Please write the whole thing -- dialogue and JSON -- in American English.
