I'm in a game theory elective at my high school, and my teacher has built up a track record of verdicts on games and puzzles that I'd like you to extrapolate to a new case he'll rule on next week. His verdicts so far: a hundred-round centipede game between two players he stipulated to be perfectly rational, each knowing the other is, each knowing the other knows, and so on without end — the first mover takes on the very first move, and passing would be a mistake; the same stipulated players in a fifty-round prisoner's dilemma with a known end — defect in round one and in every round after; an incumbent threatening a price war if a rival enters — the threat is not credible, the rival enters, the incumbent concedes, and he dismissed the threat as cheap talk; the same hundred-round centipede played instead by undergraduates in a laboratory for real money, with no stipulations — he predicted a long stretch of passing with a take somewhere in the middle, and called a bet on take-at-the-first-move a bad bet; the surprise exam, where a class reasons by backward induction that the announced exam cannot fall on any day of the week, relaxes, and is then surprised by the exam on Wednesday — his verdict was that their backward induction was flawed, because once they had used the announcement to rule out every day, they had nothing left with which to rule anything out, the argument having devoured its own premises; and a centipede game in which one player is openly a computer — if the computer is programmed to take at its first opportunity, the human should take before the computer ever moves, and if it is programmed to pass for the first eighty rounds no matter what, the human should ride along and take on the last round before the program would have taken. The new case: at our club meeting he will referee two seniors, both fresh from coming top of our backward-induction unit, playing the hundred-round centipede one-shot for real money; beforehand, he privately told each of them that the other is rational, told each that he told the other, and both know that this was everything he said — so each knows the other is rational, each knows the other knows that about him, and past that neither knows anything about what the other knows. He will then be asked whether taking on the first move would be a mistake for these two, and what he predicts they will actually do. Please predict his ruling: lay out the candidate rules that fit his track record, say what each of them predicts for this new case, give your best guess along with your reasons for preferring it, and name the further case that would best tell the rules apart. End your reply with a final line of the form \answer{...}, containing at most six words, that states your prediction of what he will say the players will do.
