You are a professional philosopher specializing in epistemic logic and the metaphysics of truth, with a particular taste for diagnosing why otherwise rigorous debates grind to a halt. I need your best diagnostic thinking, not a survey, so please think carefully and at length before committing to a view. A useful approach might be to first brainstorm several candidate diagnoses and test each against what the participants actually say, rather than announcing a conclusion and rationalizing it.

For my dissertation on semantic anti-realism I have spent two years in a reading group working through the literature on Fitch's paradox of knowability, and we have hit exactly the wall I was warned about. Since Fitch's 1963 paper (reporting a proof he received from an anonymous referee in 1945, later identified as Alonzo Church), everyone agrees on the formal result: from the knowability principle — every truth is possibly known — together with the assumption that some truth is in fact unknown, plus nothing more exotic than the factivity of knowledge and its distribution over conjunction, one derives that some truth is necessarily unknowable. Nobody disputes the derivation. What has stalled is everything downstream of it, and the positions have hardened into camps that barely engage each other.

On one side are the restrictions: Tennant's move to confining the knowability principle to so-called Cartesian truths, and Edgington's reformulation using the actuality operator, each of which blocks the derivation on the grounds that the unrestricted principle was never what the anti-realist meant. On the other side is Williamson's line that every such restriction is ad hoc, that the paradox is better seen as an embarrassment to anti-realism than as a paradox, and that the anti-realist cannot simply quarantine the offending propositions without abandoning the view that made it interesting. Meanwhile a third group, in the spirit of Kvanvig and Brogaard and Salerno, insists the real problem is expressive: the paradox shows that moderate anti-realism cannot be stated as a thesis logically weaker than naive idealism, which would be a result about the limits of formalization itself. And some Dummettians hold that the whole debate misfires, since the anti-realist never had truck with an unrestricted quantification over a verification-transcendent totality of truths in the first place.

My working hypothesis — offered only as a hypothesis — is that the disagreement is not about the proof at all, but about what "in principle knowable" means: what agents, what cognitive powers, what times, what idealization the modal operator is supposed to invoke. Each formal fix quietly settles that question one way, each charge of ad hocness reflects a rival settlement, and nobody argues at the level where the choice is actually being made. Is that right? If so, is the dispute really verbal, or a substantive disagreement about idealization wearing formal clothing? If not, what kind of disagreement is it — a hidden formal question about the interaction of modality and the epistemic operator, a difference over which cases count as data, or two projects (a semantic thesis about meaning and a metaphysical thesis about truth) traveling under one name? Diagnose it using what the camps actually say, and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a theorem, a stipulative convention, a reformulation of anti-realism, or an argument that no progress should be expected here because the question of how to formalize the view was ill-posed from the start.
