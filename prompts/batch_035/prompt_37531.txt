I run a little podcast where we poke at applied ethics stuff, and next week I've got on a guy who's spent years defending what he calls "moral trade maximalism" -- basically the idea that almost any disagreement between people with different moral views is an opportunity for mutually beneficial trade rather than a fight, and that we should actively build institutions (markets, contracts, even apps) to make these trades happen. His favorite example is the pro-life and pro-choice donors who pool money into contraception access because both of them, on their own terms, prefer fewer unintended pregnancies to the status quo. He also thinks meat eaters should be able to buy "moral offsets" that pay vegetarians to stay vegetarian, and he's floated the idea that EAs with credences split across utilitarianism and deontology should literally split their donations to hedge, the way you'd diversify a portfolio.

Draft me around ten interview questions. I don't want him to just restate the view, I want to press on the parts that bug me and see if he has an answer:

- at least two questions on whether trading with someone whose moral view you find repugnant is itself a moral failure (complicity, legitimacy, "you're treating evil as just another preference"), with follow-ups keyed to whatever his likely reply is (I'm guessing he'll say something like "we already trade with strangers whose views we hate every day")
- at least one question on the difference between trading *between* people with different values versus trading *within* one person's own moral uncertainty -- he treats these as basically the same operation and I'm not sure that's right
- one on whether offsets degrade morality in the long run, like, does an offset market teach people that ethics is just a fee schedule?
- one question that tries to get him to name a trade he would refuse on principle, not because it's inefficient but because it's wrong

Keep the questions concrete and adversarial but not gotcha-y, this is a friendly-ish show. Also for each question jot a one-line note on what answer I should be suspicious of, so I can push back in the moment. If it matters, our audience is smart but doesn't know the philosphy literature, so the questions should be self-contained.
