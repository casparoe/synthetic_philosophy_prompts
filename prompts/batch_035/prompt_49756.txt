My study group spent most of last term on the justification of logic — we read the Lewis Carroll tortoise piece from Mind (1895) alongside some Hume, and then argued for weeks about whether inference rules can be justified without going in a circle. I finally wrote down where I actually stand, planning to send it around before our next meeting, but I have a nagging feeling the position set doesn't hang together and I can't see where. So here's roughly where I've landed: (1) the tortoise shows that you can never get from premises to conclusion by adding the rule of inference as another premise — accept p, accept if-p-then-q, accept the hypothetical that these two entail q, and you can still refuse the conclusion, forever — so justification of deduction can't be propositional all the way down; at some point you just apply the rule. (2) What does justify deduction is that it's truth-preserving — it's a reliable method, and that's a fact about the method, not another proposition, so the regress doesn't touch it. (3) I'm generally a reliabilist: the warrant of a belief-forming method is a matter of how well it tracks truth, and I want that one story to cover perception, memory, testimony, and logical inference alike. (4) Hume is right that induction can't be justified non-circularly, and I'm fine biting that bullet for induction — but deduction's epistemic standing is supposed to be different in kind and cleaner than that, which is part of why the tortoise is a puzzle about logic specifically. (5) I've also become sympathetic to the thought that what the tortoise really shows is that the tortoise doesn't understand the premises at all — someone who genuinely accepts p and if-p-then-q but won't infer q has failed to count as believing anything, because following rules is constitutive of having beliefs in the first place, not an extra commitment you take on top of them.

Two things bug me. First, I can't tell whether (5) is a natural reading of Carroll or me retrofitting my favorite view onto his dialogue — and if (5) is right, I'm not sure what work (2) is even doing. Second, whenever I try to say out loud what makes deduction "cleaner" than induction per (4), I notice that saying deduction is reliable sounds like a claim about a track record, and track records are exactly the thing Hume says you can't lean on. But maybe that's sloppy thinking on my part and the two notions of reliability just come apart.
