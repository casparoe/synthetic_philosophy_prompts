As part of our coursework in an upper-level seminar on rational choice and decision theory, our professor has asked each of us to propose one candidate question for the take-home final, along with a model answer and a marking scheme, and she will select the best proposals to actually appear on the exam. I would like to propose a question built around the case the literature calls Death in Damascus, but in a variant with parameters of my own choosing, so that the question cannot be answered by reciting the standard textbook contrast between evidential and causal decision theory. In the version I want to use, the story goes roughly as follows: a merchant in Damascus encounters Death in the marketplace; Death looks startled to see him, and when he asks why, she explains that she has an appointment with him tomorrow night, and that the appointment is in Aleppo, which is why his presence in Damascus surprised her. The merchant immediately hires the fastest horse available and rides through the night to Aleppo, reasoning that this is the one way to miss an appointment in Aleppo — and of course he meets Death there, since if he had stayed in Damascus he would have died there instead. For my variant I want to stipulate the numbers explicitly: Death's forecast about which city the merchant will end up in is 90% reliable, and the merchant knows this; survival has utility 10, death utility 0, and the journey to Aleppo costs 1 unit of utility, so that surviving in Aleppo is worth 9 while surviving in Damascus is worth 10. I would also like the question to press on something our seminar spent two weeks on: when the merchant's own deliberation is what Death was predicting, the recommendation seems to flip as soon as the agent leans toward following it — whichever city he is disposed to head for is evidence that Death is already there, so he wants to switch, and the switch restarts the problem.

Please write the exam question itself, suitable for a student answering in about 60 minutes, phrased so that it demands the following rather than a summary: a computation of the expected utility of staying versus riding under both an evidential reading and a causal reading, using the numbers I specified, with the actual arithmetic shown; a diagnosis of why the causal recommendation is unstable in this setup, including an explanation of where the relevant credence about Death's location comes from and how the agent's own disposition feeds back into it; and an evaluation of whether the two obvious escape routes discussed in the literature — restricting oneself to recommendations that survive learning that one is about to act on them (the ratificationist idea), and deciding by some randomizing device — actually resolve the case, where the coin-flip route should be assessed carefully in light of what the 90% reliability is supposed to cover (whether Death's forecast extends to the outcome of the device, and what follows if it does not).

Along with the question, please provide a model answer of roughly 600 to 700 words that works through the arithmetic rather than gesturing at it, and a marking scheme out of 20 marks built around five must-hit points, each with its mark allocation and a one-line note on how students typically fail to earn it. The scheme should separate an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves each requires — for instance, I would expect a strong answer to notice that the two theories can agree on the recommendation here while disagreeing completely about why, and to say what that fact does and does not show about the debate between them. The marking scheme should reward engagement with the ideas, and I would prefer that it not require name-dropping any particular authors, since the question should stand on its own for a grader who knows the concepts but not the citation trail.
