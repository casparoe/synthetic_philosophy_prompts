I'm taking a philosophy of science course and also work adjacent to ML, and a friend who does ML walked me into an argument I can't get out of. He started from the no-free-lunch theorems — the result that if you average a learning algorithm's performance over all possible target functions, every algorithm scores exactly the same as every other, random guessing included. Then he hooked it up to Hume on induction:

(1) Any method for turning past observations into predictions is a learning algorithm, so no-free-lunch covers scientific induction, simple enumerative induction, counter-induction (predict the opposite of the pattern you observed), tea leaves, whatever.
(2) Averaged over all possible ways the world could behave, all of these methods do exactly equally well.
(3) So there can be no reason, in advance of looking, to prefer induction to counter-induction.
(4) And you can't escape by pointing at induction's past successes: using those successes to justify induction is circular, and by (3) we have no standing to treat them as more than a lucky streak.
(5) So induction is no more reasonable than any rival method, and science's track record is a run of good fortune.

The conclusion is obviously nuts, but each step looks okay to me individually. My professor hinted that something slips between the "averaged over all possible worlds" in (2) and the flat "no reason" in (3), and also that the circularity charge in (4) might be less devastating than it sounds — though I didn't get her to say why.

Can you take on the role of a philosopher of science who takes the no-free-lunch results seriously rather than waving them off? Please brainstorm a bit before settling on an answer, then walk me through the argument step by step and tell me exactly which premise or inference to reject and what rejecting it costs — in particular whether I have to help myself to some assumption that the actual world is simple or structured rather than drawn uniformly at random, and whether that assumption can be defended without circularity or only vindicated pragmatically after the fact (and whether that after-the-fact vindication is worth anything). Finally, be upfront about where your own diagnosis is weakest: what argument or consideration would move you to reject a different step instead, or even to bite the bullet and accept something close to the conclusion. If your answer hinges on a technical detail of the theorems themselves (like what exactly gets averaged over), spell that out, since I've only ever seen them secondhand.
