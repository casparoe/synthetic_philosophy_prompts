Something about copyright terms has bugged me for a while and I want your read on it.

Here in the US (and in most of Europe), if I write a novel on my own, it's protected for my entire life plus 70 years after I die. The clock is literally tied to my body — if I live to 95, I get 95 years of protection, and my estate gets 70 more. But if I write the same novel as an employee, as a work made for hire for a publisher, the protection runs 95 years from publication (or 120 from creation, whichever hits first), and nobody's lifespan matters at all. Same creative act, same economic function, two completely different clocks.

So what theory of what copyright *is* does this setup presuppose? The life-plus-70 half smells like a natural-rights, personhood kind of view — the work is an extension of me, so protection tracks me and then passes to my heirs like other property. The flat corporate half smells like a pure incentive story — the term just needs to be long enough to motivate the up-front investment, and since companies don't die, you can't anchor it to a lifespan. But can a single theory actually make sense of both halves, or does the system quietly presuppose two different views depending on who holds the rights? And if you think it's really the incentive view all the way down, I want you to take that seriously: the incentive to create is heavily front-loaded (most works earn most of their money in the first few years), so on an incentive story it's weird that protection gets *longer* the longer I happen to live, and weirder still that my estate collects for 70 years when they contributed nothing. Maybe that part is just underdetermined — several theories could license it — but I'd like you to say which and why.

Then tell me this: if we rebuilt the term structure on a straightforward incentive theory alone, what would it look like? Would terms be flat, would they vary by industry or by work type, would there even be a post-mortem tail? And what would have to change in how we talk about copyright — e.g. all the rhetoric about authors' moral connection to their work — for the system to be honest about it?

Please answer in American English. And dont be afraid to just say the current arrangement is philosophically incoherent if thats your conclusion — I'd rather have that than a forced unification.
