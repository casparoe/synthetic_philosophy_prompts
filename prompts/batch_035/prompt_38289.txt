I'm a TA grading finals for a course on moral revolutions (the unit was built around Appiah's The Honor Code and some Bernard Williams on shame and guilt), and this answer has me genuinely torn, so I'd love a second pair of eyes. The question was: "Appiah claims that moral revolutions such as the end of dueling, footbinding, and the transatlantic slave trade are driven less by moral argument than by shifts in honor. Explain his distinction between honor and morality, and show how the dueling case is meant to illustrate it. Then assess whether the honor story generalizes to the abolition of the slave trade, and say what role, if any, Williams's work on shame and guilt plays in Appiah's account."

Here's the student's answer:

"Appiah distinguishes honor from morality by their scope. Morality makes claims on everyone equally, while honor is tied to a particular social world: it's the right to respect that attaches to a status within a group, so what counts as honorable for a gentleman can be totally different from what counts as honorable for a soldier. As Williams showed, shame is essentially about being seen — in an honor culture, morality is done through the eyes of others, since you only lose standing when others witness your failure — whereas guilt is the internal voice of conscience, which is why guilt belongs to morality and shame to honor. Appiah takes this contrast over wholesale and it does a lot of work for him.

The dueling case illustrates this nicely. For centuries everyone already knew dueling was wrong — it was against Christian teaching and against the law — and gentlemen kept doing it anyway, because their honor demanded that they answer insults with pistols. Moral argument had no purchase because the beliefs were already in place; what was missing was a change in the honor code. What happened was that the honor world of the gentleman contracted: once dueling was no longer necessary to being a gentleman, refusing a duel no longer marked you as a coward, and the practice collapsed. Appiah thinks ridicule helped drive this home — once the papers treated duelists as ridiculous rather than noble, the honor code shifted fast.

The slavery case is harder, and here I think Appiah's story mostly fails. Since the slave trade was only abolished once it had stopped being profitable, the real cause was economic interest, and the honor story must be wrong here. Appiah's response, as I read him, is that honor simply followed interest: once slavery stopped making money, abolitionists could afford to make slaveholders feel ashamed, so the apparent moral revolution is really just interests wearing an honor costume. The only genuine honor at work was that of the enslaved themselves, whose revolts — culminating in the Haitian revolution — destroyed the trade from below.

Footbinding shows the mechanism at its cleanest. The anti-footbinding movement of the 1890s didn't win an argument; it solved a collective action problem. No single family could stop binding daughters' feet while everyone else's marriage prospects depended on bound feet, so the movement had families publicly pledge not to bind and not to let their sons marry women with bound feet. Once enough people pledged, the equilibrium flipped. This is why Appiah calls these moral revolutions even when people's moral beliefs never changed: the central belief that the practice is wrong often existed for ages, and what shifted was the honor code that kept the practice running."
