I'm taking a seminar on democratic theory this term, and we've just finished the unit on rational choice approaches to elections. Before I turn in my position paper I want a consistency audit of the package of views I've drifted into, because I suspect at least one of them is doing damage to the others, but I genuinely can't see which. Here is the set:

1. The instrumental case for voting in a large national election is essentially empty. The probability that a single ballot is pivotal is so small that expected utility calculations about outcomes give me no reason to turn out, and I think a purely instrumentally rational agent could abstain without any irrationality. I take this to be the upshot of the Downsian reasoning, and I don't think the standard move of multiplying tiny pivotality probabilities by enormous stakes rescues the instrumental argument.

2. Despite (1), I believe there is a pro tanto moral duty to turn out and vote in national elections — a duty to vote, not merely a duty to vote well if one happens to vote. Abstention, on my view, is a (minor) moral failing even for the well-informed citizen in a safe state or a landslide district.

3. In consumer ethics, I follow the difference-making line consistently: since one person's purchase of sweatshop-made clothing neither causes nor prevents any harm, and one person's refusal to fly does not change the climate, there is no individual duty to abstain in these cases — not because the harms don't matter, but because individual contributions to diffuse harms don't make a difference.

4. My best guess at what grounds the duty in (2) is fairness or reciprocity: citizens benefit from the cooperative scheme of majority rule and its associated stability, and fairness requires contributing one's small part to sustaining the scheme, rather as one is required to do one's share in a collective enterprise from which one benefits.

5. I categorically reject expressive or communicative accounts of the duty to vote — the idea that voting is like cheering for a team, that it's about sending a signal, showing solidarity, or identifying with one's fellow citizens. If the only thing going for turning out were expression, I'd concede there is no duty.

6. I hold that the duty in (2) is not sensitive to how close the election is expected to be: it doesn't get stronger in nail-biters and weaker in foregone conclusions.

Which of these commitments are actually in tension, which only look like it, and what is the cheapest revision? I have my suspicion about where the pressure is, but I'd rather not steer you, so please treat the whole set as up for grabs.

For your response format: please give me a JSON object with the following fields. "tensions": an array of objects, each with "commitments" (the two or more numbered views involved), "severity" (an integer 1–5), "diagnosis" (a short prose explanation of the tension), "cheapest_fix" (a specific amendment or abandonment to one of the views), and "cost" (what adopting that fix would cost me elsewhere). "apparent_tensions": an array of objects, each with "commitments" and "why_it_dissolves" — please use this for pairs that look like they clash but don't, since I want to be able to tell these apart in the paper. "revisions_ranked": an array of objects with "revision", "cleanups" (which tensions it resolves), and "collateral" (what it damages or requires accepting).
