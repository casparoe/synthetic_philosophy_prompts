I'm a philosophy professor, and at a population ethics workshop last month two colleagues got into an argument about the procreation asymmetry over drinks. I watched the whole thing and honestly couldnt tell who won.

The first, call him D., defends the asymmetry: creating a person whose life will be worth not living is wrong, but creating a person whose life will clearly go well is not good in a way that makes it wrong to refrain — at most supererogatory. He opened by saying this is bedrock: any theory implying that couples with easy access to good conditions for childrearing fail a duty by staying childless has gone wrong somewhere, so theories must accomodate the asymmetry, not argue it away.

The second, R., came back with the pregnant teenager case. A 14-year-old who will conceive either now or at 25; the child conceived now has a much worse life than the child she'd have later if she waits. It's a non-identity case — each child exists in only one of the two outcomes. Intuition says conceiving now is the worse choice, and worse partly for that child's sake. But that compares existence with existence, so the asymmetry's own contrast (existence vs. no existence) can't be what's carrying the intuition.

D. replied that the teenager case is fully explained by the girl's own welfare: pregnancy at 14 damages her education and prospects, so the wrongness is a wrong to her. Keep morality person-affecting and the problem dissolves.

R. said fine, then hold the parents fixed: a clinic choosing between two viable embryos, one with a genetic profile predicting a much worse life, the other a good one. Choosing the worse embryo seems wrong, and not because of anything that happens to anyone who exists either way. If the doctor is required to pick the better embryo, there's a duty to maximize welfare at the point of creation — the mirror image of D.'s asymmetry. If picking the better one is merely optional but better, then creating better lives is good without being required, which is exactly the structure D. denies at the level of whether to create at all. Either way, she said, D. owes us a story about why "whether to create" and "which to create" behave differently, and she didn't see one.

D. muttered something about regret versus grievance and about lives worth living not being commensurable with other values, and then last call came and it broke up. Can you adjudicate this move by move — who's right at each step, whether the embryo case is actually a good argument or a distraction, and what, if anything, both of them are missing? I don't want a survey of the literature, I want a verdict.
