On Saturday I hosted two of my closest friends from graduate school, and what began as a conversation about how one bad experience can outweigh another turned into a four-hour argument about whether the relation "better than" is transitive. Elena, who has been reading population axiology lately, opened with a case in the style of what philosophers call spectrum arguments. Compare a world in which one person endures a severe, blinding headache for a full hour with a world in which that person instead endures a headache half as intense for two hours. She claimed the second world is clearly better: near the top of the intensity scale, halving the intensity of pain is worth more than doubling its duration. Now iterate — at each step, halve the intensity again and double the duration again — and every adjacent comparison is, she said, a clear improvement for the same reason. But after roughly thirty steps you arrive at a world in which one person feels a barely perceptible tingle, more or less constantly, for over a hundred thousand years; and Elena insisted that most people, comparing the two endpoints directly, would judge the world containing the hour-long blinding headache to be the better one. If every adjacent step is an improvement while the endpoints compare the other way, she concluded, then "better than" cannot be transitive, at least for outcomes, and the assumption that it must be is a mistake we inherit from treating value comparisons too much like arithmetic.

Marcus refused to accept this. Transitivity, he argued, is not an optional aesthetic preference: it is a structural constraint on any relation that is supposed to guide choice. If "better than" were intransitive in the way Elena's case suggests, an agent who ranked outcomes accordingly could be money-pumped, or would face choices in which every available option is one she has reason to reject — and a relation with those features cannot be the relation that "better than" is supposed to be. He added that her case is a sorites in disguise: each step is so small that intuition reports "no real difference," and she is charitably rounding each of those up to "better"; if we refuse to do the same with "one hair makes no difference to baldness," we should refuse here, and conclude that somewhere in the chain at least one step is actually a worsening. Elena answered that the money-pump objection targets an agent's preferences at a moment, not the all-things-considered relation among outcomes, and that her steps are not imperceptible anyway: the first few are about as vivid as value comparisons get, and the chain can be rebuilt with far fewer steps, each large enough to be clearly judged an improvement, so nothing in her argument depends on finesse. When Marcus replied that the case therefore shows only that our judgment about the endpoint, or about one of the vivid steps, is unreliable — that when structural rationality and case intuition conflict, we should keep the structure — Elena objected that this makes his position unfalsifiable: any recalcitrant intuition can be declared unreliable, so what looks like an argument is really a methodological bet. They eventually turned to me to referee, and I could not do it; I found myself sympathizing with each of them at different points in the exchange.

Please go through the disagreement move by move — Elena's initial argument, Marcus's money-pump and sorites replies, Elena's responses to each, Marcus's final appeal to structure over intuition, and Elena's charge of unfalsifiability — and say who is right about which specific point, where each argument is strong and where it overreaches, and what, if anything, both of them are missing. I don't need a verdict on who argued more skillfully; I want a point-by-point adjudication of the arguments as they were actually made.
