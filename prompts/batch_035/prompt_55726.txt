I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm at work on a long-form feature about the philosophy embedded in welfare policy. The practice I keep circling is benefit conditionality as it operates in the UK, the US, and much of Europe: unemployment payments arrive only if the claimant documents a set number of job applications per week, attends appointments, and accepts any "reasonable" offer of work, with financial sanctions imposed for missed interviews or refused placements. Meanwhile, people living on dividends, inheritance, or occupational pensions face no work-search requirements whatsoever, and the same systems that police the unemployed so closely exempt carers and the disabled on grounds that are never stated as an articulated principle. What theory of desert and responsibility does that regime actually presuppose? Does it only make sense on a view where the poor must earn their support in a way the comfortable need not, or is there a reciprocity-based or insurance-based story that would license it — and do the exemptions fit that story or quietly refute it? I'd also want to know where the practice is genuinely underdetermined, such that several rival theories of justice would all permit it, and what would have to change in the regime if its implicit theory were replaced by, say, the view behind an unconditional basic income. Please answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion, covering in order: the core view of deservingness the regime presupposes, the view of responsibility for unemployment it rests on, which rival theories it quietly rules out, where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, and what a regime built on a different underlying theory would visibly alter.
