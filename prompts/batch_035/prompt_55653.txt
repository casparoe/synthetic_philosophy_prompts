So, context: I got made redundant about a year ago and spent seven months on unemployment benefit while job hunting, and it broke something in how I see the whole system. Long story short, I've somehow gotten myself invited to give a talk at a regional meeting of employment-services managers and workfare practitioners — the people who actually run the job clubs, the back-to-work schemes, the sanction decisions — plus a couple of local councillors who campaigned on cracking down on benefit fraud. My thesis: conditionality (the actively-seeking-work requirements, the sanctions for missed appointments, the whole deservingness apparatus) doesn't actually do much to move people into jobs — what it mostly does is perform toughness for taxpayers, a ritual display that the system isn't soft, and the cost of the display is paid by the people least able to pay it. I want to argue we should drop conditionality entirely for a basic tier of support. My arguement leans on two ideas I've been chewing on: first, that sanctions select for compliance skills rather than employability, so they push the least organized claimants toward worse outcomes; second, that the deserving/undeserving line is doing psychological work for the majority (letting them feel the system is fair and hard-nosed) rather than practical work for claimants, i.e. it's a feature aimed at an audience, not a tool for the people it's applied to.

Include the question I'm praying nobody asks. And be brutal about which of my two ideas is the weaker one, and tell me what evidence or consideration would genuinely sink my position rather than just dent it — if I'm wrong I'd much rather find out before the talk than in front of that room.
