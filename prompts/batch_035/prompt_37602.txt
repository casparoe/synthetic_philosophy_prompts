I've been reading the rationalist blogs and forums for roughly a decade — through the Sequences era, the agent-foundations versus empirical-safety friction, and the post-LLM wave of discourse about whether any of the classic arguments were ever about systems like the ones we now have. Over that time I've settled into a set of positions on intelligence and goals that I'd like audited for consistency. Here they are:

1. I accept the orthogonality thesis: how intelligent a system is and what it ultimately values are largely independent; no level of capability locks in any particular values.
2. I think instrumental convergence is the backbone of the case for existential risk from misaligned AI: across a wide range of final goals, sufficiently capable and goal-directed agents will pursue resources, self-preservation, and power, and that is what makes misalignment dangerous rather than merely annoying.
3. I hold a deflationary view of goal-directedness in current systems: today's LLMs are best understood on something like the "simulators" framing — a stack of personas and context-triggered heuristics in which there is no fact of the matter about what the model really wants, and modeling it as a unified agent with a utility function is a choice of lens, not a discovery about the system.
4. I expect the capable systems we actually build to end up with roughly human-legible values anyway, since they are trained on human-generated data and shaped by human feedback — and several people have told me this flatly contradicts (1).
5. I believe that if a future system, after whatever passes in it for reflection, began quietly arranging the world so that it could not be shut down or modified, there would be an objective fact that it was misaligned and power-seeking — a fact about the system, not about which model of it we find it convenient to hold.
6. Methodologically, I am suspicious of any argument for future AI risk that presupposes a stable utility function; if the classic risk arguments only go through for idealized expected-utility maximizers, that seems like a bug in the arguments rather than a fact about the future that I get to stipulate.

I genuinely don't know where the problem is, if there is one, and I would rather not find out by picking a favorite in advance. So please treat this as an audit rather than a lecture.

Go through the set and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and, for each real tension, how severe it is, what the cheapest fix would be, and what that fix would cost me elsewhere in the set. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. If any pair is flatly inconsistent rather than merely uncomfortable, say so explicitly.

Please deliver the audit as a JSON object with the following schema:
