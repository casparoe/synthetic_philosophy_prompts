In my second-year seminar on theories of truth we've spent a whole term on the Liar paradox, and I've come away with the uncomfortable sense that the field has been running in place for about fifty years. The main positions were all on the table by the mid-nineties: Tarski's hierarchical approach (now mostly regarded as a non-starter for ordinary language), Kripke's fixed-point account from 1975 on which the Liar sentence comes out neither true nor false because it's ungrounded, Graham Priest's dialetheism on which it is both true and false and classical logic gives way to a paraconsistent logic, and the revision theory of Gupta and Belnap, on which truth is a circular concept and the Liar's status never settles into a stable extension. Everyone in the seminar could recite the standard objections each camp aims at the others: the gap theorist gets hit with the strengthened Liar (if the Liar is neither true nor false, then it is not true, so the sentence saying of itself that it is not true seems true after all); the glut theorist is charged with collapsing into triviality or with an inability to say what's wrong with contradictions elsewhere; the revision theorist is accused of changing the subject, since "no stable verdict" starts to look like refusing to answer. Yet I know of no one who has switched camps because of these arguments. Each side seems to treat its own burden-shifting as decisive and the other side's as a technicality.

My working hypothesis, and I'd like you to treat it as just that, a hypothesis: the disagreement is not substantive in the way a scientific dispute is, because there is no shared body of data that one theory fits better than another — every "datum" (our firm judgments about particular truth-tellings, the intuitive validity of the T-schema, the intuitive invalidity of explosion) is itself contested ground. Instead, each theory is a different answer to a prior normative question: when the T-schema and non-contradiction can no longer both be kept, which one do you demote, and what does that demotion say about what you think a theory of truth is for? If that's right, the question "which theory of the Liar is correct?" may be ill-posed, or at least not the kind of question that argument alone can settle.

So here is what I'm asking for. Diagnose the structure of this disagreement: is it verbal, a clash of constitutive choices masquerading as a factual dispute, or is there a hidden formal or empirical question doing real work that could in principle settle it? Ground the diagnosis in what the camps actually say, not in a neutral survey of positions. Then tell me what, given your diagnosis, would count as genuine progress — a revenge-proof gap semantics, a non-triviality proof for some paraconditional logic, a consensus on how to evaluate conditional theories — and whether we should expect any of it. Finally, please be explicit about what would change your mind about the diagnosis itself: name the kind of development or argument that would make you reclassify the dispute as substantive or as verbal. Keep the whole answer under 500 words; I'd rather have a sharp, committed diagnosis I can push back against than a menu of possibilities.
