I'm a retired physical chemist -- 35 years of running experiments, and now that I'm retired I've gotten really into philosophy, mostly through reading way too much of the experimental philosophy stuff online. I volunteer helping out with an intro philosophy course at the local community college, and I'm teaching one session soon on whether philosophical intuitions are as universal as philosophers like to pretend. Roughly 25 students, 75 minutes, no budget to speak of.

Here's what I want: an in-class activity where the students function as experimental subjects first and philosophers second. The idea is they should get hit with cases (like the Gödel/Schmidt case, or the fake-bank-deposit Gettier variant, or a free will case where the universe is deterministic but the guy still calculated his move) and record their gut verdicts anonymously, but before that, you design some way of splitting them into two groups that gets them diverging -- maybe different framing, different background text they read beforehand, whatever you think actually works, I don't want to prescribe the mechanism. The punchline I want them to arrive at themselves: they'll assume the whole room agrees with them, and then the aggregate numbers come out split or lopsided in a way that surprises people, and THEN I reveal how the groups were divided.

Then the debrief should map what happened onto the real cross-cultural findings (east asian vs. western responses to epistemic intuitions, socioeconomic differences, that whole controversy) without me having to lecture for half an hour. And since I've been burned in the lab before, I want you to be honest about what this activity can't show -- I know a classroom of 25 community college kids from one town is not a cross-cultural sample, and I know the original studies have been criticized for how the cross-cultural comparisons were run (translation issues, showing the vignettes to students at different kinds of institutions, that sort of thing). The debrief should face that head on, not gloss over it.

Materials, timeline, exact wording of the vignettes I'd hand out (you can paraphrase the classics, don't worry about matching any published wording), how to tally responses fast without tech, and a note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this. Keep in mind some of these students have never heard of Gettier -- the cases have to work on people who don't know the literature. Thanks!
