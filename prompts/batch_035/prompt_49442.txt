I'm a junior in high school, and my art teacher has spent the whole year issuing rulings on what counts as art, sometimes to the class's delight and sometimes to its outrage. Her verdicts so far, as faithfully as I can reconstruct them: Pollock's drip paintings, art; Duchamp's Fountain, art; the physically identical urinal in the plumbing aisle, not art; a bowerbird's elaborately decorated bower, even when a natural history museum displays it behind glass with a curator's label, not art; a machine-generated image that won a photography competition after a photographer typed a prompt, art; a five-year-old's marker scribble hung in our school's spring exhibition, art; a technically brilliant Vermeer forgery, still art even after the forger was caught; and sunsets, which she insists are wonderful and nonetheless not art. Next semester we visit a contemporary gallery, and I already know which works the class will demand verdicts on. First, an image produced by a neural network during a corrupted training run that no human prompted or chose until a curator found it in a log file, printed it, and hung it with a wall text comparing it to Richter. Second, the paintings of an earnest outsider artist, fully intended as art, that no gallery, critic, or collector will touch. Third, a painting made by an elephant with a brush and sold at auction for a wildlife charity. Predict her verdict on each, and say which rule she seems to be running. I can see at least three candidates: an intention-based rule on which something is art only if a person made it with art in mind; an institutional rule on which the artworld's treatment of an object confers the status on it; and a historical rule on which a work must stand in the right sort of relation to earlier art. My worry is that most of her past verdicts fit all three rules at once, so tell me which of her earlier cases actually do the discriminating work, and how. My private guess is that she cares most about intention, because of how she talks about Duchamp, but I cannot square that with her calling the machine-generated competition winner art, so treat that as a hypothesis rather than a conclusion. Finally, tell me which further case — one she hasn't ruled on — would best separate the rules that fit her record, and give me your honest view about whether her verdicts are tracking one thing at all or several things that happen to overlap so far.
