I'm on the committee of a medium-sized student society, and for years our officer elections have been run by simple first-past-the-post. After a particularly galling result this spring, we're reviewing the rules, and I've been trying to work out what I actually believe a good electoral method owes us. Rather than argue from labels, I've taken a friend's advice and collected my gut reactions to concrete scenarios. Here they are. In one, nine voters rank three candidates: four put A first (A over B over C), three put C first (C over B over A), and two put B first (B over A over C). First-past-the-post elects A with four votes, yet B beats A head-to-head by five to four and beats C by six to three. My gut says B should win, full stop; a method that hands it to A is defective. In another, 100 voters choose among a moderate M (40 votes), a more radical R (31), and a second moderate M' (29), whose supporters would all have gone to M had M' not stood. R wins under first-past-the-post even though sixty-nine of a hundred voters prefer M to R. That seems obviously wrong to me: a candidate shouldn't win merely because her opponent acquired a near-identical rival, and more generally the contest between M and R shouldn't be disturbed by who else happens to be on the ballot. Then a cyclic case: three voters in a committee rank options X over Y over Z, Y over Z over X, and Z over X over Y, so that X beats Y, Y beats Z, and Z beats X, each by two to one. Whatever else, I feel the method owes us a coherent output — announcing that the committee prefers X to Y, Y to Z, and Z to X would be an embarrassment — though I half suspect the electorate, not the procedure, is at fault there. I also react badly to two pathologies I've read about: a candidate who would win under our ballots, but who would lose if some of her own supporters moved her from second place to first; and a bloc of voters whose favourite loses when they stay home but wins when they turn out and vote for her. Both feel unacceptable, the first more fiercely than the second. Two more verdicts seem trivially right: if two voters exchange their entire ballots, the outcome shouldn't change; and if every single voter ranks one candidate above another, the group's result can't reverse that.

The trouble is that I can't see the principle behind this jumble, and I suspect some of the verdicts are quietly at war — the head-to-head one and the one about not disturbing contests seem hard to satisfy together, though I can't pin down why. So here is what I'd like. Tell me whether there is a single principle, or a short list of clauses, that delivers all of these verdicts; state it as precisely as you can, and say which of my scenarios motivates each clause. Then tell me what it implies for situations I haven't described — in particular, whether it pushes us towards a specific family of methods for the society's elections — and, crucially, be honest about which of my verdicts, if any, it cannot accommodate, and what it would cost me to give each one up. And finally, apply it to the case I keep relitigating: suppose the Condorcet winner in our election were a candidate whom not a single voter ranked first — everyone's tolerated second choice, nobody's enthusiasm — while a rival were the first choice of 46 per cent and the despair of the rest. Should the compromise really win, and does whatever principle you've extracted from my verdicts force that conclusion, or does it leave me room to waver?
