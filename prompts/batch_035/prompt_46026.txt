My roommate is deep into anthropics and walked me through the doomsday argument until I couldn't find the flaw, which annoys me. (1) I should reason as if my birth rank were randomly sampled from all the humans who will ever live. (2) My birth rank is somewhere around a hundred billion. (3) If the total ends up huge -- trillions of people, space colonisation, whatever -- then almost all observers have ranks way above a hundred billion, so my coming this early would be an astronomical coincidence. (4) If the total ends up small, say a couple hundred billion, my rank is unremarkable. (5) So my birth rank is serious Bayesian evidence that the total is small, i.e. that we go extinct or crash relatively soon. My problem is that the same move seems to prove too much. Run it on anything with a "count so far" and an unknown total and you get the same confident answer: I'm about two thirds of the way through the total number of books I will ever read, through my own remaining lifespan regardless of my health, through the number of times this kettle will ever be switched on, and apparently I should conclude none of those totals will be much more than ~1.5x the count so far. Also the reference class is doing something suspicious -- run it with "all humans" vs "all mammals" vs "all observers anywhere in the universe" and you get different predictions about the same future. So which step actually fails? If it's the random-sampling premise, why does it fail here but work when I literally am randomly sampled, like being dealt a card from a shuffled deck? If the premises are fine, is the conclusion just true and unpalatable? And is the reference-class arbitrariness fixable, or does any fix quietly build the answer in? For what it's worth I'd rather keep the Bayesian-looking steps (3) and (4), they seem obviously correct to me.
