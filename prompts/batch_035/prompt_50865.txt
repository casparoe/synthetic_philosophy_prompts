Two people in my program got into it after our reading group last week. We'd just worked through Hofstadter's Ant Fugue — the dialogue where the Anteater turns out to be a doctor to his friend Aunt Hillary, who is a colony, and the whole thing is a fugue on levels of description. I'm a philosophy PhD student (mind stuff); Maya's in the neuroscience program, and Dev does comp sci, swarm robotics-ish things. They stayed after and went at it for over an hour about whether the colony could literally have a mind, and I couldn't tell who won. It's been bugging me since.

(1) Dev started with the biology. Colonies already do a bunch of what we'd want a subject to do: there's the classic house-hunting setup where scouts check out candidate nests and recruit to them, and the colony commits once one site hits a quorum — basically a colony-level decision rule with a threshold, like a neural integrator but slower. Colonies have stable personalities too — aggressive colonies stay aggressive across years, even after every original worker is dead and replaced. And queens of some species live a couple decades while workers live weeks, so the colony massively outlives its parts. So we have persistence, individuality, and decision-making at the colony level, and if mind is a pattern, the pattern can live at that level as easily as at the neuron level. (2) Maya said that's all competence without comprehension (Dennett's phrase, she admitted). A quorum threshold is a nice circuit, but a thermostat has a threshold and nobody thinks there's anybody home in there. The colony's cleverness is real but it's the cleverness of a market or an ecosystem — the product of alot of tiny local interactions, not a property of anyone. (3) Dev answered that brains don't have a place where it all comes together either — that's the homunculus fallacy — and that there IS a colony-level medium: pheromone trails are an external memory and map that the colony writes and reads, and a trail network can outlast every ant that laid it. If distributed representation counts in cortex, why not on the ground? (4) Maya said: because in the brain, representations get consumed by further processing — there's a next level up that reads and integrates them. The only readers of the trail are the same dumb ants that wrote it, reacting to local gradients. Nothing inside the system ever takes a stand on how things are going for the colony as a whole. A colony-level belief would be a belief that no one has. (5) Dev said that begs the question — further processing just means more of the pattern playing more roles, and colony states do have colony-level downstream effects (a trail reshapes the whole colony's future searching). Besides, neurons only ever read local chemistry too; if nobody in the brain has the whole picture, then by Maya's standard no one has beliefs at all. (6) Maya's closing move was the one I couldn't get a grip on: you can't build a mind out of things that are already trying to be minds. Ants are agents with their own interests — workers sometimes lay eggs behind the queen's back, and ant interests don't always track colony interests. The brain trick works because neurons are not little agents; there's nothing it is like to be one, so the system gets to be all agent at the top. Stack a layer of minds and you don't get a second-order mind, you get politics.

Can you go through the exchange round by round and tell me who's right at each step — not just who wins overall? And what, if anything, are both of them missing? One more thing: if you think the whole framing is off — like maybe mind is being used in two senses here and the fight dissolves once you split them, or maybe there's just no fact of the matter — say that straight up. I'd rather hear its a confused question than get a polite tie.
