I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm at work on a long piece about the Kelly criterion — the formula that came out of Bell Labs in the 1950s, drifted into blackjack and then into quantitative finance, and is now being pushed by some of its enthusiasts as something much bigger than a betting rule. I'd like you to do the conceptual groundwork for me: a disciplined case analysis of the mathematician Edward Thorp, the closest thing the story has to a hero, run through the lens of Kelly betting and what its recent defenders call geometric rationality. By way of the theory: Kelly's 1956 result says that a bettor facing repeated favorable gambles should stake so as to maximize the geometric growth rate of wealth, and that betting beyond a certain multiple of the optimal stake is self-defeating — it drives long-run wealth to zero almost surely even though every individual bet is favorable. The newer framing I want to take seriously goes further: it holds that this is not merely a money-management rule but a constraint on rationality itself, that classical expected utility theory commits a kind of category error when it adds up values arithmetically across sequential decisions, and that the rational agent is the one whose choices maximize multiplicative growth across the whole sequence of gambles that a life or a career consists of. Adjacent to this is the ergodicity critique of expected value: ensemble averages and time averages come apart whenever payoffs compound, and the claim is that classical decision theory has mistaken the former for the latter. The episode: Thorp worked out in the early 1960s that the player-favorable situations in blackjack could be exploited by card counting, tested the system in Nevada on bankrolls staked by professional gamblers, laid it all out in a 1962 book that made casinos change their rules, and then spent decades running quantitative hedge funds that, unlike almost all their peers, never blew up. The standard contrast in the literature is Long-Term Capital Management, which in 1998 ran leverage far past any Kelly-like bound and had to be unwound in a rescue coordinated by the New York Fed. Here is what I need from the analysis, in order. First, the careful mapping: who is the bettor and what counts as a bet at each stage — the small, frequent, fluctuating-edge bets of blackjack versus the infrequent, enormous, estimation-dependent bets of a leveraged fund — what the Kelly formula actually prescribes in each setting, and whether the common practice of betting a fraction of the full Kelly stake to guard against error in one's edge estimate is still Kelly or an implicit retreat to ordinary risk aversion. Second, where the theory breaks: blackjack's edge varies hand to hand and is sometimes negative, so does the formula even apply cleanly to a fluctuating edge; and what is the theory supposed to say about choices that don't obviously compound — a journalist's decision to spend a year on one story, a scientist's bet of a career on one unfashionable hypothesis? I want you to say where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak. Third, what the episode shows about the theory, running in both directions: is Thorp a confirmation of Kelly, or would plain expected utility theory with a modestly concave utility function have told him to do exactly the same things, so that geometric rationality is a genuine rival to the standard view rather than a relabeling of it? Is Long-Term Capital Management the anomaly that proves the point, or does the Kelly critique lose its bite there, since if the fund had no genuine edge at all then no staking rule was going to save it and the failure is about belief rather than bet sizing? And what should I make of the well-known episode of the crypto exchange founder who argued, on linear expected-value grounds, that he would accept a fair coin flip that doubled the world's wealth or destroyed it — followed by the collapse of his own overleveraged empire: is that a real data point for geometric over arithmetic rationality, or too convenient a parable to lean on? Finally, two honesty checks. Thorp himself seems to have described his risk rules as practical discipline, not a metaphysics of sequential choice — flag where the geometric-rationality framing outruns what the episode actually supports. And tell me whether the ergodicity critique and the Kelly-as-rationality thesis should be presented in my piece as one view or two, since I don't want to fuse distinct arguments just because they rhyme.
