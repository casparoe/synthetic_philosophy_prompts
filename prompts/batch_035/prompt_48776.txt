You are to act as a professional philosopher specializing in aesthetics and the philosophy of art, with a strong sideline in value theory more generally. Please think carefully and systematically; before delivering any verdicts, take a moment to brainstorm freely about how the views below might interact with one another, and feel free to show some of that preliminary work before turning to the audit itself.

I have been asked to write a short position piece on authenticity and the value of originals, and before drafting it I would like an audit of my views, because I suspect something in my package does not hang together, though I cannot see what it is. Here is where I currently stand:

1. Supervenience. The aesthetic value of a work supervenes on its perceptible properties — for a painting, how it looks; for a novel, what the words on the page say. Two visually indistinguishable paintings are, as far as their aesthetic value goes, on a par.

2. Forgery is not merely an ethical or economic offense. A forgery is aesthetically worse than the original it imitates: its lack of originality is itself an aesthetic defect. This is why a fake, once unmasked, seems to look worse — and the "seems" is not an illusion.

3. The price premium on authentic originals is not mere snobbery, status display, or investment logic. If you offered me a visually indistinguishable duplicate of a painting I love alongside the authenticated original, it would be rational for me to pay substantially more for the original, and the extra payment would be for something of genuine artistic value.

4. Artistic value is experiential. The value of a work just is the value of the experiences it is able to afford to appropriately situated perceivers; there is no further residue of value left over once those experiences are accounted for.

5. A van Meegeren-style case. Suppose I spend years moved by a forged painting I take to be a masterpiece, and it is then exposed. My considered view is that my earlier experiences were exactly as valuable as they seemed at the time, and that the painting's aesthetic value did not drop at the moment of exposure — what collapsed was my admiration for the supposed maker, not the quality of what was before my eyes.

6. On the moral side: I think forging a painting is a serious wrong, paradigmatically a wrong of deception. Yet I also think that if a perfect duplicate were secretly substituted for an original in a museum, with the original preserved in a vault and the substitution never discovered, no one would be deceived in any way that mattered, everyone's experiences would be exactly as they otherwise would have been, and nothing of value would have been lost.

What I want from you: go through these six commitments pair by pair and as a package, and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be. Rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as an undifferentiated list. For each genuine tension, explain precisely what generates it and what would have to give — which commitment must be revised, weakened, or abandoned, and at what cost to the rest of the package. For each merely apparent tension, show how it dissolves on closer inspection. Finally, identify the single revision that would clean up the most, and be honest about what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set. Please do not lecture me on the general philosophy of art; treat my position set itself as the object of examination.
