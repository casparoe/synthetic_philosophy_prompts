You are a philosopher of mind and cognitive scientist with deep expertise in consciousness research — equally at home with the distinction between access and phenomenal consciousness, the classic experimental paradigms, and the running dispute between overflow theorists and global-workspace theorists. You reason carefully, resist reciting textbook positions, and when asked to predict someone's judgment you enumerate the possible rules before committing to one. Accordingly, begin your response with a short brainstorm: list, without yet evaluating them, the rules that could generate the four verdicts below, and only then proceed to the analysis.

I am a PhD student in philosophy of mind. Over the years my adviser has issued verdicts on whether phenomenology is present in a series of cases drawn from the empirical literature, and I have slowly realized her rulings are generated by some implicit rule I cannot quite pin down. Her track record:

1. Blindsight. A patient with cortical blindness in half his visual field guesses the location and orientation of stimuli in his blind field far above chance while insisting that he sees nothing there. Verdict: no phenomenology. The information is accessed by his guessing system, but nothing is experienced.
2. Sperling's partial-report task. A three-by-four grid of letters is flashed for fifty milliseconds; the subject reports having seen all the letters but can name only four or five; when cued after the display to a single row, she reports nearly that entire row correctly. Verdict: yes — she experienced all twelve letters; the bottleneck was in report, not in experience.
3. Masked priming. Words presented too briefly for detection nonetheless speed the subject's classification of subsequent targets. Verdict: no phenomenology.
4. The absent-minded driver. A man drives home preoccupied, negotiates the traffic competently, and later remembers nothing of the journey; asked, he says that something in him must have seen the road. Verdict: no phenomenology of the drive, or at most a fringe too thin to count; what guided the car was subpersonal processing.

Last week I showed her a new case, and she will rule on it at Friday's seminar. I want to have anticipated her ruling — partly to test whether I actually understand her framework, and partly because I have a small wager with a labmate.

The new case: change blindness with conviction. A subject studies a photograph of a beach for ten seconds. Asked immediately, before any test, what her experience was like, she insists she took in the whole scene at once — the color of every umbrella, the positions of all the bathers. The experimenters then run a flicker test: the photograph alternates with a near duplicate in which one umbrella has changed color, and the subject, given a full minute and every encouragement, never notices. Asked directly afterward how many umbrellas there were, or what color the one on the left had been, she cannot say — and she is startled, because she would have sworn she had experienced exactly that.

Please give me, in this order:

(a) The brainstorm: the candidate rules that fit all four of her earlier verdicts, including at least two that diverge on the beach case. Say which simpler criteria her track record has already excluded — in particular, whether the record is consistent with her running a strict global-workspace criterion on which phenomenology requires ignition and report, and why not if not.

(b) The prediction: for each surviving rule, what it yields on the beach case, and then your best guess at her verdict, with your reasons for preferring that rule over its rivals. If you expect her ruling to be mixed — some phenomenology but not of the details — say which rule each component of the mixed verdict would be evidence for. In the course of this, place her on the theoretical map: where the overflow view and the workspace view each come down on the beach case, and which of her four earlier verdicts pull in each direction.

(c) The discriminating cases: one or two further cases — she has a standing habit of ruling on anything we bring her — designed so that the surviving rules give opposite verdicts. For each, state what each rule predicts and what her ruling on it would reveal. Consider whether some variant of the blindsight patient would serve, and if so, specify exactly what would need to be stipulated.

Close with a paragraph of no more than one hundred words on the one thing I should keep in mind at the seminar whichever way she rules.

Aim for roughly 900 to 1200 words. Write for me, not for a textbook: concrete, unhedged where you can afford to be, and explicit about where your prediction could go wrong.
