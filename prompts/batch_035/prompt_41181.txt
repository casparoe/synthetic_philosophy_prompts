For a unit in my graduate seminar on philosophy of mind and machine intelligence, I want a thought experiment that isolates a precise disagreement about belief attribution to AI systems — not another round of "does ChatGPT really understand?" Construct a realistic, single-paragraph scenario in which a deployed AI system in an ordinary institutional setting (a hospital, a platform's trust-and-safety team, a workplace tool — no science fiction, and no perfect predictors or demons) has the following property: the system's behavior is best predicted by attributing to it a belief whose truth the system's own actions manufacture — say, the system acts as though a certain class of users or patients has a trait, and its acting that way makes the attribution true (they adapt, or get sorted, in ways that confirm it). The attribution must be genuinely predictive: someone who refuses the intentional stance and insists on predicting the system purely by its design or training details should do measurably worse. After the scenario, do the demonstration: state what the intentional stance says about the case, then what a tracking or causal-informational view of mental content must say — that view should be driven to deny that the system has the belief at all, or to assign the belief a different content, because the fact is not causing the belief; the belief is causing the fact. Then make the divergence practically consequential rather than merely verbal: whether we say the system believed the proposition should change who bears responsibility for a specific downstream harm to a person. Finally, tell me which structural feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to both verdicts if you sever that feature (for instance, if the users' adaptation is removed), and how a committed intentional-stance theorist would resist the case rather than concede that belief attribution here has gone wrong. The point of the constraints is to prevent cheating: a case that stipulates the divergence instead of generating it from the two views is worthless for my purposes, since I will be asking students to check each verdict against each theory themselves.
