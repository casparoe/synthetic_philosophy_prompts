My flatmate Sam, who finished a philosophy MA a couple years back and now runs a small newsletter about philosophy and AI, just sent me his latest post because he knows I'm taking a philosophy of language course this term. It's about whether chatbots should be allowed to assert things, and before the chatbot part he does a big run-up summarising this debate about the "norm of assertion", which apparently comes from an Oxford philosopher called Williamson. The gist of his summary, as best I can follow it: philosophers of language have basically settled that assertion is governed by one rule, the knowledge rule — you should assert something only if you know it. His evidence for this is that saying "it's raining but I don't know that it's raining" is literally self-contradictory, so anyone who asserts it's raining without knowing it has contradicted themselves, which supposedly proves knowledge is the norm. He then says the rival views (that you should assert only what's true, or only what you reasonably believe) all collapse into the knowledge rule anyway, since "if your assertion turns out false, that's exactly a case where you didn't know". And then the punchline: chatbots can't know anything, so every flat assertion a chatbot makes violates the norm of assertion, meaning properly designed chatbots should hedge everything — phrase every output as a suggestion or a guess, never an assertion.

Something about the post bugs me but I can't put my finger on what. In my course we covered Moore's paradox (the "it's raining but I don't believe it" thing) and I have a feeling the self-contradiction claim is sneaking past something there — my tutor kept insisting those sentences are perfectly consistent, just weird to say.
