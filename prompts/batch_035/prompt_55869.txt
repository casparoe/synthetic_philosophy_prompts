I hold Berkeley's view of money, as developed in his 1730s pamphlet The Querist: money is essentially a ticket or counter — a conventional device for conveying, recording, and transferring entitlement to real wealth, which is labor and its fruits. On this view nothing about the material of money (gold, silver, paper) matters intrinsically; metal is only a convenient register of the convention, and gold's value is itself sustained by nothing more than tacit agreement among its users. The position carries both a metaphysical claim (money is nothing over and above a system of reciprocal confidence, comparable to a shared ledger) and a policy claim (Ireland's poverty stemmed from a mercantilist confusion of money with the wealth it measures, so a national bank issuing paper currency could work, since what sustains money is acceptability, not metal).

Tell me what would count against this position. I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they would be, drawing on the following at minimum: episodes from monetary history the ticket view should predict better than the metallist alternative (successful fiat currencies, demonetization of silver, colonial paper issues) versus episodes that seem to embarrass it (hyperinflations, the flight to specie in panics, unbacked notes that never circulated at all); the theoretical worry that commodity money converges cross-culturally on precisely the scarce, durable, divisible metals, which looks like more than arbitrary convention; and the conceptual objection that the view cannot non-arbitrarily distinguish money from coupons, casino chips, or a ledger of favors between neighbors — with the further question of whether that indistinguishability is a fatal collapse or exactly what a conventionalist should say, given that Berkeley's own theory of signs arguably vindicates it. Also address whether Gresham's law favors the view or cuts against it, whether the position predicts anything its rivals do not (for instance, on whether a currency can outlive the state that issued it, or what happens when the convention frays), and which of these defeaters a committed Berkeleyan — or a modern chartalist, who inherits much of this picture — would concede as genuine evidence against the view, as opposed to explaining away as a failure of the contingent institutions supporting the convention rather than of the convention itself. Finally, an honest assessment: has the view been progressively qualified (from backing, to state authority, to mere acceptance, to collective belief that there is collective belief) into something that could accommodate any historical outcome whatsoever, and if so, is that unfalsifiability a defect in a theory of money, or simply what an adequate theory of a social kind must look like?
