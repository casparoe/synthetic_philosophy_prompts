You are a professional philosopher of religion with expertise in the problem of evil. Think carefully about what follows; you may find it useful to brainstorm possible diagnoses before committing to one.

For decades, theists and their critics have been stuck on what is sometimes called the heaven problem. The critics' argument runs: (1) the free will defense holds that God cannot both create genuinely free creatures and guarantee that they never sin, since a free choice cannot be determined to the good; (2) yet traditional doctrine maintains that the blessed in heaven never sin, and never could; (3) so if the blessed remain free, God can create free creatures who never sin — and the defense collapses; while if they are not free, then freedom was never necessary for the greatest good in the first place. Either way, the defense fails. Theists reply that heavenly freedom differs in kind: once the blessed see God face to face, their wills are confirmed in the good, and this confirmation perfects rather than abolishes their freedom. Critics answer that this is a plain concession — God can create free creatures who never sin after all, he merely does it later. Nobody has moved in forty years.

My working hypothesis is that the two sides are answering different questions under one heading: one side is asking whether a state of affairs is logically possible, the other whether we have any reason to think it actual or even likely. Is that right? Or is this instead a verbal dispute about 'freedom', a hidden disagreement about what an explanation is owed for which evils, or a clash of bedrock intuitions about the value of freedom that neither side can argue for? Please diagnose the disagreement using what each side actually says, and then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress — a distinction, a shift in the question, a formal result — or argue that none should be expected, and why.
