I'm writing my IB extended essay on the situationist critique of virtue ethics, and after a term of reading I've hit a wall I want to understand rather than climb over. The debate, as I understand it: psychologists like Milgram with the obedience experiments, and the Princeton seminary students who stepped over a slumped stranger because they were told they were late, suggest that behavior is driven by trivial situational features rather than stable character traits. Philosophers such as John Doris and Gilbert Harman took this to threaten virtue ethics, since Aristotle seems to need global traits that manifest consistently across situations. The defenders replied in three main ways: attacking the strength of the empirical evidence, arguing that genuine global virtues are rare but possible and that Aristotle never promised they were common, and reinterpreting the traits as local or mixed. And then the exchange just stopped moving. Both sides seem to agree on what the experiments showed, both seem to have read Aristotle carefully, and yet in the literature and in my school's philosophy club's attempt to reenact it, nobody concedes anything.

My working hypothesis, which I'd like you to commit to evaluating rather than just survey: the disagreement isn't empirical and isn't about Aristotle's text, but about what virtue ethics is trying to do — whether Aristotle's virtuous person is meant as a descriptive generalization about how humans actually behave or as a normative ideal nobody has to instantiate for the theory to work. If that's right, then situationists and virtue ethicists are answering different questions under one heading, and the debate should be dissolved rather than won. But I can also see a rival diagnosis: that there's a hidden empirical question about how much cross-situational consistency people actually display, and both sides are quietly betting on opposite answers that current social psychology can't settle.

Please give me a definite diagnosis: say which of these is the real structure of the disagreement, or propose a better one if neither fits. Explain what each side's own words commit them to, and tell me concretely what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a kind of study, a reinterpretation — or argue that no progress is possible and why. I need to take a position in my essay, so don't lay out the options and leave the choice to me; commit to one and defend it, and flag honestly where your reading of Aristotle is contestable.
