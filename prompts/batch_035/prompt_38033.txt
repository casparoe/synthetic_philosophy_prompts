Write a dialogue between two members of a city government's ethics advisory panel: one a threshold lexical negative utilitarian, the other a classical hedonistic utilitarian.

The scenario they must work through: the panel is deciding between two uses of the same limited budget. The first would fund a large annual riverside festival that independent surveys project will produce a mild, brief enjoyment for roughly 400,000 residents. The second would fully fund a program of implanted pain-control pumps for around 150 patients with severe, treatment-resistant chronic pain — pain that is not life-threatening but is unrelieved by ordinary medication and that those patients describe as dominating every hour of their lives. The hedonistic utilitarian's rough model says the festival wins by a wide margin on aggregate welfare; the negative utilitarian insists the two figures were never on the same scale to begin with.

The negative utilitarian's position is that there is a threshold of suffering above which no quantity of mild pleasure, however large, could compensate: such pain is lexically prior, and the only morally decisive comparison is among the bads themselves. The hedonistic utilitarian accepts no such threshold: pleasure and pain are on a single scale, trade-offs are always possible, and refusing them is a failure of consistency rather than moral seriousness.

Let the dialogue be a genuine argument, not a lecture. The hedonist should press hard on the threshold itself — why this degree of pain and not one notch less, whether the view collapses under sorites-style pressure, whether "lexically prior" is just a refusal to answer the question. The negative utilitarian should press back on what aggregation is doing in the festival case — whether mild pleasures can be summed into something that "outweighs" an agony anyone would trade their whole recreational life to avoid, and what consent and the asymmetry between causing relief and causing pleasure have to do with it. Both should concede what their opponent forces them to concede. Do not end with a tidy conversion of either side; end with the two of them stating as precisely as they can exactly what would have to be true for the dispute to be settled.
